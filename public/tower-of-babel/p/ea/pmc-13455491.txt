Investigating the relationship between psychophysiological parameters and behavioral patterns in children aged 7–10 years during dental treatment
Authors: Aida Mehdipour, Mohammad Hassan Kalantar Neyestanaki, Masumeh Zamanlu, Abolfazl Ahmadi, Mohammad Aghaali, Ali Saleh, Mojtaba Hossein Nattaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between psychophysiological parameters and observed behavior, assessed using the Frankl scale, in children undergoing dental treatment. A secondary objective was to evaluate children’s self-reported discomfort using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).Results descriptionPost-treatment VAS scores increased significantly (P = 0.003), with the Frankl 2 behavior group showing the greatest increase (P = 0.001). Significant changes were observed in HRV parameters, including VLF (P = 0.005), LF (P = 0.014), HF (P = 0.024), and LF/HF ratio (P = 0.022), while SDNN, PNN50, and heart rate showed no significant differences. GSR responses varied by behavioral group, showing a significant positive shift in the Frankl 3 group and a negative shift in the Frankl 2 group (P < 0.05). Among clinical variables, VLF differed significantly according to treatment type, with the highest values observed during dental fillings (P = 0.015). These findings suggest that combining psychophysiological indicators with behavioral assessment provides a more comprehensive understanding of pediatric dental anxiety.

Background

Dental anxiety is highly prevalent among children and poses significant challenges to dental care, with nearly one in five children exhibiting disruptive behaviors that compromise cooperation, prolong treatment, and increase clinical risks. Poorly managed anxiety also contributes to avoidance of dental care, worsening oral health and reinforcing fear-related behaviors, highlighting the need for reliable assessment and management strategies in pediatric dentistry [1]. Dental anxiety is a multifactorial condition influenced by psychological, environmental, and biological factors, including personality traits, previous negative experiences, and misconceptions about dental procedures [2–4]. Anxiety and behavior during dental treatment are commonly assessed using observational tools such as the Frankl scale and self-report measures like the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) [5]. Although useful, these methods are inherently subjective, relying on clinician judgment or the child’s ability to express distress, and may not accurately reflect internal anxiety states. Consequently, objective and quantifiable measures are needed to complement traditional assessments [6, 7].

Dental procedures can elicit marked autonomic nervous system (ANS) responses, making physiological monitoring particularly relevant in children [8]. Psychophysiology examines interactions between psychological states and physiological processes and has benefited from advances in autonomic neuroscience and biofeedback-based interventions [9, 10]. Common physiological responses to dental stress include alterations in heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), galvanic skin response (GSR), and other autonomic indicators [11, 12]. HRV and GSR provide non-invasive, real-time measures of autonomic activity: GSR reflects sympathetic arousal, while HRV captures sympatho-vagal balance through frequency-domain indices (VLF, LF, HF, LF/HF ratio) and time-domain measures such as SDNN and PNN50 [11–14].

HRV has been supported by many anxiety disorder studies as a candidate biomarker indicating changes in psychological symptoms. HF is often regarding as parasympathetic-specific indices whereas LF indicates more sympathetic activity than HF [15].

Technological advancements have facilitated their use in clinical and pediatric settings, and biofeedback interventions targeting autonomic regulation have been approved for anxiety reduction [16, 17]. Previous studies have demonstrated associations between physiological responses and dental anxiety, reporting changes in HR and GSR during stressful procedures [18–20]. However, most research has focused on isolated physiological markers without systematically integrating behavioral observations [21, 22]. Given the limitations of subjective measures and the scarcity of integrative approaches, this study aimed to simultaneously assess HRV indices, GSR, and VAS scores and examine their relationship with Frankl behavioral ratings in children undergoing dental treatment. A secondary objective was to evaluate self-reported distress using VAS. Children aged 7–10 years were selected in this study because this age range represents a critical developmental stage in which coping mechanisms and autonomic regulation are still maturing. They are typically capable of understanding instructions, cooperating with physiological measurements, and reliably expressing behavioral responses, while still exhibiting measurable dental anxiety.

The null hypothesis assumed no significant association between psychophysiological parameters and behavioral patterns in children aged 7–10 years.

Methods

Study Design and Participants: This exploratory cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between July and November 2024 in Qom, Iran, and was designed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The initial sample size was estimated based on the study by Lueken et al. [23], using the formula for comparing means, targeting 25 participants per Frankl behavioral category with a Type I error rate of 5% and a Type II error rate of 10% [24]. Due to limited eligibility during the study period, this distribution could not be fully achieved. Ultimately, 40 children aged 7–10 years who were undergoing dental treatment at a public dental center in Qom were enrolled after obtaining written informed consent from their legal guardians and verbal assent from the children.

Clinical Setting and Procedure: Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, prior dental experiences, type of dental treatment, use of local anesthesia, and behavioral patterns, were recorded. All dental treatments, behavioral scoring, and data collection were performed by a highly experienced and calibrated experienced pediatric dentist to minimize examiner-related bias and inter-examiner variability. Sessions were conducted in a quiet dental treatment room maintained at 22–27 °C, with an average session duration of 77.8 min. To reduce interference with psychophysiological measurements, external stimuli, positional changes, and electrical or mechanical noise were minimized. To control for circadian influences, treatments were scheduled around noon.

Behavioral management was standardized using the Tell-Show-Do technique, whereby procedures were explained using age-appropriate language, demonstrated on a safe surface, and then performed as described. An expert pediatric dentist evaluated children’s behavior and anxiety, while recordings of psychophysiological variables were blinded to the anesthetic technique and dental procedure used [25].

Assessment of child’s anxiety: Children’s subjective anxiety and discomfort during the dental session were assessed using a 0–10 Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), where 0 represented complete comfort and 10 indicated extreme discomfort. VAS is a validated and widely used subjective tool in pediatric clinical research for assessing pain and emotional distress due to its simplicity and intuitive nature [5] (Fig. 1).

Measurement of psychophysiological Parameters: Psychophysiological measures included galvanic skin response (GSR) and heart rate variability (HRV). GSR, an indicator of sympathetic nervous system activity sensitive to emotional arousal, was recorded as electrodermal activity. Skin conductance was measured using the BioLine system (Medinateb Co., Tehran, Iran) with Ag/AgCl electrodes placed on the palmar surface of the non-dominant hand (index and middle fingers). Skin preparation, electrode placement, stabilization, and signal acquisition followed standardized manufacturer guidelines and were conducted by a trained psychophysiology researcher [26]. Sensors were placed on the non-dominant hand and secured using medical-grade adhesive to minimize displacement. Children were gently instructed to limit hand movements and measurements were performed in a controlled clinical environment to reduce external disturbances. However any child who exhibited hand movements, crying, or physical resistance during the procedure was excluded from the study.

HRV metrics included Very Low Frequency (VLF), Low Frequency (LF), High Frequency (HF), heart rate (HR), and the LF/HF ratio, reflecting autonomic balance. Time-domain HRV indices included the Standard Deviation of Normal-to-Normal intervals (SDNN) and the Percentage of Differences Between Normal-to-Normal intervals exceeding 50 ms (PNN50), both commonly used indicators of autonomic regulation.

All parameters were recorded before and after dental treatment under three conditions: resting state, cognitive challenge, and post-challenge. During the resting state, children lay quietly on the dental chair prior to intervention. The cognitive challenge consisted of counting backward from 50, a validated mental stressor shown to elicit sympathetic activation in school-aged children [27, 28]. Measurements were repeated during the post-challenge period while the child remained in the same position. For each condition, three recordings were obtained and averaged. Two GSR variables—peak and mean levels—were extracted [23, 29, 30]. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using the BioLine system with blood volume pulse and GSR sensors,and Biosees Evo software,which applies advanced algorithms to derive numerical outputs.

Behavioral Assessment: Children’s behavior during dental treatment was assessed using the Frankl Behavioral Rating Scale,a validated and reliable tool widely used in pediatric dentistry. This scale categorizes behavior into four levels ranging from highly cooperative to extremely uncooperative, providing a structured framework for behavioral evaluation [31, 32] (Fig. 2).

Blinding Procedure: The researcher responsible for analyzing psychophysiological data was blinded to Frankl ratings and VAS scores to minimize analytical bias. Potential clinical assessment bias was further reduced through standardized procedures and use of validated measurement tools.

All data segments affected by excessive motion artifacts or loss of electrode contact were excluded.

On average, approximately 10–20% of the recorded data were excluded due to artifacts, which falls within the typical range reported in psychophysiological studies. In the present dataset, the mean rejection rate was approximately ~ 15% (SD ≈ 5%) across participants, indicating acceptable data quality.

Overall, these preprocessing steps ensured that only high-quality, artifact-free data were included in subsequent neurofeedback analyses [33, 34].

Bias: The risk of bias for the clinical assessment was limited by the clinician’s judgment using valid and reliable methods to assess children’s psychophysiological parameters, pain and discomfort and behavior patterns during dental setting.

Variables: Primary outcomes included psychophysiological parameters (GSR, HRV indices: VLF, LF, HF, LF/HF ratio, SDNN, and PNN50) measured pre- and post-treatment across the three experimental conditions. VAS scores were included as a secondary outcome to assess subjective distress and to compare self-reported anxiety with clinician-rated behavior. Additional variables included demographic characteristics, prior dental experiences, type of dental treatment, and local anesthesia.

Statistical Analysis: Data were analyzed using Biosees Evo software and SPSS version 28.0. Paired t-tests assessed within-subject pre- and post-treatment differences. Unpaired t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to evaluate associations between psychophysiological parameters and clinical or behavioral variables. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

A significant interaction was observed between behavioral category (Frankl scale) and changes in GSR across treatment conditions (Fig. 3). While no significant overall change in GSR was observed (Table 3), a striking pattern emerged when children were grouped by their Frankl score (Table 4; Fig. 3). Specifically, children in the Positive behavior group exhibited significant increases in GSR from pre- to post-treatment, indicating heightened sympathetic activation across the assessed conditions. In contrast, children in both the Negative and Definitely positive groups demonstrated decreases in GSR values, reflecting reduced sympathetic responses. All between-group differences reached statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Discussion

The present study investigated the association between psychophysiological parameters and behavioral responses in children during dental treatment. The null hypothesis was partially rejected, as significant relationships were identified between Frankl behavioral categories and changes in galvanic skin response (GSR), as well as between dental treatment type and the very low frequency (VLF) component of heart rate variability (HRV). In contrast, most cardiac HRV indices did not demonstrate significant associations with behavioral or demographic variables. Given the high prevalence of dental anxiety in children, these findings underscore the value of psychophysiological measures for understanding stress and discomfort during dental care and for improving patient-centered management strategies. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) proved to be a simple and reliable tool for assessing children’s perceived pain and anxiety during dental procedures. Higher VAS scores reflected greater discomfort, and the concurrent use of VAS with Frankl behavioral ratings enabled cross-validation of observed behavior with children’s subjective experiences, supporting the reliability of behavioral categorizations in pediatric dental settings. In the present study, VAS scores increased during treatment, with the greatest rise observed in the Negative Frankl group, a moderate increase in the Positive group, and a decrease in the Definitely Positive group, indicating distinct stress profiles across behavioral categories [35]. Children undergoing procedures requiring local anesthesia exhibited higher post-treatment VAS scores, consistent with the findings of Alshatrat et al. (2022), who reported increased pain perception during invasive procedures, despite demonstrating that virtual reality distraction could significantly reduce pain intensity [36]. In contrast, no anxiety-reducing interventions were applied in the present study, which may explain the overall increase in VAS scores. Similarly, Yee et al. (2017) showed that children using the electronic Self-Assessment of Anxiety and Information for Dentists (eSAID) system experienced reduced VAS scores, whereas controls showed increased anxiety during treatment [37]. Conversely, Jalili et al. (2019) found no significant differences in VAS scores between repeated dental sessions involving local anesthesia, suggesting variability in children’s pain perception and coping strategies [38]. Our findings are supported by the literature. The waning effect of local anesthesia or not providing 100% anesthesia may lead to increased perception of pain or discomfort after treatment [39]. Additionally, Cognitive and emotional fatigue following dental treatment may elevate perceived distress or Children may retrospectively evaluate the experience more negatively after completion, particularly in anxiety-provoking contexts [40].

Psychophysiological monitoring enables objective, quantitative assessment of anxiety during dental treatment. Among these measures, GSR is a sensitive indicator of sympathetic nervous system activity and emotional arousal. GSR reflects eccrine sweat gland activity, which is modulated by sympathetic activation through catecholamine release, leading to increased skin conductance during stress or pain [41–43]. Although no significant overall change in GSR was observed before and after treatment, notable differences emerged across Frankl behavioral groups. The Definitely Positive group demonstrated reduced GSR levels, consistent with lower VAS scores and diminished stress. Interestingly, the Negative group also showed decreased GSR despite exhibiting high VAS scores and uncooperative behavior.

This apparent paradox suggests that reduced electrodermal activity does not necessarily indicate low anxiety. As described by Barlow, modest GSR reductions may reflect relaxation, whereas pronounced decreases may indicate extreme stress accompanied by sympathetic withdrawal or shutdown, depending on clinical context [1].

Rather than reflecting an absence of emotional processing, the observed GSR decrease in the Negative Frankl group likely represents altered central–autonomic coupling. Electrodermal responses are driven by sympathetic outflow regulated by limbic and brainstem circuits, and attenuation may result from central hypoarousal, rapid habituation, attentional disengagement, or top-down inhibitory processes [44]. These mechanisms differ in their physiological and neural signatures, emphasizing the need for additional analyses, such as baseline skin conductance levels, habituation slopes, and correlations with HRV or subjective measures, to clarify underlying processes. Decreased skin conductance may also reflect habituation, defensive inhibition, tonic immobility, or sympathetic exhaustion when children enter the clinical environment with high baseline arousal, limiting further phasic responses [45]. Such patterns are associated with distinct neural substrates and autonomic profiles involving amygdala–brainstem circuits or prefrontal regulatory mechanisms. Importantly, methodological artifacts were excluded as explanations for reduced GSR, as data quality was continuously monitored and recordings with noise or instability were excluded according to established electrodermal activity guidelines [46–48]. That phenomena such as reduced electrodermal activity following acute stress exposure have been described in psychophysiology as part of adaptation or recovery processes [49]. However, that invoking mechanisms such as “shutdown” or “tonic immobility” in a routine dental setting is likely overstated. Despite implementing artifact rejection protocols, it is possible that segments reflecting peak distress were disproportionately excluded due to poor signal quality. This may have resulted in an underestimation of true sympathetic activation in this group. Therefore, the observed pattern is more plausibly explained by technical and measurement-related limitations caused by excessive movement or loss of electrode-skin contact during the child’s resistance, rather than a true inverse autonomic response.

No significant associations were found between GSR and demographic or most clinical variables. Şimşek et al. (2021) reported increased GSR and HR with escalating anxiety during dental procedures, particularly extractions, and observed age-related effects in adults [20]. Differences between adult and pediatric populations may explain discrepancies, as developmental factors influence emotional regulation and autonomic reactivity. Najafpour et al. (2017) reported significant correlations between GSR and dental anxiety in children aged 5–7 years, supporting its validity in pediatric populations [50]. Other studies have demonstrated varied GSR responses depending on dental stimuli and intervention types [18, 23, 51]. reinforcing the sensitivity of GSR to context-specific stressors.

Cardiac psychophysiological parameters reflect the dynamic interaction between sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system acting on the sinoatrial node [42, 52]. The HRV indices selected in this study provide complementary insights into autonomic regulation. Frequency-domain measures (VLF, LF, HF, LF/HF ratio) indicate sympatho-vagal balance, while time-domain indices (SDNN, PNN50) reflect overall variability and vagal tone [19, 53, 54]. Our study found significant changes in VLF, LF, HF, and the LF/HF ratio during dental treatment, indicating increased stress and discomfort in children. HF decreased, while VLF, LF, and LF/HF increased. Other cardiac parameters (PNN50, SDNN, HR) showed no significant changes, nor were there differences across Frankl behavioral groups. Consistent with previous findings, HRV may reflect general autonomic regulation but it may not be a sensitive enough tool to distinguish between specific behavior levels in this study’s setting [55, 56].

Among clinical variables, only treatment type was related to VLF changes, with tooth filling causing the highest stress, followed by extraction and pulpectomy. The relatively small and uneven subgroup sizes (n = 10 for fillings vs. n = 12 for extractions and pulpotomies groups) may have influenced the statistical outcome. Besides, very low frequency (VLF) components are known to be less reliable in short-term recordings and are influenced by multiple probable non-specific physiological processes [57]. On the other hand, the prolonged continuous use, high-frequency noise and vibratory stimuli of the handpiece during cavity preparation compared to shorter, intermittent use in procedures such as extraction or pulpotomy are well-documented stressors and might be the primary trigger for the observed VLF elevation in children. This issue provides a more clinically plausible explanation for our findings [58, 59].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size, largely due to exclusion of uncooperative children with extreme dental fear or behavioral dysregulation classified as Frankl 1, may limit generalizability and statistical power primarily to children with mild-to-moderate behavioral management challenges (Frankl 2–4). To further address the issue of reduced sample size, effect sizes (partial η²) and observed statistical power were additionally examined for the main outcomes. While adequate power was observed for variables with larger effect sizes (e.g., skin conductance measures), lower power was noted for several heart rate variability indices, indicating that smaller effects may not have been detected with sufficient statistical sensitivity. Although validated instruments were used, reliability was not directly assessed within this dataset. Additionally, previous dental experiences were not analyzed statistically due to insufficient subgroup sizes, despite their potential influence on anticipatory anxiety and autonomic responses. Additionally, a highly experienced and calibrated single experienced pediatric dentist performed the treatments and conducted the behavioral scoring, therefore in the future, studies should incorporate blinded assessment or video-based evaluation to improve objectivity. Due to cross-sectional design of this investigation, causal relationships between variables cannot be established, and the temporal sequence between exposure and outcome cannot be determined. Finally, the exploratory nature of the study and multiple comparisons warrant cautious interpretation of significant findings. Future multicenter studies with larger samples are recommended to confirm and extend these results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated relevant associations between psychophysiological parameters and children’s behavioral responses during dental treatment, while also highlighting the value of self-reported distress assessed by VAS. Although galvanic skin response appears to be potential, its reliability in highly uncooperative children is limited by significant methodological challenges and must be interpreted alongside behavioral observations. Some cardiac autonomic parameters further supported the presence of treatment-related stress. Overall, integrating psychophysiological measures with behavioral assessments may enhance the accuracy and effectiveness of anxiety management strategies in pediatric dentistry and support the development of more personalized approaches to care.
