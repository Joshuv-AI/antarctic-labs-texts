Psychometric Properties of the Drug Stigma Scale Among Community Pharmacists Concerning Individuals Who Use Over‐The‐Counter Drugs
Authors: Munenori Katayama, Takuya Shimane, Norihito Shirakawa, Kanna Sugiura, Ken Inada, Jun Konishi, Yoshihito Sakurai, Keiji Kobara, So Fujishiro, Toshihiko Matsumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimStigma among pharmacists toward individuals who use over‐the‐counter (OTC) drugs for unintended purposes may hinder effective support. This study aimed to assess the psychometric properties of the Drug Stigma Scale (DSS) when applied to pharmacists and to examine the factors associated with stigma levels in this context.MethodsA survey was anonymously distributed to pharmacists attending voluntary online workshops organized by a prefectural pharmaceutical association in Japan from August 2023 to March 2024. These workshops focused on OTC drug use for unintended purposes and the gatekeeping role of pharmacists. A total of 490 responses were analyzed. The DSS, originally a 24‐item scale developed for the Japanese context, was adapted to specify “OTC drug use.” Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and internal consistency analyses were conducted. Concurrent validity was assessed using correlations and group comparisons based on demographic variables, confidence in knowledge, and gatekeeper self‐efficacy (GKSES).ResultsThe adapted 18‐item structure of the DSS demonstrated a good model fit (CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, GFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.061). Internal consistency ranged between α = 0.68 and 0.88. Female pharmacists scored significantly lower on several subscales than male pharmacists, indicating less stigmatizing attitudes. Additionally, lower DSS scores were observed among pharmacists who had frequent contact with individuals who misuse OTC drugs, had a family history of such misuse, or sought help for related issues. The correlations between the DSS and GKSES scores were weak but significant.ConclusionThe DSS is a valid tool for measuring stigma among pharmacists concerning OTC drug use. The findings suggest that direct or familial experiences with OTC drug misuse may reduce stigmatized attitudes. Efforts to reduce stigma should extend beyond knowledge‐based interventions and consider community engagement and opportunities for pharmacists to hear individuals' lived experiences.

Introduction

A significant increase in the use of over‐the‐counter (OTC) drugs for non‐medical purposes has recently been observed in Japan. According to a nationwide survey targeting medical institutions, the number of individuals whose primary drug used over the past year was an OTC drug was higher than that of several illegal drugs, making it the second most common, after stimulants [1]. Commonly used OTC drugs include dihydrocodeine, dextromethorphan, diphenhydramine, bromovalerylurea, allylisopropylacetylurea, caffeine, and codeine [2]. Notably, Japan allows the availability of OTC drugs that include substances such as allylisopropylacetylurea and bromovalerylurea without prescriptions, which have been banned from sale in most countries owing to their limited medical value, potential for abuse, and high harm to the human body [3, 4, 5]. This rather unique OTC drug market, in addition to the government's recent policy to permit the sale of dextromethorphan without a prescription, aimed at reducing public medical expenses during the COVID‐19 pandemic, created a social situation that encouraged young individuals to use such non‐arrestable OTC drugs for unintentional purposes [6]. A significant increase was observed in downtown Tokyo concerning the number of young individuals sent to the emergency room owing to overdosing on such drugs [7]. Given that most individuals purchase their drug of choice at a local pharmacy, the pharmacists who work there play a key role in preventing overdoses because they are the only healthcare professionals whom most individuals who purchase OTC drugs meet. In such situations, pharmacists are expected not only to prevent potential harms such as overdose but also to act as “gatekeepers,” identifying at‐risk individuals and connecting them with appropriate care and community‐based support services. Their role is therefore critical in the early detection and intervention of OTC drug overdose, contributing to broader public health efforts to reduce harm [8].

Besides having adequate knowledge of how to interact with individuals who use drugs, less stigmatized attitudes of medical professionals, including pharmacists, toward these individuals play a crucial role in their successful encounters [9, 10]. Examples of stigma toward people who use drugs include the prejudice that these people lack willpower and are dangerous, or the notion that drug use is a sign of personal failure and that these people cannot be trusted [11, 12]. Stigmatized attitudes of healthcare professionals cause hesitation among individuals who use drugs regarding help seeking, resulting in unsuccessful interactions and further exacerbating drug use [13, 14, 15]. Further studies also show that pharmacists' stigmatized attitudes influence the overall well‐being of individuals who use drugs they encounter at local pharmacies where they work [16, 17]. This means that in the case of people who use OTC drugs for unintended purposes, an accurate understanding of pharmacists' stigmatized attitudes toward this population is crucial given their unique role in preventing overdoses and connecting this population to a source of help, and here lies the need to develop a quantitative scale to measure this. The Drug Stigma Scale (DSS) is a quantitative scale designed to measure drug‐related stigma in Japan [11, 12]. The DSS originated from Link's Perceived Devaluation and Discrimination Scale, which was modified to fit the Japanese context of drug‐related stigma based on literature reviews and interviews with individuals who use drugs. Although the DSS's psychometric properties have been assessed as adequate among healthcare professionals in the public sector and medical staff of addiction‐specialized facilities, the DSS's validity and reliability among pharmacists have not been examined. Therefore, the current study aimed to examine the psychometric characteristics of the DSS to determine whether it could be applied when measuring pharmacists' stigmatizing attitudes toward individuals who use OTC drugs for unintended non‐medical purposes.

Methods

Study Design

We carried a cross‐sectional survey design for our study. One of this study's authors lectured in multiple online workshops organized by the prefectural pharmaceutical association in Japan from August 2023. The goal of this voluntary workshop was to disseminate knowledge on the problem of OTC drug use for unintended purposes and train pharmacists to act as “gatekeepers” in preventing further harmful use of OTC drugs and connecting individuals with resources to recover from the problems they face. After the workshop, willing attendees were asked to anonymously participate in the study's survey. The sample size was set to 480, which was the required sample size to conduct a confirmatory factor analysis at a rate of 20 samples per item. Data collection continued until an adequate sample size was obtained.

Drug Stigma Scale (DSS)

The DSS is a quantitative scale designed to measure stigma toward individuals who use drugs. The DSS was developed to fit the Japanese context, which is recommended for studies that measure social phenomena, such as stigma, that are sensitive to each local culture. This scale was originally developed to include 24 items. Respondents were asked to select the degree to which they agreed with each item on a four‐point Likert scale. Several previous studies excluded six items based on their statistical analyses; however, given that the purpose of this study was to examine its psychometric properties, the original 24‐item version was applied. Notably, some wording was changed to meet the aims of the study. For example, the wording on item 1, “Most people would willingly accept a person with a history of drug use as a close friend,” was changed to “Most people would willingly accept a person with a history of abusing OTC drugs as a close friend.” (Appendix S1).

Other Collected Variables

Demographics

Participants were asked to indicate their sex, highest education, years of experience as a pharmacist, employment status (full‐time/part‐time), whether they were a supervising pharmacist (a licensed pharmacist responsible for overseeing the operations of a pharmacy), whether the pharmacy they worked in sold potentially abusive OTC drugs, frequency of involvement in selling OTC drugs (nearly every day/3–6 times per week/1–2 times per week/few times per month/few times per year/never), experience of attending similar workshops, experience in abusing OTC drugs themselves, family history of abusing OTC drugs, and experience in seeking help for OTC drug abuse.

Confidence in Knowledge in Supporting Individuals Who Abuse OTC Drugs

Participants were asked to rate their confidence concerning the various degrees of knowledge required to support individuals who abuse OTC drugs on a five‐point Likert scale, including the current status of OTC abuse in psychiatric medicine, current status of OTC abuse among young individuals, psychological state influencing individuals who abuse OTC drugs, pharmacists' role as gatekeepers for OTC drug abuse, communication with individuals who have a higher risk of OTC drug abuse, pharmacists' confidentiality obligations, and the role of self‐help groups and other community‐owned resources.

Gatekeeper Self‐Efficacy Scale (GKSES)

The GKSES is a 9‐item questionnaire developed to measure an individual's self‐efficacy as a gatekeeper for suicide prevention [18]. The scale requires individuals to rate their degree of confidence in various domains of working as a gatekeeper for suicide prevention. In this study, the wording “suicide” was changed to “OTC drug abuse” to fit the study's aim. Although there are no existing studies that have validated the rewording of the Gatekeeper Self‐Efficacy Scale (GKSES) from “suicide” to “OTC drug abuse,” we determined that this adapted version would be appropriate for assessing the external validity of the Drug Stigma Scale (DSS) in our study. This research aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the DSS when applied to pharmacists interacting with individuals who use OTC drugs for unintended purposes. Several prior studies have demonstrated that stigma is inversely associated with gatekeeper self‐efficacy in the context of suicide prevention [19, 20, 21]. Given this established link, we reasoned that similar mechanisms could be expected in the context of OTC drug use, especially considering the well‐documented connection between overdose involving OTC drugs and elevated suicide risk [22, 23]. In clinical and public health settings, overdose is often not easily distinguishable from suicidal behavior, and pharmacists are increasingly recognized as key gatekeepers in identifying and intervening in such situations. Still, no existing scales specifically capture pharmacists' confidence in supporting people who use OTC drugs for unintended purposes. Therefore, we adapted the wording of the GKSES items to fit this context. While we acknowledge that this modified version of the GKSES has not been formally validated, we believe it provides a useful and theoretically grounded proxy for evaluating the DSS's concurrent validity.

Analysis

After totaling the number of responses to each question, a confirmatory factor analysis of the DSS was conducted to determine whether the previously confirmed factor construct [11, 12] exhibited an adequate fit for pharmacists. Upon confirming the factor construct, internal consistency was measured by calculating Cronbach's alpha.

Subsequently, concurrent validity was examined by comparing the DSS with the other variables collected. Spearman's rank correlation analysis was conducted for continuous variables, and either Welch's t‐test or Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was conducted for all other variables.

Replicating a previous study, a combination of the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), goodness of fit index (GFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was used to evaluate the model's fit index. A model with a good fit has CFI, TLI, and GFI values exceeding 0.9, and an RMSEA value below 0.06. The significance level for all analyses was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R [24]. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using the “lavaan” package.

Ethical Consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Zenkoku Seishin Hoken Fukushi Center Head Committee (Head Committee of Japan's Mental Health and Welfare Center). The anonymity and voluntary nature of the study were ensured before the participants responded to the survey. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Demographic Variables

The demographic variable results are presented in Table 1. In total, 572 individuals responded to the survey in eight workshops. A total of 1397 people attended the workshop, with a response rate of 40.9% for our study. After excluding respondents with missing values, 490 samples were analyzed. The mean age was 52.47, 312 participants (63.7%) were women, 378 (77.1%) finished a 4‐year university course, 421 (85.9%) were employed full‐time, and 281 (57.3%) were supervising pharmacists. Additionally, 285 (58.2%) participants worked at a pharmacy that sold potentially abusive OTC drugs, 17 (3.5%) sold drugs of concern nearly every day, 167 (34.1%) had attended other workshops, 22 (4.5%) had experience abusing OTC drugs themselves, 43 (8.8%) had a family history of OTC drug abuse, and 25 (5.1%) sought help for OTC drug abuse.

Factor Construct of the DSS

Figure 1 presents the results of the CFA for the DSS. The fit indices for the confirmatory factor analysis concerning CFI, TLI, GFI, and RMSEA were 0.93, 0.91, 0.92, and 0.061 (95% Confidence Interval: 0.054–0.069), respectively. The 18‐item structure confirmed in previous studies indicated adequate fit in this study. The internal consistencies for the Mistrust, Disregard, Devaluation, Denial, Discrimination, and Dangerousness subscales and the total scale were 0.77, 0.70, 0.78, 0.68, 0.74, 0.81, and 0.88, respectively. Concurrent Validity of the DSS

The item statistics for the subjective confidence questions are presented in Table 2, and the results of the series of analyses to measure concurrent validity are presented in Table 3. Female respondents scored significantly lower on the Disregard, Devaluation, and Discrimination subscales and the total DSS scale (p < 0.05) than male respondents. The ANOVA indicated a significant between‐group difference in the frequency of involvement in selling potentially abusive OTC drugs for the Denial and Dangerousness subscales and the total scale (all p < 0.05). Post hoc analysis indicated a significant difference in the Denial subscale, in which respondents who sold potentially abusive OTC drugs nearly every day scored lower than those who never sold/sold a few times per year/sold 1–2 times per week. Respondents with a family history of OTC drug abuse scored significantly lower on the Denial subscale (p < 0.05) than those without. Those who had experience seeking help for themselves or their family's OTC drug abuse scored significantly lower on the Denial and the Discrimination subscales (p < 0.05) than those without experience. The DSS had a weak correlation with the GKSES for the Mistrust subscale (𝝆 = 0.22, p < 0.05) and the total scale (𝝆 = 0.20, p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to measure the psychometric properties of the DSS by applying the DSS to pharmacists who meet individuals who use OTC drugs for unintended purposes. Confirmatory factor and internal consistency analyses indicated an adequate fit of the structure discovered in previous studies, indicating that the 18‐item structure can be applied to this group of professionals. A series of concurrent analyses revealed notable characteristics of this scale. First, women were more likely to have less stigmatized attitudes. In addition, respondents who met individuals who used OTC drugs for unintended purposes more often and those with a family history of OTC drug abuse were less likely to hold the misbelief that no individual in their lives used OTC drugs for unintended purposes. Moreover, respondents who felt confident in acting as gatekeepers for individuals who used OTC drugs for unintended purposes held relatively less stigmatized attitudes toward this population.

The results align with those of previous studies, indicating that the DSS is a valid tool for measuring the stigmatized attitudes of pharmacists toward individuals who use OTC drugs for unintended purposes. Pharmacists' frequent interactions with this population group were assumed to have changed their commonly held belief that only a limited number of individuals use OTC drugs for unintended purposes and, therefore, are easy to detect. This study found that more than 4% had experience using OTC drugs for unintended purposes, and the numbers were even higher when asked about their family histories. Moreover, experience in seeking help for their or their family's OTC drug problems was associated with a low discriminative view and lesser likelihood of denying the existence of this group of individuals, indicating that their stigmatized attitudes were, in some cases, related to their personal experiences, and that this can be overcome with adequate help, as reported in previous studies [25].

Notably, the complex nature of pharmacists' stigmatized attitudes toward OTC drug use for unintended purposes was unexpected. Although the results aligned with previous studies stating that the stigma phenomenon is a complex phenomenon that developed from an interplay of various personal and social factors [13], this study unwillingly supported this assertion, given that pharmacists' self‐efficacy toward acting as gatekeepers to support individuals who use OTC drugs for unintended purposes or their confidence in knowledge to support this population indicated weak or negligible correlations with most domains of their stigmatized attitudes. Furthermore, meeting individuals who use OTC drugs for unintentional purposes correlated with pharmacists' sense of denial or prejudice that these individuals are dangerous; however, it did not correlate with their discriminatory perspective or lack of respect. Previous studies indicated that simple educational interventions aimed at improving knowledge and skills do not always decrease the stigma toward individuals who use drugs [26, 27, 28]. Meeting individuals who have recovered from drug use and successful engagement experiences are important [11, 12]; however, the insular nature of Japanese pharmacies, which have not established effective collaborations with local organizations, such as public health centers, may have limited their opportunities. Since the fiscal year 2016, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare initiated a system of “Health Support Pharmacies,” which focuses not on the sale of pharmaceuticals but on providing residents with health consultations and advice regarding medications. This initiative fosters collaboration with various local stakeholders and enhances the role of pharmacies in contributing to community health. However, the inability to establish such collaborative frameworks resulted in isolation within communities, which may limit opportunities for collaboration.

This study's results indicate that future interventions aimed at reducing pharmacists' stigmatized attitudes toward individuals who use OTC drugs for unintentional purposes and increasing the odds of a successful encounter in clinical settings may consider providing opportunities to hear the voices of individuals who use drugs. Additionally, systemic interventions aimed at connecting pharmacies to the social networks of local communities should be considered. Furthermore, future research should explore how the Drug Stigma Scale can be utilized not only to assess pharmacists' attitudes but also to evaluate the effectiveness of such interventions. In line with this direction, the authors are currently developing an online training program for pharmacists to support patients at risk of OTC drug misuse, with the DSS incorporated as one of the outcome measures to scientifically examine the effectiveness of this program.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, selection bias cannot be ignored, given that the sample was obtained from individuals after attending non‐mandatory workshops specifically focused on OTC drug use, indicating that participants were likely to have had higher baseline awareness and motivation than the average pharmacist. In addition, the average age of the participants was higher than that of the average pharmacist, according to Japanese national statistics, and the proportion of female participants was relatively large, which may have further restricted the study's sample representativeness. Second, information bias may have been prevalent owing to the self‐reporting nature of the survey, given that the study questioned the participants' stigma toward their potential customers. Furthermore, derivations were applied to the GKSES to measure the psychometric properties of the modified DSS. Such derivations have not been formally validated and may have affected the accuracy and validity of the findings. In addition, although the DSS was originally designed to minimize social desirability bias, the possibility of its influence cannot be completely excluded. Finally, this study was not a prospective study but rather utilized cross‐sectional observational data; therefore, it only demonstrated a correlation.

Conclusion

This study found that the Drug Stigma Scale (DSS) can be applied to pharmacists to measure stigma toward individuals who use OTC drugs for unintentional purposes. Lower values for each item indicated less stigmatized attitudes. This study suggests that this scale can be applied in various contexts to examine the situation and determine an effective way to overcome stigma toward individuals who use drugs in pharmacy settings, which is a crucial point of interaction for meeting individuals who use OTC drugs for unintentional purposes.
