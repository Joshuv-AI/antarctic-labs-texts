A Narrative Review of Bedaquiline and Delamanid: New Arsenals Against Multidrug‐Resistant and Extensively Drug‐Resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Nazanin Ahmad Khosravi, Mehrandokht Sirous, Azar Dokht Khosravi, Morteza Saki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe treatment of multidrug‐resistant (MDR‐) and extensively drug‐resistant tuberculosis (XDR‐TB) is a formidable challenge. Treatment of MDR‐ and XDR‐TB using bedaquiline (BDQ) and delamanid (DLM), two newly introduced medications, is steadily increasing. This narrative review aimed to present a concise overview of the existing information regarding BDQ and DLM, and elucidate their antimicrobial characteristics, resistance mechanisms, synergism with other drugs, and side effects.MethodsTo collect the required information about the antimicrobial properties, a search for scientific evidence from the Scopus, PubMed, and Embase databases was performed, and all recently published articles up to May 2024 were considered.ResultsBDQ had potent antimicrobial effects on various types of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), including rapid‐growing and slow‐growing species, and MDR/XDR Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The mechanisms of BDQ resistance in M. tuberculosis primarily involve mutations in three genes: atpE, mmpR (Rv0678) and pepQ. BDQ may have synergistic effects when combined with DLM, pyrazinamide, and pretomanid/linezolid. BDQ has a low incidence of side effects. The use of BDQ may prolong the QTc interval. Similarly, DLM showed potent antimicrobial effects on NTM and MDR/XDR M. tuberculosis. The main resistance mechanisms to DLM are induced by mutations in fbiA, fbiB, fbiC, fgd1, and ddn genes. The DLM had synergistic effects with BDQ and moxifloxacin. The DLM also has few side effects in some patients including QTc prolongation.ConclusionBDQ and DLM are suitable antibiotics with few side effects for the treatment of MDR/XDR‐TB. These antibiotics have synergistic effects when combined with other antituberculosis drugs.Keywords: bedaquiline, delamanid, extensively drug‐resistant tuberculosis, multidrug‐resistant, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, XDR‐TB

Introduction

Among the various communicable diseases, tuberculosis (TB) holds a prominent place on a global scale as one of the primary causes of mortality worldwide [1]. Based on the latest published report (2023) of the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022, the prevalence of TB affected nearly 10.6 million individuals (with a range of 9.9–11.4 million), indicating a 3.5% increase from the 10.3 million (with a range of 9.6–11.0 million) reported in 2021 [1]. The global TB incidence rate in 2022 was estimated at 133 new cases per 100,000 population per year, with a 95% uncertainty interval of 124–143 [1]. The rate of TB incidence between 2020 and 2022 increased by 3.9% [1]. In 2022, the world's TB cases were mainly concentrated in thirty high TB burden countries, which made up 87% of the total [1]. Among these, eight countries, namely India, Indonesia, China, the Philippines, Pakistan, Nigeria, Bangladesh, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, accounted for two‐thirds of the global total, with India having the highest percentage at 27% [1]. The breakdown of TB cases in 2022 shows that 12% were children (0–14 years), 33% were female, and 55% were male [1].

In 2022, Mycobacterium tuberculosis caused an estimated 1.30 million deaths worldwide and nearly returned to the level recorded in 2019 [1]. After the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) epidemic, the number of deaths from TB increased to 1.6 million in 2021 (1.4 and 1.5 million deaths from tuberculosis in 2019 and 2020, respectively) [2]. The year 2022 saw an increase in global case notifications from the previous year, with 7.5 million cases diagnosed and started on treatment, accounting for 70% of estimated incident cases. This marked a 16% increase in notifications from 2021 and a 5% increase from 2019. TB mortality decreased by 7% in 2022 compared to 2021 and was 2% lower than that in 2019, prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic [1, 2]. The combined efforts of India, Indonesia, and the Philippines led to a substantial reduction in the number of newly diagnosed TB cases globally in 2020 and 2021, with all three countries surpassing 2019 levels in 2022 [1].

Currently, in accordance with WHO guidelines, standardized treatment regimens for tuberculosis, which consist of rifampicin (RIF), ethambutol (EMB), pyrazinamide (PZA), and isoniazid (INH) as first‐line antibiotics, have proven to be successful in treating the majority of drug‐susceptible M. tuberculosis isolates [3]. According to the most recent reports by the WHO, multidrug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB) is classified as a disease that exhibits resistance to both RIF and INH. Furthermore, extensively drug‐resistant TB (XDR‐TB) is defined as a disease that is resistance to RIF. Additionally, resistance to all fluoroquinolones (FQs), and either linezolid (LZD) or bedaquiline (BDQ) is present [1].

In 2019, a total of 201,997 new cases of drug‐resistant TB were reported worldwide. Among these cases, 132,222 were identified as multidrug‐ and rifampicin‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR‐TB), while 25,681 cases were classified as either pre‐XDR‐TB (resistance to INH, RIF, and any FQ) or XDR‐TB (resistance to RIF, any FQ and at least one of BDQ or LZD) [3].

Furthermore, the most recent report by the WHO revealed that generally, 3.6% of newly identified TB cases and 18% of individuals who had formerly received treatment were diagnosed with MDR‐TB. In 2021, the prevalence of MDR/RR‐TB was predominantly observed in three nations, namely, Pakistan, the Russian Federation, and India, which collectively accounting for 42% of the new cases. The proportion in India was the highest at 26%, followed by that in the Russian Federation at 8.5%, and that in Pakistan at 7.9%. Notably, the Russian Federation, along with various countries in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, bears the heaviest burden of MDR/RR‐TB cases, surpassing 50% among individuals previously treated for the disease [1].

Currently, worldwide concern lies in the spread of different forms of drug‐resistant TB (DR‐TB). In comparison to cases susceptible to drugs, the treatment for MDR‐TB is lengthier, with reduced success rates, and necessitates the use of more costly antibiotics that possess greater levels of toxicity [4]. It is evident that there is a clear necessity for the production of newer anti‐TB agents, considering that treatment regimens are presently undergoing validation via clinical trials [5].

A comprehensive evaluation carried out in multiple nations revealed that the expenses associated with treating MDR‐TB patients vary from 2423 USD in Peru to 14,657 USD in Tomsk, Russia [6]. The treatment of drug‐susceptible TB in Europe incurs an average cost of €10,282 per patient, taking into account both direct and indirect expenses. However, the cost rises significantly to €57,213 for MDR‐TB and further escalates to €170,744 for XDR‐TB [7].

Revised policy recommendations from the WHO now support the use of novel anti‐TB drugs such as LZD, BDQ, and delamanid (DLM) for the treatment of DR‐TB [5]. Research findings indicated that incorporating DLM or BDQ into the treatment protocol leads to lower mortality rates and enhanced treatment response [8, 9]. BDQ formerly known as R207910 and then TMC207, was the first new anti‐TB drug that belongs to the diarylquinoline family [10]. BDQ received approval for the treatment of MDR‐TB following the approval of rifampin in the United States in 1971 [9]. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted accelerated approval for BDQ in December 2012 [10]. Within the class of nitroimidazoles, DLM (previously OPC‐67683) is a compound that was approved by the European Union and Japan for the treatment of MDR‐TB patients in 2014 [9, 11]. The combination of BDQ and DLM has shown effectiveness in increasing sputum smear and culture conversion, and in increasing success rates at the end of treatment of MDR/XDR‐TB strains. However, there are concerns about cardiac side effects that may result in cardiotoxicity (increasing QT interval) [12]. This narrative review aimed to present a concise overview of the existing information regarding BDQ and DLM, and elucidate their antimicrobial characteristics, resistance mechanisms, synergism with other drugs, and side effects.

Materials and Methods

To collect the required information about the antimicrobial properties, a search for scientific evidence from the Scopus, PubMed, and Embase databases was performed, and all recently published articles up to May 2024 were considered. The keywords “delamanid,” “bedaquiline,” and “tuberculosis” were searched without the use of filters.

BDQ

Structure of BDQ

BDQ is a new anti‐TB agent that fits in to the chemical combinations known as diarylquinolines [10, 13]. The antimycobacterial activity of BDQ can be attributed to its quinolinic central heterocyclic nucleus, which is accompanied by amine compounds and alcohol as side chains [13]. This antibiotic has two main parts: a hydrophobic part of this compound containing N,N‐dimethyl(‐N(CH3)2), which is responsible for binding to ATP synthase and hydrogen group bond acceptor/donor pairs. The stability of this drug is provided by the hydrogen bonding [14].

Based on former studies, BDQ has a molecular weight of approximately 555.51 g/mol and has the chemical name 1‐(6‐bromo‐2methoxy‐quinolin‐3‐yl)‐4‐dimethylamino‐2‐naphtalen‐1‐yl‐1‐phenyl‐butan‐2‐ol and a molecular formula of C32 H31BrN2O2.C4H4O4 [15, 16]. Research on BDQ has indicated that the effectiveness of this medication against tuberculosis is associated with the diarylquinoline structure, the side chain containing a hydroxyl group, the naphthalene component, and the N,N‐dimethyl amino end [16, 17]. Research has shown that subunit c in the C ring of the mycobacterial ATP synthase is the primary binding site for BDQ [16, 17]. Bedaquiline fumarate is a fumarate salt consisting of BDQ and fumaric acid (1:1) [18]. The molecular weight of bedaquiline fumarate is 671.6 g/mol, and it has a molecular formula of C36H35BrN2O6 [18, 19]. BDQ is an enantiomer compound with two adjacent chiral centers. These two chiral centers make the chemical synthesis of this novel antituberculosis drug difficult and expensive in the route of production [20]. Several ongoing studies are dedicated to streamlining the structure of BDQ in order to preserve its antituberculosis activity while minimizing its complexity. Therefore, novel analogues of BDQ are needed to deliver potential new leads [20].

Mechanism of Action of BDQ

Although diarylquinolines have been proven to be closely related to quinolones, they have no inhibitory effect on DNA gyrase [9, 16]. By impeding the activity of mycobacterial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthase, BDQ, an anti‐TB drug, has demonstrated efficacy in eradicating both non‐replicating and actively replicating mycobacteria [9, 13]. Mycobacterial mitochondria contain ATP synthase, an indispensable enzyme that is bound to the inner membrane and is accountable for producing energy to fuel the metabolic reactions in the cells of developing Mycobacterium [16]. The enzyme is composed of two parts including membrane sector (F0) and the cytoplasmic sector (F1). The F1 part consists of five subunits α3, β3, ϒ, δ, and ε. Three subunits, a, b2, and c, form the F0 membrane sector [21, 22]. F1 has 3 catalytic positions that combine ADP with a phosphate to form ATP or hydrolyze ATP. F0 functions as an ion‐conducting pathway. The rotation of a cylindrical ring of subunit c within the α3β3 hexamer of F1 is caused by proton translocation through F0, resulting in the coupled rotation of the subunit ϒ and leading to the synthesis of ATP [22]. The evidence suggests that the protonated form of BDQ has a high affinity for binding to the ATP synthase complex [23]. BDQ can bind to the oligomeric and proteolipid subunit c in the F0 domain of mycobacterial ATP synthase and block rotation of the ring‐shaped assembly of the c‐subunit, leading to inhibition of ATP synthesis (Figure 1) [13, 17, 18, 21, 24, 25]. Research findings indicate that BDQ can hinder F0F1‐ATP synthase activity by specifically targeting the ε subunit of the enzyme, while also binding to its c‐subunit (Figure 1) [13, 17, 26]. BDQ demonstrates a notably specific mode of action against mycobacteria, with an inhibitory impact on mitochondrial ATP synthase in humans that is 20,000 times lower than its effect on mycobacteria [18, 27, 28].

Mechanism of action of bedaquiline (BDQ). BDQ can bind to the oligomeric subunit c in the F0 domain of mycobacterial ATP synthase and block rotation of the ring‐shaped assembly of the c‐subunit, leading to inhibition of ATP synthesis. Also, BDQ can hinder F0F1‐ATP synthase activity by specifically targeting the ε subunit of the enzyme.

Spectrum of Activity of BDQ

Investigations into BDQ have shown its potent antimicrobial properties against various types of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), including rapid‐growing and slow‐growing species, and both drug‐susceptible and drug‐resistant M. tuberculosis [15, 29]. Rapidly growing NTM including Mycobacterium vaccae, M. fortuitum, and M. phlei are susceptible to BDQ with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) <0.5 μg/mL. Among the slowly growing NTM, M. kansasii, M. gordonae, M. simiae, and other strains of this group are also susceptible to BDQ [29]. Some Mycobacterium species such as M. shimoidei, M. xenopi, and M. novocastrense are naturally resistant to BDQ with MICs >8 mg/L. Because the substitution of methionine for alanine at position 63 of subunit c has occurred [16, 29, 30].

BDQ Resistance

One of the major problems in treating MDR‐TB patients is the lack of effective drugs. A 57% overall treatment success rate poses a burden on the healthcare system [3]. Therefore, it is necessary to use effective and new drugs to treat MDR‐TB strains [31]. By the end of 2015, more than 2500 patients in 50 countries had already received BDQ. By the end of 2020, the WHO reported that 109 countries had initiated the use of BDQ for the treatment of MDR/XDR‐TB. However, since the inclusion of BDQ in treatment regimens for DR‐TB, BDQ‐resistant TB strains have gradually emerged [32, 33]. The WHO points out that the occurrence of resistance to BDQ can be due to misuse of this drug, which should be carefully monitored [33]. Thus, there is an urgent need to increase the availability of rapid drug susceptibility testing (DST) for all drugs that can be used to personalize antituberculosis treatment [34]. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) has provided specific guidelines regarding the MIC breakpoint for BDQ. The MIC of BDQ ≤0.25 mg/mL is considered sensitive, while the MIC of ≥0.25 mg/mL indicates resistance [16, 35].

Mechanisms of Resistance to BDQ

The evidence reveals that the resistance mechanisms to BDQ are mostly related to chromosomal mutations [36, 37]. Several reports have explained that the mechanisms of BDQ resistance to M. tuberculosis primarily involve mutations in three genes, namely, atpE, mmpR (Rv0678), and pepQ [16, 21, 37, 38]. BDQ inhibits subunit c of F0F1‐ATP synthase, which is encoded by the atpE gene. Mutations in the atpE gene are associated with BDQ resistance [16]. A recent publication by Huitric et al. [39] detailed the gene sequencing of 53 in vitro mutants. The study identified five single point mutations (I66M, A28P, A28V, G61A, and A63P) in the atpE gene in only 28% (15 out of 53) of the isolates that showed resistance to BDQ [39]. Notably, the atpE gene of the remaining 72% (38 out of 53) of the isolates demonstrated no mutations [39]. This evidence suggests that there may be other mechanisms of resistance to BDQ that need further investigation. Moreover, the absence of resistant mutants was noted following exposure to a concentration of 3 mg/L [39].

Efflux pumps are considered the second mechanism in both inherent and acquired BDQ resistance [40]. It has been found that the activation of the MmpS5‐MmpL5 efflux pump due to the mutations in the Rv0678 gene leads to the emergence of BDQ resistance [40]. A research conducted by Hartkoorn, Uplekar, and Cole [40] revealed that the mutations occurring in MmpS5‐MmpL5 are responsible for the development of cross‐resistance between clofazimine (CFZ) and BDQ. Although the majority of Rv0678 mutants have been detected in patients undergoing BDQ treatment, recent findings of these mutants in the sputum of both drug‐sensitive and MDR‐TB patients who have not received BDQ or CFZ therapy have also been reported [41, 42]. The functional impairment observed in MDR‐TB patients receiving BDQ for 6 months may be linked to a mutation at nucleotide position 2 (GTG → GCG) in Rv0678. This mutation causes the loss of the start codon, leading to resistance [43].

Recently, a study revealed that mutations in the regulatory gene Rv0678 (2 T > C, leading to M1A) were also associated with resistance to BDQ and CFZ in a patient with MDR‐TB [44]. Non‐target mutations in the pepQ gene represent an alternative mechanism for the development of resistance to CFZ and BDQ [44]. Hence, alterations in the gene encoding aminopeptidases, specifically pepQ, are correlated with a lower level of cross‐resistance to both BDQ and CFZ in M. tuberculosis [44]. A mutation in pepQ led to a minor four‐fold increase in the minimum inhibitory MICs of CFZ and BDQ. Although these mutations decreased the efficacy of the drugs in vivo, they did not confer full resistance. Consequently, a fascinating investigation utilizing a mouse model of tuberculosis demonstrated that a pepQ mutant displayed increased virulence and decreased susceptibility to BDQ and CFZ [37]. The findings of this research suggest that the resistance resulting from the pepQ mutation could be associated with increased drug efflux, but not with the overexpression of mmpL5 or mmpS5. Additionally, this detailed investigation indicated that impaired pepQ function is sufficient for decreased susceptibility both in vivo and in vitro [37].

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

BDQ is administered orally and reaches its maximum concentration 4–6 h after administration [18]. Taking food at the same time doubles the bioavailability of the antibiotic compared to taking it while fasting [18]. Therefore BDQ should be administered with food. The metabolism of the active substance primarily occurs in the liver through the action of the enzyme CYP3A4, resulting in the formation of its primary metabolite (N‐monodesmethyl) M2. This metabolite exhibits antimicrobial activity that is 3–6 times lower than that of BDQ and is subsequently excreted in the feces [18]. The use of BDQ alongside drugs that enhance CYP3A4 activity, such as rifampicin and rifapentine, is likely to result in decreased BDQ concentrations in the blood and a potential reduction in its therapeutic effectiveness [45, 46, 47, 48]. Patients co‐infected with TB and HIV necessitate antiretroviral therapy alongside TB treatment. The potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, lopinavir/ritonavir, elevates plasma levels of BDQ and M2; therefore, the toxicity of BDQ may be enhanced. M2 is mostly eliminated in the feces and only 1%–4% in the urine [46, 47]. BDQ has a multiphasic distribution and an effective half‐life of 24 h, which is significantly longer than that of most other drugs used to treat TB [18, 48]. It is important to note that BDQ has a very long terminal elimination half‐life of about 164 days, while that of M2 is 159 days [18].

Mild to moderate renal dysfunction did not affect the pharmacokinetics (PKs) of the drug. In people with moderate renal or hepatic insufficiency, adjustment of the drug regimen is not necessary. However, caution is advised in patients with severe renal and hepatic impairment. There have been no reports demonstrating the impact of variables such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI), and concurrent HIV infection on the PK profile of BDQ. A previous study has shown that individuals with black skin among different ethnic groups have lower BDQ concentrations than individuals of other races [49].

Synergy

Synergism of BDQ With DLM

Two new drugs, BDQ and DLM, have recently been approved for the effective treatment of MDR‐TB [50]. DLM is a nitro‐dihydro‐imidazooxazole derivative that inhibits the biosynthesis of cell wall mycolic acid in M. tuberculosis [51, 52]. Adverse events (AEs) and the risk of death linked to BDQ, such as QTc prolongation and elevated liver enzymes, have raised concerns, particularly when used in combination with DLM [50]. Both BDQ and DLM cause a moderate increase in the QTc interval, possibly through the inhibition of delayed cardiac rectifier potassium channels [53]. Tadolini et al. [54] outlined the first instance of XDR‐TB treatment involving both DLM and BDQ. Patient resistance to second‐line drug treatment has led to a lack of available pharmaceutical options. Consequently, combination therapy was initiated [54]. After the administration of eight doses of BDQ with DLM, the recommended therapy was discontinued due to the ongoing risk of QTc prolongation [54]. The condition of the patient was carefully observed and the electrocardiogram (ECG) was repeated every 14 days [54]. A QTc interval surpassing 500 ms is deemed to be a notable risk factor associated with severe cardiac arrhythmias. In the presence of this sign in patients, healthcare professionals should discontinue treatment and engage in careful patient monitoring [55]. The utilization of BDQ was recommenced after an interval of close to 1 month, leading to a negative sputum smear and culture during the 2‐month follow‐up, in conjunction with the adjustment of electrolyte balance, serum albumin levels, and ECG QT wave duration [54]. Furthermore, in 2018, Ferlazzo et al. [56] conducted a comprehensive study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the combined treatment approach involving BDQ and DLM regimens in individuals suffering from MDR‐TB in Armenia, India, and South Africa. This retrospective cohort study shed light on the efficacy and safety of these treatment combinations in diverse geographical settings [56]. The results of this study suggest that combined therapy involving BDQ and DLM does not have an additive or synergistic impact on QTc prolongation [56].

Synergism of BDQ With PZA

The primary targets of PZA are crucial bacterial mechanisms such as ribosomal translation and proton drive. With BDQ acting as an inhibitor of ATP synthase, its combined use can lead to the suppression of bioenergetic functions and the potential depletion of cellular energy reserves [57]. The first studies on the efficacy of BDQ in the intravenous infection of Swiss mice showed that the combination therapy of BDQ and PZA is more bactericidal than the standard first‐line therapy and has a synergistic effect in the sterilization phase [58]. Recently, a study described the response of BALB/c and C3HeB/FeJ mice to treatment with BDQ and PZA administered singly or in combination and showed that the bacterial load in the lungs decreased after treatment for a period of 4 weeks [58]. Additionally, the co‐administration of BDQ and PZA demonstrated potent synergistic effects, enhancing bactericidal efficacy in vitro. The inhibitory impact of BDQ on energy metabolism, when paired with PZA, led to a synergistic impact in diminishing Mycobacterium bovis BCG, serving as a representative model for bactericidal activity [57]. Moreover, Diacon et al. [59] documented the outcomes of a randomized EBA (early bactericidal activity) trial involving therapy‐naive individuals with sputum smear‐positive, drug‐susceptible pulmonary TB. The participants were treated with BDQ, BDQ‐PZA, PA‐824‐PZA, BDQ‐PA‐824, PA‐824‐moxifloxacin (MFX)‐PZA, or standard anti‐TB medications as the control group [59]. The mean 14‐day EBA of PA‐824‐MFX‐PZA was significantly higher than that of the other combinations and was comparable to that of standard drugs [59]. They were well‐tolerated and appeared to be safe. The initiation of BDQ activity experienced a delay due to a single inflection point occurring at 6.5 days [59]. This outcome is attributed to the enhancement of BDQ activity by PZA, leading to a synergistic increase in the activities of both BDQ and PZA compared to BDQ acting alone [59]. The addition of PA‐824 to BDQ appeared to have little, if any, effect on BDQ activity, but with no apparent contradiction [59].

Synergism of BDQ With Pretomanid

Since 2019, pretomanid (PA‐824), an oral nitroimidazo‐oxazine, has emerged as a novel antimycobacterial drug that can be administered either as a monotherapy or in combination with other novel agents to combat TB [60]. PA‐824 kills active MTB by inhibiting mycolic acid synthesis, thereby blocking cell wall production. Under hypoxic or anaerobic conditions, PA‐824 is effective against non‐replicating bacilli [61]. Several studies have explored the combined impacts of BDQ and PA‐824 in treating tuberculosis in mouse models and individuals suffering from pulmonary TB [62]. Several research studies have investigated the antagonistic interplay between PA‐824 and BDQ, revealing that the effectiveness of BDQ combined with PA‐824 for 2–3 months is comparable to that of conventional first‐line therapy. However, it was observed that during the initial month, the combination therapy was less effective than BDQ monotherapy [63, 64]. In recent years, due to promising clinical data on improving bactericidal activity, preventing acquired BDQ resistance, and reducing relapse time in drug‐susceptible isolates, several studies have been initiated in which PA‐824 has been added to BDQ‐containing treatment regimens [65]. Currently, the use of PA‐824 in BDQ‐PA‐LZD‐MFX (BPaLM) and BDQ‐PA‐LZD (BPaL) combination regimens is approved for the treatment of pulmonary RR, MDR, or pre‐XDR‐TB patients aged 14 years or older [66, 67].

Adverse Effects

Examination of the safety of BDQ has demonstrated that its prolonged administration is considered safe despite the occurrence of mild side effects such as nausea and peripheral neuropathy [68]. The use of BDQ may prolong the QTc interval on ECG. Consequently, it is essential to exercise caution when combining BDQ with other drugs that possess comparable risks [69]. An initial evaluation of the adverse effects of BDQ in 75 patients by Rustomjee et al. [70], revealed that the administration of a daily dose of 400 mg BDQ resulted in minimal side effects. These included hemoptysis (21.4%), drowsiness (7%), diarrhea (7%), and skin rash [70]. The FDA committee is particularly concerned about the safety of BDQ following an unexpected spike in mortality during a randomized controlled trial [71]. In a research conducted by Hewison and colleagues [72], the culture conversion rate reached 84.4%. Furthermore, 79.3% of the participants experienced some form of adverse reaction, while unfortunate circumstances led to the death of 10 individuals [72]. Among these fatalities, there is a possibility that two deaths were associated with the utilization of BDQ [72]. Guglielmetti et al. [73] conducted a separate retrospective cohort investigation, in which 45 patients diagnosed with MDR‐TB were treated with BDQ. The prevailing AEs observed in this study included gastrointestinal complications, affecting 32 individuals (71.1%); otovestibular impairment, affecting 25 patients (55.6%); and peripheral neuropathy, affecting 18 individuals (40.9%) [73]. Serious AEs was observed in 27 patients (60.0%), whereas seven patients (17.8%) encountered major AEs [73]. Peripheral neuropathy (n = 13) and QTc prolongation (n = 8) were the two most frequently observed serious AEs. Among the patients, 11% exhibited Fridericia‐corrected QT interval (QTcF) standards exceeding 500 ms, but no arrhythmias or symptomatic cardiac AEs were reported [73]. In three cases (6.7%), BDQ had to be discontinued due to QTcF prolongation. There were no notable disparities in outcomes or occurrence of AEs between patients who underwent standard BDQ treatment and those who received prolonged BDQ treatment [73]. Treatment regimens incorporating BDQ showed encouraging outcomes in a significant number of patients. The group demonstrated good tolerance to long‐term BDQ treatment [73]. In general, the findings suggest that BDQ has a low incidence of side effects and a high rate of culture conversion. This indicates that BDQ is generally safe and well‐tolerated and has minimal adverse effects. Over an extended period, the utilization of BDQ may give rise to unfavorable responses, which could potentially lead to fatality. Thus, it is essential to exercise caution when combining BDQ with other medications that have similar adverse effects, such as CYP3A4 inhibitors or FQs. QTc prolongation is a potential occurrence that should be considered. However, it is worth noting that levofloxacin, which belongs to the FQ class of drugs, has a lower propensity for drug interactions and a reduced likelihood of causing QTc prolongation [74, 75].

Delamanid

Structure of the DLM

DLM is a new drug for TB that belongs to a class of bicyclic nitroimidazoles. It was first discovered in 1989 [9]. Subsequently, researchers from two distinct groups identified the related compounds PA‐824 and OPC‐67683, recognizing their potential as powerful bio‐oral agents and viable options for tuberculosis treatment. Otsuka Pharmaceuticals Co. (Tokyo, Japan) spearheaded the development of OPC‐67683, which was then converted into DLM [76]. The compound 2‐nitroimidazole, a bicyclic nitroimidazo‐oxazole derivative similar to azomycin, was obtained from Streptomyces eurocidicus. This compound exhibited potent anti‐TB activity both in vitro and in vivo [77]. Derivatives of 6‐nitro‐2,3‐dihydroimidazo [2, 1‐b] oxazole were obtained by substitutions at position 2, which decreased mutagenicity and increased antituberculous activity. Substituting a heteroatom at position 2 of the side chain led to the removal of the mutagenic properties. 6‐Nitro‐2,3‐dihydroimidazo [2, 1‐b] oxazole is a racemic mixture that consists of enantiomers that are dextrorotatory but not levorotatory. This compound has been identified as active against the M. tuberculosis complex (MTBC). Deltyba, also known as 6‐nitro‐2,3‐dihydro‐imidazooxazol (C25H25F3N4O6), was developed to provide a secure blend of in vivo and laboratory conditions for maximum efficiency [78, 79].

Mechanism of Action

DLM is a prodrug whose nitro group must be biologically reduced by M. tuberculosis to produce a reactive species with antimycobacterial activity [80]. This drug prevents the biosynthesis of methoxy‐ and keto‐mycolic acid, which are important and specific lipid components of the mycobacterial cell wall and play essential roles in the survival and cell wall formation of the mycobacterial genus (Figure 2) [81, 82]. The activation of the DLM is dependent on the F420 system. The structure of this system is composed of two enzymes, namely, F420‐dependent glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) produced by Rv0407, FGD1 and deazaflavin‐dependent nitroreductase (Ddn) generated by Rv3547, alongside four coenzymes: FbiC (Rv1173), FbiB (Rv3261), Rv0132c, and FbiA (Rv3361) [83]. The process of biosynthesis and recycling of the cofactor F420 relies on these genes and coenzymes. Through the action of the Ddn enzyme, DLM was transformed into an active intermediate metabolite with anti‐M. tuberculosis properties and a desnitroform (inactive). The hypothesis posits that reactive intermediate metabolites are crucial in the process of inhibiting mycolic acid synthesis by DLM. The elimination of this essential component from the cell wall of Mycobacterium ultimately leads to destruction of the bacterium [84].

Mechanism of action of delamanid. This antibiotic prevents the biosynthesis of methoxy‐ and keto‐mycolic acid, which are important and specific lipid components of the mycobacterial cell wall.

Spectrum of DLM Activity

DLM has potent in vitro and in vivo clinical activity against both drug‐susceptible strains and drug‐resistant MDR‐TB strains. This drug has no cross‐resistance with drugs such as RIF, INH, and ETB and there is no antagonistic activity for these drugs. Therefore, DLM is recommended for the treatment of TB [9, 13].

In their clinical trial study, Diacon et al. [85] investigated the EBA of DLM in a cohort of 48 patients who had been diagnosed with pulmonary TB. The patients were given daily doses of 100, 200, 300, and 400 mg of DLM for a period of 14 days [85]. A mean EBA of 0.040 over Days 0–14, 0.069 over Days 0–2 and 0.035 over Days 2–14 was observed. A higher proportion of patients in the 100, 200, 300, and 400 mg daily treatment groups showed a response ≥0.5 log10 cfu/mL compared to the 100 mg daily treatment group [85]. PK studies in this report showed that DLM exposure did not increase with drug dosage and reached a plateau at the 300 mg dosage [85]. In a separate clinical trial by Gler et al. [86], a total of 481 individuals diagnosed with pulmonary MDR‐TB were enrolled. Patients were administered optimized background regimens (OBRs) in conjunction with 100 mg (161 patients), 200 mg (160 patients), or a placebo (160 patients) of DLM twice daily over a period of 2 months [86]. The primary outcome measure, sputum‐culture conversion (SCC), was defined as the occurrence of five or more consecutive cultures that exhibited no growth of M. tuberculosis. Patients diagnosed with MDR‐TB exhibited an increase in SCC after 2 months of DLM treatment. These findings suggest that DLM may offer improved treatment possibilities for individuals with MDR‐TB [86]. An investigation conducted by Yang et al. [87] involved the determination of the MIC of DLM against MDR and XDR strains of M. tuberculosis. The results of this study revealed a range of MIC values, which varied from ≤0.025 to >1.6 mg/L [87]. The findings from South Korea indicated that 32 individuals diagnosed with MDR‐TB were administered DLM for 24 weeks [88]. The research findings indicated that following an 8‐week DLM therapy regimen, the rates of culture conversion were 72.2% on solid medium and 50% on liquid medium [88]. After 24 weeks of DLM treatment, culture conversion was 94.4% in the solid medium and 92.9% in the liquid medium. No serious adverse effects or deaths were reported during treatment with DLM [88]. A separate investigation was carried out across Asia, Africa, Europe, and South America involving individuals diagnosed with pulmonary MDR‐TB [89]. These patients were administered an OBR regimen along with 100 mg DLM twice daily for 2 months, followed by a reduced dosage of 200 mg once daily for the subsequent 4 months. The study included a total of 226 patients, while another group of 101 patients received a placebo regimen identical to the aforementioned treatment [89]. The findings of this study suggest that there was no statistically significant difference in the median time to SCC reduction between the two groups at 6 months [89].

In a research conducted by Chen et al. [90], the effective dosage of DLM in a guinea pig model of TB was determined to be 100 mg/kg. This dosage exhibited significant bactericidal activity against M. tuberculosis and effectively eradicated M. tuberculosis in hypoxic lung lesions [90].

Activities of the DLM Against NTM Species

In recent decades, the incidence and prevalence of pulmonary infections caused by NTM have increased globally. In some countries, they have even overtaken TB. As a result, they have become a new threat to human health [91]. The worldwide prevalence of pulmonary infections caused by NTM is primarily attributed to three main pathogens: Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Mycobacterium abscessus, and Mycobacterium kansasi. These particular NTM species are widely recognized as the most frequently encountered pathogens responsible for pulmonary infections [92].

Krieger et al. [93], reported that DLM showed low and varying MICs against MAC strains (from 0.013 to 0.4 μg/mL). The MAC strains exhibited a minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) against DLM, which ranged from 0.013 to 0.4 μg/mL. This study's findings provide initial evidence suggesting that DLM could be a viable treatment option for diseases caused by M. avium‐intracellulare [93]. A study conducted by Kim et al. [94], demonstrated that DLM displayed notably high MIC values for all NTM, excluding M. kansasii. Yu et al. [91] showed that DLM exhibited very different antimicrobial activities against the slow‐growing Mycobacterium species: M. kansasi and M. gordonae.

Mechanisms of Resistance

Global control programs for TB are currently facing a significant global challenge in the form of the spread of DR‐TB. The emergence of drug resistance to anti‐TB drugs has posed a significant challenge to global TB control efforts in recent years. This has resulted in an increase in MDR‐TB and XDR‐TB [95]. The treatment of TB can become complicated due to the resistance to new drugs developed in this particular situation [96].

The frequency of spontaneous resistance to DLM has been reported to be between 6.44 × 10−6 and 4.19 × 10−5 for M. tuberculosis H37Rv, while for M. bovis BCG Tokyo, it ranges from 2.51 × 10−5 to 3.95 × 10−5. To maintain the efficacy of this medication, it is recommended to combine DLM with other active anti‐TB drugs during the course of treatment [97]. DLM is a prodrug whose anti‐TB activity is carried out by activation of deazaflavin‐dependent nitroreductase (F420) (ddn). Resistance to DLM induced by non‐synonymous mutations in one of the five genes (fbiA, fbiB, fbiC, fgd1, and ddn) involved in the biosynthesis of the different stages and modifications of F420 [51, 98]. The fbiA gene encodes a transferase enzyme that facilitates the transfer of a phosphoenolpyrovyl group to FO, resulting in the production of dehydro‐F420‐0. FbiB, an enzyme classified as a γ‐glutamyl ligase, converts dehydro‐F420‐0 to F420‐0 by sequentially attaching L‐glutamate residues to F420‐0. This enzymatic process results in the formation of a poly‐γ‐glutamate tail [51, 98]. The FbiC gene encodes an enzyme known as F420‐0 synthase, which plays a crucial role in the synthesis of the F420 precursor F420‐0. This enzyme facilitates the formation of F420‐0 through its catalytic activity. The Fgd1 gene encodes G6PD, an enzyme that is vital for the redox recycling of F420. The utilization of coenzyme F420 as an electron acceptor facilitates the oxidation of glucose‐6‐phosphate to phosphogluconolactone [51, 98]. Ddn is a dasaflavin (F420‐dependent nitro reductase) that converts DLM to the desnitroform. Resistance to DLM is caused by the loss of this ability to activate DLM. The concentration of DLM decreased when incubated with sensitive strains of M. tuberculosis, because it converted to the desnitroform, whereas no change was observed in DLM‐resistant M. tuberculosis. However, the resistance mechanism of DLM is similar to that of another biocyclic nitroimidazole, PA‐824 [52].

Combination of the DLM With Other Anti‐Tuberculosis Drugs

In the recent years, the effectiveness of two emerging drugs, BDQ and DLM, has been demonstrated in the treatment of MDR‐TB. The treatment approach for MDR‐TB necessitates the use of a combination of several potent drugs. These two new drugs have great potential to improve treatment efficacy, especially for people with complex TB resistance profiles [56, 99].

The efficacy and safety of DLM‐containing therapies against MDR/RR‐TB were evaluated in a meta‐analysis review [100]. The results of 22 observational studies (with 1276 patients) indicated that the treatment success rate was 80.9% for DLM‐containing therapies (591 patients) and 72.8% for DLM‐ and BDQ‐containing therapies (685 patients) [100]. In three experimental studies (with 411 patients), the treatment success rate in the group with DLM‐containing therapies was 72.5% [100].

Ferlazzo et al. [56] conducted a retrospective cohort study that provided evidence of the early safety and efficacy of treating drug‐resistant TB by combining BDQ and DLM in a group of 28 patients in Mumbai (India), Yerevan (Armenia), and Khayelitsha (South Africa) who received 400 mg BDQ once daily for 2 weeks, then 200 mg BDQ three times weekly and 100 mg DLM twice daily. The results presented in this report revealed the absence of any additive or synergistic QTcF‐prolonging impacts when BDQ and DLM were administered together [56]. Das et al. [101] conducted a retrospective cohort study in India involving 24 patients aged 0–19 years with RR‐TB, all of whom were resistant to FQs. The study, which took place from September 2014 to January 2020, utilized a treatment regimen consisting of a combination of BDQ and DLM or administered separately. The results indicated a 96% success rate for the final treatment outcomes, with only 2 out of 12 patients experiencing AEs associated with the new TB drugs [101]. In a comprehensive prospective cohort study carried out by the Global Tuberculosis Network (GTN), a total of 883 patients from 29 different countries were enrolled and treated with BDQ and/or DLM [8]. Among the 477 patients who successfully completed the treatment, 344 patients (72.1%) achieved treatment success [8]. Of the 383 patients who underwent BDQ treatment without DLM, 284 patients (74.2%) experienced successful treatment outcomes. Moreover, 25 patients (6.5%) passed away, 11 patients (2.9%) failed to respond to treatment, and 63 patients (16.5%) were lost to follow‐up [8].

In a retrospective cohort study performed by Mohr‐Holland et al. [102] in South Africa, 22 RR‐TB patients with FQ resistance and HIV‐positive adolescents (10–19 years) treated with BDQ or DLM and a combination of BDQ and DLM were described. The study outcomes demonstrated that 17 individuals (77%) achieved successful treatment, 2 (9%) were lost to follow‐up, 2 (9%) experienced treatment failure, and 1 (5%) passed away [102]. Research has suggested that treatment regimens incorporating BDQ and/or DLM are efficacious, safe, and well‐tolerated among adolescents [102].

The resazurin microtiter assay (REMA) demonstrated that the MICs of DLM alone and in combination with BDQ were 0.063 and 0.015 μg/mL for INH‐monoresistant strains, both alone and in combination with BDQ, as well as for DEL and MFX [103]. For the RIF‐monoresistant strains, the MICs were 0.063 and 0.015 μg/mL for DLM alone, in combination with BDQ, and with DLM and MFX, respectively [103]. The MICs for MDR‐TB isolates were 0.031, 0.015, and 0.015 μg/mL for DLM alone, in combination with BDQ, and with DEL and MFX, respectively. Finally, the XDR‐TB isolates showed MICs of 0.125, 0.015, and 0.015 μg/mL for DLM alone, in combination with BDQ, and with DEL and MFX, respectively [103]. Through the REMA‐based calorimetric chequerboard assay, the fractional inhibitory concentration indices (FICIs) were computed to assess the level of synergy. A FICI ≤0.5 indicated a strong synergistic effect [103]. This suggests that the combination of BDQ + DLM, BDQ + MFX, and DLM + MFX has significant synergistic effects against XDR, RIF‐monoresistant, and INH‐monoresistant clinical MTB isolates [103].

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

Taking DLM with food increases its absorption rate by more than two times, because its solubility and absorption in water is low [97]. The metabolism of DLM is unique. It is primarily metabolized by plasma albumin, which is a non‐hepatic process. In patients taking DLM, four main metabolites (M1–M4), which are not antituberculosis, are detected in plasma by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, mainly CYP3A4 [104]. The synthesis of metabolite M1 (DM‐6705) occurs when the amino acid groups of albumin react with the 5‐carbon of the nitro‐2, 3‐dihydroimidazo [2,1b] oxazole core of DLM, resulting in the cleavage of the imidazooxazole moiety [104]. In humans, the primary pathway for the formation of M2 (DM‐6720) involves the hydroxylation of the oxazole moiety M1, followed by the successive oxidation to ketone form M3 (DM‐6718). The DLM metabolite DM‐6718 is then metabolized via hydrolysis to M4 (DM‐6704), with both processes predominantly catalyzed by CYP3A4 [104]. NADPH‐dependent reactions are not responsible for metabolizing DLM. CYP enzymes in the liver microsomes of animals and humans play a crucial role in catalyzing this drug. At concentrations below 100 μmol/L, which are within the therapeutic range of DLM, eight CYP isoforms (CYP3A, CYP2A6, CYP2D6, CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2E1, CYP2C8/9, and CYP2C19) are not affected by DLM. Studies have shown that the inhibition of some other CYP isoforms by DLM metabolites was observed only at concentrations higher than those found in human plasma after the administration of therapeutic doses. The results of some studies showed that DLM does not pose serious metabolic risks due to drug–drug interactions (DDIs) [104, 105].

In healthy volunteers, concomitant administration of DLM with the first‐line anti‐TB drugs, RIF, reduced exposure to DLM by 47% and increased ETB plasma concentrations by 25% [97].

In vitro studies revealed that DLM and its main metabolites have no inhibitory effect on the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP/ABCG2), P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp; MDR1/ABCB1), solute carrier transporters (SLC transporters), efflux ATP‐binding cassette transporters (ABC transporters), organic cation transporter 1, or organic anion‐transporting polypeptides. According to the results of these studies, clinical interactions of DLM with drugs that are absorbed and excreted by this group of transporters are impossible [104].

The complete bioavailability of DLM is not well known but is estimated to be between 25% and 47%. More than 99% of DLM is bound to proteins and has a high volume of distribution. The half‐life of DLM is 30–38 h and it reaches a stable state 10–14 days after taking DLM. DLM is mainly excreted via the feces, while excretion via urine is <5% [52].

Side Effects of DLM

Clinical studies have shown that in addition to its numerous positive effects on MDR‐ and XDR‐TB, DLM also has few side effects in some patients. Research shows that DLM is well‐tolerated and does not show any genotoxicity or carcinogenic potential in humans and animals [105]. The side effects of DLM include QTc prolongation, anorexia, gastritis, fatigue, anemia and mental disorders and may lead to liver damage and a reduction in white blood cell count; therefore, these side effects must be monitored to ensure the safety of DLM use [9].

It appears that the effect of DLM on QTc interval is related to drug dose and increases 6–10 weeks after treatment, with mainly the metabolite DM‐6705 of DLM being associated with this effect. DM‐6705 levels may be increased by inducers and inhibitors of CYP3A4, which requires more intensive cardiac monitoring. It has recently been recommended that people taking DLM should have an ECG at least once a month. If the corrected QT interval is more than 500 ms, it is not recommended to start or continue taking DLM [97]. To document the negative consequences of DLM in South Africa, Hughes and his team monitored 58 patients with RR‐TB. The study highlighted that vomiting, QTcB >450 ms, and/or myalgia were the most prevalent adverse effects observed in individuals who were prescribed DLM. Moreover, one patient experienced a progressive prolongation of the QT interval and complained of cardiac symptoms, such as palpitations, dizziness, and chest pain [106].

A study was carried out by Mok and colleagues [88] in South Korea on 49 patients who were taking DLM. During the treatment period three patients died due to sudden cardiac complications, acute myocardial infarction, or pneumonia occurring within 1–3 months after the conclusion of DLM therapy [88]. Four weeks after the administration of DLM, three patients demonstrated a QTcF interval of more than 500 ms [88]. An examination carried out in South Korea, involving 318 patients diagnosed with MDR‐TB, who underwent a 6‐month treatment with either BDQ or DLM, revealed that two patients experienced QTcF prolongation (506 and 511 ms) without any cardiac incidents [107]. On the other hand, gastrointestinal issues were reported in five individuals from either the DLM group or the combined drug group [107]. Limitations

The lack of information about studies of BDQ and DLM on children and pregnant women with TB is one of the limitations of the current study. Another limitation is the scarce data of BDQ and DLM in patients with TB who are simultaneously suffering from other bacterial, viral, or fungal infections.

Conclusion

According to this review, the prescription of BDQ and DLM holds great promise for all individuals suffering from MDR/XDR‐TB. These medications provide the potential for a shorter, safer, and more effective treatment option. The meta‐analysis yielded similar data, with 29 studies being selected from 2679 articles [8], and treatment efficacy was observed in 74% of cases. The mere addition of a single new drug to the treatment protocol for patients suffering from extensively drug‐resistant tuberculosis (XDR‐TB) is evidently insufficient [8, 31]. A more comprehensive approach is needed, incorporating multiple new and potent antituberculosis drugs to create a regimen consisting of at least four medications. This strategy will not only shorten the duration of treatment but also ensure sustained positive outcomes, including long‐term benefits [31]. BDQ and DLM are crucial component that should be incorporated into the treatment regimen for patients with MDR‐TB, as per the WHO guidelines. Besides, studies have demonstrated that the inclusion of BDQ in the treatment plan for patients with Charlson Comorbidity Index did not lead to a significant change in therapeutic results (40.9%) compared to the control group (34.7%) [108, 109]. Furthermore, as the optimal treatment for TB involves a blend of medications, the minimal side effects linked to the combination of BDQ and DLM alongside other antituberculosis drugs, are of utmost clinical importance.
