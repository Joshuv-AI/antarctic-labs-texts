Fresh-blood immune profiling of immune-related adverse events in real-world patients identifies organ-specific and steroid responsiveness signatures
Authors: M Velasco Santiago, RS Jurlander, B Albieri, IM Noringriis, JE Sohlin, AH Kverneland, IM Svane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) provides durable benefit across several cancers but carries a substantial risk of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Management relies primarily on corticosteroids, yet responses are variable and difficult to predict. In this observational study, we explored whether early immune phenotypes in fresh whole blood reflect organ-specific irAEs and corticosteroid responsiveness.Materials and methodsWe prospectively enrolled patients receiving ICI who developed grade 2-4 colitis, hepatitis, or nephritis. Peripheral blood was collected at irAE onset (T0) and during early management (T1). Immune profiling was carried out on fresh blood by flow cytometry and unsupervised clustering.ResultsTwenty-seven patients were included (colitis n = 13, hepatitis n = 11, nephritis n = 3). Organ-specific immune patterns were observed at irAE onset: hepatitis showed higher CD27+ T cells and naive B cells; nephritis exhibited reduced T cells and expansion of CD16− natural killer cells; and colitis displayed a more variable profile. Patients were categorized as corticosteroid responders (n = 8), refractory (n = 9), or relapse (n = 10). At T0, responders showed more CD38+ T cells and naive B cells with fewer classical monocytes, relapse patients had increased memory B cells, and refractory patients had lower CD27+ T cells and elevated classical monocytes. Immune shifts from T0 to T1 were modest, though fold-change analyses revealed response-associated patterns, such as preserved CD56+ T cells and monocyte expansion in responders.ConclusionsIn this explorative study, fresh-blood immunophenotyping suggested associations between irAE type, early immune signatures, and corticosteroid response. These hypothesis-generating and preliminary findings support further research into a more individualized immune-guided approach to irAE management.Key words: immune checkpoint inhibitors, immune-related adverse events, irAE management, irAE pathogenesis, flow cytometry, immune profiling

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment landscape of many solid cancers providing significant and durable clinical benefits. Indications for ICI therapy evolve constantly across both disease stages and cancer types.1, 2, 3 However, ICI treatment comes with a significant risk of toxicity, termed immune-related adverse events (irAEs). irAEs can affect virtually any tissue throughout the body, and the severity ranges from mild to potentially life-threatening. The incidence varies in relation to treatment regimen, with up to 55% of patients treated with combination therapy [anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4)] experiencing severe irAEs. Therefore, these irAEs are a growing challenge for both clinicians and patients.4,5

Management of irAEs typically involves prolonged immunosuppressive therapies. The first-line treatment is corticosteroids, except for endocrine irAEs that are primarily treated symptomatically with hormone substitution.6,7 However, not all patients have an adequate response to corticosteroids. This has been addressed in the consensus guidelines from the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC), which describe steroid-unresponsiveness, steroid-resistance, and steroid-dependence patterns.8 Moreover, severe irAEs [Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 3-4] often result in both high peak dose and accumulated dose of steroids, which have been associated with poorer anticancer response to ICI treatment.9,10

To improve the management of irAEs, it is crucial to better understand the underlying pathogenesis. While irAEs share features with classical autoimmune diseases (AIDs), important differences exist, and many aspects remain unclear. In contrast to irAEs, AIDs typically develop spontaneously, persist chronically, and are organ-specific, while irAEs are iatrogenic, frequently acute (although potentially chronic over time), and can affect both any and multiple organs throughout the body.11 Furthermore, distinct pathological features have been described, i.e. in hepatitis.12 Management strategies are also different: while AIDs are most often treated according to disease-specific guidelines with defined immunosuppressants, irAEs are primarily managed with first-line corticosteroids and, if needed, other immunosuppressants based on affected organ and available guidelines from AID treatment.6,13

Flow cytometry is a powerful technique that enables detailed immunophenotyping of blood immune cells at the single-cell level.14 This method is particularly valuable for quantifying dynamic immune cell changes over time and thus can be used to study immunological dynamics during irAE development and management. The method can be carried out on a day-to-day basis on fresh-blood samples and is already used routinely in the clinic, especially for hematological cancers.15,16

In this study, we correlate the results of flow cytometry analysis carried out on fresh peripheral blood with organ-specific irAEs and management outcome. We hypothesize that specific immune profiles are associated with the onset of toxicity and successful management with corticosteroids.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment

In a prospective cohort, we included patients from the Department of Oncology at Herlev Hospital, Denmark. The samples were collected between January 2022 and November 2022. Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: diagnosed with a grade 2-4 colitis, hepatitis, or nephritis, according to CTCAE v5.017; treatment of a solid cancer with ICIs within the previous 6 months; and initiation of corticosteroid management no more than 24 h before inclusion. All patients provided both oral and written informed consent before inclusion in the cohort. The study was approved by the regional ethics committee in Region Hovedstaden, Denmark (registration No. H-21027448).

Sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected at two timepoints: T0 (onset)—within 24 h of corticosteroid initiation, and T1 (management)—2-7 days after initiation of initial management, before initiation of any additional immunosuppressive therapy. Trial design is visualized in the Graphical Abstract.

Clinical assessment and follow-up

Patients were graded according to CTCAE at sampling timepoints and followed through electronic patient charts until corticosteroid tapering was completed, with an ongoing maximum allowed dose of prednisolone 5 mg. Depending on the clinical response to corticosteroids, patients were categorized into three subgroups based on the SITC consensus guidelines: (i) responder—clinical improvement upon steroid initiation and tolerated tapering without complications; (ii) refractory—no sign of clinical response to corticosteroids within the first 72 h of management; (iii) relapse—required secondary additional immunosuppressive treatment (i.e. infliximab) and/or an increase in corticosteroid dose of at least 25 mg during tapering. Eligibility criteria and definitions of steroid responsiveness are summarized in the Graphical Abstract.

All clinical data were gathered in a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap)-built database.18

Sample analysis

Phenotypic characterization of fresh whole blood

Peripheral blood was drawn into EDTA tubes (Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria; # 455045), and 100 μl of blood was transferred into a 5-ml Round Bottom Polystyrene Test Tube (Avantor, Radnor, PA, USA; #734-0442). Whole blood was stained with 30 μl of pre-made antibody mixes (see Supplementary Figure S1A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101587, for antibody panel) within 2 h of blood collection. Samples were incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Afterward, 2 ml of a lysis solution (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA; #349202) was added to remove red blood cells, and the samples were incubated for 10 more minutes at room temperature in the dark. Samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 400 g, washed once with wash buffer [0.5% bovine serum albumin; (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA; #A4503-50G) and 2 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; #15575-038) in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich, #D8537)], and resuspended in 100 μl of wash buffer before acquisition on the Novocyte Quanteon (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Data analysis

Unsupervised clustering of peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Data analysis was carried out using the Flowjo software (version 10.6.1, BD Biosciences) and R (version 4.2.2, R Core Team, 2023) with the Spectre R package19 (instructions and source code are available at https://github.com/ImmuneDynamics/spectre). Initial gating of CD45+ cells was conducted in Flowjo, where 5000 gated events per sample were exported for further analysis in R. The merged dataset was analyzed using the FlowSOM algorithm, and the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction algorithm was employed for cellular visualization.20,21 Percentages of population were exported for each patient, and the graphical plots were created with GraphPad Prism (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA; version 10.4.0) and Inkscape (The Inkscape Project, Boston, MA, USA; version 1.3.2).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out in R studio (version 4.2.2.) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0). We used dosing equivalences to convert corticosteroid doses (https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/2040/steroid-conversion-calculator). Continuous outcomes were presented as means and ranges, while categorical variables were presented with frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of immune phenotyping data across patient groups were carried out using ordinary two-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons. For paired analyses between T0 and T1 within the same patients, we applied non-parametric paired testing (Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test) with Holm–Šidák’s correction for multiple comparisons. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, we included 27 patients hospitalized with colitis (n = 13), hepatitis (n = 11), or nephritis (n = 3). Most patients were male (56%), with a median age of 66 years (range 52-78 years). Most patients were diagnosed with malignant melanoma (51%). Half of the patients (48%) received checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy [anti-PD-1/anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (n = 12) and anti-CTLA-4 (n = 1)], and the other half (52%) received combination therapy (anti-PD-1 + anti-CTLA-4). In total, 15 (56%) of the included patients experienced more than one irAE during ICI treatment; 3 (11%) at inclusion, 7 (26%) during management, 3 (11%) at a previous timepoint, and 2 (7%) experienced other irAEs both previously, at inclusion, and during management. Multi-system toxicity was observed in all three subgroups of patients. In total 18 (67%) had the T0 sample collected before corticosteroid initiation (corticosteroid naive). The median time from T0 to T1 was 4 days. All baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1, and an overview is visualized in Figure 1.

Immune cell subsets at irAE onset and during corticosteroid management

We carried out high-dimensional flow cytometry on fresh whole-blood samples collected at the irAE onset (T0) and 2-7 days after corticosteroid-mediated management (T1). Initial manual gating was carried out to select CD45+ cells, and granulocytes were excluded due to the lack of markers for these populations in our antibody panel (Supplementary Figure S1A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101587). Across all samples, we identified 14 distinct clusters, which were annotated based on the expression of lineage-specific markers including T cells (clusters 1-4, red tones), B cells (clusters 5-8, blue tones), natural killer (NK) cells (clusters 9-10, yellow tones), monocytes (clusters 11-13, green tones), and other immune cells (cluster 14, gray). Presumably, cluster 14 included residual granulocytes that were not fully excluded during manual gating (Figure 2A). The immunological identity and functional characteristics of each subset are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101587.

We then assessed the relative abundance and dynamics of these immune cell populations at onset (T0) and during irAE management (T1). As illustrated by the density plot in Figure 2B and C, corticosteroid treatment was associated with a relative reduction of T-cell subsets (red tones), accompanied by an increase in the proportion of B cells (blue tones) and monocytes (green tones).

Immunological signatures related to irAE type and ICI type

When comparing the IrAE types [colitis (n = 13), hepatitis (n = 11), or nephritis (n = 3)], density plots of the UMAP projections showed differences in the distribution of immune cell subsets across the three groups at irAE onset (Figure 3A). When examining the overall composition of the immune populations, the stacked bar plots suggested visual differences across irAE types with hepatitis samples showing a relatively higher proportion of T cells, whereas nephritis samples displayed lower relative T-cell frequencies and a higher contribution of NK cells (Figure 3B).

To further dissect these differences, we compared the frequencies of individual immune subsets among the irAE groups. CD27+ T cells were significantly higher in hepatitis (mean ± standard deviation: 30.77% ± 9.91%) compared with colitis (23.11% ± 15.72%, P = 0.048) and nephritis (17.36% ± 3.32%, P = 0.029). Naive B cells were also more abundant in hepatitis (4.56% ± 3.56%) relative to colitis (3.06% ± 1.45%, P = 0.049). In contrast, nephritis was characterized by a significantly higher proportion of CD16− NK cells (4.73% ± 6.27%) compared with colitis (0.40% ± 0.28%, P = 0.045) (Figure 3C). Together, these findings indicate that distinct irAE manifestations might be associated with different lymphocyte populations.

We also compared patients treated with ICI monotherapy (n = 13) versus those receiving combination therapy (n = 14) at T0. No significant differences were observed in the percentage of the different immune populations at T0 across the patient groups (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101587).

Clinical response to corticosteroid treatment

Eight patients (30%) were categorized as responsive, with a mean corticosteroid peak dose of 109 mg (50-225 mg) and a mean cumulative 6-week dose of 1561 mg (600-2925 mg). Nine patients (33%) were categorized as refractory, four of whom (44%) needed secondary immunosuppressive management; overall, this group received higher doses of steroids, with a peak-steroid dose of 160 mg (50-200 mg) and a mean 6-week cumulative-steroid dose of 3374 mg (1925-5413 mg). Finally, 10 patients (37%) were categorized as relapse patients, 7 of whom (70%) needed secondary immunosuppressive management. In this group, we found a mean peak-steroid dose and mean 6-week cumulative-steroid dose of 103 mg (50-200 mg) and 1923 mg (775-3638 mg), respectively. The mean peak dose and 6-week cumulative dose of corticosteroids for the entire cohort were 124 mg and 2328 mg, respectively. Clinical data on responsiveness are summarized in Table 2.

Immune signatures of corticosteroid responsiveness

To explore the immunological correlates of treatment outcome, we compared immune cell subsets across response groups. Specifically, we assessed whether onset (T0) immune landscapes could discriminate between responders (n = 8), refractory (n = 9), and relapse (n = 10) patients, and whether corticosteroid-induced shifts from T0 to T1 differed according to response. This approach allowed us to identify both potential predictive signatures of steroid responsiveness and dynamic changes associated with successful versus insufficient disease control. Density plots of the UMAP revealed differences in the distribution of immune cell subsets across response groups (Figure 4A). Overall, stacked bar plots illustrated a general reduction in T-cell subsets from T0 to T1 across all response groups. Visual inspection suggested that refractory patients exhibited lower T-cell frequencies and higher proportions of monocytes at both timepoints compared with responders and relapsing patients (Figure 4B).

At onset (T0), distinct immune signatures were observed. Compared with relapse patients, responders displayed a higher frequency of CD38+ T cells (9.76% ± 7.70% versus 18.64% ± 10.70%, P = 0.036), whereas refractory patients showed lower frequencies of CD27+ T cells (30.71% ± 14.39% versus 20.70% ± 7.00%, P = 0.015). Naive B cells were more abundant in responders (5.27% ± 4.65%) relative to both relapse (3.16% ± 1.54%, P = 0.019) and refractory patients (3.34% ± 1.60%, P = 0.036), while relapse patients exhibited the highest overall memory B-cell frequencies (2.57% ± 3.54%) compared with both responders (0.47% ± 0.24%, P = 0.020) and refractory patients (0.80% ± 0.51%, P = 0.043). Finally, classical monocytes were lower in responders (15.94% ± 8.72%) compared with relapse (25.75% ± 17.75%, P = 0.017) and refractory patients (29.19% ± 9.60%, P = 0.002) (Figure 4C). These onset differences suggest that specific immune populations may serve as potential predictors of corticosteroid responsiveness.

Although onset differences were evident, few significant changes in the immune populations were observed from T0 to T1, aside from a consistent increase in memory B cells and a decrease in intermediate monocytes (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101587). Nevertheless, fold-change analysis revealed distinct patterns of immune modulation across the response groups. CD56+ T cells decreased in the refractory group (fold change: 0.64 ± 0.37) while remaining largely stable in responders (1.09 ± 0.49, P = 0.045). Conversely, classical monocytes exhibited a markedly higher fold increase in responders (2.34 ± 1.37) compared with both relapse (1.25 ± 0.75, P = 0.036) and refractory patients (1.25 ± 0.54, P = 0.043) (Figure 4D). These findings suggest that differential modulation of specific T-cell and monocyte subsets may reflect or contribute to corticosteroid responsiveness.

Discussion

As the number of cancer patients being treated with ICIs continues to rise, the need for better management of irAEs also increases. In this observational prospective study including patients with severe immune-related colitis, hepatitis, or nephritis, we describe the use of flow cytometry as a feasible method for monitoring early irAE management.

The immunophenotyping data revealed that different irAE types are associated with different immune profiles at baseline, pointing toward organ-specific immune remodeling. Hepatitis was characterized by enrichment of CD27+ T cells and naive B cells, reflecting a mixed immune profile with expanded memory-like T cells and a reservoir of unprimed B cells ready for activation, consistent with prior reports linking B-cell activity to liver injury and autoimmune liver conditions.22 In contrast, nephritis was marked by reduced T-cell frequencies and expansion of CD16− NK cells, a subset typically associated with cytokine-driven inflammation and tissue infiltration, aligning with previous observations of NK and NK T-cell involvement in renal inflammation such as lupus nephritis.23 Colitis, by comparison, showed fewer consistent baseline alterations, suggesting a more heterogeneous immunological basis. Together, these findings reinforce the concept that irAEs are not driven by a single shared mechanism but instead reflect organ-specific immune responses, with implications for both clinical presentation and therapeutic strategies. The B-cell alterations in hepatitis further align with emerging evidence of B-cell involvement in irAEs,24 a line of investigation our group is now pursuing on a larger patient cohort.

The immunophenotyping data also revealed potential signatures of corticosteroid responsiveness. Importantly, patients from all three irAE types were represented across the response groups, indicating that these patterns are not solely driven by toxicity type. Notably, no nephritis patients fell into the relapse category. However, given the small number of nephritis cases (n = 3), this observation likely reflects limited sample size rather than a true biological absence. Responders were distinguished by higher frequencies of CD38+ T cells, reflecting a transient immune activation state, alongside an expanded pool of naive B cells, suggestive of preserved or restored immune homeostasis following corticosteroid treatment. Relapse patients were characterized by elevated memory B cells, consistent with ongoing antigenic stimulation and possible persistence of autoreactive clones. Refractory patients, in contrast, showed reduced CD27+ T cells, suggesting impaired T-cell maturation, and increased classical monocytes at baseline, a population linked to inflammatory recruitment and tissue infiltration. This aligns with previous studies reporting steroid non-responders having higher CD8+ effector memory subsets (CD27− cells) and lower proportions of naive CD8+ T cells (CD27+ cells),25 suggesting shared alterations in T-cell differentiation states across studies, despite differences in panel design and the inability to directly resolve CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets in the present study.

Dynamic changes following treatment revealed further differences: responders exhibited a pronounced expansion of classical monocytes from irAE onset to management, whereas monocyte levels in refractory patients remained relatively stable over time. In parallel, responders showed stability of CD56+ T cells, which possess regulatory and cytotoxic capacity, whereas the refractory group showed a decline in these CD56+ T cells, potentially impairing immune regulation. These patterns suggest that corticosteroid responsiveness could be linked not to absolute monocyte abundance at onset, but rather to effective monocyte mobilization during early management, as well as preservation of specific T-cell subsets. While previous studies did not identify an association between monocyte abundance and steroid responsiveness, the dynamic monocyte modulation observed here may reflect early innate immune responses that are more readily captured using fresh whole-blood immunophenotyping, particularly during the acute phase of irAE management. In our cohort, paired follow-up samples were collected at a median of 4 days after corticosteroid initiation, a time window that may capture evolving immune dynamics distinct from studies focusing on later or unpaired timepoints. Importantly, our findings extend beyond prior T-cell-centric studies by highlighting B-cell and monocyte subsets as potential early biomarkers of treatment outcome, complementing previous studies that primarily described irAE-associated peripheral blood T- and B-cell dynamics.26 However, differences in sampling strategy, timing relative to corticosteroid initiation, and use of fresh-blood versus frozen peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (as in the study by van Eijs et al.25) may contribute to the partially divergent immune associations reported.

We observed notable differences in corticosteroid dosing and the need for secondary immunosuppressive therapies across patient subgroups. In particular, the relapse group exhibited the lowest mean peak-steroid dose, suggesting that while these patients initially respond to corticosteroids, they tend to develop dependency or complications during tapering, necessitating additional treatment. In contrast, the refractory patient group received the highest peak dose of corticosteroids. Interestingly, none of the nephritis patients experienced relapse, although two were classified as steroid-refractory. It should be noted that categorization of corticosteroid responsiveness remains clinically complex, encompassing partially overlapping concepts such as steroid refractoriness, steroid resistance, and steroid dependence.8 While more granular stratification may further refine immune correlates of treatment outcome, such subdivision was not feasible within the present cohort and underscores the need for larger, more homogenous cohorts to enable refined patient grouping in future studies.

The complexity of both irAE development and, importantly, the management of irAEs is the reason for irAEs being referred to as the ‘Achilles’ heel of immune checkpoint inhibitors’.27 Exploring strategies to guide clinicians in personalized and optimized treatment of irAEs is crucial since both high corticosteroid doses and secondary immune suppression have been linked to poorer ICI outcomes.28 Furthermore, chronic or persistent irAEs are associated with a significant decrease in patient-reported quality of life, while treatment with corticosteroids comes with the risk of several severe side-effects such as diabetes, infections, gastrointestinal bleeding, and osteoporosis.29,30

Some limitations in this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the included cohort is heterogeneous, including patients with various cancer types and treatment regimens; notably, only one patient received anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy, limiting our ability to assess ICI-specific immune effects. Furthermore, 15 (56%) patients experienced multi-system toxicity at different timepoints throughout the course of ICI therapy, reflecting toxicity patterns in routine clinical practice. By study design, inclusion did not preclude initiation of irAE management and therefore one-third of the population was exposed to steroids before initial blood sampling. These limitations could affect the interpretation of the immunological signatures described. Importantly, the study included a small sample size, especially for nephritis patients, which limits the statistical power and the ability to draw definitive conclusions about subgroup differences. The final cohort size reflects strict inclusion criteria, including the requirement for paired fresh-blood samples collected at both irAE onset and early management, clear clinical classification of corticosteroid responsiveness, and exclusion of irAE types represented by single patients. In addition, the flow cytometry panel was designed to enable broad immunophenotyping across major immune lineages rather than detailed characterization of individual T-cell subsets. As a result, canonical T-cell populations such as CD4+ and CD8+ T cells could not be directly studied, which limits a more detailed interpretation of T-cell-specific effects. Accordingly, the findings of this study should be regarded as exploratory and require validations in larger and more homogeneous cohorts, for which patient recruitment is currently ongoing.

Despite these limitations, the prospective design and timely sample collection (within 24 h of corticosteroid initiation) and processing (within 2 h) strengthen the study’s clinical relevance. In the study, we carried out flow cytometry of fresh blood during the acute phase of an irAE, enhancing the potential relevance of this method in the clinic as an early prognostic tool for corticosteroid responsiveness. The focus on the immunological landscape during the critical window of corticosteroid initiation and early management adds a valuable dimension to understanding irAE management in real-world clinical practice unlike many other studies that focus primarily on the pathogenic mechanisms driving irAEs following ICI therapy.31, 32, 33

In conclusion, this observational study highlights the potential of fresh-blood flow cytometric immune profiling as a feasible method to potentially identify organ-specific irAE signatures and predict corticosteroid responsiveness, paving the way toward more personalized irAE management strategies. Future directions include expanding this analysis to a larger and less heterogeneous prospective cohort, integrating proteomic investigation and expanded flow panels for a more comprehensive immune characterization, especially on the different B-cell subsets.
