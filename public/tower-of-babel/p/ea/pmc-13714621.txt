Pharmacokinetics and safety of tezepelumab in children with mild, moderate or severe asthma and adolescents with mild‐to‐moderate asthma
Authors: Anna Lundahl, Jonathan Grigg, Lubna Abuqayyas, Piotr Kuna, Gillian Hunter, Beata Pawlikowska, Jacob Leander, Michael Levin, Atul Gupta, James Paton, Katharine C. Pike, Jane R. Parnes, Gun Almqvist, Stephanie L. Roseti, the study investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTezepelumab is a human monoclonal antibody that blocks the activity of thymic stromal lymphopoietin and is approved for adolescents and adults with severe asthma. This analysis assessed pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics and safety of tezepelumab up to 85 days postdose in adolescents and children.MethodsThe adolescent PK study (NCT02512900), in adolescents aged 12–17 years with mild‐to‐moderate asthma, and TRAILHEAD (NCT04673630), in children aged 5–11 years with mild, moderate or severe asthma, were both open‐label, single‐dose, Phase 1 studies.ResultsIn the adolescent PK study, 21 adolescents (median age: 14 years) received a single 140‐mg subcutaneous tezepelumab dose. The mean maximum concentration (C max; 24.0 [SD: 6.6] μg/mL) was observed at a median time of 6 (range: 1–20) days postdose, with a mean terminal half‐life of 25.3 (SD: 4.7) days. The mean area under the concentration–time curve (AUC0–infinity) was 952 (SD: 289) μg*day/mL. In TRAILHEAD, 18 children (median age: 8 years) received a single 70‐mg subcutaneous tezepelumab dose. The C max (27.1 μg/mL [SD: 11.9]) was observed at a median time of 3.5 (range: 2–10) days postdose, with a mean terminal half‐life of 25.7 (SD: 5.9) days. The mean AUC0–infinity was 974 (SD: 320) μg*day/mL. Changes from baseline in blood eosinophil count, fractional exhaled nitric oxide and serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) reflected expected pharmacodynamic effects of tezepelumab. No new safety signals were identified.ConclusionsThese results were consistent with previous studies of tezepelumab in other groups, supporting development of tezepelumab for asthma in the paediatric population.

INTRODUCTION

The worldwide prevalence of childhood asthma has been estimated at 10%. 1 Approximately 3%–20% (depending on diagnostic criteria and region) of children with asthma experience severe disease, 2 , 3 exhibiting higher morbidity and an increased risk of mortality compared with those with mild‐to‐moderate asthma. 4 , 5 Uncontrolled paediatric asthma is associated with a reduced health‐related quality of life, persistent bronchial obstruction and a higher rate of asthma exacerbations, which can be severe and life‐threatening. 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, children with severe, uncontrolled asthma face considerable personal, familial and economic burdens, including school and parental work absences, hospital stays and medication costs. 8 , 9 Treatment of such patients contributes significantly to the overall healthcare costs related to asthma and presents a considerable clinical challenge to paediatricians.

Biologic treatments that target the Type 2 (T2) inflammatory pathway involved in asthma pathogenesis (specifically omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab and dupilumab) are currently approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of allergic and eosinophilic asthma in children aged 6–11 years. 10 , 11 , 12 However, no biologic treatment that works across asthma phenotypes is currently approved for children younger than 12 years old.

Thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), an epithelial cytokine at the top of the inflammatory cascade, plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of asthma. 13 It is primarily expressed by the airway epithelium and is released in response to environmental triggers, activating multiple downstream inflammatory pathways, including T2 and non‐T2 processes, through its interactions with various adaptive and innate immune cells and structural cells. 13 TSLP levels have been shown to positively correlate with disease severity and impaired lung function in patients with asthma. 14 , 15 , 16

Tezepelumab is a human immunoglobulin (Ig)G2λ monoclonal antibody that blocks the activity of TSLP 17 and is approved globally at a dose of 210 mg subcutaneously (SC) every 4 weeks (Q4W) for the treatment of adults and adolescents (aged 12–17 years) with severe asthma. 18 , 19 In the Phase 3 NAVIGATOR study (NCT03347279), treatment with tezepelumab 210 mg SC Q4W vs. placebo led to significant reductions in the annualized asthma exacerbation rate and clinically meaningful improvements in lung function (pre‐bronchodilator [BD] forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1]) and health‐related quality of life in adults and adolescents with severe, uncontrolled asthma. 20 The pharmacokinetics (PK) and dose–response of tezepelumab are well characterized in adults 21 , 22 but have not yet been reported in children.

This analysis reports the results of two Phase 1 studies, the adolescent PK study (NCT02512900) and TRAILHEAD (NCT04673630), which assessed the PK, immunogenicity, pharmacodynamics (PD), safety and tolerability of tezepelumab after a single subcutaneous dose in adolescents (aged 12–17 years) and children (aged 5–11 years), respectively. These studies were performed to support the dose selection of tezepelumab in two Phase 3 studies in patients with severe, uncontrolled asthma: NAVIGATOR, which included adolescents aged 12–18 years, and HORIZON (NCT06023589), which has enrolled children aged 5–11 years.

METHODS

Study design

The adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD were Phase 1, open‐label, single‐dose studies of tezepelumab conducted in adolescents with mild‐to‐moderate asthma and children with mild, moderate or severe asthma, respectively. The primary objective of both studies was the characterization of PK in adolescents and children with asthma.

The adolescent PK study was performed at six investigational sites in Poland. Eligible participants were adolescents aged 12–17 years (inclusive) who had physician‐diagnosed asthma for at least 6 months before screening. Participants must have been receiving low‐, medium‐ or high‐dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS; total daily dose ≥100‐μg fluticasone propionate dry powder inhaler or equivalent) plus another controller, with a stable dose for at least 3 months before screening. Pre‐BD FEV1 at screening must have been at least 70% of the predicted normal value, with an increase in post‐BD FEV1 of at least 12% or at least 200 mL. Detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria are provided in Table S1.

At least 20 participants were planned to be enrolled in the adolescent PK study, 10 aged 12–14 years (Cohort 1) and 10 aged 15–17 years (Cohort 2). In addition, at least five participants were to weigh between 30 and 50 kg. All participants received a single subcutaneous dose of tezepelumab 140 mg on Day 1, with a 12‐week (84 days) PK and safety follow‐up period (Figure S1a). Treatment was administered via two injections (70 mg/mL, 2 × 1 mL), one in each anterior thigh, in a formulation with excipients including sodium acetate, sucrose and polysorbate 20.

TRAILHEAD was performed at six centres in the United Kingdom, Hungary and South Africa. Eligible participants were children aged 5–11 years (inclusive) who had physician‐diagnosed asthma for at least 6 months before screening. Participants must have been receiving low‐, medium‐ or high‐dose ICS (total daily dose ≥100‐μg fluticasone propionate dry powder inhaler or equivalent) plus another controller for at least 6 months, with a stable dose for at least 3 months before screening. Pre‐BD FEV1 at screening must have been at least 50% of the predicted normal value. Detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria are provided in Table S2.

Approximately 24 participants were planned to be enrolled in TRAILHEAD, with approximately 14 participants receiving tezepelumab. At least four participants were to have a body weight of below 25 kg and at least three participants were to have a body weight of between 25 and 40 kg. All participants received a single subcutaneous dose of tezepelumab 70 mg on Day 1, with a 12‐week (84 day) PK and safety follow‐up period (Figure S1b). Tezepelumab was administered via a single injection (110 mg/mL, 1 × 0.65 mL) in the anterior thigh, in a formulation with excipients including acetate, L‐proline and polysorbate 80.

In both studies, tezepelumab was given as an add‐on to an existing asthma controller medication regimen. Additional controller or rescue medication use during the study was allowed.

Outcomes and assessments

The primary objective of both studies was to describe the PK profile of tezepelumab in the respective patient populations. In the adolescent PK study, serum samples for PK analysis were collected before tezepelumab administration on Day 1 and on Days 2, 4, 7, 11, 15, 22, 29, 43, 57 and 85/end of study. In TRAILHEAD, serum samples for PK analysis were collected before tezepelumab administration on Day 1 and on Days 3, 7, 11, 15, 29, 57 and 85/end of study. Serum tezepelumab concentrations were measured by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. The limit of quantification for the assay in both the adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD was 10 ng/mL. PK parameters were derived from tezepelumab serum concentration/time profiles using noncompartmental analyses. The parameters assessed were the area under the concentration–time curve to the last quantifiable time point (AUC0–t ) and to infinite time (AUC0–infinity), maximum mean serum concentration (C max), time to reach C max (t max), terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2), apparent clearance from the central compartment (CL/F) and apparent steady‐state volume of distribution.

To compare exposures across age groups, the established population PK model of tezepelumab 23 was used to derive individual post hoc (empirical Bayes) estimates of CL/F for participants in the two Phase 1 studies (the adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD) and the Phase 3 NAVIGATOR study. Individual CL/F estimates were then used to calculate predicted steady‐state area under the concentration–time curve (AUCss), which was compared graphically across studies and age groups.

Immunogenicity assessment was a secondary objective of both studies. In the adolescent PK study, serum samples to measure the presence of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) were collected before tezepelumab administration on Day 1 and on Days 29, 57 and 85/end of study. In TRAILHEAD, serum samples for ADA analysis were collected before tezepelumab administration on Days 1, 29 and 85/end of study (Figure S1). Confirmed ADA‐positive samples were tested using a neutralizing antibody (nAb) assay.

PD/efficacy evaluation was an exploratory objective in both studies. In the adolescent PK study, pulmonary function (pre‐BD FEV1) was measured at Days 0, 29 and 85, blood eosinophil counts (BECs) were measured at Days 0, 2, 4, 7, 11, 15, 22, 29, 43, 57 and 85, and serum total IgE levels were measured at Days 0 and 43. In TRAILHEAD, pre‐BD FEV1, BEC, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels and serum total IgE levels were measured at Days 0, 14, 28 and 84.

Safety was assessed in terms of adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs at enrolment and at every subsequent study visit. AEs were coded by Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (Version 19.0 for the adolescent PK study and Version 25.0 for TRAILHEAD), and the type, incidence, severity and relationship to the study drug were summarized.

Statistical analyses

No formal statistical hypothesis tests were performed, and no formal sample size calculation was conducted. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize PK and non‐PK results.

Ethics

The protocols and protocol amendments, informed consent forms and other relevant documents for both studies were reviewed and approved by independent ethics committees and/or institutional review boards before the studies were initiated. The studies were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were consistent with International Council for Harmonisation/Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Central approvals for the adolescent PK study were obtained from independent ethics committees in Poland (RNN/121/15/KE). Central approvals for TRAILHEAD were obtained from independent ethics committees in London, United Kingdom (20/LO/1041), and Cape Town, South Africa (702/2021). See Tables S3 and S4 for further details on the ethics committees that provided approvals for the adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD, respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants' legal representatives, and written informed assent was obtained from the patient, together with any locally required authorization. The authors confirm that the principal investigators for this paper are Piotr Kuna (the adolescent PK study) and Jonathan Grigg (TRAILHEAD).

RESULTS

Participant disposition and baseline clinical characteristics

In the adolescent PK study, between 22 September 2015 and 17 May 2016, 21 participants (15 males and 6 females) received tezepelumab (Figure 1a and Table 1). The median age (range) was 14 years (12–17 years). Six participants weighed between 30 and 50 kg, inclusive, at baseline. The median body weight was 56.5 kg (range: 39–92 kg), and the median body mass index was 21.2 kg/m2 (range: 17–27 kg/m2). No participants had visited the emergency department up to 12 months before study entry owing to their asthma; no participants had been admitted to the hospital in this period. All participants were using at least one ICS at baseline, with most participants receiving budesonide (42.9%) or fluticasone propionate (38.1%).

In TRAILHEAD, between 23 February 2021 and 27 September 2022, 18 participants (11 males and 7 females) received tezepelumab (Figure 1b and Table 1). The median age (range) was 8 years (5–11 years). Nine participants had a body weight below 25 kg, five participants had a body weight between 25 and 40 kg, and four participants had a body weight above 40 kg. Eight of the 18 participants experienced an exacerbation within the 6 months before screening. All participants were using ICS at baseline, with the most commonly used ICS being budesonide (38.9% of the participants).

PK

In the adolescent PK study, the mean serum concentrations of tezepelumab peaked at approximately 6 days after administration (t max), with a C max of 24.0 μg/mL. The drug exposures declined with a mono‐exponential phase until 84 h postdose, with a t 1/2 of 25.3 days. Exposure within the study (after administration of 140 mg SC) was slightly higher in the participants with the lowest body weights (30–50 kg) in comparison with the participants above 50 kg. The mean AUC0–t were 1110 and 739 μg*day/mL, and mean C max were 29.8 and 21.7 μg/mL in participants weighing 30–50 kg and >50 kg, respectively. Immunogenicity

In the adolescent PK study, there was one participant (4.8%) who was ADA‐positive at baseline and persistently ADA‐positive post baseline (ADA‐positive on Days 29, 57 and 85) (Table S3); however, the PK profile of this participant was not altered, suggesting no impact due to the presence of ADA. Because the ADA was present at baseline, it is unlikely to be treatment‐emergent. No other participants were ADA‐positive at baseline or at any post‐baseline time point, and no participants developed nAbs following administration of tezepelumab.

In TRAILHEAD, there were three ADA‐positive participants (16.7%); none of them was classified as treatment‐emergent ADA positive (Table S5). No participants were nAb‐positive.

Pharmacodynamics

In the adolescent PK study, following administration of tezepelumab 140 mg SC on Day 1, an initial reduction in BEC was observed from baseline to Day 4 in both 12‐ to 14‐year‐olds (Cohort 1; from a mean [SD] of 371 cells/μL [337 cells/μL; n = 11] to 238 cells/μL [238 cells/μL; n = 10]; mean [SD] change from baseline: 158 cells/μL [140 cells/μL; n = 10]) and 15‐ to 17‐year‐olds (Cohort 2; from a mean [SD] of 269 cells/μL [266 cells/μL; n = 10] to 156 cells/μL [72 cells/μL; n = 10]; mean [SD] change from baseline: 113 cells/μL [216 cells/μL; n = 10]), which continued to Day 15 (Figure 4a). By Day 85, mean BEC was gradually returning to baseline in Cohort 2 but remained decreased relative to baseline in Cohort 1. FeNO levels were not measured in the adolescent PK study.

Changes from baseline in biomarkers of inflammation in the adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD: (a, b) blood eosinophil count, (c) FeNO level and (d, e) serum total immunoglobulin E level. FeNO levels were not measured in the adolescent PK study. FeNO, fractional exhaled nitric oxide.

A modest decrease in serum total IgE level from baseline (mean [SD]: 477.5 KU/L [341.6 KU/L; n = 10]) to Day 43 (mean [SD]: 410.2 KU/L [342.9 KU/L; n = 11]) was seen in Cohort 1, representing a mean (SD) change from baseline of −33.8 KU/L (57.5 KU/L; n = 10) (Figure 4d). There was no notable change in serum total IgE level in Cohort 2, with the discrepancy between age groups likely attributable to sample size effects. Although a modest increase in pre‐BD FEV1 was observed from Days 29 to 85 (Figure S2a), overall, there was no substantial change in pulmonary function parameters following a single dose of tezepelumab.

In TRAILHEAD, following administration of tezepelumab 70 mg SC on Day 1, there was an initial mean (SD) reduction in BEC of 248 cells/μL (352 cells/μL; n = 16), from a mean (SD) of 438 cells/μL (389 cells/μL; n = 18) at baseline to 188 cells/μL (112 cells/μL; n = 16) on Day 28, followed by a gradual return to baseline by Day 84 (Figure 4b). FeNO levels decreased by a mean (SD) of 17.4 ppb (20.6 ppb; n = 12), from a mean (SD) of 32.6 ppb (26.1 ppb; n = 13) at baseline to 13.7 ppb (10.6 ppb; n = 15) on Day 14, followed by a return to baseline by Day 84 (Figure 4c). No notable changes in serum total IgE levels (Figure 4e) or pulmonary function parameters (Figure S2b) were observed following a single dose of tezepelumab.

Safety

Overall, clinical laboratory and other safety assessments were consistent with the reported safety profile of tezepelumab. No new safety or tolerability concerns were raised in these studies.

In the adolescent PK study, tezepelumab was well tolerated in 12‐ to 17‐year‐old participants with mild‐to‐moderate asthma. In total, 8/21 (38%) participants receiving tezepelumab reported AEs (Table 3). One participant (9.1%) in Cohort 1 (age 12–14 years) experienced a mild AE of pyrexia that was considered by the investigator to be related to tezepelumab but which resolved without further issues. Five participants reported AEs of moderate intensity; all other AEs were of mild intensity. The AEs reported as moderate intensity were acute sinusitis, bronchitis, nasopharyngitis, pharyngitis and a ligament sprain, each reported as a single event in a single participant. No AEs with the outcome of death were reported, and no serious AEs were reported during the study. No participants reported AEs leading to discontinuation of the study.

In TRAILHEAD, tezepelumab was well tolerated in 5‐ to 11‐year‐old participants with mild, moderate or severe asthma; there were no safety findings of concern. In total, 7/18 (39%) participants reported AEs (Table 3). None of the AEs reported was considered causally related to tezepelumab by the investigator. Two participants reported AEs of moderate intensity; all other AEs were of mild intensity. The AEs reported as moderate intensity were asthma and cough, each reported as a single event in a single participant. No AEs with the outcome of death were reported, and no serious AEs were reported during the study. No participants reported AEs leading to discontinuation of the study.

DISCUSSION

The adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD were open‐label, Phase 1 studies and provided initial insights into the PK, PD and safety profiles of tezepelumab in adolescents and children with asthma. This analysis is the first reported PK/PD evaluation of tezepelumab administration in children aged 5–11 years. The doses investigated in the adolescent PK study and TRAILHEAD were selected to achieve systemic exposures comparable to those associated with an acceptable safety profile and clinical benefit in adults. This was achieved using population PK modelling and simulations with allometric scaling to account for lower body weight, and exposure–response analyses. 23 As asthma pathophysiology and the upstream T2‐inflammatory mechanism targeted by tezepelumab are shared between children and adults, with no evidence of age‐specific differences in target biology, 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 the exposure–response relationship is expected to be similar in paediatric patients and adults. This exposure‐matching approach was therefore used to support dose selection in children and adolescents. Although body weight is the primary determinant of tezepelumab exposure, an age‐based dosing scheme was employed to align with existing regulatory age definitions and with the approved adult dosing framework.

Given the linear PK of tezepelumab and extensive data from single‐ and multiple‐ascending‐dose studies in adults, multiple‐dose exposure can be reliably extrapolated from single‐dose PK. 23 An open‐label, single‐dose design was therefore selected to support dose selection for subsequent Phase 3 studies while minimizing unnecessary paediatric exposure. A limitation of these studies is that it is not possible to evaluate or extrapolate efficacy without repeated dosing, and any potential benefit to the patients is limited. However, efficacy will be assessed comprehensively in the ongoing HORIZON Phase 3 paediatric study (NCT06023589).

Notably, no serious AEs were reported, and none of the participants developed nAbs. These results are consistent with the low immunogenicity rate of tezepelumab observed across adult and adolescent populations. 20 , 29 , 30

Tezepelumab has been evaluated in several Phase 1 clinical studies in adult participants, with initial single and multiple ascending dose studies (Studies 20070620 and 20080390, respectively), 29 one allergen challenge study (Study 20101183) 17 and studies conducted in healthy Japanese and Chinese participants (Studies D5180C00003 31 and D5180C00020 [unpublished], respectively). Multiple intravenous doses of tezepelumab 700 mg demonstrated an acceptable safety profile in healthy participants and those with mild atopic asthma (Studies 20080390 and 20101183, respectively). 17 , 29 Tezepelumab exhibited linear PK with highly consistent and reliable PK profiles across doses, populations and studies; this provided the basis for dose selection in the clinical development programme for different populations and age groups. 17 , 29 , 31

The adolescent PK study was performed in parallel to the Phase 2b PATHWAY study in adults with severe, uncontrolled asthma, which evaluated three subcutaneous dose levels (70 mg Q4W, 210 mg Q4W and 280 mg every 2 weeks [Q2W]). The evaluated dose was selected to comprehensively characterize PK and result in exposures that would be within the safe and efficacious range in the adult Phase 2b PATHWAY study 21 to properly guide dose selection for the Phase 3 study. 20 The results from the adolescent PK study and PATHWAY were evaluated to select the Phase 3 NAVIGATOR dose. The same dose (210 mg Q4W) was evaluated in adult and adolescent patients with asthma in NAVIGATOR, which demonstrated the efficacy and safety of tezepelumab in these populations. 20 Data collected during the adolescent PK study, together with pooled data from the studies in healthy adults and in patients with asthma (a total of eight studies, Phases 1–3), subsequently contributed to population PK modelling that supported the exposure–efficacy and body weight–response analyses of tezepelumab in patients with asthma. 22 , 23 Notably, when adolescents (12–18 years of age) were given the same tezepelumab dose as adults, they had 33.2%–40.7% higher mean exposures than adults (aged 18–65 years), which is mainly attributed to their lower average body weight (see Figure 3). However, these differences were small compared with the overall variability of exposure in the NAVIGATOR population. The mean exposures across all body weight quartiles and age groups in NAVIGATOR were well below the exposures at the highest dose evaluated in PATHWAY (280 mg Q2W), which demonstrated acceptable safety, and above the exposure at 70 mg Q4W, which demonstrated efficacy in PATHWAY. The 210 mg SC Q4W dose is now the approved dose for adolescents (>12 years old), and the results from the adolescent PK study were important for the characterization of PK in this age group. 18 , 19 , 23

The TRAILHEAD study results were used to guide dose selection for the ongoing, Phase 3 HORIZON study of tezepelumab in children aged 5 to <12 years with severe, uncontrolled asthma. PK simulations scaled for the lower body weight of children identified a single subcutaneous dose of 70 mg of tezepelumab to match the PK exposure of 210 mg in adults. The evaluated dose was selected to comprehensively characterize PK and was predicted to result in exposures within the safe and efficacious range as defined in the adult Phase 2b and Phase 3 studies. 20 , 21 Indeed, the 70‐mg dose of tezepelumab used in this study demonstrated a generally similar PK profile in this age group (mean AUC0–inf: 974 μg*day/mL; mean C max: 27.1 μg/mL) to that of tezepelumab 210 mg in adolescents and adults in the population PK model (mean simulated steady‐state AUC: 901 μg*day/mL; mean simulated steady‐state C max: 40.9 μg/mL) (see Figure 3 and Table 2). 23

CONCLUSIONS

Tezepelumab was well tolerated by adolescents (aged 12–17 years) with mild‐to‐moderate asthma after a single 140‐mg subcutaneous dose in the adolescent PK study and by children (aged 5–11 years) with mild, moderate or severe asthma after a single 70‐mg subcutaneous dose in the TRAILHEAD study. The PK, PD and safety profiles of tezepelumab in children were as expected from previous studies in other age groups and suggest that tezepelumab can be administered to children. Data from these Phase 1 PK studies were used to inform dose selection in Phase 3 studies of tezepelumab by matching concentration range with adults at an efficacious dose and within the tested exposure range of tezepelumab considered safe. Tezepelumab is currently approved in over 60 countries for adolescents (aged 12–17 years) and adults with severe, uncontrolled asthma. These findings support the inclusion of children and adolescents into Phase 3 studies and the further development of tezepelumab for the treatment of asthma in children.
