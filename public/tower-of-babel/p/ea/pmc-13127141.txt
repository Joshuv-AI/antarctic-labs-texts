Telework perceptions in hospital workers throughout the COVID-19 pandemic—the ADAPTAWORK2 study
Authors: Charline Mourgues, Candy Guiguet-Auclair, Claire Doplat, Julien S Baker, Bruno Pereira, Frederic Dutheil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic has changed work organization in hospitals, notably with the expansion of telework. This study aimed to assess perceptions of telework among hospital workers throughout the pandemic and to identify factors associated with telework perceptions in the post-pandemic period. An observational cross-sectional study was conducted from October to December 2023. All hospital workers, regardless of their occupation or status, were invited to participate in an online survey. Perceptions of telework were assessed using visual analog scales, ranging from 0 (very negative) to 100 (very positive), across three periods: before the COVID-19 pandemic, during the first French lockdown, and after the pandemic. A total of 882 hospital workers were included in the analysis. Throughout the pandemic, 41.4% reported adopting telework. Overall, perceptions of telework became significantly more positive over time, rising from a mean score of 54.3 ± 25.3 before the pandemic, to 62.7 ± 27.4 during the first lockdown, and to 66.0 ± 27.5 after the pandemic (P < .001). Experiencing telework for the first time was associated with more favorable perceptions, with high levels sustained among those who continued teleworking post-pandemic. Perceptions of telework improved significantly among hospital workers throughout the pandemic with notable shifts in work practices, with nearly one third of participants teleworking in the post-pandemic period. Experiencing telework was associated with more positive perceptions.

Introduction

The health crisis caused by the COVID-19 pandemic has led to changes in human resource management, including the introduction of new work organization methods such as telework [1]. These changes have partially transformed management methods and the relationship with work, with workers placing greater emphasis on their well-being and quality of life in the workplace [1, 2]. The hospital sector, which was particularly hard hit by this health crisis, has also undergone organizational adaptations. All hospital workers who were not involved in direct care, including healthcare workers, were offered the opportunity to telework, a practice that was very rare before the pandemic [1].

The changes surrounding telework during the pandemic has led to many qualitative studies [3–7]. Those studies highlighted some positive effect of telework, such as flexibility, increased concentration and efficiency, and better work-life balance. Most interviewed workers would like to continue telework if given the opportunity. Very few studies have been carried out into the way people feel about telework in the public hospital sector [8–11]. Those qualitative studies did not quantitatively assess perceptions of telework, nor changes over the pandemic. The quantitative studies assessed, for example, well-being or organizational changes[12–14], but not telework perceptions of workers. Moreover, those studies did not simultaneously assessed outcomes throughout three consecutive periods, i.e. before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic, but focused only on one or two specific periods.

An initial qualitative study in hospital administrative staff have highlighted changes in telework perceptions during the pandemic [15]. Very interestingly, workers reported different perceptions of telework depending whether they previously experienced telework or not. This pilot study also allowed us to retrieve some putative factors that may influence feelings about telework. Some factors were already shown to influence perception of telework such as age [16], gender [17, 18], education [19], or managerial role [20].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess telework perceptions among hospital workers throughout the COVID-19 pandemic and to identify factors associated with telework perceptions after the pandemic.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The ADAPTAWORK2 study was an observational cross-sectional study carried out from October to December 2023. Hospital workers from 19 public hospitals were invited to participate in an online survey, regardless of their occupation or grade within their establishments. All hospital workers received an informational flyer by email, describing the objectives of the study and containing the survey link. Participants were informed that their data would be processed anonymously and used solely for research purposes. A reminder email was sent three weeks before the survey closed. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at University Hospital Centre of Clermont-Ferrand, France [21]. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies. The study received approval from the French South East VI Ethics Committee (n° 2023/CE 11, 27 April 2023) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06316037).

Outcomes

The level of telework perceptions was measured using a visual analog scale (VAS), which is a non-calibrated horizontal line ranging from 0 (very negative perceptions) to 100 (very positive perceptions), providing a quantitative way to measure a subjective feeling [22]. Participants assessed their perceptions by selecting a point on the VAS that best represented their telework perceptions. Perceptions were assessed for three periods: before the COVID-19 pandemic (i.e. before March 17, 2020), during the first French lockdown (i.e. between March 17 and May 10, 2020) and after the pandemic (i.e. in 2023).

The online survey also included sociodemographic (gender, age, marital status, children, educational level) and occupational characteristics (telework or not during each period–at least one day per week, work experience, caregiver or not, managerial role or not, part-time work, worked hours per week, screen work, night work, week-end work, commuting time to work).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as number of participants and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations (SD).

Participants were included in the analysis if they assessed their level of telework perception for at least one of the three periods. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were used to assess the normality telework perception levels. Since the distributions were not normal, the evolution of telework perceptions throughout the COVID-19 pandemic was analysed using the Friedman test. Post-hoc analyses were conducted to compare the levels of telework perceptions across three distinct periods: pre-pandemic versus during the first lockdown, during the first lockdown versus post-pandemic, and pre-pandemic versus post-pandemic using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Adjusted P-values for multiple comparisons were calculated using the Bonferroni correction.

Post-pandemic levels of telework perceptions were compared according to sociodemographic and occupational characteristics using non-parametric Man-Whitney tests. Hedges’ g effect sizes (ES) with 95% confidence intervals (95 CI) were presented and interpreted according to Cohen’s recommendation: absolute value ≥ 0.20 indicated a small ES, ≥ 0.50 a moderate ES, and ≥ 0.80 a large ES [23]. Multivariate fractional regression models with the logit link function were used to test the association of the sociodemographic and occupational characteristics with post-pandemic levels of telework perceptions. Independent variables were included if they were significant in the bivariate analyses at P < .20. Fractional regression was selected because it is appropriate for modeling variables bounded between 0 and 1 (including 0 and 1), such as proportions [24]. As telework perception levels ranged from 0 to 100, they were divided by 100 for inclusion as the dependent variable in the multivariate models. Models minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Consistent Akaike Information Criterion (CAIC), and Hannan–Quinn Information Criterion (HQIC) were retained. Results from the fractional regression models are presented as beta coefficients (β) and their standard errors (SE).

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software package v9.4 (SAS Institute INC., Cary, NC, USA). All tests were two-sided and a P values < .05 was required for statistical significance.

Participants reported teleworking at least one day per week before the COVID-19 pandemic at 8.9%, during the first lockdown at 40.1%, and after the pandemic at 32.7%. Subgroups of telework conditions during the pandemic were defined as follows: never teleworked (52.7%), teleworked only during the first lockdown (14.3%), teleworked only after the pandemic (8.0%), teleworked starting from the first lockdown (19.1%), and teleworked throughout the pandemic (6.0%) (Table S1).

Evolution of telework perceptions

Telework perceptions among all participants became more positive across the three periods (P < .001) (Fig. 1, Table S1). Levels increased from 54.3 ± 25.3 before the COVID-19 pandemic to 62.7 ± 27.4 during the first lockdown (P < .001), and to 66.0 ± 27.5 after the pandemic (P < .001 compared to both pre- pandemic and during the first lockdown).

Evolution of telework perceptions throughout the pandemic among hospital workers and according to telework conditions.

The levels of telework perceptions were measured using visual analog scales, ranging from 0 (very negative perception) to 100 (very positive perception). Significance was set for P-value < .017 (Bonferroni adjusted P-value): *** <.001, ** <.01.

Telework perceptions differed significantly across the pandemic according to the subgroups of telework conditions (Fig. 1, Table S1). Participants who never teleworked had a mean level of 52.2 ± 23.2 pre-pandemic, that increased significantly to 60.4 ± 26.2 during the first lockdown, and decreased significantly to 56.3 ± 26.2 post-pandemic. Hospital workers who only teleworked during the first lockdown had a mean level of 52.1 ± 26.4 pre-pandemic, that increased significantly to 64.6 ± 28.0 during the first lockdown, and remained stable at 61.6 ± 28.1 thereafter. Those who started teleworking after the pandemic had more negative perceptions of telework pre-pandemic and during the first lockdown (51.6 ± 26.0 and 57.7 ± 27.6 respectively), compared to post-pandemic, when perceptions became significantly more positive (82.0 ± 22.5). Participants who started teleworking from the first lockdown had continuously and significantly more positive telework perceptions, with levels from 54.8 ± 26.6, then 69.0 ± 28.3, to reach 86.3 ± 14.8 post-pandemic. Hospital workers who teleworked throughout the pandemic had stable levels of telework perceptions until a significant increase after the pandemic to reach levels close to pre-pandemic (70.8 ± 27.1 pre-pandemic, 62.3 ± 29.7 during the first lockdown, and 73.0 ± 28.4 post- pandemic).

Factors associated with post-pandemic telework perceptions

Post-pandemic telework perceptions were compared based on sociodemographic and occupational characteristics Since the experience of telework was associated with more positive perceptions, these analyses were stratified by post-pandemic teleworking status.

Participants were 32.7% to report teleworking after the pandemic. These participants were more likely to be women, in relationships, hold a Master’s degree or higher, have less work experience, and longer commuting time to work. Caregivers, participants working nights or week-ends, and those alternating screen work with other activities were less likely to telework (Table 1). Telework perceptions were significantly more positive in the sub-group of participants reporting post-pandemic telework (82.5 ± 20.4 vs. 57.9 ± 26.8, P < .001).

Post-pandemic telework perceptions were not significantly associated with gender, marital status, having children, screen work, night work, or commuting time to work, regardless of post-pandemic teleworking status (Table 2).

Among participants who teleworked, post-pandemic perceptions of telework were significantly associated with educational level, caregiver status, managerial role, weekly worked hours, and weekend work. More negative perceptions of telework were reported by those with an educational level below a Master’s degree (ES = −0.42, 95 CI −0.79 to −0.05 compared to Bachelor’s degree or lower), caregivers (−0.59, −0.88 to −0.29), individuals in managerial positions (−0.44, −0.74 to −0.15), those working more than 41 hours per week (−0.44, −0.87 to −0.01 compared to fewer than 30 hours), and those working on weekends (−0.81, −1.22 to −0.39) (Fig. 2). In the multivariate fractional regression models, being a caregiver (β = −0.45, SE = 0.23, P = .044), working more than 40 hours per week compared with fewer than 30 hours (β = −0.91, SE = 0.42, P = .032), and working on weekends (β = −0.69, SE = 0.32, P = .029), were associated with more negative perceptions of telework (Table S2).

Participants characteristics.

Among participants who did not telework, post-pandemic perceptions of telework were significantly associated with age, educational level, work experience, and part-time work (Table 2). More negative perceptions of telework were reported by with individuals aged 41 years and older (−0.38, −0.67 to −0.09 for ages 41–50; −0.63, −0.93 to −0.33 for ages 51 and above, compared to those aged 18–30), and by those with more than 16 years of work experience (−0.40, −0.66 to −0.13 for 16–25 years; −0.55, −0.83 to −0.26 for more than 25 years, vs. compared to those with less than 3 years of experience). In contrast, more positive perceptions were associated with having an educational level higher than a Bachelor’s degree (0.33, 0.10–0.56 for less than a Master’s degree; 0.42, 0.16–0.67 for a Master’s degree or higher), and with part-time work (0.32, 0.10–0.54) (Fig. 2). In the multivariate fractional regression models, older age was associated with more negative perceptions of telework, with stronger associations observed for participants aged 41–50 years (β = −0.46, SE = 0.18, P = .009) and 51 years or older (β = −0.70, SE = 0.18, P < .001), compared with those aged 18–30 years. In contrast, higher educational level was associated with more positive perceptions of telework, both for individuals with less than a Master’s degree (β = 0.43, SE = 0.14, P = .003) and for those with a Master’s degree or higher (β = 0.45, SE = 0.17, P = .006), compared with those with a Bachelor’s degree or lower (Table S2).

Discussion

The levels of telework perceptions were measured on visual analog scales providing a continuous spectrum (from 0 to 100), which allows for a detailed range of responses rather than just categorical answers (e.g. negative/neutral/positive). By using the same scale across different periods (pre- pandemic, during the first lockdown, and post-pandemic), participants’ perceptions were measured consistently and changes in their perceptions over time were assessed. The main findings revealed more positive perceptions of telework among hospital worker throughout the pandemic, accompanied by a significant change in work habits towards telework. Experiencing telework was associated with more positive perceptions, which remained highly positive among those who continued to telework after the pandemic.

Greater positive telework perceptions during the pandemic

This is the first study showing and assessing more positive perceptions of telework among hospital workers throughout the pandemic, while other studies focused on a dedicated time of the pandemic or on mental health [13]. In the public sector, and more particularly in hospitals, the studies carried out during the pandemic showed a significant psychological impact on professionals who were very involved in the crisis [25–27]. The pandemic had a major impact on anxiety and depression disorders of healthcare workers who teleworked [8]. However, telework perceptions were never assessed simultaneously, and future studies should assess both mental health and telework perceptions, especially considering benefits and disadvantages of telework. Many studies have shown the clear benefits of telework on personal and family life, health, environment and economy [26, 28, 29]. Working from home reduces the number of interruptions to tasks that can lead to significant stress, allowing workers to concentrate more effectively and perform better. Telework also promotes flexibility, autonomy and motivation. Telework not only reduces perceived work-related stress, but also provides a resource for dealing with it [26]. Benefits of telework can also be organisational and economic, with facilitating service delivery, increasing satisfaction, supporting healthcare providers, and reducing costs [28]. Commuting time is reduced, but caution must be paid to increased working time–and thus screen time–and prolonged sedentary behaviour (27), with detrimental health consequences [30].

Experiencing telework for the first time increased positive telework perceptions

In our study, teleworking conditions and telework perceptions were assessed before, during, and after the pandemic. Almost half hospital workers experienced telework during the first lockdown or after the pandemic, and more than a quarter continued to telework after the pandemic. Despite the lack of facilities and support during the pandemic [30], experiencing telework led to more positive telework perceptions, which remained highly positive among those who continued to telework after the pandemic. This original result seems natural, however it was never assessed for telework. Barriers to the use of new technology often exist at the workplace [28]. By analogy, use of smarthphone in older individuls increased its acceptance [31], with also a positive effect on mental health [32]. In our study, hospital workers who already teleworked before the pandemic were not satisfied of telework during the first lockdown. As reported in some studies, this may be explained by social and professional isolation i.e. reduced interactions between hospital workers [28, 33]. In addition, mothers had to balance telework against their family lives (e.g. house chores, childcare), which reinforced their roles as traditional housewives, and led to higher levels of psychological distress during the pandemic [17, 34].

Factors associated with post-pandemic telework perceptions

After the pandemic, as expected, telework perceptions were significantly more positive among hospital workers who were teleworking. In our study, perceptions of telework did not differ between men and women, regardless of teleworking status. However, previous research has indicated that telework enabled women to better plan their work and family time, contributing to an improved work-life balance [35]. This, in turn, was expected to lead to more positive perceptions of telework.

Among participants who did not telework post- pandemic, more positive perceptions of telework were reported by younger hospital workers and those with a Masters’ degree or higher. Younger individuals have been stereotypically described as with less barriers to information technology use [36]. However, older individuals are capable of using new technologies with an adapted technology support [31].

Among post-pandemic telecommuters, less positive perceptions were observed among caregivers, individuals working more than 41 hours per week or on weekends. Even if telework is increasingly used in hospitals, not all healthcare professionals use health information technologies. Organizational changes linked with telework should be further studied to be successfully implemented for caregivers, when possible [37]. The success of remote working is also dependent on managers’ understanding of the virtual supervision of employees. Some authors argued that managers might not accept remote working because they might consider that it affects employees’ performance negatively, which can lead to the adoption of micromanagement that can be perceived by employees as a lack of trust toward them, which can create tension between them and their supervisor [36]. In this context, the HRM practitioners should play a strategic role by supporting and training these managers on how to manage telecommuters, to help them to overcome these difficulties and to cope with remote working challenges in order to be able to support their team members [1] Indeed, support from managers may be a key factor to implement telework [38].

Implications for hospital telework policy and practice

In light of the study’s findings, particularly the less positive telework perceptions observed among caregivers, managers, and employees with long or weekend working hours, hospitals may benefit from adopting more targeted telework policies [39]. In our institution, telework management and oversight have been strengthened through the human resources department by providing dedicated remote work tools (e.g. secure extranet access, collaborative platform such as Microsoft Teams) and by limiting telework to a maximum of two days per week. These measures aim to preserve continuity of hierarchical communication, support staff autonomy, and contribute to improve the quality of work life of hospital workers [38].

More broadly, the results suggest that telework policies in hospital settings should be adapted to occupational roles and working conditions [8]. Hybrid work arrangements, workload adjustments for staff with demanding schedules, and specific managerial training in remote supervision may help address the less positive perceptions identified among certain professional groups [38].

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. Firstly, asking respondents to recall their perceptions during different periods may not yield reliable results. However, our aim was to assess how perceptions of telework evolved across the three periods (pre-pandemic, during the first lockdown, post-pandemic). Although each individual assessment carried a certain degree of uncertainty, what mattered were the differences between the three periods. Moreover, a cross-sectional prevented attrition bias due to lost to follow-up. Secondly, hospital workers who experienced telework might have been more prone to participate to the online survey, inducing a selection bias, but this putative bias is common to any study based on voluntary participation. Thirdly, the study was carried out at a regional level, with telework conditions that could be different in other areas. The French specificities of the practice of telework in healthcare centers could prevent a generalization of results in other countries. Nevertheless, this is the first French nationwide study on feelings about telework. Fourthly, the generalizability of our results may also be precluded by the high proportion of female respondents, which is however current in healthcare establishments. Fifthly, we could not compare the characteristics of the respondents and non-respondents, since data were not available for the non-respondents. Finally, the lack of reliable information on telework intensity, in particular the number of teleworking days per week, was another limitation. Although telework intensity may plausibly influence perceptions—occasional telework potentially being experienced differently from more regular or intensive arrangements—this variable was affected by a high proportion of missing data in our sample (over 25%). As a result, it could not be meaningfully incorporated into the analyses. This unmeasured heterogeneity may partially explain some of the observed associations and should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

Perceptions of telework became more positive throughout the COVID-19 among hospital workers, accompanied by a significant change in work habits towards telework. Experiencing telework was associated with more positive perceptions. Integrating flexible telework options could enhance workforce well-being and productivity, thereby informing future public health workforce policies.
