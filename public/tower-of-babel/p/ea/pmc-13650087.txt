Brucellosis in the One Health Context: Gaps in Diagnosis, Control, and Global Epidemiology

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrucellosis is a neglected zoonotic infection with persistent transmission in many endemic regions despite long-standing control efforts. Introduction

Brucellosis is a globally distributed zoonotic disease caused by Gram-negative intracellular bacteria of the genus Brucella. The disease affects a wide range of domestic and wild animals and continues to pose significant public health and economic concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where livestock production constitutes an essential component of food security and rural livelihoods [1, 2]. Human infection is primarily acquired through direct contact with infected animals or consumption of contaminated unpasteurized dairy products, while occupational exposure remains common among veterinarians, farmers, abattoir workers, and laboratory personnel [3, 4].

Despite longstanding recognition of brucellosis as a significant zoonosis, the disease continues to be neglected in many endemic regions. The global burden of brucellosis remains difficult to estimate accurately due to underreporting, inadequate surveillance systems, and the nonspecific clinical manifestations of infection [5, 6]. In humans, brucellosis may present with fever, fatigue, arthralgia, and constitutional symptoms that frequently resemble other infectious diseases, contributing to delayed diagnosis and prolonged morbidity [7]. In livestock, the disease is associated with abortion, infertility, reduced milk production, and substantial economic losses affecting both local and international trade [8].

The epidemiology of brucellosis demonstrates marked regional variation influenced by differences in animal husbandry practices, public health infrastructure, veterinary control programs, and socioeconomic conditions. Although several high-income countries have successfully reduced disease incidence through coordinated vaccination campaigns, pasteurization policies, and surveillance systems, brucellosis remains endemic in large parts of the Middle East, Africa, Asia, and Latin America [9, 10]. Increasing livestock movement, urbanization, and gaps in intersectoral collaboration continue to complicate disease control efforts in many regions.

Accurate diagnosis of brucellosis remains particularly challenging. Conventional diagnostic approaches, including serological assays and bacterial culture, are affected by important limitations such as cross-reactivity, variable sensitivity, prolonged turnaround times, and biosafety concerns [11]. Molecular methods have improved diagnostic capabilities in some settings; however, their accessibility remains limited in many endemic countries due to cost and infrastructure requirements [12]. These diagnostic challenges contribute substantially to underdiagnosis, delayed treatment, and ongoing transmission.

Given the close relationship between animal reservoirs, human infection, and environmental factors, brucellosis represents a critical One Health issue requiring coordinated collaboration between medical, veterinary, and public health sectors. However, implementation of integrated One Health strategies remains inconsistent across many endemic settings, limiting the effectiveness of prevention and control programs [13].

This review critically examines gaps in diagnosis, epidemiology, and control of brucellosis, with particular emphasis on limitations affecting disease detection, surveillance, and implementation of One Health strategies.

Methods

This study was conducted as a structured narrative review to synthesize current evidence on brucellosis with a focus on diagnostic limitations, epidemiological trends, and control challenges within a One Health context.

A literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Keywords included combinations of “brucellosis,” “diagnosis,” “epidemiology,” “transmission,” “treatment,” “control,” and “One Health.” The search focused on peer-reviewed articles published in English, with priority given to studies addressing human and animal disease, diagnostic performance, surveillance systems, and public health implications.

Relevant original studies, systematic reviews, and authoritative reports were screened based on title and abstract, followed by full-text review where appropriate. Articles were included if they provided data or analysis related to clinical presentation, diagnostic methods, regional epidemiology, or control strategies. Studies with limited relevance, duplicate data, or insufficient methodological clarity were excluded.

Data were extracted and synthesized qualitatively rather than quantitatively. Emphasis was placed on identifying recurring themes, methodological limitations, regional disparities, and gaps in current knowledge. Given the narrative design, no formal meta-analysis or statistical pooling of results was performed.

This approach was selected to provide a comprehensive and critical overview of brucellosis while highlighting key areas requiring further research and improved public health intervention.

Microbiology and Taxonomy of Brucella

Species of the genus Brucella are small, nonmotile, non–spore-forming, facultative intracellular Gram-negative coccobacilli that primarily infect mammals [14]. The genus belongs to the family Brucellaceae and is characterized by a marked ability to survive and replicate within host phagocytic cells, a feature that contributes substantially to chronic infection and immune evasion [15]. Due to their intracellular persistence and low infectious dose, Brucella species remain among the most important bacterial zoonotic pathogens worldwide.

Several Brucella species are associated with human disease, most notably B. melitensis, B. abortus, B. suis, and, less frequently, B. canis [16]. Among these, B. melitensis is considered the most virulent species in humans and is responsible for the majority of severe infections globally, particularly in regions where sheep and goat farming are prevalent [17]. B. abortus is predominantly associated with cattle, while B. suis commonly infects swine and has been linked to sporadic human outbreaks in certain endemic regions [18].

In addition to the major zoonotic species, several less common Brucella species have been identified, including B. neotomae, B. pinnipedialis, B. ceti, and B. inopinata. Although these species are less frequently associated with human disease, their identification highlights the expanding ecological diversity of the genus and the importance of continued surveillance across wildlife and marine reservoirs [19]. The emergence of atypical and newly recognized Brucella species also raises concerns regarding evolving transmission dynamics and diagnostic challenges.

The pathogenicity of Brucella is closely linked to its capacity for intracellular survival within macrophages and other host cells. Following phagocytosis, the organism can evade lysosomal destruction through formation of a specialized Brucella-containing vacuole, enabling replication within the endoplasmic reticulum-derived intracellular niche [20]. This intracellular persistence contributes not only to chronic infection and relapse, but also complicates microbiological diagnosis and treatment.

Although advances in molecular taxonomy and genomic sequencing have improved understanding of Brucella diversity and evolution, significant gaps remain regarding host adaptation, virulence variation, and the epidemiological role of wildlife reservoirs. Improved characterization of circulating Brucella species remains important for strengthening surveillance and guiding both veterinary and public health control strategies.

Modes of Transmission

Brucellosis is primarily transmitted from infected animals to humans through direct or indirect exposure to contaminated animal products and secretions. Domestic livestock, particularly goats, sheep, cattle, swine, and camels, represent the principal reservoirs of human infection in endemic regions [21]. Human-to-human transmission is extremely rare, with most infections occurring through occupational exposure or foodborne routes. Major pathways of brucellosis transmission

Consumption of unpasteurized milk and dairy products remains one of the most common mechanisms of transmission worldwide, especially in regions where traditional dairy practices are common and food safety regulations are inconsistently implemented [22]. Fresh cheeses, raw milk, and other unpasteurized dairy products have been repeatedly implicated in outbreaks and sporadic cases of human brucellosis [23].

Direct contact with infected animals or contaminated tissues also represents a major route of transmission, particularly among veterinarians, farmers, abattoir workers, and laboratory personnel [24]. The organism may enter the body through skin abrasions, mucosal surfaces, or inhalation of infectious aerosols generated during animal handling, slaughtering, or laboratory manipulation [25]. Laboratory-associated brucellosis remains one of the most frequently reported laboratory-acquired bacterial infections due to the low infectious dose and aerosol transmissibility of Brucella species [26].

Although less common, transmission through organ transplantation, blood transfusion, sexual contact, and vertical transmission from mother to child has been documented in isolated cases [27]. However, these routes contribute minimally to the overall global burden of disease.

Persistent transmission in endemic regions is strongly influenced by socioeconomic and cultural practices, including informal livestock trade, limited veterinary infrastructure, unrestricted animal movement, and continued consumption of unpasteurized dairy products [28]. In many low-resource settings, insufficient public awareness, delayed diagnosis in livestock, and inadequate occupational protection further contribute to sustained transmission among high-risk populations.

The epidemiology of transmission varies substantially across regions depending on livestock management systems, veterinary surveillance capacity, public health infrastructure, and food safety measures. Consequently, effective prevention requires coordinated One Health strategies integrating veterinary control programs, food safety interventions, occupational protection, and public health education [29].

Pathogenesis of Brucellosis

The pathogenesis of brucellosis is closely related to the ability of Brucella species to survive and replicate within host cells, particularly macrophages and dendritic cells [30]. Following entry through mucosal surfaces, ingestion, inhalation, or direct skin contact, the bacteria are rapidly phagocytosed and disseminated through the lymphatic and reticuloendothelial systems [31]. The intracellular nature of Brucella represents a key virulence characteristic that enables persistent infection and contributes to chronic disease.

Unlike many extracellular bacterial pathogens, Brucella species possess mechanisms that allow evasion of host immune responses and intracellular killing. After internalization, the organism can inhibit phagosome-lysosome fusion and establish a specialized intracellular compartment known as the Brucella-containing vacuole [32]. This environment facilitates bacterial replication within an endoplasmic reticulum-derived niche while limiting immune recognition and destruction.

Several virulence factors contribute to the pathogenicity of Brucella, including lipopolysaccharides with relatively low endotoxic activity, type IV secretion systems, and the ability to modulate host inflammatory responses [33]. These mechanisms allow the pathogen to maintain prolonged intracellular persistence while inducing comparatively subtle inflammatory reactions, which may partially explain the nonspecific clinical presentation commonly observed in human brucellosis.

The intracellular persistence of Brucella contributes substantially to chronic infection, diagnostic difficulty, relapse, and prolonged treatment requirements. In many patients, delayed diagnosis and incomplete eradication of intracellular organisms may result in recurrent or focal disease involving the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, or nervous systems [34].

Host immune responses against Brucella rely predominantly on cell-mediated immunity, particularly activation of macrophages and T lymphocytes [35]. However, variability in host immune responses and bacterial virulence may influence disease severity and clinical outcomes. Despite advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of Brucella pathogenesis, important gaps remain regarding factors influencing chronicity, host susceptibility, and persistence in animal reservoirs.

Improved understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying intracellular survival and immune evasion may support future development of more effective diagnostics, vaccines, and targeted therapeutic approaches.

Clinical Manifestations of Brucellosis

Human brucellosis is characterized by a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from asymptomatic infection to severe multisystem disease [36]. The disease often presents with nonspecific symptoms, including fever, malaise, fatigue, night sweats, anorexia, arthralgia, myalgia, and weight loss, which may resemble several other infectious and inflammatory conditions [37]. This nonspecific presentation frequently contributes to delayed diagnosis and underrecognition, particularly in endemic regions where healthcare access and laboratory resources may be limited.

The clinical course of brucellosis may be classified as acute, subacute, or chronic depending on symptom duration and disease progression [38]. Acute disease commonly manifests as an undifferentiated febrile illness, whereas chronic infection may involve persistent constitutional symptoms and focal organ involvement. Relapsing fever patterns are frequently reported and may reflect incomplete eradication of intracellular bacteria [39].

Musculoskeletal involvement represents one of the most common complications of human brucellosis and may include arthritis, sacroiliitis, spondylitis, and osteomyelitis [40]. Osteoarticular disease is particularly associated with prolonged morbidity and functional impairment. Neurological complications, although less common, include meningitis, encephalitis, radiculopathy, and peripheral neuropathy, collectively referred to as neurobrucellosis [41]. Cardiovascular involvement is rare but remains clinically significant because endocarditis represents the leading cause of brucellosis-related mortality [42].

Additional manifestations may involve the hepatobiliary, genitourinary, respiratory, and hematological systems [43]. In endemic settings, the wide variability in clinical presentation may complicate differentiation from other febrile illnesses such as tuberculosis, malaria, typhoid fever, and viral infections. Consequently, clinical suspicion remains essential for timely diagnosis, particularly among individuals with relevant occupational or dietary exposure histories.

Clinical Impact on Animals

In animals, brucellosis primarily affects the reproductive system and is associated with abortion, infertility, stillbirth, orchitis, reduced milk production, and decreased fertility [44]. These manifestations contribute to substantial economic losses through reduced herd productivity, trade restrictions, and costs associated with disease control programs [45]. Infected livestock also represents persistent reservoirs for human infection, reinforcing the importance of integrated veterinary and public health interventions within the One Health framework.

The clinical manifestations of brucellosis in both humans and animals highlight the complexity of disease recognition and surveillance. Nonspecific presentations, overlapping symptoms with other infectious diseases, and variability in disease severity continue to represent major obstacles to early diagnosis and effective disease control. Global and Regional Epidemiology of Brucellosis

Brucellosis is among the most widespread zoonotic diseases globally and continues to impose a substantial public health and socioeconomic burden in endemic regions [46]. The disease persists across large areas of the Middle East, Africa, Asia, and Latin America, where close human–animal interaction, limited veterinary infrastructure, and gaps in surveillance systems facilitate ongoing transmission [47]. However, the true global incidence is likely underestimated due to underreporting and diagnostic limitations, with recent estimates suggesting substantially higher annual global incidence than previously assumed [46, 48].

The epidemiology of brucellosis varies considerably across regions, reflecting differences in livestock management, food safety practices, and public health capacity [49]. Countries with established vaccination programs, pasteurization policies, and integrated surveillance systems have achieved substantial reductions in incidence, whereas endemic regions continue to experience persistent transmission.

Regional Overview

In the Middle East, including Saudi Arabia, brucellosis remains endemic and is strongly associated with consumption of unpasteurized dairy products, occupational exposure, and persistent infrastructural and One Health implementation barriers [50–52]. In Africa, underdiagnosis is common due to limited laboratory infrastructure and overlap with other febrile illnesses such as malaria and tuberculosis [53, 54].

In Asia, particularly in China and Central Asia, expanding livestock production, increased animal trade, and spatiotemporal clustering continue to drive transmission [55–58]. In Europe, the disease has been largely controlled in Western countries but persists in parts of the Mediterranean and Eastern Europe [59]. In Latin America, brucellosis remains endemic in several countries, particularly in rural communities where occupational exposure and traditional food practices contribute to transmission [60].

Overall, regional variability in reported incidence likely reflects differences in surveillance capacity, healthcare access, reporting practices, and evolving regional transmission patterns rather than true epidemiological differences alone [48, 57, 61]. Persistent underdiagnosis and surveillance gaps highlight the need for strengthened One Health strategies integrating veterinary surveillance, food safety measures, and improved access to diagnostic tools.

Diagnosis of Brucellosis

Accurate diagnosis of brucellosis remains one of the greatest challenges in disease management and control, particularly in endemic and resource-limited settings. The nonspecific clinical manifestations of brucellosis, combined with variable disease presentation and limitations of currently available diagnostic tools, frequently contribute to delayed recognition and underdiagnosis [62]. Early diagnosis is essential to reduce complications, prevent chronic disease, and limit ongoing transmission; however, achieving timely and reliable diagnosis remains difficult in many healthcare settings.

The diagnosis of brucellosis relies on a combination of clinical suspicion, epidemiological exposure history, laboratory findings, and microbiological testing [63]. A detailed occupational, dietary, and travel history remains particularly important, especially in patients presenting with prolonged fever, constitutional symptoms, or osteoarticular manifestations in endemic regions.

Bacterial Culture

Isolation of Brucella species through bacterial culture remains the definitive diagnostic method and allows species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility assessment [64]. Blood cultures are most commonly used, although bone marrow cultures may demonstrate higher sensitivity, particularly in chronic or previously treated cases [65]. Despite its diagnostic value, culture-based diagnosis has important limitations.

The slow growth characteristics of Brucella may result in prolonged incubation periods and delayed diagnosis, while prior antibiotic exposure can further reduce culture sensitivity [66]. In addition, laboratory isolation of Brucella requires biosafety precautions because of the organism’s high infectivity and potential for aerosol transmission, making laboratory-acquired brucellosis a recognized occupational hazard [67]. Consequently, culture facilities may be unavailable or underutilized in many endemic low-resource settings.

Serological Testing

Serological assays remain the most widely used diagnostic tools for brucellosis due to their relative simplicity, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness [68]. Commonly utilized tests include the Rose Bengal test (RBT), standard agglutination test (SAT), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and Coombs anti-Brucella test [69]. These methods are particularly valuable in endemic regions where advanced molecular diagnostics may not be readily available.

Despite their widespread use, serological tests possess important diagnostic limitations. Cross-reactivity with other Gram-negative bacteria, including Yersinia enterocolitica, Escherichia coli O157, Salmonella species, and Francisella tularensis, may reduce test specificity and contribute to false-positive results [70]. In endemic regions, previous exposure and persistent background seropositivity may further complicate interpretation of serological findings.

Variability in sensitivity and specificity between serological assays also represents a major challenge, particularly in chronic, focal, or relapsing brucellosis where antibody titers may fluctuate or remain persistently elevated despite treatment [71]. Additionally, differences in laboratory standardization and interpretation criteria across regions contribute to inconsistent diagnostic performance and complicate epidemiological comparisons.

Molecular Diagnostics

Molecular techniques, particularly polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays, have improved the rapid detection of Brucella DNA and may enhance diagnostic sensitivity in selected clinical settings [72]. PCR methods can provide earlier diagnosis compared with culture and may be especially useful in cases with prior antibiotic exposure or localized infection [73]. Real-time PCR assays have additionally demonstrated value in species identification and epidemiological surveillance.

However, important limitations continue to restrict widespread implementation of molecular diagnostics. Variability in assay protocols, lack of universal standardization, limited validation across endemic settings, and the requirement for specialized laboratory infrastructure may affect reliability and accessibility [74]. In many low- and middle-income countries, financial constraints and insufficient laboratory capacity continue to limit routine use of molecular testing.

Diagnostic Challenges in Endemic Regions

In endemic regions, diagnosis of brucellosis is frequently complicated by overlapping with other febrile illnesses, limited healthcare access, and inadequate laboratory infrastructure [75]. Delayed diagnosis remains common because symptoms may mimic tuberculosis, malaria, typhoid fever, rheumatologic disorders, and viral infections. In addition, low clinical awareness among healthcare providers in non-specialized settings may contribute to missed or delayed diagnoses.

Reliance on serological testing in resource-limited settings often occurs despite known limitations in specificity and interpretation. Limited access to confirmatory culture and molecular diagnostics therefore contributes to inconsistent surveillance data and underestimation of disease burden [76]. Furthermore, the absence of universally standardized diagnostic algorithms remains a major barrier to effective surveillance, case definition, and disease monitoring across endemic regions.

Future Directions in Brucellosis Diagnosis

Improving brucellosis diagnosis remains a critical priority for global disease control efforts. Future research should focus on development of rapid, affordable, highly sensitive, and standardized point-of-care diagnostic tools suitable for endemic low-resource settings [77]. Strengthening laboratory capacity, improving clinician awareness, and promoting integration between human and veterinary surveillance systems are also essential components of effective diagnostic and surveillance strategies within the One Health framework.

Overall, persistent diagnostic limitations continue to hinder accurate case detection, epidemiological surveillance, and timely treatment of brucellosis worldwide. Addressing these challenges will be essential to reducing the long-term public health and economic burden of this neglected zoonotic disease.

Treatment of Brucellosis

Effective treatment of brucellosis requires prolonged combination antibiotic therapy because of the intracellular persistence of Brucella species and the associated risk of relapse [78]. The primary goals of treatment are eradication of infection, prevention of complications, reduction of relapse rates, and minimization of chronic disease manifestations. However, treatment of brucellosis remains challenging due to delayed diagnosis, variable disease presentation, prolonged therapeutic regimens, and inconsistent access to healthcare resources in endemic regions.

Current treatment recommendations generally involve combination therapy using doxycycline in conjunction with rifampicin or streptomycin [79]. The World Health Organization commonly recommends doxycycline plus rifampicin for at least six weeks in uncomplicated cases, while alternative regimens incorporating aminoglycosides may be used in severe or focal disease [80]. Combination therapy is preferred because monotherapy has been associated with significantly higher relapse rates and treatment failure.

The selection of treatment regimens may vary according to disease severity, focal organ involvement, patient age, pregnancy status, and regional antimicrobial availability [81]. Complicated forms of brucellosis, including neurobrucellosis, endocarditis, and osteoarticular disease, often require prolonged multidrug therapy and close clinical follow-up [82]. Endocarditis remains the most severe complication and may require combined medical and surgical management.

Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy, several important limitations continue to affect treatment outcomes. Relapse remains a major clinical challenge and may occur due to delayed diagnosis, inadequate treatment duration, poor adherence, intracellular bacterial persistence, or focal infection sites with limited antibiotic penetration [83]. Reported relapse rates vary substantially across studies and may be influenced by differences in treatment protocols and follow-up duration.

Treatment adherence may be particularly difficult in resource-limited settings because recommended regimens require prolonged therapy and may be associated with adverse drug effects [84]. In addition, limited access to healthcare services and inconsistent patient follow-up may contribute to incomplete treatment and recurrent disease. These challenges are especially important in endemic rural communities where healthcare infrastructure may be insufficient.

Although antimicrobial resistance in Brucella species remains relatively uncommon compared with other bacterial pathogens, concerns regarding emerging resistance patterns and inappropriate antibiotic use continue to warrant ongoing surveillance [85]. Furthermore, much of the current treatment evidence is derived from relatively small clinical studies, and large randomized controlled trials evaluating optimal therapeutic strategies remain limited.

Given these challenges, improving treatment outcomes requires not only effective antimicrobial therapy but also earlier diagnosis, standardized treatment protocols, improved patient follow-up, and strengthened healthcare access in endemic regions. Continued research into novel therapeutic approaches and optimization of existing treatment regimens remains important for reducing relapse, chronic disease, and long-term complications associated with brucellosis. Prevention and Control of Brucellosis

Effective prevention and control of brucellosis require integrated strategies targeting both animal reservoirs and human exposure pathways. Because transmission is closely linked to infected livestock and contaminated animal products, successful disease control depends heavily on coordinated veterinary, public health, food safety, and environmental interventions within a One Health framework [86]. Despite substantial progress in some regions, brucellosis continues to persist in many endemic countries due to ongoing challenges affecting surveillance, livestock management, and implementation of control measures.

Vaccination of livestock remains one of the most effective approaches for reducing transmission and controlling disease spread among animal populations [87]. Commonly used vaccines include Brucella abortus strain S19 and RB51 for cattle, and Brucella melitensis Rev.1 vaccine for sheep and goats [88]. Sustained vaccination programs have contributed significantly to reductions in human and animal brucellosis incidence in several countries.

However, implementation of livestock vaccination programs remains inconsistent across endemic regions. Inadequate veterinary infrastructure, insufficient financial resources, limited vaccine coverage, unrestricted animal movement, and poor compliance with animal identification and surveillance systems continue to compromise control efforts [89]. In many low-resource settings, informal livestock trade and cross-border animal movement further complicate disease containment and facilitate persistent transmission.

Food safety measures also represent a critical component of brucellosis prevention. Pasteurization of milk and dairy products remains highly effective in reducing foodborne transmission [90]. Public health education campaigns promoting safe food consumption practices, particularly avoidance of unpasteurized dairy products, are essential in endemic communities where traditional consumption practices remain common.

Occupational protection is particularly important for high-risk groups including veterinarians, farmers, abattoir workers, and laboratory personnel [91]. Recommended preventive measures include use of personal protective equipment, safe handling of animal tissues and biological samples, implementation of biosafety protocols, and increased awareness regarding routes of transmission and early disease recognition.

Despite the availability of veterinary vaccines, no licensed human vaccine currently exists for brucellosis [92]. This limitation continues to represent a major obstacle for disease prevention among high-risk occupational populations and in highly endemic regions. In addition, concerns regarding vaccine safety, efficacy, and long-term protection remain important challenges affecting future vaccine development efforts.

Surveillance systems play a central role in identifying outbreaks, monitoring disease trends, and evaluating effectiveness of control programs [93]. Nevertheless, many endemic countries continue to face substantial surveillance limitations due to inadequate laboratory capacity, underreporting, fragmented data systems, and insufficient integration between human and animal health sectors. Weak intersectoral collaboration may reduce the effectiveness of outbreak detection and coordinated response strategies.

Socioeconomic factors further complicate brucellosis control efforts. In many rural communities, livestock represent a major source of income and food security, making culling of infected animals financially difficult and potentially reducing compliance with eradication programs [94]. Cultural practices, limited public awareness, and restricted healthcare access may additionally contribute to sustained transmission and delayed disease recognition.

Given the complex epidemiology of brucellosis, long-term control requires sustained political commitment, strengthened veterinary and public health infrastructure, improved surveillance systems, and enhanced collaboration between human and animal health sectors. Expansion of One Health-based approaches integrating surveillance, vaccination, food safety interventions, and community education remains essential for reducing the global burden of brucellosis.

Knowledge gaps and Future Directions

Several important gaps continue to hinder progress in the global control of brucellosis. First, the true burden of disease remains uncertain due to underreporting, inconsistent surveillance systems, and limited diagnostic capacity in endemic regions [46, 61]. Standardized reporting frameworks and integrated surveillance programs are urgently needed.

Second, currently available diagnostic methods remain imperfect. Serological assays are affected by cross-reactivity and variable performance, while molecular techniques are often inaccessible in resource-limited settings [70, 74]. Development of rapid, affordable, and highly specific point-of-care diagnostics should therefore represent a major research priority.

Third, important gaps persist in understanding the long-term epidemiological impact of livestock vaccination programs and wildlife reservoirs on disease persistence and re-emergence [44, 87]. More longitudinal and region-specific studies are required to better characterize transmission dynamics.

Finally, implementation of One Health approaches remains inconsistent across endemic countries. Strengthening collaboration between veterinary, public health, and environmental sectors will be essential for sustainable brucellosis control [47, 86].

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although a structured literature search was conducted using major databases, the review is not a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. Therefore, the selection of studies may be subject to selection bias, and some relevant publications may not have been included.

Second, variability in study design, diagnostic criteria, and reporting practices across different regions may limit direct comparison of epidemiological data. Differences in surveillance systems, laboratory capacity, and case definitions further contribute to heterogeneity in reported incidence and disease burden.

Third, much of the available literature on brucellosis originates from specific endemic regions, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other settings. In addition, underreporting and diagnostic challenges in low-resource areas likely result in underestimation of the true global burden of disease.

Finally, the rapidly evolving nature of diagnostic technologies and control strategies means that some emerging developments may not be fully captured within the scope of this review.

Despite these limitations, this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current knowledge and highlights key gaps in diagnosis, epidemiology, and control of brucellosis within a One Health framework.

Conclusion

Brucellosis remains an important yet neglected zoonotic disease with persistent challenges affecting diagnosis, surveillance, and control worldwide. Despite advances in understanding the pathogen and its epidemiology, important limitations continue to hinder effective disease management, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Diagnostic constraints, underreporting, and limited integration between human and veterinary health systems contribute to ongoing transmission. Addressing these challenges requires strengthened surveillance, improved access to reliable diagnostic tools, and sustained implementation of One Health strategies.

Future efforts should prioritize standardized diagnostics, evaluation of long-term control interventions, and development of effective preventive strategies to reduce the global burden of brucellosis
