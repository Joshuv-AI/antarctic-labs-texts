Resolving the mechanism of inclusion-controlled corrosion in advanced high-strength steels with scanning electrochemical probe microscopy
Authors: Deepthi Mariam Geevarghese, Soroosh Hakimian, Emmanuel Mena-Morcillo, Elliott Asare, Li Sun, Cecile De Medeiros, Samantha Michelle Gateman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced high-strength steels (AHSS) enable lightweight automotive design through reduced steel thickness, increasing the need for corrosion-resistant materials. Due to their high alloying element content, localized corrosion in AHSS is often driven by microstructural heterogeneities such as nonmetallic inclusions. In this work, the initiation and propagation of inclusion-induced localized corrosion in AHSS were investigated using a complementary combination of scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM) and scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM). Short-term immersion experiments revealed that corrosion initiates preferentially at spinel-type (Al–Mg–O) inclusions and their interfaces with the steel matrix, where micro-crevice formation precedes localized dissolution and eventual inclusion detachment. SECM feedback-mode mapping demonstrated reduced mediator regeneration currents over inclusions relative to the surrounding matrix, consistent with their electrically insulating nature. In contrast, SECCM provided direct, spatially resolved electrochemical measurements under confined electrolyte conditions, revealing enhanced local anodic activity at inclusion–matrix interfaces associated with crevice formation. By combining SECM and SECCM, this study establishes a multiscale electrochemical framework for investigating complex corrosion phenomena. The results show that spinel inclusions promote micro-crevice corrosion, whereas AlN inclusions do not exhibit enhanced reactivity and are not initiation sites for localized corrosion.Subject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Lightweighting of vehicles is a global priority aimed at reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, improving fuel efficiency, and meeting increasingly stringent environmental regulations1. Steel continues to play a central role in automotive design because of its favorable combination of strength, cost, manufacturability, crashworthiness, and recyclability2. In particular, advanced high-strength steels (AHSS), with ultimate tensile strengths typically ranging from about 500 to 1500 MPa3, have become increasingly important for enabling weight reduction without compromising performance. The enhanced strength of AHSS arises from their complex microstructures and alloying strategies, which allow thinner gauge components to be used for the same structural role4. This substitution results in reduced vehicle mass, increased fuel economy, and extended driving range in electric vehicles (EVs)5. Recent life-cycle assessment studies indicate that lightweighting strategies using AHSS can reduce steel-related embodied emissions in vehicle manufacturing by 12–15%, while also yielding lower overall life-cycle emissions per unit of mass removed due to favorable recyclability and lower production energy relative to alternative materials6,7. In fleet-level modeling of lightweighting in China, substantial annual GHG savings are projected, further suggesting per-vehicle benefits at scale8. These benefits are amplified in the EV sector, where weight reduction directly translates to improved battery performance and range.

With all these advantages, understanding chemical durability of complex steel compositions remains critical to support downgauging and lightweighting of future cars built with AHSS. Corrosion can reduce service life and increase lifecycle costs of automotive components. Understanding the corrosion mechanism of AHSS is particularly difficult due to their complex microstructures, multiphase morphologies, and higher levels of alloying9. Microstructural heterogeneities such as inclusions, precipitates, and segregated phases were often identified as preferential sites for corrosion initiation10. In general non-metallic inclusions, particularly MnS, oxides, and oxy-sulfide composites, have been consistently recognized as key triggers for pit initiation in steels11–13. Their chemical activity differs from the surrounding matrix: sulfide inclusions, for example, often dissolve preferentially under chloride exposure, generating local acidification and result in crevice corrosion that destabilize passive films14,15. Inclusions can also create microgaps or strained interfaces with the matrix, further accelerating localized attack. The size, morphology, and distribution of these inclusions strongly influence the severity of corrosion propagation, with elongated or cracked inclusions leading to more aggressive localized damage16–18.

Although classical corrosion tests and macroscale electrochemical methods have provided valuable insight into the average corrosion behavior of AHSS19,20, they fail to capture the microscale and nanoscale events that govern initiation and propagation. Increasingly, localized electrochemical techniques are required to resolve how specific microstructural features, such as inclusions and their interfaces with the steel matrix, control corrosion susceptibility. Scanning Kelvin probe force microscopy (SKPFM) and scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM) have therefore been employed to probe spatial variations in surface reactivity and electrochemical behavior21,22. In particular, SECM has proven effective for mapping local redox activity, distinguishing conductive and resistive phases, and identifying electrochemically active regions associated with inclusions, passive films, and corrosion products23. However, because SECM operates through mediator-based feedback and diffusion fields extending beyond the immediate surface, it primarily provides indirect information on local corrosion processes21, and careful experimental design is required to ensure that the measured response is reliable24. Scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM), in contrast, allows direct electrochemical interrogation of surfaces at the micrometer to nanometer scale using electrolyte meniscus confined at the tip of pipettes25. This method has emerged as a powerful tool for mapping local corrosion currents, assessing passive film stability, and correlating electrochemical reactivity to microstructural features26. Recent studies have demonstrated the utility of SECCM in identifying localized electrochemical activity associated with inclusions and microstructural heterogeneities in steels, highlighting its potential for mechanistic studies of localized corrosion27–30.

In this work, SECM and SECCM are combined to exploit their complementary strengths. SECM is employed to probe local electronic conductivity and relative electrochemical reactivity across inclusions and the surrounding steel matrix, while SECCM directly quantifies localized corrosion kinetics and interfacial anodic activity under well-defined, spatially confined electrochemical conditions. Applied to AHSS containing Si, Al, and Mn alloying elements, this combined approach enables a multiscale, mechanistically resolved description of inclusion-induced corrosion. By integrating high-resolution electrochemical mapping with microstructural characterization, the study establishes direct correlations between inclusion chemistry, alloy microstructure, and localized corrosion reactivity. The mechanistic understanding gained from this approach will provide insight into how inclusions drive corrosion initiation in AHSS and will inform strategies to optimize alloy design, inclusion control, and protective measures for enhancing the durability of next-generation automotive steels.

Results and Discussion

High density of spinel inclusions causes maximum degradation

The influence of microstructural features on the corrosion performance of AHSS was first evaluated at the macroscale using cyclic corrosion testing (CCT). It is typical for steels to undergo CCT to become approved by automotive corporations, which has been designed to simulate in-field corrosion to provide correlation to severe corrosive environments. Three types of AHSS (S01, S02, and S03) with varying spinel inclusion densities were subjected to CCT, and the average mass loss was determined as a function of exposure time (Fig. 1A). The average mass loss of all samples increased over the 120-day exposure, indicating that the samples continued to corrode over the course of CCT. Regardless of the withdrawal time, the ranking for highest to lowest mass loss (corrosion) was S02 > S01 > S03. At 100 days, the average mass loss values were S02: 100%, S01: 79.1 ± 0.7%, and S03: 61.0 ± 0.8% (mean ± SD).

The observed corrosion ranking correlates directly with the areal number density of spinel inclusions present in each steel (Fig. 1B), which were quantified by automated SEM–EDX analysis (PSEM eXplorer, ASPEX LLC, Delmont, PA, USA). Among the different inclusions, AlN, CA6, TiN and spinel (Al-Mg oxides) inclusions were the most predominant in S02.

To determine which inclusions initiated localized degradation, short term (5 min) immersion studies in standard cyclic corrosion testing electrolyte (i.e., 0.5% NaCl, 0.1% CaCl2, and 0.075% NaHCO3) were conducted using freshly polished AHSS samples. During the initial minutes of immersion, small and discrete regions of localized corrosion emerged on the surface, which gradually increased in size and eventually coalesced (Fig. 1C). The associated optical video of the first 5 min of exposure is provided in Supporting Information (Video S1). SEM and EDX analyses confirmed that these features correspond to the partial dissolution of steel around spinel inclusions suggesting the onset of local interfacial degradation between the inclusion and the surrounding steel matrix (Fig. 1D, E). From multiple sites, the inclusions were classified as mainly spinel-type (Al–Mg–O; commonly MgAl2O4), together with composites featuring TiN attachments or sulfide rims (MnS and/or CaS), or occasional pure spinel particles. The ranking of corrosion rates obtained by CCT agrees with the ranking of the samples for the highest to lowest spinel inclusion density, indicating that such inclusions play a major role for corrosion initiation and the increase in corrosion rate for AHSS with higher spinel inclusion density. However, differences in other microstructural features, such as phase constitution and secondary phase distribution, may also influence corrosion propagation during cyclic exposure. Thus, while spinel inclusions appear to be a key factor in early stage localized initiation under the present conditions, they may not be considered the sole contributors to the overall corrosion propagation rate. These findings also suggest that, despite the AlN inclusions being the most common precipitate present in the AHSS samples, localized degradation did not occur at such regions.

To gain insight into subsurface corrosion processes, focused ion beam cross-sections were prepared at a single spinel inclusion to observe the corrosion evolution of the exposed region after 5 and 60 min of immersion (Fig. 1F–H). Before exposure, a micro crevice between the spinel inclusion and the metal matrix was present (Fig. 1F). After 5 min, the narrow micro-crevice was clearly observed at the inclusion-matrix interface, indicating the initiation of localized corrosion at this region. With increasing immersion time, the crevice first became filled with corrosion products, followed by uniform coverage of the metal’s surface after 1 h of exposure, suggesting active dissolution from the crevice and deposition of corrosion products on surrounding regions. The interfacial gap provides a crevice-like geometry that likely contributes to localized corrosion initiation and propagation, together with the formation of Fe-based corrosion products and the development of an occluded chloride-rich environment (Fig. S5). Continued exposure up to 3 h led to the eventual detachment of the inclusion from the surrounding matrix, leaving behind a shallow cavity partially filled with corrosion product residues (Fig. S6). This result also indicates the high chemical stability of MgAl2O4 inclusions over time in the selected immersion media.

These observations collectively demonstrate that corrosion initiation in the AHSS samples analyzed is influenced by the interfacial stability of spinel inclusions and that corrosion rate is proportional to the density of such inclusions. The micro crevices most likely originate during solidification and/or thermomechanical processing. Spinel inclusions have a very high melting temperature and are already solidified while the steel is molten during fabrication. During cooling and later processing (e.g., rolling), the spinel inclusion and the steel shrink and deform at different rates because they have different thermal expansion and mechanical properties, creating micro crevices at the interface13,31. These micro crevices may also arise from the brief exposure to water during sample preparation steps, where soluble species (i.e., CaS) may dissolve and result in micro crevice formation. The early formation of a micro-crevice at the inclusion-matrix interface may act as a precursor for localized dissolution, ultimately leading to inclusion detachment and propagation of corrosion. However, these surface-averaged experiments do not elucidate local electrochemical reactivity of the proposed microenvironments, which is required to verify the proposed mechanism.

Crevice corrosion mechanism confirmed using scanning electrochemical probe microscopy

To probe the microenvironment at the inclusion-matrix regions, localized electrochemical measurements were conducted. This study utilizes both SECM and SECCM to verify inclusion conductivity and investigate local corrosion parameters directly, respectively.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain inclusion-triggered localized corrosion in steels. Using localized electrochemical impedance measurements, Jin et al. suggested that Al2O3-enriched inclusions may induce a micro-galvanic interaction with the surrounding steel matrix32. However, non-metallic oxide inclusions such as MgAl2O4 are generally considered electrically insulating, with reported resistivities on the order of 1015 Ω cm at room temperature33, making direct galvanic coupling between the spinel phase and the matrix controversial. Other studies have proposed that apparent micro-galvanic effects may arise indirectly, for example, from potential differences between defect-rich matrix regions adjacent to inclusions and the bulk matrix, or from electrically active sub-phases in complex inclusions22,34. As a result, the relative contributions of micro-galvanic effects and occluded-cell (micro-crevice) chemistry at the inclusion–matrix interface remain debated, motivating localized electrochemical measurements to distinguish these mechanisms.

SECM in feedback mode was employed to investigate the electrical conductivity of the spinel inclusions and explore whether microgalvanic coupling between the inclusion and matrix is possible. A larger current represents faster regeneration kinetics of the redox mediator at that region, implying electrical conductivity. The SECM map acquired over a spinel-rich (MgAl2O4) complex inclusion containing CaS revealed lower current values compared to the current measured over the steel matrix (Fig. 2A–C). This indicates that substrate-facilitated mediator regeneration was suppressed over the inclusion, consistent with the negative feedback response observed while using SECM over non-conductive or poorly conducting regions35, and suggesting that the spinel inclusions are electrically resistive.

Once the electrically insulating nature of the spinel inclusions was confirmed using SECM, SECCM was conducted to extract local corrosion parameters at the inclusion-matrix interface to further investigate the localized corrosion mechanism. SECCM measurements were carried out using a 5 × 5 landing grid with 8 µm lateral spacing (Fig. 2D). The retraction distance reflects the droplet size at each landing site, with higher distance observed at the inclusion, indicating a larger droplet footprint (Fig. 2E). The spatially resolved extracted corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (jcorr) values from PDP measurements are shown in Fig. 2F, G, respectively. To account for variations in droplet size and wettability, current values were normalized on a point-by-point basis using droplet footprint estimates obtained from retraction curves36, an approach further validated by post-experimental SEM observations. Under these conditions, droplet diameters were consistently in the ~2–3 µm range, minimizing variations in oxygen transport and supporting that the measured localized currents predominantly reflect intrinsic corrosion kinetics rather than meniscus-induced artifacts.

From Table 1 it can be inferred that the inclusion site displayed a more positive Ecorr and the larger jcorr among all measured points of the matrix, indicating locally enhanced anodic activity relative to the surrounding matrix.

It should be noted that direct quantitative correlation between jcorr SECCM-derived values and macroscopic corrosion rates is not expected due to differences in measurement scale and environment as well as the generally porous, poorly adherent, and non-protective nature of corrosion products formed under CCT conditions37.

As shown in Fig. 2H, a micro-scale gap was visible at the inclusion–matrix interface before testing, suggesting incomplete interfacial bonding between the inclusion and the surrounding steel. The post-test SEM image shows a droplet footprint centered over the inclusion, extending slightly beyond its boundary to also encapsulate some of the steel matrix (Fig. 2I). After removal of corrosion products, EDX analysis (Fig. 2J) shows that the MgAl2O4 spinel inclusion remained intact, with TiN detected as a secondary phase at the periphery of the inclusion. Figure 2K shows that the OCP response measured on the inclusion differed from that on the surrounding matrix. The OCP initially shifted to a more positive value (~ −530 mV vs Ag/AgCl), then exhibited a rapid decrease to more negative potentials than the other sites, before stabilizing at a slightly more positive potential (~ −550 mV) than the matrix. The transient drop may indicate the onset of crevice corrosion, while the subsequent plateau suggests the system reached a quasi-steady mixed potential within 30 s.

Although the spinel inclusion is insulative in nature (as verified above using SECM), several factors may cause the anodic activity enhancement. For instance, conductive TiN phases (resistivity ~27 Ω.cm)33 at the perimeter of the spinel inclusion may cause the mixed potential to increase to more positive values and increase corrosion rates initially, thus increasing the micro crevice size38. In addition, the enhanced wettability over the inclusion-matrix interface may alter the oxygen diffusion profile to promote a higher concentration of the oxidant within the droplet39. Lastly, due to the micro-crevice at the interface, a local anodic region within the crevice and a local cathodic region outside of the gap may form to promote enhanced localized corrosion rates. It is also possible that some/all these factors are occurring simultaneously and synergistically during the corrosion initiation/propagation period. In any case, the increase in corrosion rate at the inclusion-matrix site, in combination with the demonstrated insulative nature of the spinel inclusions, provides evidence that a micro crevice corrosion mechanism is likely.

The schematic in Fig. 2L illustrates the proposed mechanism at pure spinel inclusions, where the SECCM meniscus encapsulates both an inclusion and metal matrix: restricted transport at the crevice mouth drives O₂ depletion, acidification (via Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ hydrolysis), and Cl⁻ enrichment inside the gap, i.e., formation of a critical crevice solution. The steel within the crevice acts as the anode, while the surrounding matrix within an aerated environment is the cathode. The spinel core (MgAl2O4) is electrically insulating, but chemically stable, enabling the crevice formation. Spinel inclusions consisting of conductive TiN sub-inclusions around their perimeter may experience an accelerated degradation pathway due to initial microgalvanic coupling at the inclusion–matrix interface. Previous work has reported that TiN inclusions can act as cathodic sites relative to the steel matrix40.

In short-term immersion tests, localized corrosion was also observed at inclusions consisting of an MgAl2O4 core surrounded by calcium sulfide phases. Given the known reactivity of Ca-bearing phases in aqueous environments and their potential to modify the inclusion–matrix interface33, SECCM measurements were therefore extended to this class of inclusions to assess their localized electrochemical behavior.

Figure 3A shows an SEM image of a representative spinel-rich inclusion prior to SECCM testing, where pre-existing gaps are visible at the inclusion–matrix interface. These gaps may form because of higher thermal expansion coefficient of CaS as compared with the steel matrix, similar to what was described previously41, or are due to the initial dissolution of CaS during grinding and polishing preparation steps that use water as a lubricant. EDX mapping (Fig. 3B) confirms an MgAl2O4-rich core surrounded by Ca- and Al-containing oxy-sulfide phases, with a minor TiN phase also detected. After SECCM testing (Fig. 3C), the interfacial gap surrounding the inclusion became more pronounced. EDX analysis (Fig. 3D) indicates substantial depletion of Ca and S from the inclusion’s perimeter, while the MgAl2O4 core remained intact, demonstrating preferential alteration of the CaS perimeter during exposure.

This preferential chemical dissolution of CaS from the inclusion periphery widens pre-existing gaps at the inclusion–matrix interface, thereby enhancing an occluded geometry characteristic of crevice corrosion42. The following steps are similar to what has been previously described for pure spinel inclusions: the confined region promotes restricted oxygen transport, chloride enrichment, and local acidification, thereby establishing conditions that favor localized anodic dissolution of the adjacent steel matrix. In addition, transient sulfur species released during CaS dissolution may promote continued metal dissolution by increasing the aggressiveness of the local chemistry, while variations in CaS distribution are expected to influence gap formation and local chemistry, thereby affecting the spatial corrosion morphology43.

The SECCM studies on both types of spinel inclusions confirm that, regardless of the presence/absence of the CaS perimeter, the different thermal expansion and mechanical properties between the inclusions and the steel matrix cause the formation of micro crevices that are responsible for localized corrosion initiation. Overall, the observed behavior likely reflects a combination of interacting mechanisms, with crevice-controlled processes dominating the early stages of corrosion initiation.

AlN Inclusions do not Initiate localized corrosion

Since AlN inclusions are the most common type present in the investigated AHSS, local electrochemical measurements were performed at these inclusions to explore their reactivity and verify that they do not play a role in localized corrosion initiation, as previously determined during the short-term immersion tests.

AlN inclusions have been reported in AHSS and related steels, and their role in pitting susceptibility has been discussed primarily in comparison with sulfide-bearing inclusions. For example, Park et al. reported that steels containing MnS inclusions exhibited more severe pitting than steels containing AlN inclusions under comparable conditions, suggesting that AlN is comparatively less effective as a pit initiator than sulfide-containing inclusions19. Nevertheless, reviews of inclusion–pitting mechanisms include AlN among inclusion types that may be associated with localized attack depending on interfacial integrity and the surrounding microstructure13,33. SEPM studies explicitly targeting AlN inclusions in steels appear scarce compared with the extensive literature on MnS, TiN, and oxide/sulfide composite inclusions.

Using the same experimental parameters employed in the previous section, SECM measurements were conducted over an identified AlN inclusion using SEM/EDX. As seen in the SECM current map in Fig. 4A, the current measured over the AlN inclusion was lower than that of the steel matrix. The reduced feedback current reflects a lower rate of mediator regeneration at the inclusion surface, consistent with its low electrical conductivity with a resistivity >1014 Ω cm, indicating that the inclusion is insulating44.

A key distinction from spinel inclusions is therefore interfacial geometry: spinel inclusions frequently portray interfacial gaps, whereas AlN inclusions were observed to be embedded without a pre-existing crevice. AlN forms by precipitation of supersaturated Al and N solute atoms within the steel matrix during solidification and cooling, and therefore tends to be more closely embedded in the surrounding matrix45,46. In contrast, spinel inclusions form at high temperature in the liquid steel and are incorporated into the matrix as pre-existing non-metallic particles, which may make them more susceptible to interfacial gap formation during solidification and/or thermomechanical processing47. Thermal mismatch during cooling may further contribute to interfacial stress. The presented electrochemical evidence indicates that AlN inclusions do not promote crevice-controlled initiation or microgalvanic coupling under the conditions studied.

While SECCM provides insight into localized initiation mechanisms, direct quantitative correlation with macroscopic corrosion rates is not expected, particularly as the corrosion products formed under CCT conditions are generally porous, poorly adherent, and non-protective. The mass loss percentage obtained from cyclic corrosion testing was found to scale with the density of spinel inclusions, underscoring their critical role in possibly governing macroscopic corrosion behavior. Beyond inclusion-driven initiation, variations in phase constitution between steel grades may also affect corrosion propagation and overall corrosion rate and are therefore highlighted as an important factor for future work.

This work establishes a multiscale methodology for probing localized degradation mechanisms in materials with complex microstructures. By combining immersion testing, microstructural characterization, and localized electrochemical measurements, this approach provides a framework for screening inclusion behavior and identifying critical degradation pathways, with relevance to alloy design and improved durability of automotive steels.

Methods

Materials and reagents

ArcelorMittal Dofasco’s Research & Development (Hamilton, ON, Canada) supplied three high-strength steel samples, each containing various alloying elements, notably C, Mn, Si, Al, and Nb. These included two experimental AHSS with minimum ultimate tensile strengths of 980 MPa and differing steel chemistry and microstructure, together with one high-strength low-alloy (HSLA) steel with a minimum ultimate tensile strength of 450 MPa. Due to proprietary reasons, their exact compositions are not disclosed. The steels are designated as S01, S02, and S03 throughout the manuscript, where S01 and S02 correspond to the AHSS grades and S03 corresponds to the HSLA steel. Among the investigated steels, S02 has the highest aluminum content, whereas S01 and S03 contain 0.8 wt% and 0.9 wt% less Al than S02, respectively. All samples were mechanically ground and polished to a mirror-like finish following the procedure described in the SI and were subsequently exposed to ambient laboratory conditions for 24 h to allow formation of a stable air-formed oxide layer. This pre-exposure reduced surface wettability and increased the contact angle relative to freshly polished surfaces (Fig. S1), improving control of electrolyte spreading during localized electrochemical measurements36.

Silver wire with a diameter of 0.1 mm (Goodfellow, 99.99% purity, Cambridge Limited, Huntingdon, England) was used to prepare the Ag/AgCl quasi-reference counter electrode (QRCE) by chlorination in commercial bleach (Lavo Inc., Montreal, Canada) for 30 min following established procedures48–50. Electrolyte solutions containing NaCl (Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, Canada), CaCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, Canada), and NaHCO3 (Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared using ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ.cm) obtained from a Barnstead Nanopure Diamond Water Purification System (Dubuque, IA, USA). Hydrochloric acid, ethylene glycol, and dichlorodimethylsilane were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Fisher Scientific and used as received for scanning electrochemical probe microscopy (SEPM) experiments.

Cyclic corrosion tests

Cyclic exposure was conducted in accordance with SAE J233451 using a Q-Fog CRH cyclic corrosion chamber (Q-Lab Corporation, Westlake, OH, USA). For each steel grade, three replicate coupons were mounted on a panel and exposed to daily 24 h cycles comprising: (1) 6 h at 50 °C and 100% RH, (2) 15 min salt spray (“shower” mode) at 25 °C using a solution of 0.5 wt% NaCl, 0.1 wt% CaCl2, and 0.075 wt% NaHCO3, and (3) 17 h 45 min at 60 °C and 50% RH (dry stage). Specimens were removed at 20-day intervals up to 100 days. Prior to exposure, each specimen was weighed (Mettler Toledo digital analytical balance, 5-digit resolution). After exposure, corrosion products were removed by abrasive blasting (identical procedure for all specimens), and the specimens were re-weighed. Percentage mass loss was used as a comparative metric to assess the relative severity of corrosion among the investigated steels, since all specimens had identical dimensions and were tested under identical exposure conditions. Short-term immersion tests

Freshly polished samples were immersed in an electrolyte of 0.5 wt% NaCl, 0.1 wt% CaCl2, and 0.075 wt% NaHCO3 with a pH of 7.6 ± 0.1 for specified durations at ambient temperature (22 °C). After testing, the samples were rinsed with ultrapure water and dried under argon. Surfaces were examined with a VHX 7000 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan), and corrosion-affected areas were then analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) to identify the inclusion types remaining after exposure.

Scanning electrochemical probe microscopy

SEPM measurements were performed using an ELP1 electrochemical probe scanner coupled with a PG 618 USB bipotentiostat (Heka Elektronik, Reutlingen, Germany). The system was housed inside a Faraday cage and mounted on a vibration-isolated table to minimize electrical and mechanical noise. Data acquisition and control were carried out using PotMaster software v2x92 (HEKA Elektronik).

For all experiments, an AHSS sample served as the working electrode (WE, SECCM) or substrate (for SECM). Prior to SEPM testing, SEM and EDX analyses were conducted to locate a particular inclusion, which was marked using focused ion beam (FIB) (30 kV, 10 nA) to enable precise probe positioning during SEPM mapping (Fig. S2).

SECM and SECCM measurements were conducted independently on inclusions of the same type, avoiding sequential probing of the same site and thereby minimizing any potential cross-influence between the two techniques.

For SECM measurements, a three-electrode configuration was employed, consisting of a ≈ 440 nm-radius nanoelectrode (NE) as the WE (Fig. S4), an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and a 1 mm Pt wire as the counter electrode. The AHSS sample was an unbiased substrate (at OCP). Feedback-mode maps were acquired in a non-aqueous electrolyte containing ferrocenemethanol as the redox mediator to probe local electrochemical reactivity.

For SECCM measurements, a two-electrode configuration was employed, with an Ag/AgCl QRCE housed inside the electrolyte-filled micropipette with an aperture diameter of 2.3 ± 0.1 µm (Fig. S3). Localized electrochemical measurements included open-circuit potential (OCP) monitoring and potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) at each landing site. Corrosion potentials (Ecorr) and current densities (jcorr) were extracted using Tafel analysis.

Acidified electrolyte (pH 2.5) was employed for SECCM measurements to improve droplet stability by minimizing evaporation-induced salt crystallization and to promote slight surface etching, facilitating post-experimental identification of the droplet footprint. Although acidified SECCM measurements do not directly replicate the bulk standardized electrolyte conditions (pH 7.6), they provide mechanistic insight under controlled and stable SECCM conditions. The relative trends and localized behavior observed are therefore used to inform the interpretation of corrosion processes in the standardized environment.

All data processing was performed using MATLAB (R2024b, MathWorks, USA), and statistical analyses were conducted using OriginPro 2023b (64-bit) SR1. Detailed probe fabrication procedures, experimental parameters, and data analysis methods are provided in the Supporting Information.

Surface analysis

The inclusions were analyzed by SEM (Hitachi SU3900 with accelerating voltage of 5 kV, Hitachi, Ltd., Chiyoda City, Tokyo, Japan) along with EDX (accelerating voltage of 20 kV, Oxford ULTIM MAX 65 SDD X-ray detector) for the surface characterization and chemical composition of the short-term immersion test. An ultrahigh-resolution field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Hitachi SU8230 Regulus accelerating voltage of 5 kV, Hitachi, Ltd., Chiyoda City, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to identify the droplet footprints of the matrix after the SECCM analysis. This was combined with EDX (Bruker X-Flash FQ5060 Annular Flat-Quad SSD) to determine the elemental composition of the corrosion deposits on the matrix of the material, as well as within the inclusions, following the cleaning of the sample with an ASTM standard solution from G0152. The size of each individual droplet landing was validated using ImageJ software (version 1.54j), where the diameters of the droplet footprints were manually measured. Cross-sections were prepared in a dual-beam FIB-SEM (LEO (Zeiss) 1540XB, Oberkochen, Germany) using a Ga+ ion column at 54o stage tilt. Coarse trenches were milled at 30 kV, 10 nA. Final polishing to remove curtaining was conducted at 30 kV, 200 pA.
