Metabolic and immune crosstalk between cancer-associated fibroblasts and pancreatic cancer cells
Authors: Qiyao Zhang, Zhen Cao, Shangcheng Yan, Bingrong Chen, Hao Wu, Hongtao Cao, Chen Lin, Ziwen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic cancer, specifically pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), is notorious for its aggressive nature and dismal prognosis, ranking as a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, chemotherapies, and novel therapeutic approaches, the overall survival rates remain bleak, primarily due to the complex and dynamic tumor microenvironment (TME) which fosters cancer progression and therapeutic resistance. Central to the TME are cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which exhibit significant heterogeneity and play multifaceted roles in tumor progression, ranging from remodeling the extracellular matrix to modulating immune responses. Recent breakthroughs in single-cell sequencing and spatial omics have unraveled the diverse functional states and spatial organization of various cell types within the TME, including distinct subtypes of CAFs which differ vastly in their influence on pancreatic cancer pathophysiology. This review delves into the intricate interactions between CAFs and pancreatic cancer cells, highlighting how metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation by CAFs contribute to the high malignancy of PDAC. By integrating single-cell and spatial resolution data, we offer new insights into the metabolic crosstalk and immune landscape remodeling driven by CAFs, establishing a foundation for targeting these stromal interactions in therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Globally, pancreatic cancer ranks as the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with an even higher incidence in developed countries [1]. In the United States alone, it is estimated that there are 62,200 new cases and 48,800 deaths annually. Approximately 90% of these cases are pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [2]. The incidence of this malignancy, which has a poor prognosis, continues to rise globally [3]. Thanks to recent milestone advancements in surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies, the prognosis for pancreatic cancer patients has improved [4, 5]. Nonetheless, as with other gastrointestinal solid tumors, early-stage pancreatic cancer often goes unnoticed in asymptomatic patients, leading to missed opportunities for optimal treatment [6]. Only 15–20% of pancreatic cancer patients present with surgically resectable disease [7, 8]. Furthermore, due to its low sensitivity to targeted therapies and immunotherapies, and its early metastatic characteristics, the 5-year survival rate remains a mere 11% [9, 10]. One of the primary reasons for this poor prognosis is the complex crosstalk and heterogeneity within the pancreatic cancer tumor microenvironment (TME). Compared to other tumors, PDAC’s TME is highly disorganized, with cancer cell nests scattered and surrounded by tumor-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and immune cells, indicating that various cell subtypes in the TME have profound effects on pancreatic cancer cells [11–14].

The TME of pancreatic cancer is highly disordered, and in stark contrast to the more organized and clearly defined tumor masses found in some other cancers, the scattered cancer cell nests are surrounded by a rich stroma interspersed with CAFs and immune cells, which is a hallmark of pancreatic cancer [11]. These cell components in the TME have profound effects on tumor cells, promoting tumor progression through paracrine signaling, metabolic interactions, and immune modulation [15]. Notably, CAFs play a crucial role in the formation of a fibrotic stroma, which limits drug delivery, enhances immune suppression, and provides survival signals to tumor cells [16, 17]. The high cellular heterogeneity and complex cellular interactions in the pancreatic TME are key determinants of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [18].

The latest breakthroughs in single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have made it possible to deeply characterize the various cell types within the pancreatic TME [19]. These advances allow researchers not only to examine the entire TME but also to investigate specific cell subtypes within it. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables the identification and analysis of gene expression profiles at the single-cell level, revealing the distinct cell populations present within the TME and providing insights into their functional states, interactions, and roles in tumor progression [20, 21]. Moreover, spatial omics integrates gene expression data with spatial location information, offering unprecedented insights into how cells within the TME interact in a spatially organized manner. This enables researchers to study how the proximity of specific cell types, such as CAFs and endothelial cells, influences tumor growth and immune responses [22].

Metabolic reprogramming in cancer is another key feature that distinguishes the TME of pancreatic cancer from that of other tumors. In cancer, tumor cells, CAFs, and immune cells all undergo metabolic changes that allow them to survive in the hypoxic and nutrient-deprived TME[23]. For example, similar to tumor cells, CAFs have a glycolytic phenotype and secrete lactic acid, which not only serves as an energy source for tumor cells and contributes to the formation of an acidic environment that suppresses immune cell function[24]. The metabolic crosstalk between various cell types in the TME, combined with epigenetic regulation, creates a complex metabolic environment that promotes tumor growth, metastasis, and resistance to treatment[25, 26]. The unique metabolic features of the PDAC TME distinguish it from other tumors, highlighting the importance of understanding metabolic roles in pancreatic cancer and the potential of targeting metabolic pathways as therapeutic strategies.

As discussed, the distinct characteristics of the pancreatic cancer microenvironment are rooted in its heterogeneity and complex cellular interactions. Several excellent reviews have explored the metabolic and epigenetic alterations in pancreatic cancer cells. However, the pancreatic cancer microenvironment is so unique that understanding its high malignancy traits cannot be fully achieved from the perspective of cancer cells alone. As the main stromal cells in pancreatic cancer TME, CAFs are close to tumor cells spatially, have been proven to engage in extensive crosstalk with pancreatic cancer cells, and play important roles in tumor progression [27, 28]. In recent years, the wide application of single-cell sequencing and spatial omics technologies have provided an important impetus for this kind of research [29, 30]. Previous excellent reviews have explained the important role of CAFs in pancreatic cancer. However, the progress in this field is so fast that high impact related research is constantly emerging. It is necessary to re-examine and summarize the latest developments in this field. In this article, we try to comprehensively review the metabolism and immune crosstalk of fibroblasts, immune cells and cancer cells in the microenvironment of pancreatic cancer, especially the excellent literature in recent years, in order to clarify the driving force of these mechanisms for the progress of pancreatic cancer, and find new biological clues for the diagnosis and treatment of pancreatic cancer.

Origins and subtypes of CAFs in pancreatic cancer

CAFs are a major component of TME in pancreatic cancer, characterized by high heterogeneity [16]. They play a crucial role in tumor initiation, metastasis, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and the establishment of an immune-suppressive environment [27, 31, 32]. The various functions of CAFs are largely dependent on their origin and subtype.

CAFs have a complex origin, derived from several precursor cell types. These origins significantly influence the functional characteristics of CAFs within the pancreatic cancer microenvironment. The various sources of CAFs have been identified, with pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) being one of the most common. In response to tumor-induced stress, local fibroblasts are activated through multiple signaling pathways, including TGF-β and IL-6 [15, 33]. These signals trigger the transformation of fibroblasts into CAFs, which secrete a range of matrix proteins, such as collagen and fibronectin. This transformation is essential for creating the dense stroma typically found in cancers, which supports tumor growth and provides a scaffold for metastasis [34].

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have also been identified as key contributors to the CAF population in pancreatic cancer [35]. These cells are recruited to the tumor site by chemokines, such as CCL2, and can differentiate into CAFs upon activation by TGF-β and other local tumor signals. CAFs derived from BMSCs are crucial for ECM remodeling and promote tumor metastasis by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and other proteases [36]. Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (Ad-MSCs) also participate in CAF formation in cancer [37]. These cells migrate from adipose tissue and differentiate into fibroblast-like cells under the influence of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. These cytokines stimulate Ad-MSCs to secrete collagen and other ECM components, promoting the fibrotic stroma surrounding the tumor and enhancing the pro-tumor activity of Ad-MSCs.

Endothelial cells can undergo a phenotypic transition called endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), contributing to the CAF pool in cancer. This transition is mediated by factors such as VEGF and TGF-β, which lead to endothelial cells acquiring mesenchymal characteristics [38]. EndMT-derived CAFs support angiogenesis and tissue fibrosis, both of which are crucial for pancreatic tumor progression [39]. These cells promote the formation of the tumor vasculature and provide a supportive niche for cancer cells.

CAFs are direct mediators of the interaction between pancreatic cancer and its microenvironment, playing a deep and integral role in the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Although CAFs are generally considered a single cell type, they exhibit a variety of functions in pancreatic cancer, sometimes displaying conflicting roles. Studies have shown that CAFs in pancreatic cancer can be classified into several main subtypes based on their functional characteristics:

Myofibroblast subtype (myCAF)

MyCAFs are characterized by high expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and abundant extracellular matrix components, particularly collagen I. The ARP2/3 complex, a key regulator of actin cytoskeleton organization and lamellipodia formation, plays a pivotal role in CAF migration and differentiation. Pharmacological inhibition of ARP2/3 markedly decreases CAF motility and disrupts their morphology. Moreover, ARP2/3 inhibition specifically impairs TGF-β1–mediated myCAF differentiation, while having no detectable effect on IL-1–driven inflammatory CAF (iCAF) differentiation [40]. These CAFs are mainly located in the periductal regions of the TME, where they promote fibrous tissue proliferation through direct contact with cancer cells [41]. By expressing α-SMA, myCAFs alter the contractile forces transmitted by intracellular smooth muscle actin, increasing the stiffness of the tumor microenvironment [42–44]. These cells restrict the infiltration of immune cells, such as T cells and NK cells, promote immune evasion by the tumor, and create barriers to the penetration of exogenous drugs [45, 46]. Interestingly, recent studies have shown that fkbp7 appears as an early activation marker in the precancerous matrix, precedes the expression of α-SMA, and responds to the hardening and fibrogenic process of PSC induced by FAK and TGF-β. This process may serve as a potential target for mediating ECM normalization and inhibiting PDAC tumorigenesis [47].

Inflammatory subtype (iCAF)

iCAFs are characterized by high expression of inflammatory mediators such as cytokines and chemokines, while their α-SMA expression is significantly lower. iCAFs play a crucial role in tumor progression by secreting cytokines (e.g., IL-6) and chemokines (e.g., CXCL12). iCAFs are stimulated by IL-1 and activate the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, creating a pro-tumor microenvironment. M2BPGi, secreted by iCAFs, can directly activate mTOR signaling and induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition in PDAC cells, enhancing their invasive and migratory ability [48]. Studies have suggested that the ratio of myCAFs to iCAFs may have significant implications for pancreatic cancer progression [12].

Antigen-presenting subtype (apCAF)

apCAFs are characterized by high expression of MHC class II molecules and the ability to present antigens to CD4 + T cells [49]. Research has shown that mesothelial cells identified in normal pancreatic tissue share similar gene characteristics with apCAFs, indicating that apCAFs may originate from mesothelial cells [50]. IL-1 and TGF-β induce the transition of mesothelial cells to apCAFs. However, apCAFs lack the co-stimulatory molecules required to induce T cell proliferation. Although apCAFs can directly interact with CD4 + T cells, the MHC II molecules expressed by apCAFs may act as decoy receptors, inducing non-responsiveness or differentiation into regulatory T cells (Tregs), leading to the inactivation of CD4 + T cells [51, 52]. In this case, apCAFs are expected to lower the ratio of CD8 + T cells to Tregs, thereby impairing anti-tumor immunity. Consequently, apCAFs may contribute to immune suppression in the PDAC microenvironment.

In addition to the above subtypes, CAFs exhibit further complexity in their functional diversity. Studies have shown that cancer- promoting CAF (pCAF) predominantly express FAP-α or α-SMA and secrete multiple pro-tumor factors such as TGF-β, IL-6, and CXCL12, which promote cancer cell proliferation and invasion [53–55]. In contrast, cancer-restraining CAF (rCAF) display anti-tumor properties in mouse models and human cells [56]. rCAFs express potential markers like meflin and exhibit anti-tumor activity, which partially explains the contradictory results in therapies targeting pancreatic cancer CAFs. As mentioned above, epithelial MAPK activity promotes myofibroblast differentiation of CAFs by maintaining the expression and secretion of TGF-β1. Lisa Veghini et al. identified the MAPK-high CAF (mapCAF) phenotype. This phenotype is associated with reduced frequency of basal-like tumor cells and CD8 + T cells, and is also characterized by TGF-β signaling, sensitive hypoxia response, and immune-regulatory gene programming [57]. Recently, a new subtype, FAPα⁺ CD144⁺ endoCAFs, has been identified, which is associated with poor prognosis in patients with PDAC. FAP α + CD144 + endocafs not only obtain the vascular mimicry (VM) phenotype to provide a metastatic conduit, but also promote the in situ proliferation and invasion of tumor cells through paracrine signals, thus actively promoting the metastasis of tumor cells. By activating the CD144- β—catenin-stat3 signal axis, the transcription of CD144 and downstream secreted cytokines was up-regulated to maintain the dual role of endogenous CAFs [58]. The plasticity of CAFs in pancreatic cancer allows them to undergo dynamic changes within the tumor microenvironment. For instance, iCAFs can transform into myCAFs following activation of TGF-β signaling or inhibition of IL-1-induced JAK/STAT signaling [27]. This transformation may play different roles at various stages of tumor development. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms underlying the transformation between CAF subtypes is crucial for designing precise targeted therapeutic strategies.

Metabolic crosstalk between CAFs and PDAC

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is described as a highly hypoxic cancer type due to the dense connective tissue proliferation, which leads to poor vascularization and low oxygen and nutrient levels [59–61]. Hypoxic PDAC cells alter their metabolism by increasing glycolysis and glutamine catabolism while reducing dependence on oxidative phosphorylation. In the pancreatic cancer microenvironment, hypoxia reduces FBXW7 expression in CAFs through ETS1. Loss of FBXW7 disrupts its inhibitory binding to KMT2, leading to increased H3K4 methylation and global gene upregulation. As a result, conditioned media from hypoxia-exposed CAFs enhances the migration and invasion of pancreatic cancer cells, suggesting that hypoxia promotes cancer progression at multiple levels [62]. The direct intercellular contact between CAFs and cancer cells may stimulate and enhance their mutual metabolic reprogramming. This metabolic adaptation may reflect the CAFs' survival response to tumor-induced hypoxia, which is possibly required to maintain their acquired proliferative program. These metabolic adaptations are believed to play a key role in the nutrient re-utilization by other cells in the TME and cancer cells.

Lipid metabolism

In rapidly proliferating cancer cells, maintaining an adequate pool of fatty acids is one of the key metabolic requirements, particularly for membrane synthesis. Studies have shown that cancer cells are highly sensitive to the availability of unsaturated fatty acids, and a lack of unsaturated fatty acids significantly reduces cell proliferation and increases cell death [60, 63]. The hypoxic state in the pancreatic cancer tumor microenvironment (TME) inhibits fatty acid desaturation, particularly inhibiting the activity of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), which converts stearic acid (C18:0) to oleic acid (C18:1). Since this process requires oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor, the highly hypoxic PDAC cells must rely on exogenous unsaturated fatty acids to maintain lipid homeostasis and membrane function. Exogenous unsaturated fatty acids become the primary lipid source for PDAC cells in nutrient-limited conditions, ensuring tumor cell vitality and proliferation.

In this process, CAFs play a critical role in the TME. Research shows that CAFs are the primary source of unsaturated fatty acids in the tumor microenvironment, and they promote lipid transfer to PDAC cells through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, CAFs transport lipids from blood particles to cancer cells via endocytosis, extracellular vesicle transport, and lipid transfer proteins. Cancer cells form synaptic-like membrane contacts with CAFs, inducing calcium influx into the CAF cytoplasm through the Orai channels, which activates the ANO6 protein. The activation of ANO6 leads to the exposure of phosphatidylserine on the CAF membrane, triggering phagocytosis and transferring membrane lipids, including cholesterol, to PDAC cells, thereby supporting tumor cell membrane synthesis and stability [64]. Notably, inhibition of ANO6 can block the transport of exogenous cholesterol, suppress tumor cell growth, and reverse immune suppression [65].

Additionally, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) is especially enriched in CAF-conditioned media and is preferentially absorbed by PDAC cells, where it is converted into phosphatidylcholine (PC) to maintain membrane integrity and alleviate cancer cell stress. This "cross-feeding" mechanism is not limited to LPC, extending to other lipid components. For example, in PDAC cells with SETD2 deletion, transcriptional activation driven by aberrant H3K27ac deposition induces BMP2 secretion. BMP2, in turn, promotes the differentiation of a subset of lipid-laden CAFs. These specialized CAFs shuttle lipids to neighboring cancer cells, supporting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and thereby promoting tumor progression [66, 67].

Moreover, studies indicate that PDAC cells, through TGF-β1 and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) signaling, induce mPSCs to adopt a more myCAF-like phenotype, which then secretes type I collagen to suppress tumor dissemination [41, 68, 69]. Another study showed that exosomes secreted by PDAC cell-induced CAFs, rather than by native CAFs, could inhibit gemcitabine (GEM)-induced ferroptosis in PDAC cells [70, 71]. This mechanism involves the miR-3173-5p secreted by CAF exosomes, which specifically targets ACSL4. ACSL4 is an enzyme that catalyzes the esterification of CoA into specific polyunsaturated fatty acids (such as arachidonic acid) [72]. The biosynthesis of arachidonoyl-CoA catalyzed by ACSL4 promotes ferroptosis by triggering phospholipid peroxidation.

It is noteworthy that the exosomes secreted by CAFs contain a full complement of intracellular metabolites, including amino acids, acetate, stearate, palmitate, and lactate esters. These nutrients directly contribute to the TCA cycle in cancer cells under nutrient deprivation or stress conditions, supporting tumor cell proliferation. CAF exosomes also contain fatty acids such as palmitic acid, oleic acid, and linoleic acid [73]. These lipids not only provide essential membrane components for PDAC cells but also play a critical role in supporting tumor metabolism and growth.

Carbohydrate and lactate metabolism

The metabolic characteristics of cancer cells are markedly different from those of healthy cells, particularly in terms of energy and biosynthetic demands. In tumors and other rapidly proliferating cells, glycolysis serves as the major anaerobic metabolic pathway, and cancer cells often preferentially rely on glycolysis for energy production even in the presence of oxygen [74, 75]. This not only aids in ATP production but also provides abundant biosynthetic precursors to support the high demand for anabolic processes such as the synthesis of non-essential amino acids and nucleotides [76]. The byproduct of glycolysis, lactate, is produced in large amounts in tumor cells and becomes a key metabolite involved in the interactions between different cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Lactate is transported from tumor cells to CAFs via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1). In glucose-limited tumor environments, CAFs can utilize lactate from tumor cell glycolysis as an energy source and exert immunosuppressive effects in the PDAC TME. Co-culture experiments show that most glucose is absorbed by tumor cells, while CAFs consume lactate through MCT1, promoting the TCA cycle [77]. Moreover, lactate stimulates CAFs to upregulate IL-6 expression, and IL-6, in concert with lactate, inhibits the activity of cytotoxic immune cells, further enhancing tumor immune evasion [78]. Tumor-derived TGF-β signaling promotes aerobic glycolysis in CAFs and impairs oxidative phosphorylation by downregulating IDH3A, leading to increased lactate production79, 80. High expression of HIF1A also promotes glycolytic activity and lactate secretion in CAFs, creating an overall acidic TME. Additionally, MCT4 (monocarboxylate transporter 4) is used to transport lactate from CAFs to tumor cells[75]. Once lactate is transported from CAFs to tumor cells, it can be utilized for oxidative phosphorylation (under aerobic conditions) or further processed through the TCA cycle to generate ATP, enabling tumor cells to sustain proliferation in a harsh microenvironment. In this metabolic crosstalk, CAFs exhibit high aerobic glycolysis, using energy-rich metabolites such as lactate and pyruvate, and further provide fuel to neighboring cancer cells via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [81]. In CAFs, CircABCC4 promotes IL-8 secretion by promoting nuclear translocation of PKM2 in CAFs, thereby leading to transcriptional glycolytic reprogramming of genes associated with glycolysis, a process associated with oxaliplatin resistance and tumor progression [82]. Recent studies have shown that perineural invasion-associated cancer-associated fibroblasts exhibit significantly upregulated glycolysis, creating a high-lactate tumor microenvironment conducive to cancer progression. Lactate secreted by these CAFs is taken up by tumor cells and facilitates histone H3K18 lactylation, which activates transcription of neural invasion-associated genes, such as L1CAM and SLIT1, thereby driving perineural invasion in pancreatic cancer [83].

Although glucose does not appear to play a major role in PDAC-CAF metabolic interactions, recent studies have shown that under glutamine-deprived conditions in the pancreatic cancer microenvironment, CAFs utilize autophagy processes and NUFIP1 (nuclear fragile X-interacting protein 1)-dependent degradation of their own rRNA to generate nucleosides. These nucleosides support glycolysis and the TCA cycle, and by activating MYC, facilitate the adaptation of cells to glutamine starvation, further promoting glucose utilization in PDAC [84–86].

Amino acids

Alanine serves as an alternative carbon source in PDAC, surpassing glucose and glutamine-derived carbons to fuel the TCA cycle, thereby providing fuel for non-essential amino acid (NEAA) and lipid biosynthesis [87]. This shift in fuel source reduces the tumor's reliance on glucose and serum-derived nutrients, which are limited in the pancreatic tumor microenvironment [88]. Additionally, PSCs secrete alanine in a manner dependent on PSC autophagy, a process stimulated by cancer cells [89]. Cysteine is a limiting nutrient in the TME, and an adequate supply is critical for the growth and maintenance of pancreatic cancer cells. A recent study suggests that PDAC cells regulate the cysteine transporter SLC7A11 through an autophagy mechanism to control cysteine uptake [90]. CAFs are key mediators of this process, increasing the extracellular cysteine supply in PDAC via their transsulfuration pathway to support the synthesis of glutathione (GSH) and prevent PDAC ferroptosis, a process activated by the TGF/SMAD3/ATF4 signaling pathway [91]. CAFs also stimulate branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism in cancer cells by upregulating the production of branched-chain α-ketoacids (BCKA). CAFs secrete BCKA, which is absorbed by cancer cells and further oxidized to drive the TCA cycle. Interestingly, tumor-derived TGF-β stimulates BCKA synthesis in CAFs by inducing ECM internalization and BCAT1 expression [92]. BCAT1 is a known catalyst for the transamination of BCAA to BCKA. Pancreatic cancer cells also enhance chemoresistance to gemcitabine treatment by delivering KRASG12D protein to CAFs via extracellular vesicles (EVs) [93]. Recent studies have shown that CAFs in pancreatic cancer are particularly sensitive to metabolic abnormalities, and can increase P4HA1 expression during tumor progression to increase hydroxyproline levels, ultimately enhance collagen deposition and contraction, thus stromatogenesis, and reduce T lymphocyte infiltration, which may explain the higher aggressiveness of PDAC in patients with metabolic abnormalities. Part of the reason is that there is no tumor T lymphocyte infiltration [94]. Immune crosstalk between CAFs and PDAC

CAFs are a dominant stromal component in PDAC, where they form a dense, desmoplastic matrix. This fibrotic stroma not only corrals tumor cells into nests but also creates a high-pressure physical barrier that hinders the penetration of therapeutic agents and immune cells [95, 96]. Indeed, elevated interstitial pressure and dense collagenous matrix produced by CAFs are major obstacles to effective immunotherapy in PDAC [97, 98]. Importantly, CAFs are highly heterogeneous; they comprise multiple subpopulations with distinct phenotypes and functions, and this diversity underlies their complex roles in tumor progression and immune modulation​ [99].

Regulation of CAFs

The activity and maintenance of CAFs in PDAC are tightly regulated by both tumor-intrinsic signals and cues from the immune microenvironment. Inflammatory cytokines released within the TME can sustain and amplify CAF activation. For example, IL-1β and IL-6, produced by tumor cells or infiltrating immune cells, activate NF-κB and JAK/STAT3 signaling in CAFs, driving them toward a pro-tumorigenic state [100, 101]. These signaling pathways induce CAFs to secrete more inflammatory mediators and growth factors, thereby creating a feed-forward loop that maintains CAFs in an activated, immunomodulatory phenotype. Chronic oxidative stress in the tumor—a common feature of PDAC—also modulates CAF behavior. Oxidative stress can stimulate CAFs to release factors like interleukin-33 (IL-33), which has downstream effects on immune cells (discussed later) and is associated with enhanced tumor progression under hypoxic conditions​ [102].

Tumor-derived factors are another critical regulator of CAF function. PDAC cancer cells actively communicate with and condition nearby fibroblasts. A recent study highlighted the AGR2/IGF1 signaling loop as an example of tumor–CAF crosstalk: PDAC cells secrete anterior gradient-2 (AGR2), which activates CAFs through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, causing the CAFs to upregulate and secrete insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1)​. The secreted IGF-1 feeds back to tumor cells via the IGF1R/c-JUN axis to further increase AGR2 production, thus establishing a reinforcing loop that elevates CAF activation and immunosuppressive effects [103]. This illustrates how tumors can regulate CAFs to create a more permissive microenvironment for themselves.

Intrinsic regulatory mechanisms within CAFs also play a role in modulating their behavior. One example is the hypoxia-inducible factor 2α (HIF-2α) pathway in CAFs. HIF-2α is often upregulated in stromal cells under the hypoxic conditions of PDAC and drives the expression of genes associated with fibrosis and immunosuppression. Deletion of Hif2α specifically in CAFs markedly reduced the desmoplastic stroma and altered the immune landscape, as detailed below [104].

Additionally, CAFs can be regulated by therapeutic interventions targeting these pathways. For instance, blocking IL-1 signaling in CAFs has been shown to alter their chemokine output: an IL1RAP-neutralizing antibody (nadunolimab) was able to inhibit IL-1–induced chemokine release by CAFs and consequently reduce the recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid cells [105]. Such strategies that disrupt key regulatory signals highlight the potential to modulate CAF activity and reprogram the tumor stroma.

Immunosuppressive functions of CAFs

Through various mechanisms, CAFs actively establish an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that protects PDAC cells from immune attack. One major mode of immunosuppression is the secretion of soluble ligands that inhibit anti-tumor immune cells. CAFs are prolific sources of TGF-β and CXCL12, among other factors, which can suppress the infiltration and effector function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes​. TGF-β, for example, reinforces local immune tolerance and has been implicated in the exclusion of T cells from cancer cell niches in PDAC, thereby blunting responses to checkpoint inhibitor therapy [98]. Similarly, CXCL12 (also known as SDF-1) produced by CAFs can form a chemokine barrier around tumor cells; targeting CXCL12 from fibroblasts has been shown to enhance T cell penetration into PDAC and synergize with anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy [106].

CAF-derived IL-6 is another potent immunosuppressive cytokine in PDAC. IL-6 can impair the cytotoxic function of natural killer (NK) cells and activates STAT3 signaling in tumor cells, promoting an environment conducive to immune evasion​. In fact, CAFs of the inflammatory subtype (iCAFs) are the principal source of IL-6 in the PDAC stroma [107]. Through these immunosuppressive secretions, CAFs dampen the cytolytic activity of T cells and NK cells, allowing the tumor to grow relatively unchecked by the immune system.

Besides soluble factors, CAFs exert immunosuppressive effects by modulating cell–cell interactions and the physical tumor matrix. CAFs can upregulate immune checkpoint molecules or ligands (such as PD-L1, Gal-9, etc.) on themselves or on extracellular vesicles, which can directly inhibit T cell activity upon contact (though CAF-specific checkpoint expression is less studied, their indirect effects on checkpoints in the microenvironment are recognized)​. Additionally, the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) deposited by myCAFs creates a physical exclusion zone that limits immune cell access to tumor islets. Collagens and other matrix components not only impede T cell migration but also can sequester immune-activating cytokines. High interstitial pressure in fibrotic PDAC lesions (driven by CAF activity) further prevents efficient infiltration of lymphocytes​.

Notably, activated pancreatic stellate cells/CAFs have been shown to corral CD8+ T cells at the periphery of tumor regions, effectively sequestering T cells away from malignant cells [108]. This physical and stromal-induced spatial separation is a form of immune suppression distinct from molecular signaling and represents another layer by which CAFs protect the tumor.

The functional heterogeneity of CAFs means that different subsets can have divergent immunomodulatory roles. Generally, subsets like iCAFs, LRRC15+ myCAFs, and senescent CAFs are pro-tumorigenic and immunosuppressive. LRRC15+ CAFs (a subset characterized by the LRRC15 marker and associated with a myCAF phenotype) have been identified as a TGF-β-dependent population that potently suppresses CD8+ T cell activity and correlates with resistance to immunotherapy [49, 109]. Senescent CAFs, which develop a senescence-associated secretory phenotype, express elevated levels of immunoregulatory genes and can create a strongly immunosuppressive niche; in PDAC, senescent CAFs were shown to limit the number of activated CD8+ T cells in tumors, in part via expression of factors like urokinase (PLAU) that modulate the immune milieu [110–112].

However, not all CAFs uniformly inhibit immune responses—some can indirectly support immune surveillance or constrain more suppressive elements. For instance, studies have found that depleting certain CAF subsets can have counterintuitive effects on tumor immunity. FAP+ CAFs (fibroblast activation protein-α positive) are generally tumor-promoting and their ablation can improve immune control of the tumor, whereas α-SMA+ myCAFs appear to restrain tumor progression despite their prototypical “activated” phenotype​. In a mouse model of PDAC, selective elimination of FAP+ CAFs enhanced anti-tumor immunity (leading to improved survival), whereas elimination of α-SMA+ CAFs led to an influx of immunosuppressive cells (like regulatory T cells) and accelerated tumor growth [42, 106, 113]. This dichotomy suggests that α-SMA+ myCAFs may play a protective role by regulating immune balance (for example, by keeping infiltration of Tregs in check), highlighting the nuanced, context-dependent functions of CAF subpopulations. Consequently, broad CAF-targeting therapies must be approached with caution, as removing all fibroblasts can inadvertently dampen immune surveillance, whereas targeting the right subtype can alleviate immunosuppression.

Another special CAF subtype with immunosuppressive implications is the antigen-presenting CAF (apCAF). apCAFs express MHC class II and can present antigens, but since they do not express co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80, CD86, CD40), they cannot activate T cells productively​. Instead, apCAFs might induce tolerance: it has been proposed that they act as decoy antigen-presenting cells that engage CD4+ T cells with peptide-MHC II complexes but then drive those T cells into anergy or even differentiation into Foxp3+ regulatory T cells [51, 52]. In this way, apCAFs could contribute to the local T cell suppression by misdirecting helper T cell responses. While the exact impact of apCAFs on anti-tumor immunity is still under investigation, their presence in PDAC adds another layer to how CAF diversity influences the immune landscape. In summary, through a combination of secreted suppressive factors, remodeling of the physical microenvironment, and direct cell interactions, CAFs create an immunosuppressive milieu in pancreatic cancer. This immunosuppression allows tumor cells to evade cytotoxic T lymphocytes and NK cells, posing a significant barrier to effective immunotherapy.

Immune cell recruitment and reprogramming by CAFs

Beyond suppressing effector lymphocytes, CAFs actively recruit various immune cell types into the tumor and can skew their differentiation toward tumor-promoting phenotypes. CAFs serve as orchestrators of immune cell infiltration, often attracting immunosuppressive or pro-tumor immune cells into the PDAC microenvironment [114, 115]. One mechanism is through chemokine secretion: IL-1β in the tumor milieu can stimulate CAFs to secrete chemokines that preferentially recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and neutrophils to the tumor site [116]. The influx of these myeloid cells contributes to immune evasion, as MDSCs and tumor-associated neutrophils are well-known suppressors of T cell function. Blocking the IL-1/IL1RAP signaling axis on CAFs has been shown to interrupt this pathway; treatment with an IL1RAP antibody (nadunolimab) significantly reduced IL-1–induced neutrophil and monocyte recruitment in PDAC models​ [105]. This demonstrates that CAFs are a key intermediary in translating inflammatory signals (like IL-1) into the accumulation of immunosuppressive immune cells in tumors.

CAFs also secrete a spectrum of chemokines (e.g., CXCL1, CXCL2) and growth factors that draw in neutrophils and support their pro-tumor functions. Inflammatory CAFs produce CXCL1 and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) among other factors [107], which leads to increased neutrophil migration into the tumor and extension of neutrophil lifespan in the TME​. The recruited neutrophils, often polarized into an N2 (pro-tumor) phenotype, release proteases and reactive oxygen species that facilitate cancer cell invasion and metastasis. Indeed, neutrophils activated by CAF-derived signals have been shown to directly enhance the migratory and invasive capacity of pancreatic cancer cells​. CAF-secreted CXCL2 (a chemokine for CXCR2+ myeloid cells) can similarly attract polymorphonuclear myeloid cells into the tumor. Strategies to disrupt the CAF–neutrophil axis have shown promise in preclinical studies: for example, the antifibrotic drug pirfenidone was found to inhibit IL-8 production by CAF-stimulated neutrophils, thereby reducing neutrophil-mediated enhancement of PDAC malignancy [117]. This suggests that mitigating CAF-induced neutrophil recruitment or activation can temper the pro-tumor inflammation.

In addition to neutrophils, CAFs recruit and influence monocytes/macrophages. Chemokines like CCL2 draw CCR2+ monocytes into the tumor, where CAF-derived signals then promote their differentiation into tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). CAFs can secrete factors (e.g., IL-6, M-CSF) that bias macrophage polarization toward an M2, immunosuppressive phenotype. A striking example of CAF-driven reprogramming is through exosomal communication: CAFs release exosomes carrying prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2, also known as COX-2) and other modulators, which can be taken up by macrophages​. These CAF-derived exosomes activate the NOD1 pathway in macrophages, skewing them to an M2-like state while inhibiting pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, ultimately enhancing the migration and epithelial-mesenchymal transition of cancer cells [118]. Such reprogrammed M2 macrophages produce immunosuppressive cytokines (like IL-10, TGF-β) and promote matrix remodeling, thereby further sustaining a tumor-favorable microenvironment.

CAFs also facilitate the generation of MDSCs from bone marrow precursors. IL-6 produced by CAFs not only affects lymphocytes but also acts on granulocyte-monocyte progenitors, driving their development into myeloid-derived suppressor cells. In PDAC, IL-6 from CAFs has been linked to an expansion of Gr1 CD11b+ MDSCs that inhibit T cell responses. Targeting CAF-derived IL-6 in combination with immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti-PD-L1 therapy) has shown synergistic effects in reducing tumor progression and prolonging survival in preclinical models, underlining the contribution of IL-6-driven MDSC recruitment to immune suppression in PDAC.

Furthermore, CAF-secreted factors influence the recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and other immunosuppressive lymphocytes. CAFs release abundant TGF-β, which is a critical cytokine for inducing Foxp3+ Treg differentiation and function. By creating a TGF-β–rich environment, CAFs indirectly facilitate the conversion of conventional CD4+ T cells into Tregs or the expansion of existing Treg populations in the tumor. Some subsets of CAFs might also produce chemokines like CCL5 or CXCL12 that can attract T cells, but once present in the CAF-rich stroma, these T cells may be trapped and exposed to suppressive signals, leading to a dominance of Tregs over effector T cells. The earlier mentioned dichotomy between FAP+ and α-SMA+ fibroblasts is illustrative: FAP+ CAFs were associated with accumulation of Tregs in PDAC, whereas α-SMA+ CAFs constrained Treg infiltration​. When α-SMA+ CAFs were ablated in experimental models, an unchecked influx of Tregs occurred, correlating with poorer outcomes [106, 113, 119]. Thus, CAFs help dictate the composition of the T cell infiltrate, often tilting it toward immunosuppressive Treg dominance.

In summary, CAFs actively recruit innate immune cells (neutrophils, monocytes) and adaptive immune cells (T cells), but skew their recruitment and differentiation toward pro-tumor, immunosuppressive phenotypes. They reprogram incoming immune cells through secreted factors, surface molecules, and vesicles, ensuring that the immune infiltrate is biased toward tumor promotion rather than rejection. This comprehensive immunomodulatory role is evident when CAF function is perturbed. Notably, the genetic ablation of HIF-2α in CAFs not only diminished fibrosis but also significantly reduced the intratumoral accumulation of M2-polarized macrophages and Tregs, thereby unleashing a more effective anti-tumor immune response and inhibiting PDAC growth [104]. This finding highlights that disrupting CAF-mediated immune recruitment can remodel the tumor immune microenvironment towards a more anti-tumor state. Strategies targeting metabolism and immunity to inhibit pancreatic cancer progression

As the understanding of CAF-driven metabolic and immune response suppression deepens, these cells have become one of the most promising therapeutic targets for cancer intervention. In recent decades, the number of preclinical studies targeting CAFs to restore anti-cancer immune responses has sharply increased. Currently, there are three main strategies for CAF-based immunotherapy: direct CAF depletion, CAF activation and functional suppression, and ECM remodeling limitation induced by CAFs (Fig. 3). In addition to immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, and nivolumab), CAF-specific therapies have become an important complement to immunotherapy and have provided significant clinical benefits for cancer patients [120, 121]. However, as mentioned earlier, due to the lack of specific markers for CAFs, current CAF-targeted therapies must address the challenge of simultaneously enhancing anti-tumor effects and reducing systemic side effects [122]. This partly explains why only a few CAF-targeted therapies have been translated into clinical practice. FAP-targeted therapies that deplete CAFs can enhance anti-cancer immune responses mediated by high levels of certain inflammatory mediators (such as IFN-γ and TNF-α), promoting CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity and metabolism while reducing pro-proliferative stroma in the TME [123, 124]. However, due to the adverse effects of single-agent formulations, current research is considering combining these therapies with other drugs, such as cyclophosphamide (CY), to overcome tumor stromal barriers and enhance CY’s non-specific toxicity against tumor cells. However, such attempts have not yet been conducted in pancreatic cancer. As another surface marker of myCAFs, research on α-SMA-targeted therapies has stalled because of its dual impact on tumor progression. In PDAC, depletion of α-SMA + CAFs has been shown to inhibit cancer cell metastasis and tumor angiogenesis [125]. However, other studies suggest that targeting α-SMA may cause Treg infiltration (CD3 + and Foxp3 + Tregs) in the TME, promoting disease invasion and progression [42].

Another strategy is to design therapies that utilize CAF plasticity by converting tumor-promoting CAF subtypes into tumor-suppressing subtypes. Recent studies have shown that CAF subtypes are dynamic and can change in response to microenvironmental factors such as culture conditions and surrounding cells [126]. Therefore, developing therapies that can switch tumor-promoting subtypes to tumor-suppressive subtypes is ideal. Among myCAFs, iCAFs, and apCAFs, iCAFs, in particular, have been shown to promote tumor activity through IL-1-JAK-STAT signaling [126]. In one study, a JAK inhibitor, AZD1480, was used in PDAC mouse models to target iCAFs and convert them to myCAFs. However, while JAK inhibitors effectively target cancer cells and iCAFs, they may also target CTL proliferation and activity, which complicates their combination with immunotherapies. Another study showed the potential of TAK1 in this regard. Knocking out TAK1 in CAFs reduces the iCAF feature and increases the myCAF feature. TAK1 knockdown blocks the secretion of inflammatory factors through multiple pathways associated with immunosuppression, inhibits EMT of PDAC, and promotes the infiltration of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells [127]. There are other challenges in targeting CAFs. One study showed that mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-liposomes loaded with chloroquine could precisely target CAFs, inhibit CAF autophagy, and enhance the efficacy of immunochemotherapy for pancreatic cancer. This process not only reduced the production of interleukin-6 (IL-6) but also disrupted the highly proliferative fibrotic TME, while simultaneously lowering the transcription levels of USP14 in pancreatic cancer cells [128]. However, inhibiting CAF autophagy effectively suppressed anti-tumor immune responses and anti-tumor efficacy in mouse models by upregulating CD274/PD-L1 levels [128, 129]. The diversity of CAF effects on pancreatic cancer cells has led to this paradox. A deeper understanding of CAF transformation mechanisms and the identification of more specific targets are essential for overcoming this issue.

To address the dense tumor microenvironment caused by CAFs, Yuan et al. reported a "fishing in the barrel" strategy, which involves preparing a lipid-polymer hybrid drug delivery system (PI/JGC/L-a). By transforming it into a "bucket" with anti-tumor drug storage properties, this system overcomes the CAF barrier, alleviates the immunosuppressive microenvironment, and increases immune cell infiltration. PI/JGC/L-a normalizes the CAF barrier and forms a CAF "bucket" with JQ1, stimulating exosomes loaded with gemcitabine to be secreted from the CAF "bucket" into the deep tumor. Additionally, the CAF "bucket" secretes IL-12, facilitating effective drug delivery to the tumor, activating anti-tumor immunity at the tumor site, and producing significant anti-tumor effects [130]. Furthermore, the role of CAF-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) packaged with long non-coding RNA (LncRNA) in immune evasion has been confirmed. Hanming Yao and colleagues explored the use of engineered EVs as natural and biocompatible siRNA-based gene therapy nanocarriers and demonstrated promising results [131].

Regarding metabolism, tumor cells gain an advantage by exploiting the lipid-rich TME, influencing various tumor types. Certain tumor cells, such as breast cancer and pancreatic cancer, directly utilize the pre-existing lipid-rich microenvironment to drive oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [132–134]. Interestingly, recent studies also provide evidence suggesting that stromal cells can differentiate into lipid-rich phenotypes to support these progressive traits. In the case of pancreatic tumorigenesis, PSCs undergo lipidome remodeling and secretion of enriched lipids upon activation, promoting the biosynthesis of LPA and facilitating tumor progression [135, 136]. It is important to note that the effects of lipid-rich stroma extend beyond the tumor cells, also promoting an immunosuppressive TME, as seen with lactate shuttle-induced acidosis in the TME [137, 138].

Targeting ECM deposition to impair tumor metabolism is another potential strategy [139]. Both losartan and PEGPH20 target collagen deposition in myCAFs and improve drug delivery in preclinical models [140, 141]. Although PEGPH20 combined with albumin-bound paclitaxel/gemcitabine did not improve overall survival in metastatic patients, losartan combined with FOLFIRINOX showed promising results in locally advanced PDAC [142, 143]. Besides improving drug delivery, ECM-targeted drugs subsequently re-oxygenate the tumor and increase the extracellular pH in the TME, which is likely to affect tumor metabolism. A recent study demonstrated the feasibility of integrating carbonyl iron (FeCO) and the tumor stroma-degrading drug losartan into a biodegradable nanomaterial, mesoporous polydopamine. The nanoparticles were coated with CAF cell membrane (CAFM), and the drug delivery system was constructed with plectin-1 targeted peptide targeting PC cells. The system utilizes homologous adhesion properties of CAF membrane to target CAF and deliver Lo to degrade ECM. FeCO further permeates, binds to PC tumor cells through PTP, and is released, which can effectively induce tumor cell apoptosis [144].

As discussed above, TGF-β is a crucial factor in CAF-PDAC cell crosstalk. TGF-β signaling drives myCAF differentiation and re-establishes CAF metabolism to glycolysis, which helps provide metabolic intermediates to cancer cells [73]. Galunisertib, a potent TGF-β1 antagonist, has shown promising results when combined with gemcitabine in unresectable PDAC patients [145]. The therapeutic effect of galunisertib may be the result of TGF-β-dependent myCAF inhibition.

Challenges and directions for CAF research and targeted CAF therapy in pancreatic cancer

Due to the significant role of CAFs in pancreatic cancer, targeting CAFs to restore anti-cancer immune responses and improve therapeutic efficacy has become an important area of research in cancer treatment. However, the clinical translation of CAF-targeted therapy still faces multiple challenges and requires further in-depth research and technological innovation.

Challenges of CAF heterogeneity and subtype specificity

CAF heterogeneity is a major challenge currently faced in CAF-targeted therapy. CAFs exhibit not only heterogeneity across different tumor types but also significant differences between CAF subtypes within the same tumor. CAFs can display distinct functional characteristics depending on changes in the microenvironment. Each CAF subtype has a different impact on tumor progression, and its mechanism of action has not yet been fully elucidated. The contradictory results from clinical trials targeting CAFs confirm this complexity. Therefore, accurately identifying and targeting specific CAF subtypes, especially in the context of the complex immune microenvironment, remains a problem that needs to be addressed.

Additionally, the plasticity and transformation capability of CAFs further complicate targeted therapy. CAFs can dynamically convert into different subtypes in response to various tumor microenvironment conditions through interactions with tumor cells and immune cells. Consequently, designing therapies that promote the transformation of CAF subtypes from tumor-promoting to tumor-suppressive phenotypes has become an important direction for future research.

Strategies and challenges of CAF-targeted therapy

Although some therapeutic strategies have shown promising results in preclinical studies, particularly in enhancing anti-cancer immune responses mediated by IFN-γ and TNF-α, and promoting CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity, CAF-targeted therapies still face several challenges, especially in improving the specificity of treatment and reducing systemic side effects. For example, due to the lack of specific CAF markers, current CAF-targeted therapies may affect non-target cells when attempting to target specific CAF subtypes, leading to adverse reactions. Furthermore, although some CAF-targeted therapies show good results in preclinical models, they have failed to significantly improve efficacy in clinical trials. This is partly due to the diversity of CAF interactions with pancreatic cancer cells, which may lead to unexpected side effects, thus affecting the efficacy of standard chemotherapy.

Meanwhile, single-target strategies may provoke unwanted immune responses in the TME in certain cases, necessitating the further optimization of combination therapies. For example, combining drugs like cyclophosphamide (CY) may overcome tumor stromal barriers and enhance the non-specific toxicity of CY against tumor cells, significantly improving anti-tumor efficacy. However, such attempts have not yet been conducted for pancreatic cancer.

The supportive role of CAFs in tumor cell metabolism is another important research direction. CAFs not only support tumor cell growth through metabolic interactions but also promote immune evasion through their impact on TME metabolism. In this context, targeting metabolic-related proteins in CAFs, especially enzymes involved in glycolysis and lipid metabolism, could serve as new therapeutic targets. For instance, the supply of lipids and non-essential amino acids derived from CAFs influences the energy balance and biosynthesis of tumor cells, offering potential intervention opportunities for immune therapy.

Future research directions

To address the challenges of CAF-targeted therapy, future research should focus on the following areas:

Identification and Targeting of Specific Markers:

Developing more specific CAF markers is essential for accurately targeting specific CAF subtypes in clinical treatments, minimizing side effects.

In-depth Study of CAF-Immune Cell Interactions:

Further exploration of the complex interactions between CAFs and immune cells, especially their roles in immune evasion and anti-tumor immune responses, is crucial for optimizing immunotherapy strategies.

Overcoming the Stromal Barrier:

Designing drug delivery systems capable of effectively overcoming the dense stromal barrier formed by CAFs to promote drug penetration deep into the tumor and enhance immune cell infiltration, improving the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Targeted Modulation of CAF Plasticity:

Investigating therapies that promote the transformation of CAF subtypes, particularly converting tumor-promoting CAFs into anti-tumor CAFs, provides new strategies for cancer treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, although CAF-targeted therapy shows great promise in pancreatic cancer, the complexity of CAF heterogeneity and diversity remains an undeniable challenge in the treatment process. Future research needs to focus on developing more targeted and efficient therapeutic strategies based on a deeper understanding of CAFs' biological characteristics, metabolic functions, and interactions with immune cells. This approach will ultimately improve prognosis and therapeutic outcomes for PDAC patients.
