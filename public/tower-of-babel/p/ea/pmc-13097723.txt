Occupational and non-occupational device-measured movement behaviours and low back pain: a compositional isotemporal substitution analysis
Authors: Kaja Kastelic, Nejc Šarabon, Michael D Burnard, Željko Pedišić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep were shown to be independently associated with low back pain (LBP). The aim of this cross-sectional study was to explore the associations between 24-hour movement behaviour compositions and the occurrence, severity, and estimated level of LBP impact on an individual’s life.MethodsA convenience sample of 197 adults (40% females, 37 ± 11 years of age) were asked to wear an activPAL accelerometer for at least 7 consecutive days to assess their time-use composition consisting of moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity (MVPA), light-intensity physical activity (LPA), sedentary behaviour (SB), and sleep and to complete a questionnaire on LBP and sociodemographic characteristics. Compositional isotemporal substitution analyses were conducted separately for the non-domain-specific and domain-specific (including occupational and non-occupational domains) movement behaviour compositions.ResultsReallocating time from MVPA to any other movement behaviour or from sleep to LPA was associated with a higher LBP impact score. For example, reallocating 60 min/day from MVPA to LPA was associated with on average 17 points (95% CI: 6 to 28) higher LBP impact score (on a 0–70 scale). We did not find significant associations between the domain-specific time-use composition and LBP impact score (p = 0.060). We also did not find significant associations of the time-use compositions with occurrence and severity of LBP (p-value range: 0.067 to 0.649).ConclusionOur study suggests that LBP sufferers with higher MVPA and sleep better cope with LBP. The differences in the LBP impact scores associated with theoretical reallocations between movement behaviours may be deemed clinically important. Future longitudinal and experimental studies in population-representative samples are needed to confirm our findings.Keywords: Musculoskeletal health, Physical behaviours, Time-use epidemiology

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is among the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorders globally [1]. More than 80% of people in the general population experience LBP at least once in their lifetime [2], while nearly 40% of people report suffering from LBP in the past year [3]. Although LBP is not a life-threatening condition, it can significantly affect daily life and well-being [4], and it is associated with numerous co-morbidities [5]. LBP is a multifactorial (i.e. influenced by many contributing factors) and multidimensional (i.e. involving many aspects) health issue, and there is a need for successful management strategies to lessen the population burden of LBP [6, 7].

The way people allocate their time may affect the development, frequency, and intensity of LBP [8, 9]. Studies have shown that allocation of time to different daily movement behaviours, such as physical activity, sedentary behaviour (SB), and sleep, is among the most important modifiable risk factors for chronic disease, including LBP [9–13].

Previous studies on movement behaviours and LBP have generally reported that higher levels of total physical activity [8], lower levels of SB [14], and higher levels of sleep [15] are associated with favourable LBP outcomes, including lower risk of experiencing LBP and less severe LBP among those who suffer from LBP. However, some studies have revealed that different domains and intensities of physical activity may have different associations with LBP. For example, studies on occupational moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity (MVPA) showed unfavourable relationship with LBP [16], while most studies on leisure-time physical activity showed favourable association with LBP [8, 17].

Traditionally, studies have explored relationships between time spent in a specific movement behaviour and LBP, while ignoring the fact that time-use variables are co-dependent parts of a finite whole (e.g. 24-hour day). However, physical activity, SB, and sleep are exhaustive and mutually exclusive components of time use, and they are perfectly multicollinear. For example, a change in daily time spent in SB requires an opposite proportional change in time spent in physical activity and/or sleep, because the daily sum of these three movement behaviours always equal to 24 h. In recent years, it has been widely recognised that the amounts of time spent in movement behaviours should be conceptualised as compositional data [18–20] and statistical reasoning has been provided for analysing this type of data integratively, using compositional data analysis (CoDA) [21].

One such statistical method is compositional isotemporal substitution analysis. This method enables estimating the change in a health outcome by reallocating a given amount of time between movement behaviours [21]. The method has quickly gained popularity among researchers, and it has already been used in more than one hundred studies [20]. However, a recent review revealed that no previous studies have used this method to explore the associations of reallocations of time between movement behaviours with LBP [20].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore the associations of reallocating time between the parts of non-domain-specific and domain-specific 24-hour movement behaviour compositions with occurrence of LBP, severity of LBP symptoms, and the estimated level of impact of LBP on individual’s life. We hypothesized that reallocations of time from SB and LPA to MVPA and sleep are associated with favourable LBP outcomes, and vice versa.

Methods

Participants and study design

The data for this cross-sectional study were collected between April 2019 and September 2022 (with exception of COVID-19 lockdowns) in a convenience sample of 197 Slovenian adults, including police officers, military personnel, higher education employees, office workers, and university students. They were invited to participate in the study via employee mailing lists and word of mouth. Due to missing data, nine participants were excluded from the study, and the final sample consisted of 188 participants (Table 1), all of whom were included in non-domain-specific analyses. Of these, 141 participants, who were employed and who provided sufficient data for domain-specific analyses, constituted the final subsample of working adults (Fig. 1).

Measures and data processing

Movement behaviours

Participants were asked to wear activity monitor for at least 7 consecutive days (24 h per day, except while swimming), and to keep a paper-based sleep-time diary. Employed participants were also asked to keep a paper-based work-time diary. After the monitoring period, participants returned the activity monitor and the diary, and completed a paper-based questionnaire on LBP and socio-demographic characteristics.

The activPAL4 micro activity monitor (PAL Technologies Ltd., Glasgow, Scotland) was used to record how participants allocate their time to movement behaviours, including sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA. The device was attached on the anterior aspect of the right thigh (midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the knee), using an adhesive dressing (Tegaderm™). Proprietary software was used to initialize the device and to download the recorded data (PALconnect version 8.12.6.118, PAL Technologies Ltd., Glasgow, Scotland).

We used the CREA algorithm (PALanalysis version 8.12.6.118, PAL Technologies Ltd., Glasgow, Scotland) with a 24-hour protocol that allows for up to 4 h per day of non-wear time. To determine periods of sleep time, we combined the information from the CREA algorithm and sleep time diary, as proposed previously [22, 23] and manually corrected the sleep onset and offset using the PALanalysis software. Data were then exported as “Daily Summary Outcomes” (PALbatch version 8.11.1.63, PAL Technologies Ltd., Glasgow, Scotland). Data were also exported as “Events” files, and further processed in R Studio using the package “activpalProcessing” [24] to obtain MVPA estimates. LPA was calculated by subtracting sleep duration, SB, MVPA, and non-wear time from 24 h. Non-wear time was then proportionally reallocated to SB, LPA, and MVPA [25]. Self-reported duration of daytime napping was added to sleep time estimate to obtain total sleep time, and it was deducted from SB to obtain total time spent in SB. The average amounts of time spent in sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA were calculated across valid days.

For employed participants, we also computed the durations of occupational SB, LPA, and MVPA, and non-occupational SB, LPA, and MVPA. ActivPAL data were extracted as “EventsEx” files, and further processed in R Studio using a custom-made code to obtain daily durations of occupational and non-occupational sleep, SB, overall physical activity, and non-wear time based on information from work time diary. To obtain occupational and non-occupational MVPA, “Events” files and work time diary were processed using R package “activpalProcessing”. Occupational LPA was calculated by subtracting occupational MVPA from overall occupational physical activity. Occupational non-wear time was then proportionally reallocated to occupational SB, LPA, and MVPA. The same procedure was performed also for movement behaviours during non-occupational time. Self-reported duration of daytime napping was added to non-occupational sleep time and deducted from non-occupational SB. Sleep durations reported during occupational and non-occupational time were summed to obtain total sleep time. The average amounts of time in movement behaviours were calculated separately across valid workdays and non-workdays. To obtain a weekly time-use composition, the amounts of time spent in movement behaviours on workdays and non-workdays were calculated as: 5 × the average workday + 2 × the average non-workday.

Low back pain

The Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire [26] was used to assess the occurrence of LBP. As the outcome variable in this study, we used participants’ responses to the question asking about experiencing LBP in the past 12 months with two response options: yes and no.

The 7-item Graded Chronic Pain Scale [27] was used to assess the severity of LBP, including pain intensity and pain-related disability. The questionnaire has a recall period of past 6 months, and it was completed only by the participants who experienced LBP in the past 6 months. According to the scoring criteria proposed by the authors of the questionnaire, we categorised participants in LBP severity grades as: Grade I: low intensity-low disability, Grade II: high intensity-low disability, Grade III: high disability-moderately limiting, and Grade IV: high disability-severely limiting. Due to a small number of participants reporting high LBP disability or high LBP intensity, we later grouped Grade II (n = 22), Grade III (n = 3), and Grade IV categories (n = 1) into a single category Grade II-IV: high intensity/high disability.

The Bournemouth Questionnaire [28] was used to estimate the level of impact that LBP had on participants’ life in the past week. The questionnaire was completed only by the participants who experienced LBP in the past week. The questionnaire consists of 7 questions asking about: (i) pain intensity; (ii) interference of pain with activities of daily living; (iii) interference of pain with the ability to take part in recreational, social, and family activities; (iv) anxiety symptoms; (v) depressive symptoms; (vi) fear-avoidance beliefs about work activity; (vii) and pain locus of control. Participants are asked to provide responses to the questions on a scale from 0 to 10. According to the scoring criteria proposed by the authors of the questionnaire, the total score (ranging from 0 to 70) was calculated as the sum of all responses, where higher scores suggest stronger impact of LBP on individual’s life.

Socio-demographic characteristics

Participants provided information about their age (years), sex (male / female), body height (cm), body weight (kg), and education (primary or secondary education / higher education). Body mass index was calculated as body weight divided by squared body height (kg/m2).

Statistical analysis

We used R (version 4.3.0) with R Studio (version 2023.06.2) and the packages “boot”, “car”, “codaredistlm”, “compositions”, “tidyverse”, “foreach”, “GGally”, “knitr”, “mice”, and “performance”. Participant characteristics were presented as arithmetic mean and standard deviation (for continuous, non-compositional data), absolute and relative frequencies (for categorical data), and as compositional means (for compositional data).

To examine the associations of daily time-use composition with LBP variables, in the total sample we computed the isometric log ratio coordinates (ilrs) from data on sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA (i.e. three ilrs for a 4-part composition) [29]. To examine the associations of weekly domain-specific time-use composition with LBP variables, in the subsample of working adults we computed ilrs from data on sleep, occupational SB, occupational LPA, occupational MVPA, non-occupational SB, non-occupational LPA, and non-occupational MVPA (i.e. six ilrs for a 7-part composition).

A series of regression analyses were conducted where ilrs were entered as independent variables, and LBP as the dependent variable. We used [i] binary logistic regression with the occurrence of LBP in the past 12 months as the binary variable (yes / no), [ii] binary logistic regression with the LBP severity grades as the binary variable (Grade I: low intensity-low disability / Grade II-IV: high intensity/high disability), and [iii] linear regression with the LBP impact score treated as a continuous variable. The analyses were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, and education. For model diagnostics in the binary logistics regression analyses, we used the binned residual plot. As part of model diagnostics for the linear regression analyses, we conducted posterior predictive, normality of residuals, influential observations, and homoscedasticity checks. The proportion of variance of LBP impact score explained by the time-use composition was expressed as delta R-squared calculated using a hierarchical regression analysis. In the first step of the analysis, we used age, sex, body mass index, and education as independent variables. In the second step, we added the time-use composition to the model. Delta R-squared was calculated as the difference between the multiple R-squared calculated in the second step and multiple R-squared calculated in the first step.

For models with a significant (p < 0.05) associations with time-use composition, we used compositional isotemporal substitution analysis with one-to-one reallocations of time between movement behaviours, with the mean composition as the reference composition [29].

Results

Participant characteristics

The age of participants ranged between 18 and 62 years, with the arithmetic mean and standard deviation of 37 ± 11 years (Table 1). Most of the participants were males, had a higher education degree, and reported experiencing LBP in the past year and in the past 6 months. Approximately every fourth participant reported experiencing LBP in the past week. The LBP impact score among participants who experienced LBP in the past week was on average 17 out of 70 points for working adults and 18 out of 70 points for all adults.

Associations between non-domain-specific movement behaviours and low back pain

The 4-part time-use composition consisting of sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA was associated with the LBP impact score (delta R-squared = 0.269, p = 0.004). We did not find significant associations of the 4-part time-use composition with the occurrence ( = 1.7, p = 0.649) and severity of LBP ( = 7.1, p = 0.067).

Among those who reported LBP in the past week, reallocating time from MVPA to any other movement behaviour was associated with a higher (i.e. more unfavourable) LBP impact score (Fig. 2), and reallocations in the opposite direction were associated with a lower LBP impact score. For example, reallocating 60 min/day from MVPA to sleep, SB, and LPA was associated with on average 14.0 (95% CI: 2.9 to 25.0), 15.5 (95% CI: 5.1 to 26.9), and 17.3 (95% CI: 5.7 to 29.0) points higher LBP impact score, respectively. Furthermore, reallocating time from sleep to LPA was associated with a higher LBP impact score (and vice versa). For example, reallocating 60 min/day from sleep to LPA was associated with on average 3.5 points higher LBP impact score (95% CI: 1.3 to 5.8). The reallocations of time from SB to sleep or LPA (and vice versa) were not found to be significantly associated with the LBP impact score.

Associations between domain-specific movement behaviours and low back pain

The domain-specific 7-part time-use composition was not found to be significantly associated with the occurrence of LBP ( = 5.3; p = 0.503), severity of LBP ( = 7.5; p = 0.277), and the LBP impact score (delta R-squared = 0.391; p = 0.060).

Discussion

Main findings

The main finding of this study is that the non-domain-specific time-use composition of movement behaviours is associated with LBP impact score. Specifically, our compositional isotemporal substitution analyses showed that reallocating time from MVPA to any other movement behaviour or from sleep to LPA is associated with a higher LBP impact score, while reallocations in the opposite direction were associated with a lower LBP impact score.

Comparison with previous studies

In this study, we assessed multidimensional (i.e. pain intensity, disability, affective, cognitive) estimated impact of LBP on individual’s life. Therefore, our findings are not directly comparable with results of previous studies that focused on a single aspect of LBP impact (e.g. pain intensity). Nevertheless, some comparisons with previous studies may be informative. For example, our finding that reallocating time to MVPA is associated with a lower LBP impact score is in line with results of a previous study showing that reallocations of time to vigorous-intensity physical activity are associated with a lower intensity of LBP [30]. It has been previously proposed that some types of MVPA (e.g. exercise) beneficially impact LBP by neuromuscular conditioning, improving aerobic capacity, reducing pain-related psychosocial and behavioural issues (e.g. fear avoidance, catastrophising, kinesiophobia), and via neurophysiological mechanisms (e.g. physical activity-induced analgesia) [31, 32]. In our study, reallocating 60 min/day from MVPA to another movement behaviour was associated with on average up to 17.3 points (on a scale from 0 to 70) higher LBP impact score, which may be deemed a clinically important difference [33].

Unlike the current study, a previous CoDA-based study found that reallocations from MVPA are associated with a lower intensity of LBP [34]. It might be that different LBP outcomes have different associations with reallocations of time between movement behaviours. For example, some types of MVPA may present higher biomechanical loadings on already irritated spinal tissues among individuals with LBP, which may further aggravate their symptoms of LBP [35] and lead to increased intensity of LBP. However, MVPA might help to reduce LBP-related symptoms of anxiety and depression, fear avoidance behaviour, social functioning, and coping with co-morbidities [36], leading to reduced impact of LBP on life.

In the current study, reallocations from LPA to sleep were associated with a lower LBP impact score. These findings are in accordance with a previous CoDA-based study showing that reallocations from LPA or SB to sleep are associated with lower frequency and intensity of LBP [34], as well as with findings of previous studies that did not use CoDA [13, 37]. It has been previously proposed that a lack of sleep may unfavourably alter neurochemical processes responsible for pain (e.g. inflammation, dopaminergic and opioidergic signalling, central pain modulation) [38, 39]. It may also indirectly impact LBP via psychological and behavioural mechanisms (e.g. negative affect, pain-related beliefs, coping behaviours) [38, 39]. Low sleep duration may also not provide sufficient time for spinal tissue recovery [40]. Given the large body of evidence on the importance of sleep for pain management [13, 37–39], it is interesting that most clinical guidelines for LBP do not mention sleep [41–43]. In accordance with our findings, when developing and updating future clinical guidelines for LPB, the inclusion of sleep recommendation should be considered, as in the recent Australian Low Back Pain Clinical Care Standard [44].

We did not find a significant association of non-domain-specific time-use composition of movement behaviours with occurrence and severity of LBP. This is in accordance with previous studies that used CoDA and explored the occurrence of LBP in the past year [34] and differences between individuals who did and did not experience LBP in the past 7 days [45]. However, a study that explored reallocations between wake-time behaviours found that substituting SB with MVPA is associated with a reduced risk of chronic musculoskeletal pain of at least moderate intensity [46]. It has been previously suggested that classifying participants as cases versus non-cases without applying a minimal severity threshold for LBP may partially explain differences between findings of previous studies on the association between movement behaviours and the occurrence of LBP [47]. Unlike Ryan et al. [46], in the current study we did not use a minimal severity threshold for LBP, which may partially explain the difference between findings. Due to a lack of variability in the severity of LBP in our sample, we needed to dichotomise the LBP severity variable, which may have reduced the sensitivity of the variable and attenuated its estimated association with the movement behaviours. Future studies should aim to include participants with various levels of LBP severity.

In the current study, we did not find a significant association of domain-specific movement-behaviour composition with occurrence of LBP. Unlike the current study, some previous studies found unfavourable association of occupational MVPA with occurrence of LBP [16] and long-term sickness absence among individuals with LBP [45]. These associations were thought to be attributed to the limited autonomy of workers over the duration, intensity, and variation of occupational MVPA [45]. However, previous findings on unfavourable associations of occupational MVPA with LBP come mainly from studies that included blue-collar workers (e.g. construction workers, cleaners, and manufacturing workers). Our convenience worksite sample consisted mainly of higher education employees and office workers, who are likely to have very little occupational MVPA and high levels of occupational SB. Therefore, their domain-specific time-use composition may have been much different than in the samples from previous studies. Our sample also included police officers and military personnel, who, as part of their occupational activities, may engage in physical exercise to keep fit for their job. Given that exercise is a specific type of physical activity aimed at improving fitness and health, including LBP [48], it may be that their job demands implied engaging in MVPA that has favourable effects on LBP. More research is needed to understand the associations between (domain-specific) movement behaviours and LBP in different professions.

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study are that we adequately addressed the compositional nature of time-use data and that we used device-based measure to collect data on movement behaviours. However, there are also some limitations to this study. First, the convenience sample and relatively small sample size may limit generalisability of our findings. However, this limitation should be considered in the context of relatively high costs and administrative burden associated with accelerometer-based studies, and in the light of advantages of accelerometer data, compared with self-reported data on movement behaviours [49]. Given that we recruited participants from worksites and university student population, generalisability to the clinical population may be limited. Also, given that participants from only a few occupations (i.e. police officers, soldiers, office workers) were included in the sample, generalisability to other occupations that may be exposed to different occupational movement behaviour compositions (e.g. construction workers, manufacturing workers) is also limited. Second, due to a lack of variability in our sample, we needed to dichotomise LBP severity variable, which may have led to loss of important information. Second, reallocations of time were performed only around the mean composition. Findings on reallocations might be different for populations with a different mean composition. For example, it could be that among highly physically active populations, reallocating time from SB to physical activity is unfavourably associated with LBP, while the same reallocation among highly sedentary populations is favourably associated with LBP. Third, we assessed the occurrence of LBP in the past 12 months without differentiating between first-time, recurring, and ongoing LBP. It has been previously suggested that these three LBP outcomes may have different risk factors [50]. Finally, the cross-sectional design of our study did not allow us to draw conclusions about causality. There are plausible mechanisms on how movement behaviours can influence LBP [31, 39, 40], but LBP can also influence movement behaviours [39, 51]. Therefore, future studies with longitudinal and experimental design are warranted to better understand the prospective associations and impact of movement behaviours on LBP, and the potential role of changing movement behaviours to self-manage LBP.

Conclusions

We found that the non-domain-specific time-use composition of movement behaviours is associated with the LBP impact score. In specific, reallocating time from MVPA to any other movement behaviour or from sleep to LPA was found to be associated with a higher LBP impact score. Our findings suggest that sleeping longer and increasing time spent in MVPA may help to better cope with the LBP. Future longitudinal and experimental studies in population representative samples are needed to confirm our findings.
