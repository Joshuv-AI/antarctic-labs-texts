Three-dimensional visualization of nerves in bones of the human hand in osteoarthritis
Authors: John-Paul Fuller-Jackson, Richard Tee, Jenny Thai, Eugene T Ek, Stephen Tham, Jason Ivanusic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone is innervated by nerves that have important roles in signaling bone pain, regulating bone homeostasis and remodeling, control of bone vasculature and hematopoiesis. However, a detailed knowledge of how nerves influence bone function is lacking, which has contributed to significant challenges for clinicians in successfully managing bone disease. In this study, we used tissue clearing and light-sheet microscopy to visualize and explore the 3D distribution of nerves in human bone with a view to better understand how nerves are distributed throughout different parts of the small bones of the hand. Human hand bones were obtained from patients undergoing excision due to advanced osteoarthritis. Bones were fixed and cleared using an optimized solvent-based clearing protocol. Nerves in the bone were immunolabeled using an antibody directed against a pan-neuronal marker (protein gene product 9.5). Samples were imaged using light-sheet microscopy (Ultramicroscope Blaze, Miltenyi Biotec). Three-dimensional visualization and analyses were performed with Imaris (Bitplane). There is extensive innervation of the small bones of the human hand. Immunolabeled nerves followed blood vessels into each bone via nutrient foramina and branched extensively within trabecular bone and marrow cavity spaces. Many axons had a typical corkscrew morphology associated with the innervation of the vasculature, and many terminated as free nerve endings, including in osteochondral channels beneath articular surfaces. Nerve entry points and their distribution through each of the different bones varied. There was evidence of a greater density of innervation in areas where cartilage had degenerated, relative to those where cartilage was intact. This is the first detailed 3D visualization of the neuroanatomical distribution of nerves in intact human bone. This approach to visualizing nerves in bone will be useful in informing the rational design of approaches to the management of a variety of skeletal pathologies, including pain management for osteoarthritis of the hand joints.

Introduction

Bone is richly innervated by nerves that have important roles in signaling bone pain, regulating bone homeostasis and remodeling, and controlling bone vasculature and hematopoiesis.1–3 Unfortunately, a detailed knowledge of how nerves influence bone function is still lacking, and this has contributed to the significant challenges that clinicians face in successfully managing bone disease. A key problem is that experimental access to small nerve bundles within bone is challenging, because they are encased deep within a hard outer shell of compact bone. This makes them very difficult to visualize in their entirety and very difficult to follow through to their axons and nerve terminal endings in the bone.

Our current understanding of the innervation of human bone is derived almost entirely from studies that label nerves sampled from only a few thin sections of bone.4–8 Although this approach provides valuable data, it is difficult to appreciate and comprehensively analyze dispersed neuronal projections due to the loss or distortion of important structural information when reconstructing 3D images from multiple sections.9,10 Furthermore, 3D reconstruction from serial sections is difficult and can cause inaccuracies in the images produced due to the variability of staining between sections and the introduction of cutting artifacts. While there have been some attempts to use tissue clearing and light-sheet microscopy to study nerves in bone, most have been animal studies that have labeled only the supporting cells around nerve bundles or have not identified specific subpopulations of nerves in bone.11–13 We have recently developed a modified version of the immunolabeling-enabled imaging of solvent-cleared organs (iDISCO+) protocol to examine the innervation of bone in 3D14. The protocol was optimized for hard tissue in mice, because they have small bones that are cleared and imaged relatively easily. Using this approach, we were able to label nerves to visualize distinct subpopulations of peripheral sensory neurons and follow their projections through full 3D volumes of whole, intact murine bone, and applied filament tracing to reconstruct and quantify the labeling of individual axons and/or the full extent of innervation in a bone. In the present study, we have applied a modified approach to this protocol to visualize nerves in human bone.

The aim of the present study was to visualize and explore the full extent of innervation and the neuroanatomical distribution of nerves in whole, intact, and/or fragments of human bone. We focused on nerves that innervate small bones around osteoarthritic joints of the human hand because they are easily sourced from patients undergoing bone excision for the treatment of advanced osteoarthritis (a routine procedure in which a portion of, or all of the affected bone is removed), and because they are small enough to be processed and imaged using tissue clearing and light-sheet microscopy.

Sample retrieval

Pisiforms were removed en bloc with a flexor carpi ulnaris splitting incision directly over the bone. The proximal phalangeal head was removed as part of a proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) replacement. A dorsal approach was used to expose the PIPJ. The phalangeal head was osteotomized transversely at the neck level. The trapeziums were removed en bloc using a volar radial incision.

Specimen preparation

Samples were immediately immersed in buffered 4% paraformaldehyde and transported from the hospital to the laboratory on ice. They were immersion fixed in the same fixative for at least 24 h at 4 °C and were then washed 3 times in 0.1 M PBS for 30 min. The pisiform and head of the proximal phalanx were processed as they were. The trapezium was subdissected into smaller segments first using a necropsy saw, then with a scalpel following complete decalcification. The initial cut with the necropsy saw, prior to decalcification, improved the decalcification process. The subsequent subdissection with a scalpel enabled finer sectioning of the sample, in order to: (1) reduce sample thickness and (2) improve light-sheet laser access to the region of interest (ROI).

Decalcification

Samples were immersed in Morse’s solution (22.5% formic acid, 10% tri-sodium citrate in Milli-Q H2O) for 14 d with agitation at room temperature. The Morse’s solution was replaced with fresh solution every 2 d. After decalcification, the samples were washed 6 times (15 min per wash) in Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS, Gibco) and stored for up to 2 d at 4 °C before proceeding with depigmentation.

Depigmentation

Following decalcification, the samples were dehydrated by immersion in 50%, 80%, and 100% methanol in DPBS, for 1.5 h per step while on rotation at room temperature. They were incubated in 6% hydrogen peroxide in methanol at 4 °C overnight (while protected from light) and then rehydrated through a reverse sequence of methanol in DPBS, 100%, 100%, 80%, and 50%, and then washed once in DPBS, for 1.5 h per step while on rotation at room temperature.

Immunolabeling

Following depigmentation, the samples were incubated in a blocking solution containing 0.2% gelatine, 0.5% Triton X-100%, and 0.01% thimerosal in DPBS for 48 h while on rotation at room temperature. Nerves were immunolabeled using an antibody raised against the pan-neuronal marker protein gene product 9.5 (PGP9.5) (Table 2). The antibody was diluted in DPBSG-T. Samples were incubated in the primary antibody solution for 14 d with agitation at 37 °C, washed 6 times (1 h each wash) in DPBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 (DPBST) on rotation at room temperature, incubated in the secondary antibody (Table 2) solution for 7 d with agitation at 37 °C, and washed 6 times (1 h each wash) in DPBST on rotation at room temperature. From the secondary antibody incubation step onward, samples were protected from light either by covering the vials in foil or storing them in an opaque container. On completion of immunolabeling, the samples were transferred to 20 mL glass vials with a silicon-coated cap for delipidation and clearing.

Optical clearing

All the remaining steps were performed on rotation at room temperature. Samples were dehydrated in gradually increasing concentrations (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%, and 100%) of methanol in DPBS for 1 h per step, and then incubated overnight in a mix of 2/3 dichloromethane with 1/3 methanol. The next day, they were immersed in dichloromethane for 30 min. If they did not sink to the bottom of the vial by the end of the 30 min incubation period, the dichloromethane was replaced with fresh solvent, and the 30 min incubation was repeated until the sample had sunk. To optically clear the bone samples, they were immersed in dibenzyl ether for 2 h. Cleared bone samples were stored in fresh dibenzyl ether at room temperature. At least 3 h prior to imaging, they were transferred to ethyl cinnamate.

Antibody specificity and characterization

The anti-PGP9.5 monoclonal antibody (catalog number ab108986, RRID: AB_10891773, Abcam) was raised in rabbit against PGP9.5 (proprietary immunogen). This antibody shows no cross-reactivity, except with rat, human, and mouse PGP9.5. Specificity was confirmed with a PGP9.5 (gene name uchl1) knockout cell line validation via Western blot (manufacturer’s information). This anti-PGP9.5 antibody has been shown to immunolabel nerves using clearing protocols applied to murine tissue.15 The antibody immunolabels human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived sensory neurons.16

Light-sheet fluorescence microscopy

Cleared bone samples were imaged in ethyl cinnamate using a light-sheet fluorescent microscope (Ultramicroscope Blaze, Miltenyi Biotec) with an Andor Zyla sCMOS camera (Andor, 2048 × 2048 pixel size). For low-power imaging of entire bone samples, a 1.1× objective was used (1.1× magnification, 0.1 numerical aperture, MI PLAN, Miltenyi Biotec). For higher-power imaging of specific regions, the 4× (4× magnification, 0.35 numerical aperture, MI PLAN, Miltenyi Biotec) and 12× objectives (12× magnification, 0.53 numerical aperture, MI PLAN, Miltenyi Biotec) were used. Samples were securely mounted onto the sample holder using a clamp-style plastic holder (Miltenyi Biotec). A 640-nm diode laser paired with a 680/30 emission filter was used to visualize the fluorescence of the Alexa Fluor 647 fluorophore. A 561-nm diode laser paired with a 620/60 emission filter was used to visualize autofluorescence.

Samples were illuminated with 3 combined light sheets from a single side with a final light-sheet thickness of 3.8 μm at the light-sheet waist. To image large bone samples completely, the samples were imaged from the left and the right separately with left- and right-side illumination, respectively. Optical z-stacks were generated with a step size of 10 μm for low magnification (1.1×) and 2 μm at higher magnification (4× and 12×), with 50 ms exposure per step. All imaging was performed with multiple overlapping (10%) tile regions to encompass either the entire bone sample at 1.1× magnification, or the entire ROI at 4× or 12× magnification. Image capture was performed using Imspector Pro Software (version 7.6.3, Miltenyi Biotec).

Nerve labeling, 3D visualization and analysis

The light-sheet-microscope-derived individual tagged image file (.tif) images were converted to the Imaris file format (.ims) using the Imaris File Converter (version 9.6.2, Bitplane). Tile images were fused using the Imaris Stitcher (version 10.2.0, Bitplane). Nerves, axons, and their terminals were defined by the fluorescent labeling of fine-diameter profiles that had a continuous path through multiple optical sections in the image stack, and were distinguished from autofluorescence through 2 means; comparing immunolabeling with images acquired using a wavelength of light that had no corresponding fluorophores in the same dataset and comparing them to pieces of trapezium processed without the primary antibody (primary antibody omission). Three-dimensional visualization, segmentation, and filament tracing were performed using Imaris (version 10.2.0, Bitplane). For some samples, segmentation of the bone was undertaken using the Imaris Surfaces function, manually delineating the bone in a cross-sectional, 2D view at regular intervals (approximately 20-50 μm apart). This segmentation was used to isolate the interior of the bone for better visualization of the innervation within.14 Tracing of immunolabeled axons was performed using the Imaris Filaments function. Axons were manually traced using the “Autopath (no loops)” algorithm. The dataset captured at 4× magnification was suitable for automatic tracing using the Automatic Filament Tracer function in Imaris. This method used a combination of thresholding of the image based on the size of the filament and its intensity, combined with 5 iterations of machine learning. This automatic method successfully traced large axon bundles, but failed to follow individual fine axons due to varicose labeling. It also did not work with datasets captured at low (1.1×) magnification due to the difficulty the program had in differentiating nerves from the autofluorescence of trabecular bone and heme. To create a representation of the osteochondral channels, the automatic Imaris Surfaces function was used. Smoothing was set to 10 μm. Background thresholding was set to 20 μm. Five iterations of machine learning were applied until segmentation correctly identified the autofluorescence of the osteochondral channels.

Figure production

Images were digitally colorized, and adjustments were made to the contrast (gamma set to 1.5) and brightness to best visualize the immunolabeling of nerves and autofluorescent features (Adobe Photoshop, Adobe Systems). Figures were generated using Adobe InDesign (Adobe Systems). Media were edited using Adobe Premiere Pro (Adobe Systems).

Results

Innervation of a whole, intact human bone: the pisiform

The pisiform of the left hand was excised en bloc from a patient as a treatment for chronic pain due to osteoarthritis of the pisiform-triquetral joint (PTJ). The pisiform is a small carpal bone on the palmar aspect of the human wrist (Figure 1A).17 It is the only carpal bone to possess a single articular surface; the PTJ is formed between the concave dorsal aspect of the pisiform and the palmar side of the triquetral. The palmar aspect of the pisiform is dome-shaped and rough, onto which multiple tendons and ligaments insert.17

Immunolabeling, tissue clearing, and light-sheet microscopy revealed extensive innervation of the pisiform (Figure 1B, Media 1 https://figshare.com/s/0a7adf80fab516a924cb). The innervation of the pisiform originated from multiple small nerve entry points located around its margins. Many of these entry points were derived from a small branch of the ulnar nerve that projected within a bony groove along the radial edge of the pisiform (Figure 1C). This nerve provided a number of branches that innervated the external surface of the pisiform (Figure 1C), or projected through small nutrient foramina, following blood vessels into the pisiform (Figure 1F). A surface render was applied to the internal surface of the cortical bone (Figure 1D), and was used to digitally remove the external surface and cortical bone, revealing the pattern of innervation deep inside the pisiform. At low magnification, immunolabeled nerves were difficult to discern from the autofluorescence produced by heme in blood vessels (Figure 1E), but at higher power, small nerves bundles, axons, and axon terminals were easy to identify throughout (Figure 1F-J). These small nerve bundles often followed blood vessels (Figure 1G) and branched extensively into fine axons and nerve terminals deep within the trabecular bone (Figure 1F, H, and I). Most axons ended as terminals with a simple morphology (Figure 1H and I). No specialized or encapsulated nerve terminal endings were identified in the pisiform. There was sparse innervation of the subchondral bone deep to the PTJ (Figure 1J shows a rare example of innervation). There was no degradation of articular cartilage or exposed subchondral bone on the dorsal aspect of the pisiform in this sample (Figure 1J).

Innervation of a fragment of a long bone: the proximal phalangeal head

The proximal phalangeal head of the fourth phalanx of the left hand was obtained from a patient undergoing joint arthroplasty of the proximal interphalangeal joint (Figure 2A). The head was separated from the proximal phalanx with a transverse cut during surgery. After immunolabeling, clearing, and light-sheet microscopy (Figure 2B), labeled nerves were easily identified throughout the entire phalangeal head (Figure 2C, Media 2 https://figshare.com/s/01cda5aa09703da711a4). Low-power imaging revealed that the bulk of the innervation of the proximal phalangeal head originated from several large perivascular nerve bundles in the center of the bone (Figure 2D). These typically arose from the diaphysis and projected towards the head of the phalanx. Some nerve bundles in the dorsal portion of the phalangeal head arose independently of large or obvious vasculature (Figure 2E). Higher power imaging revealed extensive branching of fine axons and nerve terminal endings throughout the trabecular bone (Figure 2F-H). Most of the axons ended as simple nerve endings (Figure 2F-H). No nerve entry points were identified on the epiphysis of the bone; all of the innervation projected into the proximal phalangeal head from its diaphysis.

Consistent with the presence of osteoarthritis, the joint surface of this sample had regions of exposed subchondral bone where the articular cartilage had degenerated. Interestingly, in areas where articular cartilage was intact, there was very little, if any innervation of the subchondral bone deep to the cartilage (Figure 2I and K). In contrast, in areas where articular cartilage had degenerated, there was a very dense innervation of the subchondral bone (Figure 2J and L). In the latter, axons branched extensively and projected into osteochondral channels that approached the exposed joint surface (Figure 2L). There was less than 50 μm of calcified cartilage between the osteochondral channels and the joint surface, and nerves were often observed projecting all the way to the ends of the channels (Figure 2L).

Innervation of a larger bone: the trapezium

The trapezium is a carpal bone with multiple joint facets, including those for articulation with the first metacarpal bone, the scaphoid, the trapezoid, and a small portion of the second metacarpal.18 In this study, the trapezium of the right hand was excised en bloc from a patient with osteoarthritis of the trapeziometacarpal joint (TMC) (Figure 3A). This was the largest of the human hand bones we sampled. To facilitate both antibody penetration and light propagation to deep structures, the bone was cut into smaller segments before processing. Each of these smaller pieces of the trapezium was independently immunolabeled, cleared, and imaged, to reveal the full extent of innervation in the trapezium (Figure 3B). Here, we present data from only the most radial segment of the dorsal half of the trapezium.

There was extensive innervation of the soft tissue on the external surface of the trapezium (Figure 3C). A surface render was applied to the internal surface of the cortical bone (Figure 3D) and was used to digitally remove the external surface and cortical bone, revealing the pattern of innervation deep inside the trapezium (Figure 3E, Media 3 https://figshare.com/s/24736b7d2fa5bfa7681c). This revealed an extensive innervation within the trabecular bone (Figure 3E). Nerves entered the trapezium through multiple foramina distributed throughout the cortical bone, some followed obvious blood vessels (Figure 3F) and some did not (Figure 3G). In contrast to the pisiform, large nerve bundles were observed deep in the trabecular bone of the trapezium. They often followed blood vessels for long distances before branching into smaller nerves, axons, and nerve terminal endings (Figure 3H-K). Most axons ended in terminals with a simple morphology (Figure 3I-K). No specialized or encapsulated nerve endings were identified.

Innervation of osteochondral channels in the osteoarthritic trapezium

To better visualize the innervation of the TMC joint facet, a trapezium was retrieved from the left hand of a patient with osteoarthritis and was bisected along the coronal midline with no further subdissection (Figure 4A and B). The sample was immunolabeled, cleared, and imaged to reveal the full extent of innervation in the subchondral bone deep to the TMC joint (Figure 4B, Media 4 https://figshare.com/s/fc6ce3fd5c765eeabd6d). Almost the whole TMC joint facet lacked articular cartilage (Figure 4C). This left most of the subchondral bone of the TMC joint exposed. There was an extremely dense innervation of the subchondral bone where the articular cartilage had degenerated (Figure 4C). Multiple large nerve bundles projected from the deeper trabecular bone and formed dense clusters of axons and nerve terminal endings that extended all the way to the joint surface through many osteochondral channels (Figure 4D and E). The osteochondral channels projected through subchondral bone to within 200 μm of the surface, and nerves projected almost all the way to the ends of the osteochondral channels, placing them in immediate proximity to the exposed joint cavity (Figure 4D and E).

3D filament tracing of nerves in the human trapezium

The 3D light-sheet datasets we have produced contain a wealth of information regarding the entry and distribution of nerves throughout human hand bones. Entire segments of a human trapezium could be imaged at low magnification (Figure 5A). However, it is difficult to appreciate the detailed neuroanatomical distribution of nerves at low power or to quantify nerve and/or axon length, the number of branch points, or endings, because background fluorescence and variations in the intensity of labeling in different parts of the bone partly obscure the 3D representation of the dataset. Using filament tracing through the original image stacks allows us to better represent and quantify the nerves in these 3D datasets and can be used to generate 3D models of the innervation of bone. Figure 5B shows that filament tracing provides a detailed representation of the immunolabeling presented in Figure 5A. It captures both small and large nerve bundles around blood vessels and some of the finer nerves, axons, and nerve terminal endings throughout the trapezium and approaching the TMC and triscaphoid joint facets (Figure 5B). Importantly, it also highlights detail of the innervation that are not captured well in the 3D reconstruction of immunolabeling presented in Figure 5A. Filament tracing was also applied to a higher (4×) power ROI of the subchondral bone at the TMC joint (Figure 5C). At 4× power, the Automatic Filament Tracer was able to trace nerve bundles with only 5 iterations of machine learning (Imaris, version 10.2.0, Bitplane). Manual filament tracing was then applied to trace the single axons and check for any tracing errors that may have resulted. Using this method applied to higher powered images, fine axons innervating the subchondral bone were traced in significant detail all the way to their fine nerve terminal endings within the trabecular bone and approaching the TMC joint surface (Figure 5D). At even higher magnification, individual axons and endings could be traced manually all the way into the osteochondral channels at the surface of the TMC joint (Figure 5E-G, Media 4 https://figshare.com/s/fc6ce3fd5c765eeabd6d). Surface rendering of the osteochondral channels in this area reveals the complexity of the organization of the osteochondral channels, and the full extent of innervation associated with them (Figure 5G). Both the osteochondral channels and the nerve endings within this region are <200 μm from the surface of the subchondral bone (Figure 5E-G).

Discussion

In this study, we have successfully applied a modified version of the iDISCO+ tissue clearing and light-sheet microscopy protocol14 to immunolabel nerve fibers within whole, intact, and/or fragments of small human hand bones, including the pisiform, the proximal head of the phalanx, and the trapezium. We were able to show the full extent of innervation of these bones in 3D, including the overall distribution of nerves within each bone, and the way they terminated within the trabecular and subchondral bone. There was a greater density of innervation in areas of subchondral bone where articular cartilage had degraded due to osteoarthritis, and at this location, the nerve endings projected through osteochondral channels towards the joint surface.

Morphology and distribution of nerves in small bones of the human hand

Nerves, axons, and nerve terminal endings were identified in all hand bones sampled. Nerve bundles generally followed blood vessels into each bone via nutrient foramina, branching extensively within trabecular bone and marrow cavity spaces. Many axons continued to follow blood vessels, and had a corkscrew morphology typically associated with sympathetic innervation of the vasculature.1,5,19,20 Other axons terminated as free nerve endings, either near articular surfaces, or deep within the trabecular bone or marrow cavities. This morphology is typically associated with sensory neurons, which in bone are mostly confined to nociceptors.2,14,20,21 No encapsulated endings were observed around axon terminals inside bone. Axons also projected towards articular surfaces through osteochondral channels in the subchondral bone, the greatest density of which occurred at articular surfaces where cartilage had degraded. The findings are broadly consistent with our recent findings of the 3D distribution of nerves murine bone,14 and indeed previous reports of nerves labeled in thin sections of decalcified human bone.4–8 However, it is important to note that all of the human studies cited here reported labeling of nerves in isolated sections of bone, and so the full extent of innervation is not possible to appreciate. In addition, while nerve profiles have been identified in different bony compartments,7 or in close apposition to osteocytes, vasculature, and adipocytes,5,6 it is impossible to determine from thin sections if they are passing axons or nerve terminal endings, or what the morphology of the endings are. Only a single study has attempted to use tissue clearing and light-sheet microscopy to show the 3D arrangement of nerves in human bone.22 The study showed cholinergic innervation of a small plug of bone sampled by trephination at the articular surface of the femur at the knee joint of a human undergoing joint arthroplasty for osteoarthritis. Although Courties et al.22 demonstrated the immunolabeling of nerves in a substantial volume of human bone, the study was limited to healthy regions of the articular surface, and there was no detail included about which part of the knee it was derived from. In the present study, we have been able to explore in greater detail the full extent of innervation, including nerve entry points and distribution, in a whole, intact bone, and substantial fragments of a number of different bones and joint surfaces, some of which had degeneration of cartilage and some which did not.

While we did note features common to all of the bones we sampled, our findings also suggested that there are clear differences in the way nerves enter into and are distributed within each of the small bones of the hand we sampled. For example, the pisiform was innervated by many small branches of a nerve that wrapped around the radial edge of the bone within a distinct bony groove. The innervation of the phalangeal head originated from nerves that entered the bone at the diaphysis, rather than the epiphysis. In the trapezium, many small nerve entry points were identified across the dorsal and volar aspects of the bone. A clear understanding of the location of nerve entry points may be useful to inform a targeted approach to the surgical management of bone pain, eg, by denervation,23 local anesthetic, or steroid injections. It will be interesting in the future to explore the differences in the ways nerves enter and travel through different bones, as this will likely impact on the development of approaches to target them for therapeutic benefit. It will also be important to determine if different subpopulations of nerves in bone have a differential distribution that may help tell us more about their function.

Osteoarthritis

There is some evidence of neurovascular invasion into osteochondral channels or sprouting of nerves in the subchondral bone in humans8,24,25 and in animal models of osteoarthritis,26–28 but because these studies have been limited to sampling only a few thin sections of bone, a detailed exploration of the full extent of innervation has not been possible. In the present study, we have shown that there is an extensive innervation of the subchondral bone around osteoarthritic joint surfaces of the small bones of the human hand. Axons projected towards articular surfaces through many osteochondral channels in the subchondral bone, the greatest density of which occurred at articular surfaces where cartilage had degraded (eg, at the articular surface of the proximal phalangeal head). Articular surfaces where the cartilage was intact (eg, at the articular surface of the same proximal phalangeal head) had fewer osteochondral channels, and a distinct lack of nerves within the osteochondral channels, suggesting that osteoarthritis results in sprouting or hyperinnervation of nerves in subchondral bone around osteoarthritis joints. While our findings are consistent with reports that there is an increase in the number of osteochondral channels that contain nerves in osteoarthritis patients, we have clearly also shown that the full extent of innervation of the osteoarthritis joint is much more significant than might have been appreciated from past studies. Our findings also provide the first evidence that sprouting or hyperinnervation might be occurring specifically in areas where there is greater degeneration of the cartilage in humans. In many cases, the nerve terminals we reported in the present study were less than 200 μm from the surface of the bone where the cartilage had eroded and so were in very close proximity to the synovial fluid in the osteoarthritis joint cavity. Inflammatory mediators and other known algesic substances are elevated in the synovial fluid in osteoarthritis,29,30 and are known to activate and/or sensitize nerve terminal endings of nociceptors to produce pain.29–32 Thus, it is likely that an increased innervation at the joint surface is likely to contribute to osteoarthritis pain through greater access to the inflamed joint. Interestingly, the pisiform we sampled did not have any articular cartilage degradation, and there was only a sparse innervation of the subchondral bone deep to the articular facet of the pisiform. Osteoarthritis of the pisiform is most commonly a result of rheumatism or trauma,33 and does not always involve exposure of subchondral bone,34 further suggesting that cartilage degeneration is important for the sprouting of nerve terminals and pain. It will be important in the future to use the approach presented here to compare bones sampled from osteoarthritis versus control, non- osteoarthritis joints to definitively identify how osteoarthritis affects the structure and distribution of nerves in human bone. Interestingly, one recent study using the same tissue clearing approach we have used here reported a reduction in innervation in the subchondral bone around the knee joint in murine MIA-induced OA.35 It is possible that this difference, compared to the findings of increased innervation in the present study, relates to species differences, the animal model used, or the type of joint studied (eg, load bearing knee joint versus non-load bearing hand joints).

Technical considerations

This study utilized protocols that were previously designed for murine bones,14 and optimized them for much larger tissues such as the human pisiform, phalangeal head, and trapezium. A number of important considerations and modifications were applied to optimize the visualization of nerves for human bone in this study. Extra time was allowed for decalcification, blocking, and antibody penetration due to the greater volume of tissue. The size of the tissue that can be imaged is also limited by the optics of the light-sheet microscope; light passing through a cleared sample is subject to absorption and scattering to the extent that after a few millimeters of tissue penetration by the light, fine details become difficult to resolve. The distance of viable illumination therefore varies between tissues of different sizes, because it is influenced by the amount of tissue light must travel through from the laser to the fluorophore, and from the fluorophore to the detector. In the present study, we reported excellent labeling of the whole, intact pisiform because it is relatively small in size. But to achieve the same quality of labeling in larger bones like the phalanges, or even larger and thicker bones like the trapezium, we had to image a fragment of the bone in isolation, or we had to cut the bone into smaller segments to process and image. Importantly, the approach to sample preparation was modified depending on the question we needed to answer. For example, one trapezium was cut into eight approximately equal-sized pieces to provide complete visualization of nerves deep within the bone, and another was bisected along the coronal midline, presenting the subchondral bone of the TMC joint for optimal imaging.

Tracing nerves in 3D is one of the major advantages afforded by tissue clearing, and in this study, we demonstrated the feasibility of tracing nerve filaments in tissue-cleared fragments of human trapezium. Not only can this provide important neuroanatomical information, but nerve density and complexity can be quantified from these data, enabling the comparison of innervation to different regions and between samples. The approach to nerve tracing we used for low power datasets is almost entirely manual. The background fluorescence of structures such as trabecular bone, BM adipocytes, and heme in blood vessels confounds more automated approaches, including those purported to be enhanced with machine learning and artificial intelligence.36 In human tissue, such automated approaches work only for larger nerve bundles, for which the signal-to-noise ratio is sufficient for automatic identification. Our findings show that nerve tracing can be applied to human bone, but the goal of the tracing must be considered when designing experiments to ensure that this labor-intensive method is applied most efficiently.

Summary

This is the first detailed 3D visualization of the neuroanatomical distribution of nerves in whole, intact and/or large fragments of human bone. It highlights an extensive innervation of the small bones of the human hand. The approach to tissue clearing and light-sheet microscopy we have reported will be important to inform the rational design of approaches to the management of a variety of skeletal pathologies, including pain management for advanced osteoarthritis of the small bones of the hand.
