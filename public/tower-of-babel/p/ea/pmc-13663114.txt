Malignant melanoma identified within a pulmonary sequestration- a case report
Authors: Tomislav Bečejac, Mihovil Penavić, Marko Vrančić, Ivan Bunjevac, Ana Mataić, Andrej Šitum, Ivan Martinez, Marko Popović, Luka Simetić, Dubravko Jalšovec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary sequestration (PS) is a congenital lung malformation, which is defined by an anomalous arterial supply from systemic circulation and an absence of communication with the tracheobronchial tree. It is further divided into intralobar and extralobar PS. Pulmonary sequestrations are usually treated surgically, especially if they are symptomatic. Malignant tumors are rarely found within such malformations, mainly adenocarcinomas. We report a case of a malignant melanoma found in a patient operated for intralobar pulmonary sequestration, which is, to the best of our knowledge, the first reported case of a malignant melanoma occurring within a pulmonary sequestration.Case presentationWe present a case of an 84-year-old male patient who underwent surgical treatment for a symptomatic pulmonary sequestration. The patient was first diagnosed with an intralobar PS two years prior to the surgery by a multislice computed tomography (MSCT) scan. It was located in the left lower lung lobe and had an anomalous arterial supply from an artery arising from the thoracic aorta. Two years later, the patient presented twice to the emergency department with hemoptysis and was thereafter referred to the thoracic surgeon. Another MSCT scan conducted before the surgery showed a newly formed solid lesion within the previously described PS. The thoracic surgeon therefore performed a left lower lobectomy and lymphadenectomy via thoracotomy. Histopathological analysis discovered two melanomas in the specimen, measuring 6 × 5 × 4 cm and 1.7 × 1.7 × 1.5 cm. The patient was referred to an oncologist. A positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scan, along with other diagnostic procedures, didn’t show any other signs of malignant disease elsewhere in the body. Several other skin lesions were excised and biopsied, but none have turned out to be melanomas. A year after surgery, the disease disseminated.ConclusionsAlthough the origin of the melanoma in this case could not be definitively established, this report demonstrates that pulmonary sequestration may harbor malignant neoplasm and highlights the diagnostic challenges in distinguishing primary pulmonary melanoma from metastatic melanoma. Background

Pulmonary sequestration (PS) is a congenital pulmonary malformation characterized by nonfunctional lung parenchyma that lacks communication with the tracheobronchial tree and receives its arterial blood supply from the systemic circulation, most commonly from the lower thoracic or upper abdominal aorta [1]. Pulmonary sequestration is classified into two types: intralobar sequestration (IS, approximately 75–90%) and extralobar sequestration (ES, approximately 10–25%) [2, 3].

Clinical presentation depends on the type, size, and anatomical localization of the sequestrum. It is typically characterized by recurrent pulmonary infections, along with fever, chronic cough, and, occasionally, hemoptysis or chest pain, although it may be asymptomatic [2]. Diagnosis evaluation includes chest radiography, multislice computed tomography (MSCT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasonography in the prenatal period [3, 4]. Modern MSCT angiography is often sufficient for a definitive preoperative diagnosis of pulmonary sequestration by demonstrating the anomalous systemic arterial blood supply, and the diagnosis is confirmed by histopathological examination following surgical resection. Surgical resection, most commonly a lobectomy or a smaller pulmonary resection for intralobar PS, is the treatment of choice, especially for symptomatic patients [2]. Neoplasms are rarely found in pulmonary sequestrations, especially other than lung adenocarcinomas [5–11].

Case presentation

We present a case of an 84-year-old male patient with hemoptysis and a left lower lung lobe infiltration. Medical history included chronic obstructive lung disease (COPD), bronchiectasis, pertussis in childhood, frequent episodes of bronchitis and pneumonia, and chronic atrial fibrillation. Nine years prior, the patient had a neck skin lesion removed, but the histopathological findings were unknown.

The patient was first diagnosed with an intralobar pulmonary sequestration two years before the occurrence of hemoptysis. An MSCT scan revealed a chronic inflammatory consolidation of the posterior segment of a left lower lung lobe with multiple areas of destruction and calcification. A 7 mm artery arising from the descending thoracic aorta supplied the aforementioned lung region, a finding that is consistent with an intralobar pulmonary sequestration. A left lower pulmonary vein was normally present. No lung lesions or enlarged hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes were detected. A fiberoptic bronchoscopy showed no tumor of the tracheobronchial tree, and the brush cytological analysis was normal.

Two years later, the patient was admitted to the emergency department twice in a period of two months because of hemoptysis. Initial chest X-ray showed a consolidation of the left lower lung lobe with normal laboratory tests, except for coagulation values due to warfarin therapy. Regarding the diagnosis of intralobar sequestration combined with hemoptysis and numerous episodes of pneumonia and bronchitis, the patient was examined by a thoracic surgeon who indicated an operative treatment.

A preoperative MSCT scan was consistent with the findings two years prior, regarding the existence of the PS. It showed a large consolidation of the left lower lung lobe, with features of an intralobar sequestration, measuring 105 × 85 × 75 mm, and with multiple calcifications and zones of destruction. The arterial supply of the PS originated from the thoracic aorta, and venous drainage was conducted by the left lower pulmonary vein. However, this MSCT scan revealed a new solid lesion in the lateral portion of the aforementioned PS, measuring 38 × 32 mm, which was not present on the prior MSCT (Fig. 1). There was still no hilar, mediastinal, or axillary lymphadenopathy.

Chronic inflammatory consolidation within the pulmonary sequestration and a lung tumor visualized on MSCT scan

Left-sided muscle-sparing thoracotomy and decortication were performed along with a left lower lobectomy of the lung and lymphadenectomy. An anomalous artery measuring 7 mm was identified 5 cm proximal to the diaphragm, originating from the descending thoracic aorta and entering the basal part of the left lower lung lobe, which was consolidated. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged from the hospital on the 7th postoperative day. After surgical treatment, the patient no longer had pulmonary symptoms and recovered fully. The histopathological analysis identified two tumors in the resected lobe, measuring 6 × 5 × 4 cm and 1.7 × 1.7 × 1.5 cm, both malignant melanomas (Fig. 2). Vascular and bronchial borders were clean. An in situ component of the tumor was not described.

Representative sections showing typical Hematoxylin and Eosin staining (left) and positive Melan-A immunohistochemical staining (right)

Immunohistochemically, the cells of the larger tumor were positive for S-100 and negative for CK7, CD68, and CD163. The cells of the smaller tumor were positive for PRAME, S-100, HMB-45, and Melan A, and negative for CKAE1/AE3, CD68, CD163, desmin, SMA, CD34, and BRAF (Fig. 3). Although the diagnosis was supported by characteristic morphology and positivity for S100, HMB45, Melan-A and PRAME, additional melanocytic markers such as SOX10 were not available and represent a limitation of this report. Berlin blue staining was negative, and BRAF mutation was not detected. All lymph nodes extracted from the lung and mediastinum were reactive and without malignant elements.

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive expression of S-100 (left) and HMB-45 positivity (right)

Upon diagnosis, the patient was referred to an oncologist and presented at a multidisciplinary tumor board for melanomas, which recommended further work-up. A postoperative positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scan performed 2 months after the operation showed a pathological fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) metabolic activity only in the ascending colon, with a standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 11.2, suggesting malignancy (Fig. 4). No other signs of metabolically active malignant disease were detected. The subsequent colonoscopy revealed a benign polyp with no malignancy.

Postoperative PET/CT scan with pathological finding in the ascending colon (left) and MSCT scan (right)

MRI of the brain revealed no secondarisms. The patient was examined by a dermatologist, who found several suspicious skin lesions. The lesions were subsequently excised, and histopathological analysis revealed basal cell carcinomas (BCC). The ophthalmologist found no ocular pathology. Lactate-dehydrogenase level was 155 U/L (ref. <241) and the S-100 value was < 0.050 µg/L (ref. < 0.100). The patient provided otorhinolaryngology history for characterization of the previously excised neck skin lesion, but it appeared illegible (possibly a BCC or cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma). All the conducted procedures did not identify a primary tumor site. Several other skin lesions were later excised, but none proved to be melanomas. Another PET/CT scan was performed 4 months later. A nonspecific FDG activity measuring SUVmax 2,3 was detected in the area of the right ear. No other signs of malignant disease activity were detected. The dermatologist suggested excision of a skin lesion on the right ear, and a BCC was diagnosed. A year after the surgery, on another PET/CT scan and MRI of the brain performed, cerebral metastasis was detected along with suspicious lesions in the lungs, L3 vertebrae, both axillae and pancreas. The patient developed hemiparesis and was thereafter referred for neurosurgical treatment. Further oncological treatment is scheduled. The timeline of events is graphically summarized in Fig. 5.

Discussion

Malignant melanoma (MM) is an aggressive skin malignancy that can spread throughout the entire body by direct extension, lymphatic dissemination, or hematogenous spread. The most common locations for malignant melanoma metastases are the skin and subcutaneous tissue, the lungs, the liver, and the central nervous system [12]. Exceptionally rare cases of primary malignant melanoma of the lungs (PMML) have been reported, which account for less than 0.01% of all primary lung tumors, although MM in the lungs occurs much more often as a metastasis originating from another localization [13].

The origin of primary malignant melanoma of the lung is controversial. The hypothesis is that melanocytes exist throughout the whole body as cells of a dispersed neuroendocrine system; they migrate there during embryogenesis, or they derive from melanoblasts and have the same origin as other melanoblasts located in the trachea, esophagus, and pharynx. Another explanation is that the tumor cells originate from pluripotent stem cells [13].

In order to distinguish PMML from metastases, criteria for PMML should include no prior diagnosis of malignant melanoma or mucosal, skin, or eye surgery, no concurrent melanoma in other parts of the body, a solitary lung lesion, histological diagnosis of melanoma confirmed by immunohistochemical staining for S-100 and HMB‐45, and melanocytic invasion of the bronchial epithelium [13, 14].

In this case report, a symptomatic intralobar PS was diagnosed with no prior knowledge of the presence of malignant melanoma. After a successful surgical treatment, a histopathological finding surprisingly revealed two tumors in the PS diagnosed as malignant melanomas.

The first problem concerns the difficulty in properly diagnosing a tumor in a pulmonary sequestration, which can resemble inflammatory consolidation with destruction and calcification.

The second problem raises a question whether these lung tumors were in fact PMMLs or metastases originating from an undetermined primary localization outside the lungs.

Initial preoperative MSCT scan revealed no lymphadenopathy; dissected hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes all came negative for malignancy. Further work-up scheduled by an oncologist did not reveal any other sites of MM. This is all consistent with the diagnosis of PMML rather than metastatic MM. On the other hand, our case does not fulfill several commonly accepted criteria for primary pulmonary malignant melanoma. The patient had a history of prior skin surgery of unknown histology; two melanoma lesions were present within the resected specimen rather than a solitary lesion; and no melanocytic invasion of the bronchial epithelium or an in situ component was present. This all suggests that a diagnosis of primary pulmonary melanoma cannot be established with certainty. The concern that remains is the neck lesion removed nine years prior, for which the histopathological diagnosis could not be established. An additional consideration is the phenomenon of tumor dormancy. Melanoma is well known for its recurrence many years or even decades after the treatment of the primary lesion. Therefore, although the neck lesion excised nine years earlier could not be histologically verified, a late metastatic recurrence originating from a previously treated cutaneous melanoma cannot be excluded. Tumor dormancy in melanomas has been well described and may serve as a potential explanation for the presented case if the neck lesion had indeed been a malignant melanoma.

Another entity that could be discussed in this case is a malignant melanoma of the unknown primary site (MUP). It is an MM in which only a metastasis is found, with no primary tumor. It could be explained by primary tumor regression or immunological mechanisms. Still, our patient cannot be included in this category, as he had a history of excision, electrodesiccation, or cauterization of a mole, which is an exclusion criterion [15].

All things considered, even though a diagnosis of PMML is possible in this patient, we do not have enough evidence to confirm it, even with the fact that no primary tumor outside the lungs was found. Moreover, the presence of two distinct lesions in the resected specimen makes a diagnosis of a secondary melanoma within the lung more likely, along with the fact that the pathologist did not find an in situ component within the specimen.

Nevertheless, what makes this case unique is the localization of MM in the lung sequestration, regardless of whether primary or secondary. There have been several reports of lung carcinoma associated with pulmonary sequestration. Lawal et al. [5] reported a case of adenocarcinoma in PS and identified 8 additional published cases. Belchis et al. [6] reported the first case of an adenocarcinoma in extralobar sequestration in 2014. Several reports of bronchial carcinoids within a PS have also been published in the literature [10, 11]. In 2024, Yarlagadda et al. [7] reported a case of a mucoepidermoid cancer within the pulmonary sequestration, and Ye et al. [8] reported a carcinoid tumorlet in 2013. Mengoli et al. [9] reported a case of a malignant perivascular epithelioid cell (PEComatous) tumor within an intralobar sequestration in 2016, a tumor that expresses melanocytic markers but differs from melanoma itself.

The association between pulmonary sequestration and malignant tumors remains poorly understood. As the existence of pulmonary sequestration is often accompanied by recurrent pulmonary infections, chronic inflammation and irritation may be among the etiological factors contributing to the development of malignancy within it [6]. Chronic inflammation is a well-known promoter of carcinogenesis. In PS, recurrent infections and impaired drainage lead to persistent inflammatory conditions, the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, which can damage DNA and stimulate cellular proliferation, increasing the possibility of malignant transformation. The altered vascular supply may lead to hypoxia within the lung parenchyme, which can then promote angiogenesis, cell survival, and metabolic adaptation, causing tumor development and progression. The microenvironment’s role in the PS is also important, as structural abnormalities, impaired ventilation, and abnormal lymphatic drainage can suppress the immune system and allow damaged or transformed cells to thrive. Also, this specific microenvironment may support tumor cell adhesion, invasion, and growth. If the melanoma in this case represented a primary tumor, chronic inflammation, recurrent infection, altered vascular supply, and local microenvironmental changes within the sequestration may have contributed to tumor development. However, if the lesion was in fact a metastatic melanoma, these same factors may have acted differently by creating a favorable metastatic niche. Chronic inflammatory processes have been associated with pre-metastatic niche formation through the production of cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular matrix remodeling, facilitating the implantation and proliferation of circulating tumor cells. In conclusion, the abnormal microenvironment of pulmonary sequestration could theoretically support either malignant transformation or metastatic implantation. These hypotheses remain speculative and require further investigation. All these factors, chronic inflammation, altered vascular supply, and microenvironment, may promote tumor development, although these conclusions remain hypothetical and further research is needed to better assess the pathogenesis of such cases. These hypotheses should therefore be interpreted cautiously. There is no strong evidence supporting the argument that pulmonary sequestration by itself carries a substantial risk of malignant transformation; thus, there is no rationale for prophylactic resection in asymptomatic and elderly patients.

A literature search was conducted in PubMed, with no time frame restrictions applied, using the following query: (melanoma[Title/Abstract]) AND (((((pulmonary sequestration[Title/Abstract]) OR (bronchopulmonary sequestration[Title/Abstract])) OR (lung sequestration[Title/Abstract])) OR (intralobar sequestration[Title/Abstract])) OR (extralobar sequestration[Title/Abstract])). To the best of our knowledge, no previously reported cases of MM occurring in a lung sequestration have been identified.

The presence of MM within a pulmonary sequestration warrants suspicion of a disseminated malignancy and necessitates further diagnostic procedures, dermatological and oncological examinations, and surveillance.

The main educational message of this case is not the definitive classification of the melanoma as primary or secondary, which remains uncertain, but rather the recognition of the fact that pulmonary sequestration can harbor malignant cells and be the site of clinically significant malignant diseases. The development of a new solid lesion within a previously known pulmonary sequestration should prompt careful evaluation and consideration of surgical resection. Furthermore, this case emphasizes the diagnostic challenges encountered when differentiating primary pulmonary melanoma from metastatic melanoma of the unknown origin.

Conclusions

Lung sequestration is a very rare entity in which respiratory symptoms may occur, and it can be a possible site for primary or secondary malignant lesions. It is crucial to diagnose this condition in order to provide the appropriate therapy and alleviate the symptoms. Surgical resection is even more crucial if a tumor is suspected to exist along with pulmonary sequestration. In elderly patients, decisions regarding surgical treatment should be individualized, balancing the severity of symptoms, radiological suspicion of malignancy, preoperative minimally invasive diagnostics, operative risk, comorbidities, and expected oncological benefit. This case report demonstrates that rare findings of various tumor types within pulmonary sequestrations are indeed possible.
