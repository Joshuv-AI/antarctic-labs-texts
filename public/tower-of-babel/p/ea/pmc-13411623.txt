Gold(III) Semiquinone Complexes: Synthesis, Structure, and Application in Photocatalysis
Authors: Miguel A Gonzálvez, Félix Léon, Vlad Martin‐Diaconescu, Baptiste Martin, Sonia Mallet‐Ladeira, Juan José Gamboa‐Carballo, Karinne Miqueu, György Szalóki, Didier Bourissou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecently, hemilabile ligands have been found to enable two‐electron Au(I)/Au(III) redox cycling. Yet, single‐electron processes and the use of redox‐active ligands in gold chemistry remain largely underexplored. Here, we report the synthesis, isolation, full characterization and first photocatalytic application of Au(III) semiquinone complexes. The latter are easily obtained via one‐electron oxidation of Au(III) catecholate precursors. Experimental and DFT studies establish that oxidation occurs at the O^O ligand while the gold center retains its +3 oxidation state. Owing to their square‐planar geometry and electron‐rich character, Au(III) catecholate complexes readily engage into electron donor–acceptor (EDA) interactions with organic substrates. Upon visible‐light irradiation, these EDA adducts undergo photoinduced single‐electron transfer, enabling the generation of aryl radicals from aryldiazonium salts. This reactivity translates into efficient photocatalytic C–H arylation of heterocycles under mild conditions, with broad substrate scope and high functional group tolerance. Variation of the redox‐active and ancillary ligands provides a simple means to tune the system, enabling catalytic optimization as substantiated with challenging electron‐rich aryldiazonium partners. Mechanistically distinct from established gold photoredox systems, this work delineates a new strategy for standalone Au(III)‐mediated photoredox catalysis and highlights the broader potential of redox‐active ligands in expanding the reactivity landscape of gold complexes.Keywords: electron donor–acceptor, gold, photoredox catalysis, semiquinones, single‐electron transfer

Introduction

The discovery of homogeneous gold catalysis in the late 1980s and its spectacular development since then have been paralleled by major advances in the organometallic chemistry of Au(I) and Au(III) complexes from both structural and reactivity viewpoints [1, 2, 3, 4]. In the past decade, the enabling role of ligands has been recognized and extensively developed, in particular to achieve Au(I)/Au(III) two‐electron redox cycling [5, 6]. However, the study and use of redox‐active ligands and single‐electron processes remain largely underdeveloped in gold chemistry. The groups of Heinze, Sarkar, Poyatos, Peris, Hey‐Hawkins, and Gimeno have made significant contributions nonetheless in the area of redox‐switchable catalysis with Au(I) complexes equipped with ferrocenyl‐containing ligands [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Comparatively, examples of Au(III) complexes featuring redox‐active ligands remain extremely scant [13]. In the 1990s, bis(benzodithiolate) Au(III) complexes were briefly investigated by Bjørnholm in the context of single‐component molecular metals [14, 15]. The structure of the ensuing one‐electron oxidation complexes, initially inferred as Au(IV) species, was revised by Wieghardt et al. in 2003 [16] as a mixed‐valence Au(III) complex with one ortho‐dithiolato dianionic and one dithiobenzosemiquinonate radical anion ligand (Figure 1a). In the mid 2010's, van der Vlugt et al. reported peripheral C‒C coupling at a mixed‐valent Au(I)/Au(III) dinuclear gold complex bridged by a redox‐active P^N^P ligand (Figure 1b) [17, 18]. Over the past few years, we have launched a research program on Au(III) catecholate complexes [19, 20, 21]. We first showed the ability of hemilabile P^N and chelating P^P ligands to enable Au(I) to Au(III) oxidation with ortho‐benzoquinones [22]. We then discovered an original catechol / ortho‐benzoquinone exchange process at Au(III) complexes featuring cyclometalated P^C or chelating P^P ligands (Figure 1c) [23]. DFT calculations supported a mechanism involving the formation of a charge transfer adduct followed by two successive single‐electron transfer events between the catecholate at Au(III) and the incoming ortho‐benzoquinone moiety while gold maintains its +3 oxidation state. Au(III) bis‐semiquinone complexes were identified as key intermediates in this transformation.

Redox chemistry arising from Au(III) complexes equipped with redox‐active ligands: (a–c) Few literature precedents and (d) Au(III) semiquinone complexes investigated here.

To advance further the study and use of redox‐active ligands in Au(III) chemistry, we questioned in this work the accessibility, stability, and electronic structure of Au(III) semiquinone complexes (Figure 1d) [24]. In addition, we envisioned to leverage the square‐planar structure and electron‐rich nature of Au(III) catecholate complexes to generate electron donor–acceptor (EDA) adducts with organic substrates and to induce single‐electron transfer (SET) upon visible‐light irradiation. Ultimately, our objective was to uncover the potential of Au(III) catecholate complexes to generate and harness aryl radicals under photochemical conditions. Our first proof‐of‐concept results along this line are reported hereafter.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Au(III) Semiquinones Complexes

The behavior of the parent (P^C)Au(O^O) complex 1a toward oxidation was first studied by electrochemistry. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) revealed a reversible one‐electron event at E 1/2 0.14 V versus Fc+/Fc (Figure 2a), in the same range as for other metal ortho‐catecholate complexes [25, 26, 27], suggesting ligand‐centered oxidation, ie catecholate ⇋ semiquinone. The reversibility of the process was apparent from the consistent oxidation and reduction currents regardless of the scan rate, while its one‐electron nature was deduced from the Randles–Ševčík equation (peak current against square‐root of the scan rate) using the diffusion coefficient of 1a as determined by 1H DOSY NMR spectroscopy [28].

Both electrolysis and chemical oxidation (with either TEMPO+, BF4 –, or AgBF4) proved suitable for the preparative scale synthesis of the semiquinone complex 1a‐sq. Most convenient is the chemical oxidation with AgBF4 at RT for 5 min, affording 1a‐sq in essentially quantitative yield upon simple filtration. In UV–vis spectroscopy, complex 1a‐sq displays two broad absorption bands at λ max 435 and 660 nm (DMSO solution, Figure S1) [22], consistent with the deep green color appearing upon oxidation of 1a. Complex 1a‐sq was characterized by EPR spectroscopy to confirm and probe its radical nature. The X‐band spectrum recorded in DCM at 193 K showed a well‐resolved triplet‐like signal that could be successfully fitted with EasySpin [29] (Figure 2b). The obtained g iso value of 1.9999 and AH couplings of 8.06 MHz fall in the typical range of semiquinone complexes [30], suggesting ligand rather than metal‐centered oxidation. To demonstrate that 1a‐sq is not a unique and specific case, related complexes featuring Ph or Cy instead of iPr substituents at P (1′a‐sq and 1″a‐sq, respectively) or a nido‐ortho‐carboranyl diphosphine as ancillary ligand (2a‐sq) were prepared. EPR spectroscopy showed patterns and giso/AH values similar to 1a‐sq [28]. The electronic assignment deduced from EPR was corroborated by x‐ray absorption spectroscopy. The XANES region of 1a showed an intense white line centered around 11922.0 eV, consistent with a Au(III) center (Figure 2c) [22, 31]. After one‐electron oxidation of 1a, no shift was observed in energy, indicating that the metal retained its +3 oxidation state and that the oxidation took place at the ligand. Very similar data were obtained for analogous oxidized complexes featuring Cy substituents at P (1″a‐sq) or two methyl groups at the 3 and 6 positions of the redox‐active O^O ligand (1b‐sq). Varying further the substitution pattern (Cy groups at P and 3,5‐tBu2 substitution of the catechol, complex 1′c‐sq), single‐crystal suitable for x‐ray diffraction study could be obtained (Figure 2d) [28, 32].

The complex adopts an ion pair structure with BF4 – as counteranion (shortest F···Au contact 2.914(10) Å) and square‐planar geometry (τ 4 value 0.04) [33]. Referring to the metrical oxidation state introduced by Brown et al. [34], 1’c‐sq can be confidently described as a Au(III) semiquinone complex (with a MOS value of –1.2(1) for 1’c‐sq vs. –2.04(5) for 1a) [28]. Finally, the stability of 1a‐sq in solution over time was assessed by EPR spectroscopy against TEMPO (Figure S7) [28]. Only minimal degradation (< 10%) was observed after 5 days at 3°C and after 23 days, more than 25% of 1a‐sq was still present. The corresponding diphosphine complex 2a‐sq also proved stable, with no sign of degradation detected after 1 day under the same conditions. This remarkable stability bodes well for the use of such Au(III) semi‐quinone complexes in photoredox catalysis.

To gain further insight into the electronic structure of the (P^C)Au(O^O) semiquinone complexes, DFT calculations were performed [28]. The optimized geometry of 1c‐sq matched very well that determined crystallographically for its Cy2P version 1’c‐sq and the MOS values found for the four considered complexes, differing in the substitution pattern of the redox‐active O^O ligand, all fall in the typical range for semiquinone complexes (–1.09 ± 2, Table S2) [28]. The g iso and A H values are also well reproduced computationally (1.999, –11.2/–10.7 MHz for 1a‐sq). The AH couplings are attributed to the Hmeta atoms, in line with the substantial spin density residing on the Cmeta atoms. Overall, the spin density (Figure 2e) is concentrated on the semiquinone fragment, with minimal contribution of the Au center (1.0 %) and none on the ancillary P^C ligand.

Electron Donor–Acceptor Adducts With Au(III) Catecholate Complexes

Our next goal was to substantiate and study the formation of electron donor‐acceptor (EDA) adducts with Au(III) catecholate complexes. We transiently observed such adducts at low temperature by UV–vis spectroscopy in the early stage of catechol / ortho‐benzoquinone exchange at Au(III) [23]. To prevent the exchange process and obtain further insight, we used a para‐benzoquinone as an acceptor.

With the Tetrafluoro‐para‐benzoquinone (TFBQ)

Upon mixing equimolar amounts of (P^C)Au(O^O) 1a and TFBQ in DCM, a green coloration immediately appeared. The UV–vis spectrum showed a characteristic charge transfer band at λ max 785 nm (Figure 3a). Although no significant modification was found by CV at 298 K, upon lowering the temperature to 193 K, a 70 mV anodic shift was observed for the oxidation of 1a (Figure S14) [28], in line with electron‐depletion of the Au(III) catecholate complex, making its oxidation harder. 1H and 19F NMR spectroscopy show small shifts of the resonance signals at 298 K, which become larger at low temperature due to stronger association (Figures S15–S18) [28]. Through‐space interaction between the two components was substantiated by a 1H–19F HOESY NMR experiment (Figure 3b) where correlation peaks were found between the TFBQ acceptor and the Au(III) catecholate complex (the Me groups of the iPr substituents at P, the protons of the catechol and the H atom of the naphthyl backbone in ortho position to Au). Consistently, 1H and 19F DOSY NMR experiments indicated increase in size (decrease of the diffusion coefficient D) of the donor and acceptor components upon mixing, all the more so at low temperature (Figures S22–S27) [28]. The association constant of the electron donor–acceptor system was estimated to be KEDA 24 ± 5 M−1 by 19F NMR titration at room temperature (Figure 3c). Finally, with complex 1’c bearing Cy substituents at P and two tBu groups at the catecholate moiety, single‐crystals suitable for XRD were obtained (Figure 3d) [28]. In the solid state, the Au(III) complex and benzoquinone molecules form π‐π adduct, as apparent from their parallel and close arrangement (with several C⋯C and C⋯O distances in the 2.9–3.2 Å range).

With Aryldiazonium Salts

We then turned to aryldiazonium salts as acceptors, with the idea that EDA adducts may undergo single‐electron transfer upon irradiation and form thereby reactive aryl radicals. Gratifyingly, mixing the Au(III) catecholate complex 1a with the para‐nitrophenyl diazonium salt led to an intense orange‐red coloration and UV–vis spectroscopy showed a low energy absorption band at λ max 505 nm in DMSO (Figure 4a). This charge‐transfer band depends on the accepting properties of the aryldiazonium salts, as observed varying the para substituent (from NO2 to F, H and OMe). The 1:1 stoichiometry of the EDA adduct between 1a and p‐O2NPhN2 + was established thanks to a Job plot experiment (Figure 4b). According to UV–vis titrations, the association constant K EDA reaches ≈ 1500 M−1 at 298 K. When increasing the steric demand of the Au(III) complex, by introducing two tBu substituents at the O^O ligand, the affinity drops by about one order of magnitude (K EDA ≈ 130 M−1 between 1c and p‐O2NPhN2 +), as when reducing the accepting properties of the aryldiazonium salt (K EDA ≈ 76 M−1 between 1a and p‐FPhN2 +) [28]. NMR spectroscopy also showed small upfield shifts upon mixing the Au(III) catecholate complex 1a with aryldiazonium salts. A 19F NMR titration experiment performed with p‐FPhN2 + in DCM led to a commensurable K EDA value, ≈ 40 M−1 [28].

Computational Analyses

To complete the study and shed light into the charge transfer, the association of the parent Au(III) catecholate complex (P^C)Au(O^O)–H4 1a with the para‐fluorophenyldiazonium p‐FPhN2 + was investigated by density functional theory (DFT). Optimizations were carried out at the SMD(DMSO)‐PBE0‐D3(BJ)/SDD+f(Au), 6–31G**(other atoms) level of theory, not taking into account the counter‐anion. Several energy minima slightly differing in the relative orientations of the donor and acceptor components were located on the potential energy surface [28]. They lie close in energy. Only the most stable CT_1 is shown and discussed here (Figure 5a). The optimized structure shows parallel arrangement of the Au(III) catecholate complex and aryl diazonium, with distances between the Au(O^O) and PhN2 + moieties between 2.7 and 3.5 Å. The EDA adduct is weakly stabilized compared to the separate fragments, with an association free energy ΔG of −5.5 kcal·mol−1. Consistent with the charge‐transfer character of CT_1, the HOMO is predominantly localized on the catecholate π‐system of the (P^C)Au(O^O)–H4 donor fragment, whereas the LUMO is mainly centered on the π* orbital of the aryldiazonium acceptor (Figure 5b). Time‐dependent density functional theory (TD‐DFT) calculations reproduce the bathochromic shift observed experimentally, the most intense charge transfer band being found at λ max 548 nm. The natural transition orbitals (NTOs) [28] associated with the corresponding S0 to S2 excitation confirm the charge transfer character of this transition. The electronic structure and bonding features of CT_1 were analyzed thoroughly [28]. Most noticeable is the electron transfer from 1a to p‐FPhN2 + apparent in the electron density difference (EDD) map (Figure 5c). According to Hirshfeld population analysis, about 0.5 e– is transferred from the donor to the acceptor.

We then sought to explore the photochemical behavior of the EDA adducts formed by Au(III) catecholate complexes and aryldiazonium salts with the aim to generate reactive aryl radicals upon photo‐induced single‐electron transfer (PET).

Photoreactivity Induced by Au(III) Catecholate Complexes

Reaction Discovery and Optimization, Scope

The C–H arylation of furans (Scheme 1) reported by Hari et al. in 2012 using Eosin Y as photocatalyst [35] was chosen as proof‐of‐concept transformation. The first tests were performed with furan and para‐nitrophenyl diazonium tetrafluoroborate as substrates. Encouraging results were obtained using 1 mol% of the Au(III) catecholate complex 1a under green LED irradiation (5 W, 530 nm, 410 lm): after 20 min, the conversion reached 65% and the NMR yield of the C2‐arylation product was 48%. As observed with other photocatalysts, the corresponding 2,5‐bisarylated furan was formed as by‐product. The reaction conditions were optimized by varying the amount of furan, the catalytic loading in Au(III) complex, the solvent, the diazonium counter‐anion and testing the addition of a base (Tables S5 and S6) [28]. Optimal results were obtained using 5 equiv of furan, 0.5–1 mol% of 1a in DMSO with the tetrafluoroborate diazonium salt and without base. Under these conditions, quantitative conversion was achieved and the isolated yield reached 84%. Control reactions in the absence of gold resulted in only low amounts of arylation products. Using 10 mol% of catechol and 2.2 eq. of K2CO3, high conversions of the aryl diazonium salt were eventually achieved within 2 h of green light irradiation (42%–99%), but the targeted product was obtained in only low yield (12%–31%) [28].

Then, the scope of the transformation was screened under these optimized conditions. The best results were obtained with aryldiazonium salts bearing electron‐withdrawing groups (NO2, CN, CF3, and CO2Et) with reaction times <1 h and 75%–81% isolated yields in 2‐arylated furan. Halogen substitution (F and Cl, but also Br and I) in para, meta, and ortho positions was well tolerated and led to good results: 0.5–4 h of reaction, 62%–88% isolated yields. As expected, aryldiazonium substrates featuring electron‐donating substituents proved to be more challenging [35, 36, 37], requiring longer reaction times (8–24 h) and leading to lower NMR yields (23%–65%). The heteroarene partner was also varied. The transformation was found to proceed well with 2‐substituted furans as well as benzofuran, thiophene, N‐Boc pyrrole, N‐Me imidazole, pyrazine and pyridine (Scheme 1).

Challenging Substrates, Catalyst Optimization

As mentioned above, electron‐donating substituents make aryldiazoniums poorer acceptors (the charge transfer band upon mixing with 1a is less intense and its bathochromic shift is much smaller than with electron‐deprived diazoniums, Figure 4a) and thus more challenging substrates in such photocatalytic arylation reactions. Here, the structural modularity of the Au(III) complex used as photocatalyst may be advantageous as both the ancillary and redox‐active ligands can be varied to improve the system. To test this idea, different Au(III) catecholate complexes 1a‐c and 2a‐c were tested against a series of challenging aryldiazonium salts p‐RPhN2 +, BF4 – (R = H, Me, tBu, OMe, OH, and NH2) (Scheme 2). Dialkyl substitution of the catecholate moiety makes the Au(III) complex more electron‐rich, as apparent from the cathodic shift of the oxidation wave in CV (Figure S10) [28]. In addition, a broad charge transfer band could be observed by UV–vis spectroscopy upon mixing 1b with p‐MeOPhN2 + (λ max ≈ 475 nm, Figure S33) [28]. Consistently, 1b very much outperformed 1a in the photocatalytic reaction of furan with PhN2 +, enabling to achieve 75% yield in only 25 min (vs. 25% yield after 8 h with 1a). The tBu2‐substituted complex 1c also proved more efficient than 1a, but less than 1b (33% yield after 5 h). Similar results were obtained with the other challenging aryldiazonium salts, 1b affording the corresponding 2‐arylated furans in significantly higher yields (44%–75%) and shorter reaction times (25 min—12 h) than 1a and 1c. Essentially the same trend was found for the Au(III) catecholate complexes 2a‐c featuring the nido‐ortho‐carboranyl diphosphine ancillary ligand: dialkyl‐substitution of the catecholate speeded up the reaction and increased the obtained yield. Of note, the tBu2‐substituted derivative 2c displayed about the same efficiency as the Me2‐substituted one 2b.

The efficiency of the Au(III) catecholate complexes 1a‐c and 2a‐c was compared with Eosin Y under similar conditions. The parent systems 1a and 2a generally require longer reaction times and give lower yields of coupling products, but the electron‐rich systems 1b and 2b tend to outperform Eosin Y. This is particularly true for the reaction of furan with PhN2 + (reaction time reduced from 6 h to 25 min, yield increased from 32% to 75%) and also noticeable with p‐MePhN2 +, p‐MeOPhN2 +, and p‐H2NPhN2 +. 1b, 2b, and Eosin Y give similar results with p‐tBuPhN2 + while for p‐HOPhN2 +, Eosin Y gives slightly better results than 1b and 2b. The Au(III) catecholate complexes show good photostability under green light irradiation. For 1a, only 3% decomposition was observed after 6 h of green light irradiation (12% after 24 h), while 1b and 1c exhibited improved photostability, with only 9% and 3% decomposition after 42 h, respectively (Figures S47, S48) [28].

Thus, Au(III) catecholate complexes appear as efficient and general photocatalysts for the arylation of furans and other heteroarenes with aryldiazonium salts. The reaction tolerates a broad variety of functional groups. It is robust and operates under mild conditions.

Mechanistic Studies

Based on previous studies [35, 36, 37], the catalytic cycle displayed in Figure 6 was envisioned to account for the photocatalytic transformation, given the accessibility and stability of Au(III) semi‐quinone complexes and the ability of Au(III) catecholate complexes to form EDA adducts with aryldiazonium salts. Accordingly, irradiation of the EDA adduct was expected to induce single‐electron transfer (PET) and initiate a radical pathway. The aryl radical formed upon N2 dissociation would then add to furan and the ensuing radical intermediate could then be oxidized either by another aryldiazonium molecule (radical‐chain path) or by the Au(III) semiquinone complex (photoredox path), the arylation product would finally be obtained by proton release. Mechanistic studies were performed to decipher the prevailing path and identify the key factors at play.

Catalytic cycle proposed to account for the photocatalytic direct C–H arylation of furan with Au(III) catecholate complexes showing the radical‐chain and photoredox paths prevailing according to the electronics of the aryldiazonium substrate.

First, to support the involvement of radicals, furan and aryldiazonium salts p‐RPhN2 + (R = NO2, F) were reacted under the same conditions as in Scheme 1 but in the presence of 2 equiv of TEMPO (Scheme 3a). Only trace amounts of furan C–H arylation product formed. Instead, products 4.1‐4.2 resulting from the trapping of the aryl radical were obtained (≈ 70% isolated yield) and unequivocally characterized (by 1H and 13C NMR as well as HRMS). The critical role of the irradiation was then demonstrated by performing control experiments in the dark (Table S7) [28]. The arylation of furan was found to proceed with the electron‐deprived aryldiazonium salts, although way more slowly than under green light irradiation (2 h instead of 18 min to reach complete conversion with p‐O2NPhN2 +, 3.5 h instead of 30 min with p‐NCPhN2 +). With the nonactivated diazonium salts p‐RPhN2 + (R = F, Cl, Me, and MeO), very little, if any, conversion was achieved. With the aim to unravel the main mechanism at play, radical‐chain or photoredox, quantum yield measurements were performed with different aryldiazonium salts. The obtained values pointed out two different regimes (Scheme 3b) [28]. ɸ values well above 1 were found for the p‐NO2 and p‐CN substrates (8.4 and 5.2, respectively) indicating that the radical chain dominates in these cases. For comparison, a ɸ value of 4.7 was reported for the same reaction with Eosin Y as photocatalyst [38]. In contrast, the F, Me, MeO, and tBu p‐substituted phenyldiazoniums led to ɸ values <1 (from 0.78 to 0.04), indicating that the photoredox path prevails for these substrates. Next, light on‐off experiments were carried out (Scheme 3c) [28]. With p‐NCPhN2 +, the reaction slowed down but kept going in the absence of green light irradiation whereas for the nonactivated aryl diazonium salts p‐RPhN2 + (R = F, Cl, and MeO), the reaction completely stopped during the light‐off periods, in line with the absence of thermal background reaction with these substrates. Electron‐withdrawing substituents make aryldiazonium better acceptors and also stronger oxidants. It is likely that the electron‐deprived diazoniums outperform the Au(III) semiquinone to close the catalytic redox loop and thus favor the radical‐chain over the photoredox path. To support this view, the ionization potential / electron affinity of the key species at play were estimated by DFT calculations [28]. The obtained values (Figure 7) showed that the radical formed upon p‐FPh• addition to furan can indeed be oxidized by the Au(III) semiquinone complex, whose electron affinity is similar to that of p‐FPhN2 + and falls between those of p‐NO2PhN2 + and p‐MeOPhN2 +. Conversely, for nonactivated aryldiazoniulms, the stable Au(III) semiquinone complex closes the catalytic cycle in a photoredox regime.

More insight into the photoinduced electron transfer and N2 dissociation steps was also obtained thanks to computational studies, considering the Au(III) catechol complex 1a and the diazonium salt p‐FPhN2 +. Upon green light irradiation, the charge‐transfer complex CT_1 is promoted to the S2* excited state (vertical transition, λ 548 nm), which undergoes internal conversion to the S1* excited state located about 44 kcal·mol−1 higher in energy than CT_1. Geometric relaxation of S1* leads to single‐electron transfer from the Au(III) catechol complex to the diazonium salt p‐FPhN2 +. The singlet and triplet states of the ensuing biradical intermediate 5_S and 5_T are very close in energy (the two radical components interact only weakly) and behave very similarly in the subsequent N2 dissociation step. Only the triplet state 5_T is shown and discussed here [28]. It lies 3.3 kcal·mol−1 1 above the ground‐state CT_1 (Figure 8). The geometry of the organic fragment differs markedly from that of the diazonium salt p‐FPhN2 + and actually very much resembles that of the p‐FPhN2 • radical: the C–N–N fragment is bent, the p‐FPh─N bond is elongated while the N═N bond is shortened. In turn, the geometry of the metallic fragment matches that of the semi‐quinone complex 1a‐sq [28]. The spin density is equally distributed over the two fragments, in line with the existence of two SOMOs centered on the Au complex and organic substrate, respectively (Figure S54) [28]. From 5_T, N2 extrusion proceeds readily, with an activation barrier ΔG ‡ of 12.7 kcal·mol−1 relative to the ground‐state CT_1, leading to the Au(III) semi‐quinone complex 1a‐sq and the corresponding aryl radical p‐FPh•. This step is slightly endergonic (ΔG 4.5 kcal·mol− 1) but subsequent arylation of furan drives the overall process forward. Thus, irradiation of the EDA adduct CT_1 provides a low‐energy path for the generation of the reactive aryl radical, in line with experimental observations. To assess the concurrent thermal mechanism (background path operating in the dark for electron‐deprived aryldiazonium salts), the activation barrier for single‐electron transfer from the ground state CT_1, without photoexcitation, was computed using Marcus theory (Figure S57) [28, 39, 40, 41]. The obtained value (ΔG ‡ 25.1 kcal·mol−1) is consistent with a process slowly occurring at room temperature.

Conclusion

One‐electron oxidation of Au(III) catecholate complexes was discovered to afford stable and crystalline Au(III) semiquinone complexes. Thorough analysis of their electronic structure by experimental and computational means revealed that the ligand, not the metal, was oxidized. Thanks to their square‐planar and electron‐rich character, the Au(III) catecholate complexes proved to readily form electron donor–acceptor adducts. This opens the door for photoinduced single‐electron transfer upon irradiation by visible‐light, as demonstrated with aryldiazonium salts as benchmark acceptors and aryl radical precursors. The system is amenable to photocatalysis, as substantiated by the C─H arylation of furans as proof‐of‐concept. The catalytic transformation is general (29 examples) and highly functional group tolerant. The broad modularity of the Au(III) complex, by variation of the redox‐active and ancillary ligands, offers a simple but powerful handle to tune the redox potentials, the photophysical properties and the photochemical reactivity, as illustrated upon arylation of furan with challenging electron‐rich diazonium salts. Further studies will aim to apply Au(III) catecholate complexes and related redox‐active Au(III) complexes to other acceptors and other C─C/C─X coupling reactions to explore further the synthetic value of the approach.

As nicely reviewed recently by Xie et al. [42], gold photocatalysis is a rich and rapidly expanding field of research. As for standalone gold photocatalysis, without external photosensitizer, gold(I) complexes largely dominate, with the dinuclear Au(I)···Au(I) complex featuring dppm (Ph2PCH2PPh2) bridging ligands as key player [43]. A few Au(III) complexes have also been shown to enable photoredox catalysis [42]. The complexes employed here and the operating photocatalytic cycle are completely different and open a new segment. It is also noteworthy that the involvement of EDA adducts as entry points in photocatalysis with Au(III) catecholate complexes bridges the gap between organic and transition metal based photocatalysts. The EDA path is well‐established and widely used in photocatalysis [44], but so far restricted to metal‐free systems and the photocatalyst is very rarely part of the EDA adduct [45, 46, 47]. The octahedral Ir and Ru complexes used widely as photocatalysts are not prone to form EDA adducts [48]. Square‐planar complexes such as the Au(III) catecholate complexes open new avenues thanks to the ready formation of EDA adducts. Related complexes with other late transition metals are also certainly worth to consider and explore.
