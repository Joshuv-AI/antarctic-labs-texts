Dynamics and optimal control of a weighted-network dengue model with seasonal transmission
Authors: Yantao Luo, Zhidong Teng, Ramziya Rifhat, Lei Wang, Tingting Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we introduce a novel dengue model on a weighted network to investigate the roles of human mobility, seasonal temperature shifts, and spatial heterogeneity. First, the positivity, global existence, and ultimate boundedness of the model solutions have also been discussed. Then, by utilizing the next generation operator theory, we derived the basic reproduction number R0 for the model, which determines the threshold dynamics of the system: When R0<1, the model possesses a unique disease-free periodic solution that is globally asymptotically stable; whereas when R0>1, dengue fever persists in both humans and mosquitoes, with at least one endemic periodic solution existing. Moreover, we formulate a corresponding optimal control model for dengue disease and derive the optimal prevention strategies with minimal implementation costs. Finally, several numerical examples are conducted to validate the theoretical results and the visualization outcomes demonstrate that spatial heterogeneity leads to heterogeneous disease transmission, and the total number of infected humans and infected mosquitoes increasing as the diffusion rate of infected humans rises and the peak of the temperature difference in the season is greater, and the epidemic is more likely to break out earlier, with more infected humans and mosquitoes. Additionally, we also present the control effects under different combinations of control measures and the spatiotemporal evolution of the optimal control solution.Keywords: Dengue model, Weighted-network, Seasonality, Threshold dynamics, Optimal control

Introduction

Dengue fever (DF) is an mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by the Dengue virus (DENV), primarily transmitted by the Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes, and is mainly prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions (Edrees et al., 2025). In the past 30 years, the number of dengue fever cases worldwide has surged from 500,000 cases in 1990 to 7.6 million cases in 2024.The highest number of DF cases was recorded in 2023, affecting over 80 countries in all regions of WHO. Since the beginning of 2023 ongoing transmission, combined with an unexpected spike in DF cases, resulted in a historic high of over 6.5 million cases and more than 7300 dengue-related deaths reported. (World Health Organization, 2024). According to estimates by the WHO, about half of the world's population is now at risk of dengue with an estimated 100-400 million infections occurring each year infection. Therefore, DF poses a significant threat to the health of billions of individuals and has a substantial burden worldwide (Ni et al., 2024).

In order to address the burden of DF on the health and lives of people worldwide, as well as on the global economy, in recent years, an increasing amount of research has been conducted on DF. Some previous studies have identified long-term urbanization, human mobility and population growth are main influential drivers shaping dengue's distribution and dynamics (Messina et al., 2019; Struchiner et al., 2015). In addition, the interactions between recent climate change and key socio-environmental drivers have also been shown to contribute to dengue's expansion (Gibb et al., 2023). As is well known, human mobility and diffusion generally follow specific network structures, such as small-world networks. In recent years, many researchers have explored the role of human mobility and diffusion in infectious diseases transmission, developing a variety of epidemic models that incorporate network-based diffusion mechanisms (Barman & Mishra, 2024; Bustamante-Casta et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2020; Gou et al., 2023; Liu & Tian, 2020; Tian et al., 2023; Tian & Liu, 2022). For instance, Liu et al. (Liu & Tian, 2020) introduced an SIRS model that employs graph Laplacian diffusion, illustrating how network structures play a crucial role in determining the early-stage transmission dynamics of epidemics. Gou et al. (Gou et al., 2023) creatively developed a reaction-diffusion SI epidemic model on a spatially embedded network, investigating the effects of network size, non-local connectivity, asymmetric connectivity, degree heterogeneity, and random connection chains on self-regulatory patterns within the spatial network. Barman et al. (Barman & Mishra, 2024) studied a delayed SEIR model with nonlinear infection rate, using graph Laplacian diffusion to describe population movement, and examined the existence of Hopf bifurcation at the endemic equilibrium. To our knowledge, existing research on the impact of population movement on DF transmission largely employs patch models (Abidemi et al., 2024; Barrios et al., 2018; Bock & Jayathunga, 2018; Ospina-Aguirre et al., 2023) or continuous Laplacian diffusion models (Li et al., 2023; Wang & Zhao, 2011; Zhang & Wang, 2020; Zhu et al., 2018). However, epidemiological models that use weighted graph networks to study how human mobility affects the transmission dynamics of vector-borne diseases are relatively rare (Tian & Liu, 2022; Zheng et al., 2025). Notably, these traditional methods have inherent limitations. Patch models are constrained by dimensional limitations and become theoretically challenging to analyze as the number of patches increases. Continuous Laplacian diffusion models, based on the fundamental assumption of isotropic diffusion and relying solely on local differential terms, are ineffective in accurately describing the non-uniform characteristics and long-distance movements of actual population flows. In contrast, weighted graph networks can more accurately simulate human movement patterns (especially long-distance heterogeneous movements such as air travel), thereby more effectively revealing the impact of population movement on disease transmission. Specifically, Zheng et al. (Zheng et al., 2025) developed a weighted graph network dynamical model for DF transmission, and found that human migration behavior, particularly international travel and large-scale population movement, plays a crucial role in the transmission of DENV.

Additionally, temperature critically modulates dengue seasonality by regulating mosquitoes life-history parameters (biting rate, extrinsic incubation period, adult survival). Lambrechts et al. (Lambrechts et al., 2011) confirmed through experiments that the peak transmission efficiency occurs between 25 and 30 °C, with sharp declines below 16 °C or above 35 °C. Chen and Hsieh (Chen & Hsieh, 2012) integrated the nonlinear temperature-dependent entomological parameters of Aedes aegypti into the transmission dynamic modeling of dengue fever, which suggest that the greatest risk of dengue transmission occurs at temperatures equal to 28 °C. Song et al. (Song et al., 2020) demonstrated that the temperature change causes the periodic oscillations of dengue fever cases. Pliego et al. (Pliego et al., 2017) developed a dynamical model of the life cycle of the mosquito Aedes for which the entomological parameters are temperature dependent and the hatchery capacity can vary with time according to the precipitation, they found that the seasonality of the low temperature rather than mean temperature is the main driving force promoting mosquitoes outbreaks and hence dengue outbreaks for Mexican. Nevertheless, However, the dengue transmission dynamics models mentioned above regarding seasonal effects are mostly based on the assumption of homogeneous environments, neglecting the impact of spatial heterogeneity on dengue. In recent years, dengue transmission models with spatial heterogeneity, established using reaction-diffusion equations, have attracted significant attention from many scholars, see e.g. (Li & Zhao, 2022; Luo et al., 2023; Wang & Zhao, 2011; Zha & Jiang, 2024). Wang and Zhao (Wang & Zhao, 2011) formulated a nonlocal reaction-diffusion model for dengue fever with periodic time delays, demonstrating that calculating the basic reproduction number using spatially averaged parameters can lead to an underestimation of infection risk in spatially heterogeneous settings. In (Li & Zhao, 2022), the authors demonstrated that shorter incubation periods and a higher rate of vertical transmission can accelerate the spread of dengue in heterogeneous environments, as shown by a reaction-diffusion model that integrated both vertical transmission and climate factors. Additionally, Zha and Jiang (Zha & Jiang, 2024) found that the spatial heterogeneity of transmission may increase the risk of dengue transmission, while the increase of seasonal developmental durations, intrinsic incubation periods and advection rates of DENV can all reduce the risk of dengue transmission.

In summary, human migration, particularly international travel and large-scale population movement, along with seasonal temperature variations, play crucial roles in the global spread of the dengue virus (DENV). However, current research quantitatively studying dengue transmission dynamics by integrating human network diffusion and seasonal temperature changes is limited. Furthermore, research quantitatively investigating the optimal control problem for dengue transmission by combining these two aspects is virtually non-existent. Therefore, this paper will primarily establish, from a dynamical perspective, a class of non-autonomous dengue transmission models with partially degenerate network diffusion. This model will be used to study the impact of seasonal temperature variations, human network diffusion, and spatial heterogeneity on dengue transmission. Subsequently, an optimal control problem for mitigating dengue spread will be proposed for quantitative study.

The structure of the paper is outlined as follows. Section 2 introduces the formulation of the weighted-network model for DF. Then, in Section 3, we introduce some preliminary knowledge and discuss the positivity and boundedness of the model solutions. Section 4 defines the basic reproduction number R0 using the next generation of operator theory and further explores the stability of the disease-free periodic solution and the existence of positive periodic solution. Section 5 presents the threshold-type results on the extinction and uniform persistence of the disease in terms of R0. In Section 6, we formulate the optimal control problems by proposing the control objectives. Finally, in section 7, several numerical examples are conducted to validate the theoretical threshold results and the visualization outcomes of the impact of different control measures, spatial heterogeneity, human network diffusion and seasonal temperature variations on dengue transmission.

Optimal control problem

In this section, we extend the basic model (2) by including some particular control measures and formulate the optimal control problems by proposing the control objectives. The first control function u1(x, t) represents the reduction of mosquitoes contact with humans through certain protective measures, such as the use of mosquitoes repellents and mosquitoes nets, thereby reducing the infection rate of susceptible populations through a factor (1 − u1(x, t)). The second control function u2(x, t) is used to represent the level of larvacide used for mosquitoes control administered at mosquito-breeding sites to eradicate breeding mosquitoes. Consequently, the reproduction rate of the mosquito population is reduced by a factor of (1 − u2(x, t)). The third control function u3(x, t) shows the level of the mortality rate of mosquito population increases by the use of electric mosquito swatting, mosquito repellent, and insecticide spraying, which increases the associated force of the mortality rate of mosquito population by a factor u3(x, t). Numerical examples and simulations

To more clearly and intuitively demonstrate the threshold dynamics of model (3) and validate the reliability of the theoretical results, this section presents several numerical simulations conducted using Matlab. The core objectives are: first, to verify the accuracy of the key conclusions through numerical solutions; and second, to explore the impacts of population mobility, seasonality, and various disease control strategies on dengue virus transmission. To better reflect the randomness of human mobility in the real world, we model the graph G as a Watts-Strogatz network. The network comprises n = 100 nodes, with an average degree of 4 and a rewiring probability of p = 1/5. This choice of rewiring probability aims to represent the inevitable random walks or chance encounters that deviate from established patterns within a population.

Conclusion

In this paper, we establish a dengue fever virus transmission model that integrates human mobility, spatial heterogeneity and seasonal factors. Based on the characteristics of human mobility, the model innovatively incorporates a weighted graph network to depict personnel mobility and to simulate the impact of human mobility on disease transmission. Firstly, using the next generation operator theory, we defined the basic reproduction number (R0) of the model and conduct an in-depth analysis of the dynamical behavior of the model: when R0<1, the disease-free periodic solution is globally attractive (indicating disease extinction), and when R0>1, the disease persists and positive periodic solution exist (indicating endemic persistence). And then, to explore effective control strategies, we further proposed an optimal control problem to find the optimal intervention plan and prove the sufficient conditions for the existence of the optimal control solution. All in all, our key innovations include:

•Construct a dengue transmission model with seasonal temperature variations and spatial heterogeneity based on weighted network diffusion

Construct a dengue transmission model with seasonal temperature variations and spatial heterogeneity based on weighted network diffusion

•Derive degenerate weighted network diffusion dynamical equations to resolve synergistic impacts of seasonal temperature variations, spatial heterogeneity and network diffusion intensity on disease transmission.

Derive degenerate weighted network diffusion dynamical equations to resolve synergistic impacts of seasonal temperature variations, spatial heterogeneity and network diffusion intensity on disease transmission.

•Designing Pontryagin's maximum principle-based control strategies under spatial heterogeneity based on weighted network diffusion.

Designing Pontryagin's maximum principle-based control strategies under spatial heterogeneity based on weighted network diffusion.

Finally, some numerical simulations are conducted to validate above theoretical results and evaluated the effectiveness of different control strategies. Numerical results demonstrate that spatial heterogeneity leads to heterogeneous disease transmission, and the total number of infected humans and infected mosquitoes increasing as the diffusion rate of infected humans rises and the peak of the temperature difference in the season is greater, and the epidemic is more likely to break out earlier, with more infected humans and mosquitoes. Moreover, key findings demonstrated that simultaneously implementing all three measures-reducing human-mosquito contact rates, decreasing mosquitoes recruitment rate (birth rates), and increasing mosquitoes mortality rates-yields the optimal outcome. If only a single measure was feasible, reducing the mosquitoes recruitment rate proves most effective for curbing transmission.

However, the parameter settings of this study were mainly based on literature and lacked real data reflecting the dynamic of spatial transmission of dengue fever virus for verification. Moreover, in the numerical simulation part of the optimal control model, the implementation cost was not considered to evaluate the seven strategies, and the most cost-effective strategy could not be determined. In future research, we will focus on collecting real data to strengthen the empirical basis of the model; and attempt to conduct in-depth cost-benefit analysis within the optimal control framework to determine the most economically effective control strategy.
