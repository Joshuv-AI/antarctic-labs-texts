Transgene sequence codon optimization and composition determines replication competence of self-amplifying RNA
Authors: Nuthan Vikas Bathula, Shekinah KV Soriano, Cynthia Huang, Eric Jan, Selena M Sagan, Anna K Blakney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-amplifying RNA (saRNA) is an emerging RNA therapeutic modality that can facilitate higher magnitude and more durable protein expression at substantially lower doses than nonreplicating mRNA. Unlike conventional messenger RNA (mRNA), alphavirus-derived saRNA must support a replicase-driven RNA amplification step in addition to translation, raising the possibility that transgene coding sequences impose sequence-level constraints on replication. Here, saRNA replication was found to be dependent on the codon composition of the transgene; multiple therapeutic transgenes were replication defective despite an intact Venezuelan Equine Encephalitis Virus (VEEV)-derived saRNA backbone. Replication defects were rescued by synonymous codon re-optimization of the same transgenes, indicating that nucleotide-level features of the coding sequence, rather than the encoded protein, govern replication competence. Comparative compositional analyses identified a distinct signature associated with productive replication, characterized by elevated GC (>53%) and GC3 (>63%) content, higher codon adaptation to human (>0.75), and reduced UpA (<43/kb) and UpU (<41/kb) dinucleotide density. Moreover, deliberate compositional perturbation of an otherwise replication-competent transgene shifted these features and abolished replication, supporting a causal and combinatorial role for sequence composition in defining saRNA replication outcome. These findings define an underappreciated constraint in saRNA therapeutics and motivate saRNA-specific payload design frameworks that incorporate alphavirus-associated compositional biases during transgene sequence optimization.Keywords: codon optimization, self-amplifying RNA, replication, alphavirus, RNA therapeutics, transgene, subgenomic, replicon

Introduction

Building on decades of fundamental and preclinical research, the field of mRNA therapeutics has undergone a marked transformation over the past 5 years. Rapid, sequence-based design, streamlined progression from laboratory concept to clinical candidates, flexibility to encode a wide range of therapeutic proteins, cell-free manufacturing, scalable production, and strong clinical performance have established messenger RNA (mRNA) as a versatile alternative to conventional protein-based biologics.1 More recently, self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) has emerged as a next-generation RNA platform capable of achieving higher-magnitude and more durable transgene expression from substantially lower doses than conventional mRNA.2 Multiple mRNA and saRNA vaccines targeting SARS-CoV-2,3,4,5,6 as well as an mRNA vaccine against respiratory syncytial virus,7 are now approved for clinical use worldwide. Collectively, these advances have catalyzed a rapidly expanding clinical pipeline of mRNA and saRNA therapeutics across diverse indications, including infectious diseases,8 oncology,9 autoimmune conditions,10 and rare genetic disorders.11

Although both modalities are RNA based, their fundamental properties diverge in several key aspects. Conventional nonreplicating mRNA comprises five core structural elements, a 5′ cap, 5′ untranslated region (UTR), coding sequence, 3′ UTR, and poly(A) tail, and it functions primarily as a cytosolic template for translation of the encoded protein (Figure 1A).12,13 In contrast, saRNA is derived from positive-sense single-stranded RNA alphaviruses and operates as a self-replicating system.14 A typical alphavirus saRNA construct contains a 5′ cap, viral 5′ UTR, an open reading frame (ORF) encoding the four nonstructural proteins (nsP1-4), a subgenomic promoter (SGP), a second ORF encoding the transgene in place of the viral structural proteins, the viral 3′ UTR, and a poly(A) tail.15 Unlike nonreplicating mRNA, saRNA undergoes a multi-step replication cycle (Figure 1B), translation of the nsPs yields the replicase complex, which recognizes cis-acting elements in the 5′ and 3′ ends and synthesizes a complementary negative-sense RNA intermediate.15,16 This intermediate then acts as a template for production of new positive-sense genomic RNA and subgenomic mRNA initiated from the internal subgenomic promoter.17 As replication proceeds, subgenomic mRNA is produced in excess over full-length genomic RNA. Accordingly, saRNA functionality depends not only on efficient translation of the nonstructural and transgene proteins, but also on accurate recognition of cis-acting elements in the UTRs, SGP, and synthesis of new genomic RNAs and subgenomic mRNAs.16

Contrarily, saRNA with noncompatible transgene sequence composition fails to replicate and express the transgene.

The mechanistic differences impose RNA-modality-specific design criteria. For nonreplicating mRNA, UTRs from diverse sources, including endogenous mammalian UTRs18 and rationally designed synthetic UTR libraries,19 have been combined and optimized to enhance translation,20 improve mRNA half-life,21 and enable cell- or tissue-specific controllable expression.19,22 In alphavirus-derived saRNA, however, the 5′ and 3′ UTRs are not freely interchangeable UTRs; they function as cis-acting replication elements recognized by the replicase complex, and perturbation of these terminal sequences can disrupt replication. Consequently, UTR designs optimized for nonreplicating mRNA are generally not directly transferable to saRNA. Nevertheless, Lello et al. reported that an alphavirus replicase (e.g., SFV) can recognize heterologous UTRs and SGPs from other alphaviruses and support replication,23 suggesting that chimeric UTR-SGP designs may offer leverage to tune replication and transgene expression. Likewise, modified nucleotide analogs, an approach used to reduce innate immune recognition of exogenous RNA, also differs across modalities. N1-methylpseudouridine is broadly compatible with conventional mRNA,24,25 whereas its incorporation into alphavirus saRNA has been reported to abrogate expression.26 Alternatively, 5-methylcytidine and 5-hydroxy-methylcytidine have been shown to be compatible with saRNA and to reduce inflammatory responses.27,28 Together, these examples illustrate how practical design criteria diverge between mRNA and saRNA and underscore the need for platform-specific optimization considerations.

Transgene coding sequence design represents another major lever to control stability and translation. Coding sequence composition can influence translation efficiency and RNA decay through effects on ribosome loading and elongation dynamics as well as local and global RNA structural features.29,30 Reflecting this, recent RNA-centric codon optimization tools implement diverse design strategies. RiboDecode leverages ribosome profiling-derived translation patterns to train predictive models of context-specific translation and optimizes synonymous codons to maximize predicted translation performance.31 GEMORNA uses transformer-based generative models to generate candidate designs across mRNA components (including coding regions and UTRs) enriched for sequences associated with higher expression and stability.32 LinearDesign applies explicit algorithmic optimization, searching the synonymous coding-sequence space under a combined objective incorporating a biophysical RNA-folding model and codon optimality via dynamic programming.33 In parallel, widely used commercial platforms such as GenScript and Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) provide publicly accessible codon-optimization workflows that, while proprietary in their internal implementations, generally account for factors such as codon usage bias, GC content, mRNA secondary structure, and cis-acting sequence elements to improve expression by balancing codon usage with features that support efficient translation, transcript stability, and ease of synthesis.34,35,36

It remains unclear whether alphavirus-derived saRNA imposes additional sequence-composition constraints on the transgene that must be considered during sequence optimization. Because most RNA design criteria and optimization pipelines were developed and benchmarked for nonreplicating mRNA, it is not known whether sequences optimized for mRNA are consistently compatible with saRNA replication, an important knowledge gap given the increasing use of saRNA to express therapeutic proteins across diverse applications. Here, we show that transgene coding sequences are not universally compatible with efficient saRNA replication and identify sequence composition parameters that distinguish replication-competent from replication-defective payloads. Moreover, targeted correction of these features rescues replication of previously defective constructs, whereas deliberate perturbation of the same features can abolish replication of otherwise competent saRNA payloads.

Results

saRNA replication is dependent on the coding sequence of the transgene

A common assumption with an saRNA system is that any coding sequence can be “plugged” into the subgenomic position and will be replicated and expressed efficiently. In practice, however, we encountered several transgene sequences encoding distinct therapeutic proteins that did not align with this assumption. To systematically assess replication competence, we transfected HEK293T cells with saRNA encoding either firefly luciferase (fLuc) or different therapeutic transgenes (sequence details in materials and methods section). Total cellular RNA was harvested at 4 h and 24 h post transfection, and genomic and subgenomic saRNA copies were quantified by RT-qPCR using primers against nsP3 and the transgene, respectively (Figures 2A and 2B). In a pilot early time course (between 1 h and 4 h) using representative constructs, no measurable increase in genomic or subgenomic RNA was observed (Figure S1), indicating that amplification is not detectably established during this early window; therefore, 4 h and 24 h were used to capture replication-associated changes.

As expected, the fLuc-encoding saRNA (positive control) showed robust amplification of the subgenomic RNA, with an ∼6-fold increase in subgenomic copy number between 4 h and 24 h (Figure 2C), consistent with exponential replication of the subgenomic RNA observed earlier.37 Similarly, genomic RNA copy number remained relatively stable over the same interval, indicative of slower genomic RNA replication and transcription compared with subgenomic RNA production, in line with the known alphavirus saRNA replication mechanism.16 Interestingly, none of the saRNAs encoding the therapeutic transgenes displayed evidence of replication. Instead of increasing, both genomic and subgenomic RNA copy numbers declined between 4 h and 24 h post transfection, representing a progressive RNA decay in the absence of active replication (Figures 2D–2F, 2H, and 2J). These data demonstrate that not all transgene coding sequences are compatible with saRNA replication, indicating that the platform is subject to unknown compatibility constraints. Overall, these observations formed the basis for the subsequent analyses to determine why certain saRNAs fail to replicate and how such transgenes can be rationally optimized to restore robust saRNA function.

Synonymous codon optimization rescues replication of previously nonreplicating saRNA

To determine whether the replication defects observed for certain transgenes could be overcome by altering just the coding sequence, the corresponding coding sequences were redesigned by synonymous codon optimization using a different tool—GenScript codon optimization tool—while all saRNA functional elements were left unchanged (Figure 2A). HEK293T cells were transfected with saRNA encoding either the fLuc control or one of five codon-optimized transgenes, which express the same therapeutic proteins as their original counterparts but differ at the nucleotide sequence level. Total cellular RNA was harvested at 4 h and 24 h post transfection, and genomic and subgenomic saRNA copies were quantified by RT-qPCR using primers against nsP3 and the transgene, respectively (Figure 2B).

Notably, saRNAs encoding the Genscript re-codon-optimized transgenes exhibited efficient replication comparable to the fLuc control. For all the re-codon-optimized transgene constructs, subgenomic RNA copy number increased by approximately 4- to 7-fold between 4 h and 24 h, similar to the fold change observed for the fLuc saRNA, indicating a clear rescue of saRNA replication. This outcome was also reproduced in BHK21 cells, supporting that the replication incompetence and rescue are not restricted to a single cellular context (Figure S2). Thus, synonymous re-coding of the transgene was sufficient to convert previously replication-defective constructs into replication-competent saRNAs. Because the only difference between the original nonreplicating and rescued versions of each transgene is the nucleotide sequence, these findings strongly support that it is the transgene RNA sequence, rather than the encoded protein, that influences the saRNA replication.

Replication-defective and replication-competent transgenes exhibit distinct compositional signatures

To identify sequence features associated with saRNA replication competence, nucleotide composition, codon usage and dinucleotide composition were compared between replication-defective and replication-competent payloads. The analysis comprised five replication-defective transgene coding sequences, their synonymous re-coded (replication-rescued) counterparts, seven additional transgenes previously observed to replicate efficiently, and the Venezuelan Equine Encephalitis Virus (VEEV) TC-83 structural polyprotein ORF as a reference for endogenous alphaviral subgenomic coding composition. In total, 5 replication-defective sequences, 12 replication-competent sequences, and 1 viral structural ORF were evaluated.

For each sequence, a panel of compositional indices was computed (Tables S1 and S2), including overall nucleotide composition (A%, U%, C%, G%, GC%), base composition at the first, second, and third codon position (AU3%, GC3%, GC1%, GC2%, GC12%), codon adaptation index (CAI) relative to human codon usage, and dinucleotide composition. Dinucleotide composition was quantified both as density (counts per kilobase) and as observed/expected (O/E) ratios to distinguish absolute motif burden from dinucleotide bias independent of global base composition. Collectively, these measures capture differences in codon usage bias, host adaptation, and dinucleotide constraints, as is standard in viral codon usage analyses.

Across all parameters examined, five features most clearly separated replication-defective from replication-competent payloads: overall GC%, GC3%, UpA density, UpU density, and CAI (Figure 3). Replication-defective transgenes exhibited lower overall GC content (≤53%), whereas replication-competent payloads consistently displayed GC content >53% (Figure 4A). The VEEV TC-83 structural polyprotein ORF had an intermediate GC content of 51.51% (Figure 3A). Differences were more pronounced at the third codon position where the nonreplicating sequences showed low GC3 values (44%–53%), indicating a reduced use of G/C-ending codons, while replication-competent sequences exhibited markedly elevated GC3 (63%–97%), reflecting a strong preference for G/C-ending synonymous codons (Figure 3B). The VEEV structural ORF again fell between these groups, with GC3 of 56.7%.

In contrast to the codon-level signatures, dinucleotide O/E ratios showed substantial overlap between groups. UpA, UpU, and CpG were generally under-represented (O/E < 1) across both replication-defective and replication-competent payloads, whereas UpG tended to be enriched (O/E > 1), with replication-defective sequences clustering closer to 1 (Figures 3F and 3J–3L). However, a clearer separation emerged when dinucleotides were assessed as absolute densities (per kb). Replication-competent payloads consistently showed lower UpA density (<42.7/kb), comparable to the VEEV structural ORF (41.4/kb), whereas most replication-defective payloads exhibited higher UpA density (>48/kb), with one replication-defective outlier (∼28/kb) (Figure 3G). A similar trend was observed for UpU with replication-competent payloads clustered at lower UpU density (<41.1/kb), while replication-defective payloads were higher (>43.4/kb), although the VEEV structural ORF exhibited relatively high UpU density (47.1/kb) (Figure 3H). By contrast, CpG and UpG densities showed broad overlap across groups and did not provide robust discrimination (Figures 3E–3I).

Codon usage bias relative to human codon usage was quantified by the CAI. Replication-competent transgenes exhibited higher CAI values (CAI > 0.77), indicating greater adaptation to the human codon usage pattern, while replication-defective sequences showed lower CAI values (<0.74). The VEEV structural polyprotein ORF had a CAI of 0.75, again clustering closer to replication-competent constructs. Across all replication-competent sequences, GC3 and CAI were strongly positively correlated (Pearson r = 0.86, n = 13, p = 0.0002), consistent with the notion that increased use of G/C-ending codons enhances adaptation to human.

Together, these analyses define a compositional signature associated with saRNA replication competence: replication-competent payloads are enriched for higher GC and GC3, depleted for UpA and UpU dinucleotide density, and exhibit higher CAI, whereas replication-defective payloads show the opposite pattern. Across these metrics, the VEEV structural ORF generally clusters closer to the replication-competent group, consistent with the interpretation that payloads compatible with saRNA amplification occupy a compositional space more similar to the native alphaviral coding context. These results identify GC3, UpA/UpU dinucleotide density, and CAI as candidate sequence-level features associated with payload compatibility in VEEV-derived saRNA.

Replication-defective and competent transgenes exhibit similar predicted structural profiles

As the replication-competent and replication-defective transgenes showed significantly different compositional signatures, the next question was whether they also differ in predicted RNA secondary structure. To address this, the RNA structure was analyzed in silico using RNAfold in a 370-nt segment spanning 70 nt upstream of the subgenomic RNA (nsP4/SGP junction) and the first 300 nt of the subgenomic RNA (transgene coding sequence). For each construct, RNAfold was used to compute the per-base unpaired probability (p_unpaired) and positional Shannon entropy for the minimum-free-energy structure ensemble (Figure 4). These quantities were summarized as regional means: mean p_unpaired and mean entropy for the upstream nsP4+SGP region and for the first 300 nt of the transgene. In addition to the positive-sense RNA, the same metrics were calculated for the complementary (anti-sense) strand, as cis-acting replication elements in positive-strand RNA viruses can depend on structural features present in both polarities.

In contrast to the clear segregation observed for GC3, dinucleotide frequencies, and CAI, no corresponding separation was evident for the predicted RNA structural metrics. Replication-defective and replication-competent transgenes showed extensive overlap in both p_unpaired and entropy for the positive-sense (Figures 4B–4E) and complementary strands (Figures 4F–4I) across all regions analyzed. A modest difference was detected only for the mean p_unpaired of the complementary nsP4+SGP segment (Figure 4F), which was slightly lower in replication-competent than in replication-defective constructs. However, this effect was small in magnitude and not accompanied by consistent differences in entropy or in the downstream transgene region. A very similar structural signature was observed even at the 3′ end of the transgene region (Figure S3).

Overall, these analyses indicate that replication-defective and replication-competent transgenes do not differ in any robust or systematic way with respect to predicted local secondary structure, either on the positive or complementary strand. In combination with the strong compositional differences described above, this suggests that linear sequence composition, rather than gross secondary-structure features, is the primary determinant of the observed inhibition of saRNA replication in replication-defective transgenes.

Disrupting the compositional signature of a replication-competent transgene abolishes saRNA replication and expression both in vitro and in vivo

The compositional analysis described above indicated that replication-competent and replication-defective transgenes differ markedly in GC content, GC3, UpA/UpU dinucleotide density, and CAI. We next sought to determine which of these parameters was imperative for saRNA replication competence. To directly test the functional relevance of the compositional signature, two key metrics were selected: (1) GC3, which in this dataset is strongly positively correlated with CAI relative to human codon usage, and (2) UpA dinucleotide density, representing dinucleotide composition signature. Three synonymous variants of the replication-competent fLuc transgene were designed that encode identical protein sequence but differ in GC3 and/or UpA content.

Variant 1 was generated by reducing GC3 of the fLuc ORF from 83.85% to 44.0%, placing GC3 within the range observed for replication-defective transgenes, while leaving other compositional parameters relatively unchanged. Variant 2 was generated by increasing the UpA dinucleotide density from 18.75/kb to 48.4/kb, again matching the range characteristic of replication-defective sequences, with minimal changes to other metrics. Variant 3 combined both alterations, with GC3 reduced to 44.0% and UpA increased to 48.4/kb. Each variant was cloned into the saRNA backbone and assessed alongside the original fLuc construct in HEK293T cells. Total cellular RNA was harvested at 4 h and 24 h post transfection, and genomic and subgenomic saRNA copies were quantified by RT-qPCR using primers against nsP3 and the transgene, respectively.

As expected, the original fLuc saRNA exhibited robust replication, with approximately a 5-fold increase in subgenomic RNA copy number between 4 h and 24 h (Figure 5A). In contrast, constructs in which either GC3 was reduced (Variant 1) or UpA density was increased (Variant 2), displayed attenuated replication, showing only ∼3- to 4-fold increases in subgenomic RNA copies over the same interval (Figures 5B and 5C). Strikingly, modifying the construct with low GC3 and high UpA (Variant 3) indirectly reduced the CAI and increased the UpU/kb density, which altogether led to a completely loss in replication competence. Both genomic and subgenomic RNA copy numbers declined between 4 h and 24 h (Figure 5D), closely resembling the kinetics observed for replication-defective transgenes in Figure 2.

To determine whether impaired replication translated into reduced protein output, fLuc expression was quantified for the original fLuc construct and all three compositional variants in HEK293T cells (Figure 6A). At 24 h post transfection, Variants 1 and 2 produced significantly lower fLuc signal than the original fLuc, consistent with their attenuated replication profiles, whereas Variant 3 (low GC3/high UpA) showed expression close to background (Figure 6B). A similar concordance between replication competence and protein output was observed for saRNAs encoding replication-defective versus replication-competent transgenes 2 and 3 (Figure S4). To assess whether this relationship was maintained in vivo, the original fLuc and Variant 3 constructs were formulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) (refer to Figure S5 for particle characteristics) and evaluated in BALB/c mice. At 24 h after intramuscular administration, Variant 3 produced no appreciable fLuc signal, whereas the original fLuc saRNA yielded robust expression (Figure 6C), consistent with prior observations for this reporter in the same platform.38 Collectively, these data indicate that the compositional constraints governing saRNA replication have direct functional consequences for transgene expression and are preserved across in vitro and in vivo settings.

Together, these data demonstrate that perturbing the compositional signature associated with replication competence, specifically, lowering GC3 into the defective range and elevating UpA to levels characteristic of nonreplicating payloads, is sufficient to convert an otherwise replication-competent saRNA into a replication-defective construct. Combined with the rescue experiments described above (Figure 2), these findings support the conclusion that transgene sequence composition can actively determine saRNA replication outcome, such that appropriate compositional tuning can either restore replication of defective payloads or disrupt replication of originally competent transgenes.

Discussion

In conventional nonreplicating mRNA therapeutics, coding-sequence optimization primarily targets translational efficiency and transcript stability39; suboptimal design therefore typically reduces protein output rather than completely abolishing RNA function.29,40 Alphavirus-derived saRNA introduces an additional constraint; the payload is embedded within a replicating RNA genome and must remain compatible with a virus-like replication driven by the replicase and its cis-acting requirements. In this setting, the transgene is not merely a translation template but a replacement for viral structural ORFs, raising the possibility of sequence-dependent “payload compatibility” constraints that extend beyond conventional mRNA optimization. Consistent with this concept, alphavirus virus-like replicon particles (VRP) studies have reported payload and context-dependent outcomes; for example, in a large SFV-VRP study evaluating 101 G protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), a small subset yielded no detectable product of the expected molecular weight, and expression varied substantially across host cell backgrounds.41 This broader context is consistent with the view that alphavirus-based expression systems impose constraints on foreign ORFs, and our study extends this by identifying coding-sequence compositional features that can determine replication competence in a VEEV saRNA system. Consistent with this model, multiple transgenes were replication defective despite intact saRNA backbone elements, and replication was restored by synonymous re-coding (Figures 2 and 3), supporting the conclusion that replication competence is dictated primarily by nucleotide-level features of the transgene rather than by the identity of the encoded protein.

The observed separation of replication-competent and replication-defective payloads by CAI and GC3 parameters is compatible with a contribution of host-oriented codon usage adaptation to productive saRNA amplification. A higher correlation between synonymous codon choice and host tRNA availability is associated with more efficient translation and higher protein output.42,43,44 Viruses, which rely on host translation, can exhibit improved replication and gene expression when codon usage is shifted toward host-preferred codons.45,46 However, the extent of this effect is virus and context dependent. Arboviruses, which replicate in both vertebrate and invertebrate hosts, maintain an intermediate codon usage strategy, rather than strong adaptation to one specific host.47,48 Consistent with this, VEEV shows only moderate adaptation to human codon usage (CAI∼0.75) and Aedes albopictus (CAI∼0.65).49 Building on observations from other viral systems in which shifting codon usage toward host-preferred codons can increase expression and replication,50,51,52 whereas shifting away can attenuate it,53 the present findings, replication failure for payloads with CAI <0.74 and robust replication for payloads with CAI >0.77, support a model in which increased host-oriented codon adaptation contributes to productive saRNA amplification, potentially by facilitating efficient synthesis of replication and transgene proteins. At the same time, CAI covaries with other compositional features in this study, indicating that codon adaptation is unlikely to be sufficient on its own and more likely reflects one dimension of a multi-parameter compatibility landscape.

GC3 provides a complementary view of this compatibility landscape. GC3% denotes the frequency of G or C at the third position of synonymous codons. As large fraction of synonymous variation is concentrated at third codon positions, GC3 often serves as a sensitive readout of the combined influences of mutation pressure and selection on nucleotide composition and codon usage.54,55 In vertebrates, including humans, many highly expressed genes preferentially use G/C-ending codons, a pattern that reflects, at least in part, the host translational landscape and tRNA pools.56,57 In RNA viruses, GC3 is therefore not merely a passive consequence of mutational biases but is an active evolutionary lever that viruses use to fine-tune their relationship with the host.49,58 VEEV structural genes exhibit a mean GC3% of ∼54.5%,49 with the TC83 strain at ∼56.7% and, similarly, chikungunya virus at ∼55.8%.58 In this study, replication competence tracked strongly with elevated GC3 among transgene payloads, whereas replication-defective constructs clustered at lower GC3, consistent with the interpretation that transgene sequences deviating from the compositional regime tolerated by the VEEV replicon are less compatible with productive saRNA amplification.

Dinucleotide composition represents the other mechanistically motivated axis of constraint. Many viruses, particularly those replicating in vertebrate and invertebrates, suppress CpG and UpA dinucleotides to evade immune recognition and improve RNA stability.59,60 CpG- and UpA-enriched RNAs can be selectively targeted by zinc-finger antiviral protein, leading to attenuation of viral replication,61,62 and UpA/UpU-enriched RNAs have been associated with increased susceptibility to cytosolic RNA cleavage.63,64 Notably, in poliovirus, elevating CpG and UpA content within just 9% of the genomic was sufficient to reduce in vitro fitness to near nonviability, highlighting dinucleotide composition as a dominant constraint on replication.65 Conversely, reducing CpG and UpA content in another picornavirus, echovirus 7 (E7), showed improved viral replication.66 Active replication of WT E7 virus when co-transfected with CpG/UpA-rich mutant E7 shows attenuation is largely RNA-intrinsic rather than mediated by a generalized antiviral state and can be relatively insensitive to the precise genomic location of the introduced dinucleotides.62,67 Although de-optimized codon pairs can attenuate viruses,53 studies argue that the phenotype often reflects accompanying increases in CpG/UpA dinucleotides rather than codon-pair bias per se.68 In the present study, although CpG and UpA were generally suppressed across constructs when assessed by O/E ratios, replication-defective payloads consistently exhibited higher UpA (and UpU) densities (per kb) than replication-competent payloads, aligning with the broader view that UpA-rich sequence can compromise RNA virus fitness. Moreover, the abolished replication in the fLuc-saRNA variant in which UpA/UpU density was increased in conjunction with reduced GC3 supports the conclusion that dinucleotide content and codon usage patterns contribute functionally to payload compatibility and that deviation from the compositional space tolerated by the VEEV replicon can drive replication failure even in a therapeutic saRNA context.

In contrast to compositional features, predicted RNA secondary structure metrics did not robustly segregate replication-competent from replication-defective payloads in the analyzed 5′ and 3′ transgene end regions. Ensemble-derived measures of base-wise unpaired probability and positional entropy, a proxy for local structural plasticity, showed substantial overlap between groups, arguing against gross differences in local RNA thermodynamic behavior in this segment as the primary determinant of replication failure. This is notable given recent work in RNA viruses demonstrating that the stability of inserted sequences can be constrained by the thermodynamic properties of viral RNA substructures, with positional-entropy and ΔG-based incompatibilities promoting selection for deletion during replication.69 In the present saRNA system, however, the absence of clear separation by these structure metrics, together with the strong segregation by GC3/CAI and dinucleotide densities, supports the interpretation that payload compatibility is dominated by sequence composition constraints rather than by gross local secondary-structure features in the region examined.

Altogether, the central finding of this study is that transgene composition can be a primary determinant of saRNA replication outcome. The reciprocal gain- and loss-of-replication evidence, replication rescue by synonymous re-coding of defective payloads and replication abrogation following deliberate compositional perturbation of an otherwise competent reporter, supports a critical role for nucleotide-level features of the coding sequence and indicates that replication competence is shaped by a combinatorial compositional landscape, including CAI, GC3, and dinucleotide frequencies, rather than by any single parameter in isolation. Collectively, these results highlight an underappreciated constraint in saRNA therapeutics: payloads optimized solely for nonreplicating mRNA expression may fall outside the compositional space required for productive replicon amplification, underscoring the need for saRNA-specific payload design frameworks that explicitly account for alphavirus-associated codon usage and dinucleotide constraints. An important next step will be to define the precise mechanistic basis of these compositional constraints, which should enable more rational saRNA payload engineering and a deeper understanding of how sequence features interface with replicon replication and therapeutic performance.

Materials and methods

In vitro transcription: RNA synthesis

Plasmid DNA (pDNA) constructs encoding the saRNA transgenes under the control of a T7 promoter were propagated in NEB 5-alpha competent E. coli and purified using the QIAGEN Plasmid Plus Maxi Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified pDNA was linearized with AscI at 37°C for 120 min, followed by enzyme heat inactivation at 81°C for 20 min. The resulting linearized template was used for in vitro transcription (IVT) of saRNA.

For in vitro studies, saRNA was synthesized using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE kits (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturers protocol. For in vivo studies, saRNA was produced using an optimized in-house IVT method, as previously described.70 The RNA was post-transcriptionally capped using the ScriptCap Cap1 System (CELLSCRIPT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was purified using the Monarch Spin RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB; 500 μg format) and stored at −70°C until use.

saRNA transgenes sources and codon optimization

Five transgene payloads were evaluated: the heavy chain (HC) of an IgG anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody (LyCoV555; transgene 1), an anti-mouse CD3 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derived from clone 2C11 (transgene 2), an anti-human CD3 × anti-human mesothelin bispecific T cell engager (BiTE-format), assembled from the blinatumomab-derived anti-CD3 scFv and the amatuximab-derived anti-mesothelin scFv (transgene 3), an anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike nanobody (VHH) clone PiN21 (transgene 4), and the transcription factor NeuroD1 (transgene 5). Amino acid sequences and original sources for each payload are provided in the supplemental information. For the initial design iteration, nucleotide sequences for all five transgenes were generated by reverse translation and codon optimization using the IDT Codon Optimisation Tool with human codon usage. The codon optimized sequences were ordered as synthetic gene fragments and cloned into the VEEV TC83 saRNA backbone using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly between the BbvCI and NotI restriction sites. In a second design iteration, transgenes 1–4 were independently re-optimized using the GenScript Codon Optimisation Tool (human codon usage), whereas the endogenous human (natural) NeuroD1 coding sequence was used for transgene 5. Nucleotide sequences for both design iterations and the VEEV TC83 saRNA backbone are provided in the supplemental information. Additionally, seven replication-competent control sequences were included for comparative analyses. The fLuc and GFP coding sequences were generated using the IDT Codon Optimisation Tool. SARS-CoV-2 spike coding sequence was obtained from the BioNTech mRNA vaccine sequence. Control antibody sequences (control Abs 1–3) were derived from their corresponding patent applications, and control antibody 4 was obtained from an internal phage-display-based antibody development screen; no codon optimization was applied to these control antibody sequences.

Cell culture and in vitro transfections

HEK293T cells were maintained in tissue culture-treated flasks in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM GlutaMAX, and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 μg/mL, respectively). Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and passaged at 70%–80% confluency using Gibco TrypLE (0.25%). All experiments were performed using cells within 20 passages post thaw to maintain phenotypic consistency.

For transfection experiments, cells were seeded at 4 × 105 cells per well in 12-well flat-bottom plates and transfected with saRNA using Lipofectamine MessengerMAX transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the early time course analysis, transfection medium was removed at 1 h post transfection, cells were washed with PBS to remove extracellular or uninternalized saRNA-lipofectamine complexes, and one set of cells was collected using Gibco TrypLE (0.25%) for the 1-h time point. Fresh complete medium was added to the remaining wells, and cells were incubated for an additional 3 h before collection at the 4-h time point. For replication analyses comparing 4 h and 24 h, the same wash step was performed at 4 h post transfection; cells were then either collected immediately for the 4-h time point or maintained in fresh complete medium until collection at 24 h.

Lipid nanoparticle formulation and characterization

saRNA-transgene payloads were formulated into LNPs by rapid mixing using a T-junction mixer, as previously described.71 Lipid mix was prepared in ethanol by combining ALC-0315 (BroadPharma, USA), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE; Avanti Polar Lipids, USA), cholesterol (MilliporeSigma, Canada), and 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG-PEG-2000; MilliporeSigma, Canada) at molar ratios of 45:17.5:36.25:1.25, respectively. In parallel, saRNA was prepared in 25 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.53) to generate the aqueous phase and achieve a final N:P ratio of 10.

The ethanol (lipid) and aqueous (saRNA) phases were rapidly mixed at room temperature using a Pump 33 DDS syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus) at a total flow rate of 20 mL/min and a 1:3 lipid-to-aqueous flow-rate ratio. The resulting formulation was buffer-exchanged by 10-fold dilution into PBS, followed by centrifugation at 2,200 rpm and 4°C using Amicon centrifugal filters (10 kDa MWCO) to concentrate the LNPs and remove residual solvent.

LNP hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index, and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano (Malvern Instruments). Instrument parameters were set to material refractive index 1.4, absorbance 0.01, dispersant viscosity 0.882 cP, dispersant refractive index 1.33, and dielectric constant 79. The RNA encapsulation efficiency was quantified using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), as previously described.71

Animal handling and in vivo imaging

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with protocol A21-0062 approved by the Animal Care Committee at the University of British Columbia. Female BALB/c mice (The Jackson Laboratory, USA), 8 weeks of age, were housed in groups of three per cage under acclimatized, pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to sterile food and water. Mice received 2 μg saRNA-LNP in a 50-μL dose by intramuscular administration.

Bioluminescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer) as previously described.38 Briefly, mice were administered D-luciferin (30 mg/mL; PerkinElmer, USA) by intraperitoneal injection (100 μL), allowed to rest for 7 min, anesthetized with isoflurane (3%), and imaged with a 3-min exposure. Bioluminescent signal was quantified using Living Image software (v4.5.5; IVIS Imaging Systems) and reported as total flux (photons/s).

Sequence composition analysis

The CAI of transgene sequence relative to human and the frequency of each nucleotide at the first, second, and third site of the synonymous codons are calculated using GenScript GenRCA Rare Codon Analysis Tool.72 Dinucleotide composition was quantified for each coding sequence using two complementary metrics: (1) dinucleotide density (counts per kilobase;/kb) and (2) observed/expected (O/E) ratios, which normalize dinucleotide occurrence to the underlying mononucleotide composition. For a sequence of length L nucleotides (after excluding ambiguous bases), dinucleotide counts NXY (e.g., NUA, NUU, NCG, NUG) were obtained by counting overlapping dinucleotides across all adjacent positions (total possible dinucleotides = L − 1). RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA from cultured cells and mouse muscle tissue was isolated using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry. Primer sets targeting nsP3, the respective transgene sequences, and the reference gene TBP (mouse or human, as appropriate) were obtained from OriGene Technologies and are listed in Table S3.

RT-qPCR was performed using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green One-Step Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Reactions were assembled on ice and contained 5 ng total RNA and 300 nM of each primer in the supplied master mix. Amplification was carried out on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using the instrument’s maximum ramp rate under the following cycling conditions: reverse transcription at 50°C for 10 min; polymerase activation at 95°C for 1 min; 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 60 s; followed by melt-curve analysis. Relative RNA levels were calculated using the Livak method.73

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed as described in figure legends using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1.
