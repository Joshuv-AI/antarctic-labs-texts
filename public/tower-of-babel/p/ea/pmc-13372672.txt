The association of chronic pain with suicidality in hospitalized patients with dementia: a retrospective study of the U.S. Nationwide Inpatient Sample, 2016–2020
Authors: Jocelyn Yong Huan, Peng Fan, Kai-Dih Juang, Mary R Janevic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesChronic pain is common in older adults and is associated with psychological distress, including suicidality. In patients with dementia, impaired cognitive and communication may further increase vulnerability. This study aimed to investigate associations between chronic pain and suicidality in hospitalized patients with dementia.Research Design and MethodsThis population-based, retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed hospitalizations of patients aged ≥60 years with dementia between 2016 and 2020, extracted from the U.S. Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS). Chronic pain, suicide attempts, and suicidal ideations were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth edition-CM diagnostic codes. Propensity score matching (1:4) was performed to balance baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race, dementia type, and major comorbidities. Multivariable logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Stratified analyses were conducted by age, dementia type, sex, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), race, and insurance.ResultsAfter matching, 29,185 hospitalized patients with dementia were analyzed (5,837 with chronic pain; 23,348 without). Chronic pain was significantly associated with higher odds of suicidal ideations (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.30–1.71, p < .001) and suicide attempt (aOR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.08–3.76, p = .028). Stronger associations were observed among patients aged 60–69 years, those with CCI ≥ 1, without Medicare/Medicaid. Only 16 suicide attempts occurred in the chronic pain group, limiting statistical precision.Discussion and ImplicationsChronic pain is associated with increased suicidality in hospitalized older patients with dementia. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: U.S. National Inpatient Sample (NIS), Suicide, Pain assessment, Mental health, Propensity score matching

This study investigates the association between chronic pain and suicide risk in hospitalized older adults with dementia using data from the U.S. Nationwide Inpatient Sample (2016–2020). Through multivariable logistic regression analysis, we found that chronic pain significantly increases the odds of suicidal ideation in this population. These findings highlight the need for improved pain assessment and mental health support in dementia care to mitigate suicide-related risks.

Chronic pain is a prevalent and debilitating health condition that significantly affects the quality of life (QoL), particularly among older adults (Cohen et al., 2021). Defined as pain that persists for more than 3 months, chronic pain can result from various conditions such as musculoskeletal disorders, neuropathic pain, and age-related degenerative changes (Treede et al., 2019). Chronic pain not only impairs physical functioning but also contributes to psychological distress, which, in some individuals, may lead to a diminished sense of well-being (Hnatešen et al., 2022). In the worst situation, chronic pain can be a risk factor for suicidality. Investigation has shown that rates of suicidal ideation range from 18% to 50% among patients with chronic pain (Campbell et al., 2015; Themelis et al., 2023).

Dementia, a condition characterized primarily by cognitive decline, often affects an affected individual’s communication ability and daily functioning (Cipriani et al., 2020). In individuals with dementia, cognitive impairment may hinder the ability to effectively communicate (express) pain, complicating the assessment and management of chronic pain (Achterberg et al., 2020). As a result, individuals with dementia are at risk of experiencing pain that goes unrecognized or undertreated. In addition to cognitive impairment, individuals with dementia often experience mood disturbances, including depression and anxiety, which further complicate their condition and care (Cravello et al., 2019).

The interaction between chronic pain and dementia is especially concerning because of its potential to amplify preexisting psychological distress (Tian et al., 2023). In patients with dementia, the presence of chronic pain may exacerbate depression and anxiety, creating a vicious cycle that worsens both cognitive and psychological status (El-Tallawy et al., 2023). Given the complexities of diagnosing and managing both pain and mood disturbances in dementia, individuals with dementia may be at heightened risk for severe psychological outcomes, including suicidal ideation and attempts (Álvarez Muñoz et al., 2020).

Suicidality risk among individuals with dementia is an emerging public health concern that warrants greater clinical and research attention. Recent studies have identified that people with dementia are more likely to experience suicidal thoughts, with a pooled point prevalence of approximately 10% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 6%–16%) (Desai et al., 2024). In comparison, suicidality in the general older adult population is typically lower, highlighting the increased vulnerability among people with dementia. The risk of suicidality is particularly pronounced within the first year following diagnosis, especially among those diagnosed at a younger age (under 65 years) and those with comorbid psychiatric conditions (Choi et al., 2021; Schmutte et al., 2022). Moreover, suicidality varies across dementia subtypes. For example, individuals with vascular dementia have been reported to have higher odds of suicidal ideation (odds ratio [OR] = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.06–3.80) and suicide attempts (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.28–2.94) compared with those with Alzheimer’s disease, with similar patterns observed in other dementia subtypes (Nuzum et al., 2025).

This study aimed to investigate associations between chronic pain and the risk of suicidality in hospitalized patients with dementia using data from the U.S. Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS). Although prior studies have demonstrated associations between chronic pain and suicidality in the general population, evidence specifically focusing on patients with dementia remains limited, particularly in large, nationally representative inpatient cohorts. Based on previous evidence linking chronic pain to increased psychological distress and suicidality risk in the general population (Álvarez Muñoz et al., 2020; El-Tallawy et al., 2023), we hypothesized that chronic pain would be positively associated with suicidality among patients with dementia. Ultimately, study findings may help to inform targeted interventions and enhance mental health support in dementia care.

Method

Study design, data source, and ethics

This population-based, retrospective observational study extracted all patient data from the NIS database, the largest all-payer, continuous inpatient care database in the United States, encompassing approximately 8 million hospital stays annually. Managed by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP)—a division of the US National Institutes of Health (NIH), the NIS provides detailed patient information, including primary and secondary diagnoses, procedures, admission and discharge status, demographics, insurance/payment sources, hospital length of stay (LOS), and hospital characteristics (e.g., bed count [hospital size], location, teaching status, and region). The NIS database, updated annually, collects data from roughly 1,050 hospitals across 44 states, representing a 20% stratified sample of US community hospitals, as defined by the American Hospital Association. All data were obtained through a request to the Online HCUP Central Distributor (available at https://www.distributor.hcup-us.ahrq.gov/), which administers the database. This study conforms to the NIS data-use agreement with HCUP. Because this study analyzed data from the NIS database, individuals and the public were not directly involved, which exempts further ethics approval by authors’ institution (Taipei Medical University-Shuang Ho Hospital). Since all data in the NIS database are de-identified, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study population, chronic pain, and covariates

All hospitalizations of adults aged ≥60 years were identified from NIS between 2016 and 2020. Among these, hospitalizations with any listed diagnosis of dementia were included in the initial cohort, as determined using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth edition (ICD-10) diagnostic code listed in Table S1. Hospitalizations with incomplete demographic information, missing covariates, or unavailable sampling weights were excluded. The final analytic cohort comprised dementia-related hospitalizations.

Because the NIS is a discharge-level dataset that lacks unique patient identifiers, each record corresponds to a single hospitalization rather than a longitudinal patient history. Consequently, all diagnostic entries, including those for dementia, chronic pain, and suicidality, represent conditions documented during the same hospitalization, without information on events occurring before admission or after discharge. In addition, the same individual may be included more than once due to repeated hospitalizations. To account for the complex survey design and potential non-independence of observations, all analyses incorporated NIS-provided sampling weights, stratification, and clustering using survey procedures, which provide robust variance estimates at the population level.

This study evaluated two primary outcomes: suicide attempts and suicidal ideation, identified using validated ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes from hospital discharge records. Participants were categorized into two groups based on the presence or absence of chronic pain, as determined using ICD-10-CM codes. This approach aligns with standard practices in claims database research and is consistent with methodologies used in previous studies (Olayinka et al., 2024; Orhurhu et al., 2019; Patel et al., 2020; Salihu et al., 2022). The specific diagnostic codes used are detailed in Table S1.

Patients’ demographic characteristics included age, sex, race/ethnicity, household income, insurance status (primary payer), admission type, and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI). Insurance status was categorized according to the primary payer variable in the NIS database. “Medicare” and “Medicaid” represent federal public insurance programs, whereas “other or no insurance” includes individuals with private or employer-based insurance, as well as self-paying and other categories, as defined by the HCUP NIS database documentation. This category is predominantly composed of privately insured individuals, with a smaller proportion of self-pay or uninsured patients. Among clinical characteristics, types of dementia included vascular dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and unspecified. Comorbidities included were: Diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), epilepsy, anemia, and chronic kidney disease (CKD), confirmed by the corresponding diagnostic codes (Bunn et al., 2014; DiBlasi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2017). Hospital-related characteristics such as bed numbers, location/teaching status, and hospital region were extracted from the database as part of the comprehensive data available for all participants. Psychiatric conditions, such as depression and anxiety disorders, were not included as covariates in the primary models. These conditions may lie on the causal pathway between chronic pain and suicidality and were therefore considered potential mediators rather than confounders. Adjusting for such variables could result in overadjustment and underestimation of the true association.

Statistical analysis

The NIS database includes a 20% sample of annual inpatient admissions in the United States. Weighted samples (DISCWT), stratification (NIS_STRATUM), and clustering (HOSPID) were applied to generate national estimates for all analyses (available at https://hcup-us.ahrq.gov/tech_assist/nationalestimates/508_course/508course_2018.jsp#weights). The SURVEY procedure in SAS was used to analyze sample survey data. Descriptive statistics are generated using PROC SURVEYMEANS and PROC SURVEYFREQ, and are presented as numbers (n) with weighted percentages (%), or as means and standard errors (SEs). Group comparisons used the Rao–Scott chi-square test for categorical data and linear models for continuous data.

To minimize selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:4 ratio, using age (continuous), sex, race, household income, admission type, type of dementia, hospital location/teaching status, hospital region, hypertension, COPD, anemia, and CKD as the matching variables. The matching process was performed using the SAS OneToManyMTCH macro (Parsons, 2004) to balance the baseline characteristics. The method prioritizes “best” matches first and then proceeds with “next-best” matches until no more can be made. The balance of covariates before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values less than 0.1 considered indicative of good balance.

Logistic regression analyses were conducted using PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC to calculate ORs and 95% CIs to examine associations between chronic pain and suicide-related outcomes, adjusting for covariates with significance after PSM. In addition, analyses on the impact of chronic pain on suicide-related outcomes were performed stratified by age, type of dementia, CCI, race, insurance status, and sex. All p-values were 2-sided, and p < .05 was established as statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software package SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient selection

The study population selection process is depicted in Figure 1. The data of a total of 86,716 hospitalized patients aged ≥60 years whose primary diagnosis was dementia were extracted from the NIS database from 2016 to 2020 and were included as the analytic sample. Patients with missing information on sex, race, household income, insurance status, and admission type (n = 5,071) were excluded. Finally, 81,645 patients were included in the analyses. After matching, 29,185 remained for subsequent analyses, consisting of 5,837 patients with chronic pain and 23,348 patients without chronic pain. This study sample can be extrapolated to a total of 145,924 hospitalized patients in the entire US after weighting (Figure 1).

Age

Analysis stratified by patients’ age revealed that associations between chronic pain and suicidal ideation weakened as age advanced. The strongest impact of chronic pain on suicidal ideation was observed in the younger group of older adult inpatients aged 60–69 (aOR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.27–2.22, p < .001), while the effect was weakest, among those aged 80 and older (aOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.15–1.76, p < .001). We also observed a significantly higher odds of suicide attempt among patients aged 60–69 (aOR = 3.19, 95% CI: 1.08–9.40, p = .035) (Table 3).

CCI

Among patients with CCI ≥ 1, the association between chronic pain and suicidal ideation appeared stronger (aOR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.31–1.80, p < .001) compared to that observed in patients with CCI = 0 (aOR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.08–1.74, p = .009). We also found that patients with chronic pain had a higher odds of suicide attempt in CCI 1+ (aOR = 2.86, 95% CI: 1.39–5.91, p = .005) (Table 3).

Insurance status/primary payer

Among patients insured by Medicare/Medicaid, the association between chronic pain and suicidal ideation appeared weaker (aOR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.27–1.68, p < .001) compared to that observed in patients with other types of insurance (aOR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.22–2.70, p = .004). Additionally, chronic pain was associated with significantly higher odds of suicide attempts among patients insured by Medicare/Medicaid (aOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.01–3.81, p = .048) (Table 3).

Sex

Sex-stratified analyses revealed that chronic pain was significantly associated with suicidal ideation among males (aOR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.37–2.06, p < .001). A similar association was also observed in female patients (aOR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.15–1.64, p < .001). In addition, chronic pain was significantly associated with higher odds of suicide attempt among female patients (aOR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.05–4.94, p = .036), compared to those without chronic pain (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that older adult in-patients with chronic pain had a significantly higher risk of suicidal ideation compared to those without chronic pain. Although the association with suicide attempts did not consistently reach statistical significance, this is likely attributable to the small number of documented events and limited statistical power. Stratified analyses indicated that the associations between chronic pain and suicidal ideation were influenced by age, comorbidity burden, and insurance status, with relatively stronger effects observed among inpatient adults aged 60–69 years, those with higher CCI, and those without Medicare/Medicaid insurance coverage. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive pain and mental health assessment in all patients with dementia, with heightened clinical awareness in potentially more vulnerable subgroups.

Chronic pain is a prevalent but frequently under-recognized condition in people living with dementia, with studies estimating that approximately 57%–58% of this population experience chronic pain, substantially higher than previously assumed (Kerckhove et al., 2023). Experimental and neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that individuals with Alzheimer’s disease retain the capacity to perceive pain and may experience comparable or even greater affective unpleasantness than cognitively intact older adults, despite exhibiting higher sensory thresholds for pain detection. Disrupted functional connectivity and altered activation in pain-modulating regions, including the periaqueductal gray, further challenge the misconception that individuals with dementia experience less pain (Anderson et al., 2021, 2022; Monroe et al., 2017; Romano et al., 2021). Notably, a substantial proportion of patients remain inadequately treated despite the availability of structured guidance for pain assessment in individuals unable to self-report, as outlined in the updated ASPMN 2024 position statement (Herr et al., 2024). Despite the known under-recognition of pain in individuals with dementia, the use of standardized diagnostic coding in a large, nationally representative inpatient database allows for the identification of clinically recognized cases of chronic pain, thereby capturing a subset of patients with significant symptom burden. Although underreporting may lead to conservative estimates, the observed associations are likely to reflect meaningful clinical relationships in real-world hospitalized populations.

In individuals with established cognitive impairment, chronic pain does not appear to play a causal role in the development of dementia but rather contributes to the worsening of psychological and behavioral outcomes, including distress, agitation, and hopelessness, which may increase vulnerability to suicidal ideation (Álvarez Muñoz et al., 2020; El-Tallawy et al., 2023). Importantly, although prior studies have suggested that chronic pain may increase the risk of developing cognitive impairment or dementia in the general population, these findings pertain to disease onset (Tian et al., 2023). In contrast, our study focuses on individuals with preexisting dementia, in whom chronic pain is more likely to influence downstream psychological and behavioral outcomes rather than cognitive decline itself. Previous studies have shown that suicidality risk is elevated in patients with chronic pain, particularly when comorbid depression is present (Orhurhu et al., 2019; Racine, 2018). Likewise, dementia itself has been independently associated with an increased risk of suicidal ideation, especially among younger individuals, males, and those with moderate-stage disease (Desai et al., 2024). Among dementia subtypes, vascular dementia has been linked to a greater neuropsychiatric symptom burden and higher odds of suicidal ideation (Nuzum et al., 2025).

Mechanistically, chronic pain may exacerbate vulnerability to suicidality through multiple pathways. Impaired communication in dementia limits effective pain expression and recognition, particularly in interactions with caregivers and healthcare providers, thereby increasing psychological distress (Banovic et al., 2018). Pain-related disruption of sleep, physical functioning, and daily activities further contributes to depressive and anxiety symptoms, which are well-established predictors of suicidal thoughts (Banovic et al., 2018; Haack et al., 2020). In addition, chronic pain is associated with neurobiological alterations, including dysregulation of stress response systems and neurotransmitter imbalances, which may heighten suicidal vulnerability (Yang & Chang, 2019). These findings emphasize that in people living with dementia, chronic pain represents a critical and potentially modifiable factor influencing mental health outcomes and overall QoL.

In the present study, older adult inpatients, particularly the younger group aged 60–69 years, those with comorbidities, and without Medicare/Medicaid insurance, exhibited higher odds of suicidal ideation. Although these associations warrant further investigation, the 60–69-year subgroup demonstrated the relatively strongest association, which may reflect greater awareness of cognitive decline, diminished resilience, and intensified psychological distress. This pattern aligns with prior evidence showing that individuals aged 50–69 years with dementia have an eight- to tenfold higher risk of suicide compared with age-matched peers without dementia, while the risk is approximately threefold in those aged ≥70 years (Erlangsen et al., 2008; Haw et al., 2009; Schmutte et al., 2022). Among the older patients (≥80 years), the associations remained significant but appeared attenuated, possibly due to under-recognition or underreporting of distress. In addition, higher CCI scores were associated with a stronger relationship between chronic pain and suicidality, likely reflecting the cumulative burden of physical and psychological comorbidities. Insurance patterns among older adults in the United States are complex and heterogeneous. Many individuals receiving Medicare are also covered by supplemental private insurance, which may facilitate access to specialized services, whereas reliance on Medicaid alone may limit provider availability (Tipirneni et al., 2025). Therefore, the observed differences by insurance status may reflect variability in healthcare access rather than a uniform effect of insurance type, underscoring the need for future studies incorporating more granular measures of coverage and care availability.

Assessment of pain and suicidality in individuals with dementia remains a critical but challenging component of care. Structured tools such as the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) or the Geriatric Suicide Ideation Scale (GSIS) have shown feasibility when adapted for older adults with cognitive impairment (Heisel et al., 2002; Ryan & Oquendo, 2020). Observational tools, including the Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia (PAINAD) and the Pain Assessment in Impaired Cognition (PAIC15) scales, provide valuable approaches for evaluating pain in individuals who are unable to self-report (Kunz et al., 2020; Warden et al., 2003). Expert guidance for pain assessment in non-verbal or cognitively impaired patients is available, as outlined in the updated ASPMN 2024 position statement (Herr et al., 2024), although these recommendations remain inconsistently implemented in clinical practice, contributing to the persistent undertreatment of pain in dementia.

Following appropriate assessment, management strategies increasingly emphasize comprehensive, multimodal approaches that address both psychological and physical dimensions of distress. Collaborative care models that integrate primary care, psychiatry, and pain specialists have demonstrated effectiveness in managing complex geriatric needs and could be tailored for dementia populations (Bruce et al., 2004; Ng et al., 2020). In addition, non-pharmacological interventions, including music therapy, massage, reflexology, sensory stimulation, personalized activity programs, and cognitive-behavioral therapy, have been shown to reduce pain, agitation, and suicidal ideation in patients with dementia (Abraha et al., 2017; Liao et al., 2021). When feasible, exercise programs tailored to cognitive and physical ability may also be beneficial. Pharmacologically, scheduled acetaminophen is recommended as the first-line agent, while NSAIDs should be avoided due to their side effects, and opioids should be used cautiously with close monitoring (Husebo et al., 2011). Although concerns about opioid dependence often lead to undertreatment, appropriately monitored opioid therapy remains an important component of pain control in selected patients (Rekatsina et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2021). These approaches support the implementation of individualized, interdisciplinary care models that may help reduce suicidality and improve QoL in dementia care.

Findings of the present study highlight the need for integrated care that addresses both chronic pain and mental health in hospitalized older adult patients with dementia. Among the adults with dementia, all patients warrant comprehensive pain assessment and integrated mental health care. Nonetheless, the present findings suggest that younger of the older adults (aged 60–69 years) and those with multiple comorbidities may merit additional vigilance and tailored interventions, such as regular mental health screening and individualized pain management strategies. These efforts may improve patient outcomes and reduce the risk of suicidality in older adult inpatients with dementia. The observed associations may differ in patients with dementia residing in community settings or living with caregivers, who generally have less severe disease and potentially greater social support. In such populations, the impact of chronic pain on suicidality may be attenuated; however, under-recognition and undertreatment of pain remain important concerns. Future studies are needed to evaluate these associations across different care settings and levels of disease severity.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Using the NIS, the largest all-payer inpatient database in the United States, provided a large and nationally representative cohort of hospitalized older adults with dementia. The use of validated ICD-10 codes for chronic pain, suicidality, and comorbidities aligns with established claims-based research practices. In addition, the stratified analyses offered clinically relevant insights into how chronic pain may differentially influence suicidality across key demographic and clinical subgroups. Several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, only 16 suicide attempts were documented among patients with chronic pain, resulting in very low event counts that limit statistical power and the precision of these estimates; thus, findings related to suicide attempts should be interpreted cautiously. Second, all diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes, which may lead to misclassification or underreporting, particularly because suicidality is not routinely screened during hospitalization. Third, the NIS lacks detailed clinical information, including pain intensity, chronicity, management strategies, and validated measures of depression, anxiety, or psychosocial status, restricting our ability to account for important confounders. Fourth, variability in dementia and pain management practices across hospitals may introduce unmeasured heterogeneity. Finally, because the NIS is a discharge-level, cross-sectional database without outpatient or longitudinal follow-up, temporal relationships between chronic pain and suicidality cannot be established, and causality cannot be inferred. Future longitudinal studies are needed with detailed clinical data on pain management and intensity, psychosocial assessments, and standardized care protocols to help inform targeted interventions for hospitalized patients with dementia.

Conclusions

Results of the present study suggest an association between chronic pain and an increased risk of suicidal ideation in older adult inpatients with dementia, particularly those aged 60–69 years and those with comorbidities or insurance other than Medicare/Medicaid. These findings emphasize the critical importance of integrating systematic pain assessment into the routine clinical management of patients with dementia, together with timely mental health screening and multidisciplinary, patient-centered care strategies. While comprehensive assessment and treatment should be provided to all individuals with dementia, heightened clinical awareness is warranted for subgroups that may be at elevated risk. Further longitudinal research is still necessary to confirm these associations and to better understand the underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic strategies to mitigate these risks.
