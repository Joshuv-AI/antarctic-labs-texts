Appendico‐Ileal Knotting; Case Series in Ethiopia
Authors: Tewodros Tadesse Tessema, Murtii Teressa Obolu, Tadesse Getachew Mulisa, Bitsue Girma Tilahun, Ermias Tadesse Woldegiorgis, Yared Assefa Beyene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAppendico‐ileal knotting is an extremely rare complication of acute appendicitis, leading to obstruction when the inflammed appendiceal shaft wraps around the adjacent ileal segment and its mesentery. Diagnosis requires high clinical suspicion and urgent surgical exploration. Based on the extent of appendiceal base gangrene, en bloc ileal resection with appendectomy or right hemicolectomy is the mainstay of treatment to prevent systemic bacterial or toxin spread and contamination.

Introduction

Appendico‐ileal knotting is a rare complication of acute appendicitis resulting in closed‐loop obstruction. The term “knotting” describes a condition where a segment of one hollow or solid organ wraps around an adjacent organ, causing complete obstruction. In appendico‐ileal knotting, the long appendiceal shaft wraps around the base of the distal ileal mesentery, creating a closed‐loop obstruction. While ileo‐sigmoid knotting is the most common form, appendico‐ileal knotting is exceptionally rare, with only a few documented cases in the literature and no comprehensive reviews to date.

Globally, small bowel obstruction remains one of the most common indications for abdominal exploratory surgery. The majority of mechanical small bowel obstructions are caused by postoperative adhesions and volvulus. However, < 1% of cases presenting to emergency settings result from rare conditions such as appendico‐ileal knotting. Despite its rarity, this condition requires urgent attention due to its potential for rapid progression to ischemia and gangrene, making it a surgical emergency [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

The exact mechanisms underlying visceral organ knotting remain unclear. Proposed factors include organ redundancy, inflammatory adhesions, hyperperistalsis triggered by rapid food intake, and wandering solid organs. Once knotting occurs, it leads to a closed‐loop obstruction syndrome, rapidly compromising blood flow and resulting in strangulation and gangrene of the affected bowel segment [6, 7, 8, 9]. Due to its nonspecific presentation, imaging modalities like CT scans may suggest a transition point but are not definitive. Diagnosis requires a high index of clinical suspicion and is confirmed intraoperatively [9, 10, 11].

Management of appendico‐ileal knotting typically involves en bloc resection of the gangrenous bowel [12, 13]. The choice between the ileal resection with appendectomy and a right hemicolectomy depends on the extent of appendiceal base involvement and the condition of the surrounding bowel. Given the closed‐loop pathology, most patients present at a stage when the knotted bowel segment is already gangrenous, necessitating prompt surgical intervention to prevent systemic bacterial or toxin spread and contamination and to improve outcomes [14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Case Presentation

Case 1

History and Physical Examination

A 17‐year‐old female patient presented with acute, severe, non‐radiating, crampy abdominal pain associated with failure to pass feces and flatus, progressive mild abdominal distension, frequent bilious vomiting, and a low‐grade fever of 2 days' duration. Otherwise, there is no history of trauma or prior abdominal surgery. Otherwise, there is no pertinent past medical and surgical history.

On physical examination, critically ill‐looking. Vital signs: pulse rate (110–124) beats/min, respiratory rate (20–24) breaths/min, blood pressure (90/56–100/71) mmHg, temperature (37.9–38.3)°C, and SO2 (95–97)% with atmospheric air. Pertinent findings were on the head‐ear‐eye‐nose‐throat; dry buccal mucosa. Chest: clear bilateral chest. CVS; S1 & S2: Well, heard no murmur, no gallop. Abdomen: Abdomen not moving with respiration flexed hip supine position with guarding and rigidity, decreased bowel sound, diffuse tenderness all over the abdomen, empty rectum on digital examination.

Investigations

Laboratory blood workups: complete blood count—white blood cell count—24 × 103 unit/μL, neutrophil predominance of 85%, Hgb—12.1 g/decilitre, platelet—301 × 103 unit/μl, serum creatinine—1.2 mg. Plain abdominal X‐ray showed centrally located multiple air‐fluid levels and absent rectal gas shadow, suggestive of complete small bowel obstruction (Figure 1A).

Management Outcomes and Follow Ups

After all possible investigations and preoperative optimization and hemodynamic correction, the parents were counseled by the surgeon for the exploratory laparotomy. With an impression of peritonitis secondary to gangrenous small bowel obstruction secondary to small bowel volvulus. Unfortunately, intraoperatively, the finding upon midline exploration was frankly gangrenous distal ileum, approximately 1000 mL of haemorrhagic reactive fluid in the general peritoneum, dilated proximal and collapsed distal bowel, and an appendix wrapped double round at the mesenteric base of the distal ileum (Figure 2), causing a closed loop obstruction of the distal ileum. Wrapped long appendiceal shaft and inflammed appendix at the tip (Figure 3).

Intraoperatively, the patient was hemodynamically stable. The identified appendico‐ileal knotting was examined; en bloc resection of the gangrenous ileal segment, appendectomy, and ileo‐transverse end‐to‐end anastomosis were done (Figure 4). Peritoneum was thoroughly lavaged with warm normal saline and the abdomen closed in layers. Postoperatively, the patient was on broad‐spectrum antibiotics and regular analgesia and started feeding on the third post‐op day and advanced after she tolerated feeding. Subsequently, she developed a deep surgical site infection, which was treated with wound care and delayed primary repair, and she was discharged on the 10th postoperative day with better improvement and doing well at the 3‐month follow‐up.

Case 2

History and Physical Examination

A 54‐year‐old female patient presented with intermittent colicky abdominal pain of 1 week, which worsened over the last 3 days. Associated with this, she had a history of vomiting ingested matter 2–3 times per day, which later became bilious with similar frequency. The pain worsened, and she had associated failure to pass feces and flatus for a day's duration before presentation to our hospital. She had a history of a Caesarean section 17 years back but had never had a similar complaint in the past. She has no known comorbidities, and she had an active lifestyle. For this complaint, she went to a nearby private hospital and was given unspecified intravenous antibiotics and a proton pump inhibitor.

During the objective physical evaluation at the emergency department, she was acutely sick‐looking and in pain, and her pulse rate was 115 beats per minute, and it was feable. The blood pressure was borderline with a measurement of 90/60 mmHg. She had dry buccal mucosa, and her abdomen was grossly distended. There was diffuse tenderness all over the abdomen, and there were hypoactive bowel sounds. On percussion, the abdomen was hyper tympanic, and there was a sign of fluid collection. On the digital rectal examination, there was stool on the examining finger.

Investigations

She was investigated with a complete blood count; white blood cell count of 11,400 unit/μl, with neutrophil predominance of 98.2%, hemoglobin of 14.1 g/decilitre, hematocrit of 44.4%, platelet count of 352,000 unit/μL, and serum creatinine of 1.2 mg. She was also investigated with ultrasound and a plain erect abdominal X‐ray. The ultrasound suggested the presence of small bowel obstruction with peritoneal fluid collection. The erect X‐ray showed dilated small bowel loops and multiple centrally located air‐fluid levels with absent rectal air shadow (Figure 1B).

Management Outcomes and Follow Ups

A double intravenous line was secured, and she was resuscitated with two bags of normal saline, a nasogastric tube, and a urinary catheter was inserted. Intravenous antibiotics (metronidazole and ceftriaxone) were started, and intravenous analgesics (tramadol) were initiated. After resuscitation, the pulse rate decreased to 95 beats per minute, and the blood pressure was 110/80 mmHg. The patient produced adequate urine output and was taken to the operating theater. Intraoperatively, there was one liter of haemorrhagic fluid in the general peritoneum and an appendico‐ileal knotting involving 90 cm of distal ileum (Figure 5), which was frankly gangrenous. The distal ileum, including the ileocecal region, was gangrenous (Figure 6). A retrograde appendectomy was done, and then after the appendix was released from the distal ileum, the gangrenous segment of distal ileum was resected and ileocolic anastomosis was done. Postoperatively, she had a smooth postoperative course up to the seventh day, at which point she had a superficial surgical site infection. She was on wound care up to the 14th day and was discharged improved and doing well on subsequent follow‐ups.

Differential Diagnosis

Among all strangulated mechanical small bowel obstructions, the closed loop syndrome is the most devastating mechanism, leading to fast progression to gangrenous bowel. All causes of mechanical small bowel obstruction that resulted in strangulation are potentially a differential diagnosis. Particularly if bowel knotting is a high index of clinical suspicion, mechanical bowel obstruction such as ileo‐sigmoid, ileo‐ileal knotting, and spleno‐sigmoid should be considered as a top differential diagnosis for appendico‐ileal knotting.

Discussion

Appendico‐ileal knotting, a rare but severe complication of acute appendicitis, is an exceedingly uncommon cause of mechanical small bowel obstruction. This condition, first documented in 1901 and followed by further reports in 1945, remains a rarely encountered pathology, with recent cases continuing to underscore its rarity. Reported in < 1% of bowel obstruction cases, this complication has been highlighted by many authors [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The rarity of this condition and its potential for rapid progression to sepsis and bowel necrosis make early recognition and intervention critical. As a complication of acute appendicitis, the inflamed appendiceal tip wraps around the base of the distal ileal mesentery, causing a closed‐loop obstruction [7, 8, 9, 10]. The resulting mechanical compression of the ileum restricts blood flow, leading to bowel strangulation and gangrene, which require immediate surgical intervention.

Although the exact mechanisms responsible for the knotting of visceral organs are not fully understood, several factors have been proposed in the literature. These include anatomical predispositions, such as the redundancy of the mesentery, inflammatory adhesions, wandering solid organs, and hyperperistalsis induced by rapid food load [11, 12, 13, 14]. The combination of these factors creates a scenario where the appendix can become adherent to the ileal mesentery, and subsequent twisting of the ileum causes mechanical obstruction. Studies also suggest that inflammation from appendicitis may increase adhesions, contributing to the formation of a knot, as seen in reports of both adult and pediatric cases [15, 16]. This condition results in a closed‐loop obstruction, rapidly compromising the blood supply to the involved bowel segment, leading to ischemia, gangrene, and a high risk of sepsis if not addressed promptly.

Clinically, patients with appendico‐ileal knotting present with severe, disproportionate, crampy abdominal pain, which can often be confused with more common causes of small bowel obstruction. The pain is typically sudden in onset and may rapidly progress to signs of peritonitis as ischemia and necrosis set in. Due to its rarity and nonspecific presentation, appendico‐ileal knotting requires a high index of clinical suspicion. Radiological imaging, including X‐rays showing signs of bowel obstruction and CT scans revealing a transition point, can suggest the presence of closed‐loop obstruction, but a definitive diagnosis is usually made only through intraoperative exploration [16, 17, 18]. This makes early surgical intervention all the more critical to avoid delays that could result in irreversible damage to the bowel.

Management of appendico‐ileal knotting involves prompt surgical exploration, with the primary goal of addressing the mechanical obstruction and preventing further complications. The surgical approach typically involves en bloc resection of the affected ileum, along with appendectomy. In more severe cases, particularly when the gangrenous bowel involves a broader portion of the small intestine, right hemicolectomy may be necessary to ensure complete removal of necrotic tissue and prevent systemic bacterial dissemination [17, 18]. Given the pathophysiology of closed‐loop obstruction, by the time the patient presents for surgery, the affected bowel segment is often gangrenous, making it crucial to address the condition expeditiously. Studies have shown that timely surgical intervention can effectively prevent systemic sepsis, which is one of the most serious risks associated with appendico‐ileal knotting. Without prompt resection, gangrenous bowel leads to widespread infection, sepsis, and a significantly worsened prognosis.

In conclusion, appendico‐ileal knotting is a rare but serious complication of acute appendicitis that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with signs of strangulated bowel obstruction. Due to its rarity, this condition requires a high level of clinical suspicion and rapid intervention. Early diagnosis, coupled with prompt surgical resection—either en bloc ileal resection with appendectomy or right hemicolectomy—offers the best chance of managing complications and controlling systemic sepsis. Timely surgical intervention is paramount to improving patient outcomes and preventing life‐threatening complications. The rarity of appendico‐ileal knotting, combined with its potential for rapid deterioration, underscores the importance of vigilant clinical awareness and swift surgical intervention. This case series highlights the significance of recognizing this condition early, contributing to the body of knowledge that guides clinicians in effectively managing this life‐threatening complication.

Conclusion

In patients suspected of having strangulated bowel obstruction, appendico‐ileal knotting should be considered as a differential diagnosis. Due to its rarity, a high index of clinical suspicion is essential. Early surgical intervention is crucial to prevent systemic sepsis caused by gangrenous bowel. Surgical intervention involves en bloc ileal resection with appendectomy or right hemicolectomy, depending on the extent of gangrene in the appendiceal base. Timely intervention is key to preventing further complications and ensuring better outcomes.
