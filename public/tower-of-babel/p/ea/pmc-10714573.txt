Arthroscopic Assisted, Lateral Ligament Reconstruction with Suture Tape Augmentation and Knotless All Suture Anchors: A Technique Guide
Authors: David P Pettit, Vikas Munjal, Paul M Alvarez, Tyler Barker, Kevin D Martin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractMore than 30,000 ankle sprains occur each day in the United States, and the majority of ankle sprains involve the anterior talofibular ligament. Up to 30% of patients develop functional ankle instability and chronic pain after a severe ankle sprain. When nonoperative measures are unsuccessful, operative reconstruction of the lateral ankle ligaments is recommended. To further strengthen the repair, accelerate rehabilitation, and allow for a quicker return to sport, augmentation with suture tape has recently become an alternative among surgeons in the reconstruction of the lateral ankle ligaments. Moreover, the advent of knotless all-suture anchors decreases the number of knots required in the procedure and, in theory, reduces lateral soft tissue irritation and accentuates recovery after surgery. Here we present our technique for arthroscopic assisted, lateral ligament reconstruction with suture tape augmentation and knotless all suture anchors.

The majority of ankle sprains involve the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), and more than 30,000 ankle sprains occur each day in the United States.1 Ankle sprains account for one third of all sports-related injuries,2 and up to 30% of patients can develop chronic pain after a severe ankle sprain and functional ankle instability.3 Cartilage damage can result from this instability and lead to the development of post-traumatic ankle arthritis.4,5 The majority of these injuries heal with nonoperative management, consisting of a period of rest, bracing, and functional ankle rehab to focus on proprioception and strengthening. When nonoperative measures are unsuccessful, operative reconstruction of the lateral ankle ligaments is recommended.

Historically, nonanatomic tenodesis procedures were performed to stabilize the lateral ankle, but there has been a shift to the more anatomic Broström procedure.6 The Broström procedure involves direct ligament repair to the distal fibula, but the quality of the repair is often limited by the amount of damage to the native tissue. Because of this, suture tape augmentation has been added to the procedure to allow for early rehabilitation and increased strength.7,8 With traditional suture anchor techniques, after repair, there are many prominent knots of nonabsorbable suture. The prominent knots are hypothesized to be a source of irritation and pain. With the advent of knotless suture anchors, we began using these devices at our institution to decrease the number of knots in the lateral ankle soft tissues. In this report, we describe our technique for arthroscopic assisted, lateral ligament reconstruction with suture tape augmentation and knotless all-suture anchors.

Surgical Technique

Indications for suture augmented lateral ankle ligament reconstruction include lateral ankle instability after failed nonoperative management of rest, bracing, and physical therapy. Patients must demonstrate both functional and mechanical instability. Patients often report rolling their ankle frequently, difficulty walking on uneven ground, difficulty going downstairs, and instability with cutting activities. Physical examination will demonstrate increased laxity when compared to the contralateral side with the anterior drawer and talar tilt tests. Occasionally, we will be more aggressive with proceeding directly to surgery in a high-level, cutting athlete, with a positive magnetic resonance imaging scan and an osteochondral lesion of the talus.

General contraindications to anterolateral ligament reconstruction include but are not limited to uncontrolled diabetes, neuropathy, active infections, and morbid obesity. Furthermore, the surgeon should be familiar and proficient in ankle arthroscopy. They should also be able to address any intra-articular pathology that is encountered.

Diagnostic ankle arthroscopy is performed with the patient under general anesthesia or intravenous sedation with supplementary regional nerve block, per surgeon preference and associated injuries. The patient is positioned supine on a regular operating room table with the heels near the edge of the bed. A short bone foam bump under the calf and knee of the operative extremity are then placed, ensuring complete mobility of the ankle and elevation of the heel. A well-padded thigh tourniquet is applied and inflated to 250 mm Hg. The leg is prepped and draped distally starting at the knee. Traction is not routinely used, but an external positioning device, such as the Trimano (Arthrex, Naples, FL), is available if needed.9 A 4.0 mm, 30° arthroscope is standard for ankle arthroscopy; however, a malleable 1.9 mm arthroscope (NanoScope; Arthrex) can further reduce the need for distraction. Standard arthroscopic pump pressure is 30 mm Hg with a high flow cannula. A thorough diagnostic examination is performed (Fig 1; 0:10 in Video 1), and then any intra-articular pathology is addressed.

After ankle arthroscopy and addressing any intra-articular pathology, we then proceed with the lateral ligament reconstruction. An oblique longitudinal incision from the central distal fibula toward the fourth metatarsal is made (0:23 in Video 1). In patients with peroneal pathology, a slightly longer proximal limb is made.

Next, a 1 cm periosteal cuff of tissue is elevated from the tip of the fibula to 2 cm proximal, following the anterolateral portion of the fibula. The residual ATFL and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) are sharply released (Fig 2A) (0:35 in Video 1). The distal fibula is then prepared with a rongeur and rasp to establish a bleeding bone, ensuring the cortical ridge remains for the repair site. The proximal band of the ATFL is sharply followed down to its insertion on the lateral wall of the talus. Electrocautery is used to mark the site just off the articular cartilage margin, and the site is drilled and tapped (0:49 in Video 1). A 4.5 mm anchor (SwiveLock; Arthrex) is inserted, and a free needle is used to pass the suture tape superficially to the ATFL (Fig 2; 0:57 in Video 1). A corresponding hole is drilled at 12 mm from the tip of the fibula for the suture-tape. Two knotless 2.4 mm suture anchors (SutureTak; Arthrex) are then inserted, one at 6 mm from the tip of the fibula for the CFL and one at 18 mm from the tip for the AFTL (Fig 3; 1:17 in Video 1). The CFL (distal cuff) is then reapproximated to the fibula by placing a horizontal mattress stitch with the knotless mechanism (Fig 4A; 1:48 in Video 1). With the ankle in a neutral position, the sutures are shuttled pulling in line with the position drilled, the surgeon will feel the knotless mechanism flip and lock as the tissue is reapproximated. The same steps are repeated for the ATFL anchor, ensuring that suture placement pulls the cuff proximal and restores the footprint (Figs 4B, 4C). No knots are needed, and the excess suture material is cut short. Next, the suture tape is measured and inserted into the fibula with a 3.5 mm anchor (SwiveLock; Arthrex), while being sure not to overtighten the reconstruction (Figs 5A, 5B; 3:32 in Video 1).

The periosteal flap is then imbricated (pants-over-vest) to the inferior extensor retinaculum with absorbable suture covering the suture-tape (Fig 5C; 4:29 in Video 1). The skin is closed with nonabsorbable suture, a local anesthetic is injected, and a soft dressing with a compression wrap is applied followed by a postoperative boot.

All anterior ankle arthroscopic procedures carry a risk of injury to the superficial peroneal nerve.10,11 Our procedure as described herein requires the establishment of a standard anterolateral portal, which could cause iatrogenic injury to the superficial peroneal nerve. Another potential complication is overtensioning the suture tape, which could result in lateral sided tightness and decreased ankle range of motion.

After surgery, patients wear a boot the first 10 to 14 days after surgery and are only weightbearing during transfers. After the sutures are removed at the first postoperative visit (∼X-X days after surgery), patients are placed in a compression sock (10-15 mm Hg) and a postoperative boot. Weightbearing as tolerated is initiated in the boot. Formal physical therapy and a home exercise program are initiated two weeks after surgery. The boot is discontinued at 3 to 4 weeks or as soon as a nonantalgic gait can be maintained. The patients are then weaned to a simple ankle lace-up brace. Stationary recumbent biking and water therapy are strongly recommended and can be used as a pause in rehabilitation if pain and swelling persist. At postoperative weeks 4-6, elliptical trainer and treadmill walking are initiated. Single-leg proprioception and heel raises are paramount, in combination with closed-chain lunges and squats. At week 6, a walk-to-run program and basic agility activities (jumping) are initiated if the patient can maintain a nonantalgic gait while performing a 20-minute treadmill-walk evaluation by physical therapy. Impact and agility activities are initially restricted and gradually introduced under the care of a therapist.

Discussion

Possible concerns of this technique include over tightening of the lateral ankle structures resulting in altered biomechanics and the development of osteoarthritis (Table 1). Overtightening could also result in altered proprioception and not allow an athlete to return to their previous level activity.

Technical Pearls and Pitfalls

A key advantage of ASAs is the small insertional diameter requiring less bone loss. ASA go into the bone in a vertical longitudinal shape and then either fold or ball up against the cortical bone as tension is pulled. The incorporation of a knotless system theoretically reduces pain associated with knots, reduces surgeon time and eliminates variability in tying knots. In revision cases, the small footprint allows for access to good bone stock with as little as a 2 mm bone bridge required. ASA also eliminates cystic absorption problems associated with other materials, while not causing distortion typically seen on magnetic resonance imaging.

Video 1

In this video, we present our technique. We begin with an arthroscopic examination (0:10). We proceed with our dissection (0:23). After reaching the fibular periosteum, we make full thickness flaps and dissect towards the talar insertion site of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) for our Internal Brace (0:35). We then drill, tap and insert the anchor just off the articular margin of the talus (0:49). Next, we pass our internal brace superficial to the ATLF (0:57). We then turn our attention to the distal fibula. We mark the insertion sites for the 3.5mm SwiveLock anchor and knotless FiberTak anchors at 6, 12, and 18 mm from the tip of the distal fibula (1:17). We then drill for each. The knotless all-suture anchors are inserted (1:30). We then repair the lateral ligaments back to the distal fibula (1:48) and tension them appropriately. We secure our internal brace into the distal fibula with a 3.5mm SwiveLock anchor (3:32). We then repair the periosteal flap and close (4:29). Finally we give our postoperative protocol (4:32).
