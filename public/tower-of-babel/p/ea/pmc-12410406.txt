Proteomic insights into collagen remodeling of the calipash in Chinese soft-shelled turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis) under Aeromonas hydrophila challenge: Implications for nutritional quality and oxidative stress response
Authors: Jiaxiang Luo, Zilian Deng, Keye Ru, Juntao Zhu, Ji Yu, Guoying Qian, Caiyan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The calipash, a collagen-rich tissue in Pelodiscus sinensis, undergoes structural degradation during Aeromonas hydrophila infection, compromising its economic value. This study investigates the underlying collagen alterations. Turtles were challenged with A. hydrophila, and samples were collected at 0 h, 6 h, 1d, 3d, 6d, and 10d post-infection. Results showed progressive collagen degradation, peaking at 10d. Early infection increased collagen content, type I collagen proportion, and collagen-related gene expression, but these declined over time. Essential amino acids decreased, while non-essential amino acids (e.g., glycine, proline) increased. iTRAQ proteomics identified 517 differentially expressed proteins (227 up-, 290 down-regulated), including collagen metabolism regulators (ITGB4, TIMP2, LTBP1 up; COL6A6, FKBP7, COL28A1 down). Protein network analysis revealed ECM stabilization and oxidative stress regulation balances collagen turnover. These findings indicated that P. sinensis modulates collagen biosynthesis and degradation under A. hydrophila stress, altering collagen structure and mitigating oxidative damage.

Introduction

The Chinese soft-shelled turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis) is primarily distributed in southern China, Korean Peninsula, Japan, and northern Vietnam (Liang et al., 2024). In recent years, Chinese P. sinensis aquaculture industry has experienced rapid development, achieving a global‑leading production of 490,000 tons in 2023 (FCAD, 2024). The calipash, a nutrient-dense dermal protective tissue surrounding the carapace, serves as a critical quality indicator for P. sinensis. With its umami flavor, elastic texture, and abundant collagen content, calipash is loved by consumers, and its quality directly determines the price of the P. sinensis (Wang et al., 2021). As a crucial economic trait, the thickness and weight of the calipash are considered the most significant determinants of the product quality in P. sinensis.

Notably, the calipash contains high levels of type I collagen and cell-protective amino acids. Collagen, the most abundant structural protein in animal skin and bones, constitutes approximately 30 % of total protein content. It features a tightly packed triple-helical structure composed of three identical or distinct polypeptide chains (Avila Rodríguez et al., 2018). Collagen sequences are highly conserved, characterized by (Gly-X-Y) n repeating motifs, where X is typically proline (Pro) and Y is predominantly 4-hydroxyproline (Hyp) (Persikov et al., 2004). This protein is ubiquitously distributed across animal tissues, including blood vessels, skin, cartilage, bone, and other connective tissues, providing structural support and organ protection. To date, 29 collagen types have been identified from diverse biological tissues, with type I collagen being the most prevalent and abundant. Each collagen type exhibits unique molecular architectures and amino acid compositions, with significant variations in peptide sequence, structure, and functionality across types (Gelse et al., 2003). Furthermore, collagen demonstrates exceptional physicochemical properties as a natural biomaterial, including water retention, low viscosity, biocompatibility, hygroscopicity, gel strength, emulsification, and moisturizing capacity. These attributes enable its extensive utilization in food, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and biomedical materials (Rezvani Ghomi et al., 2021). Its biodegradability, low cytotoxicity, antioxidant activity, and hemostatic efficacy further position collagen as a promising bioactive substance (Gómez-Guillén et al., 2011). As a primary component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and collagen fibers in connective tissues, hydrolyzed collagen generates bioactive peptides or functional amino acids, expanding its biological relevance (Harris et al., 2021). These amino acids not only function as substrates for protein synthesis but also act as signaling molecules regulating gene expression and physiological processes. For instance, Pro functions as a cellular osmoprotectant, essential for maintaining protein bioactivity and cellular homeostasis (Wei et al., 2020).

In adult individuals of P. sinensis, the calipash typically constitutes 5–10 % of total body weight (Yang et al., 2016). Healthy specimens exhibit plump, thick calipash with dense colloidal properties. However, stress conditions may induce structural alterations, including collapse, wrinkling, and tissue laxity, ultimately diminishing commercial value (Zhou et al., 2022). The calipash, a dermal extension of the carapace posterior margin in turtles, has been historically perceived as a protective structure for hindlimbs and tail, with no documented physiological functions beyond mechanical safeguarding (Zou et al., 2017). Aeromonas hydrophila, a Gram-negative bacterium ubiquitously distributed in aquatic environments, represents a significant pathogen affecting various aquatic species. Among pathogens threatening turtle aquaculture, bacterial diseases-particularly A. hydrophila infections cause the most severe economic losses (Tao et al., 2024). In healthy turtles, the calipash appears broad, thick, firm, and rich in collagen. However, A. hydrophila infections during farming can cause it to shrink, collapse, and lose muscular tension, significantly reducing its market value. Notably, limited research exists regarding the physiological maintenance of calipash tissue (the nutrient-rich dermal margin) under post-stress conditions (especially in infection with A. hydrophila), despite its critical role in commercial valuation (Zhou et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023). Under pathological or stress conditions, collagen synthesis and degradation processes work coordinately to remodel tissues and facilitate physiological adaptation (Palka et al., 2021). Stress-induced collagen degradation promotes catabolic breakdown into bioactive peptides that serve as an energy source during stress response, the resulting proline and glycine may contribute to oxidative stress resistance by modulating downstream signaling pathways (e.g., Nrf2) (D'Aniello et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2026). Our preliminary studies observed significant structural collapse and increased wrinkling in the calipash of P. sinensis following A. hydrophila infection (Zhou et al., 2022), but the response at different stages of infection and the underlying regulatory response mechanism are still unclear.

In the present study, we hypothesize that the calipash transcends its defensive role, with collagen-derived amino acids potentially mediating stress resistance mechanisms. To investigate this hypothesis, we employed an integrative approach combining histomorphological analysis, collagen metabolism profiling, oxidative stress assessment, and proteomics to systematically evaluate the physiological significance of the calipash across different phases of A. hydrophila stress response (0 h, 6 h, 1 d, 3 d, 6 d, and 10 d). Our findings redefine the calipash's functional role in stress defense mechanisms. Furthermore, this study not only provides a theoretical framework for improving meat quality in P. sinensis but also identifies key molecular targets for future selective breeding strategies.

Materials and methods

A. hydrophila infection

Preparation of bacterial suspension: Activated A. hydrophila was cultured in LB medium at 32 °C with shaking (180 rpm) for 6 h, followed by dilution with sterile physiological saline to the target concentration. Experiment 1‌: healthy turtles with an average body weight of 400 g ± 20 g were acclimated for 2 weeks in glass aquaria (35 cm × 25 cm × 10 cm, water depth nearly 5 cm, temperature 10–15 °C). Subsequently, 1 mL of A. hydrophila suspension (5 × 107 CFU/mL, according to the previous study by Zhou et al. (2022)) was intraperitoneally injected at the depression near the left lower limb of turtle. Our previous study demonstrated that A. hydrophila infection induced calipash atrophy in P. sinensis after 5 days of infection (Zhou et al., 2022). But the precise onset timing of calipash degradation and its underlying molecular mechanisms remained unclear. In the current experiment 1, total of 54 turtles were randomly euthanized via anesthesia at 0 h, 6 h, 1d, 3d, 6d, and 10d post-injection for sample collection to capture the dynamic changes in the calipash during the early stages of A. hydrophila infection. Three replicates were set for each sampling time point, with 3 turtles were used in each replicate. The damage condition of the calipash was determined through external observation and histological section analysis to assess the effectiveness of A. hydrophila infection. At the end of the infection trial, all the turtles were anesthetized and photographed, and then dissected for sampling. The blood samples were collected using heparinized tubes and subsequently aliquoted into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, then centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the serum for biochemical indices and enzyme activity. Calipash samples were divided: some were fixed for histology, while others were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent enzymatic activity, amino acid and qPCR analysis.

Experiment 2‌: Based on findings from Experiment 1 showing the most severe calipash edge atrophy at 10 d post-infection. 18 turtles were divided into experimental (T) and control (C) groups, and each treatment group was randomly assigned to three replicates with 3 turtles in each replicate. T group received intraperitoneal injections of A. hydrophila suspension (identical dosage and injection site to Experiment 1), while the C group received the same dose of 0.9 % sterile physiological saline. After 10d of observation for calipash morphological changes, calipash tissue samples were collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored in cryovials, and subjected to proteomic analysis.

Morphological analysis

The experimental procedures were performed with slight modifications based on the paraffin section method for P. sinensis calipash tissue. Freshly isolated calipash tissue was immersed in 4 % tissue fixative at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by long-term preservation in 70 % ethanol. Approximately 5 mm × 5 mm tissue blocks were excised using sterile blades for paraffin embedding (as detailed in Supplementary Table S-1). The embedded blocks were sectioned into 6 μm slices for subsequent histological staining.

Van gieson (VG) Staining: Sections were sequentially treated with gradient concentrations of xylene and ethanol (protocols listed in Supplementary Table S-2). After rinsing, specimens were stained with hematoxylin (5 min) and VG staining solution (30 s), then mounted with resinene for morphological observation under optical microscopy (DM4000, Leica, Heerbrugg, Switzerland).

Sirius Red Staining: Following the dehydration protocol in Supplementary Table S-2, sections were stained with Sirius red solution for 5 min. After thorough washing, slides were sealed with resinene and examined under polarized light microscopy (ECLIPSE LV100N POL, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) for collagen architecture analysis. The proportion of area occupied by type I collagen in Sirius Red staining was analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA) software.

Collagen analysis

Hyp level can reflect changes in the total collagen content. The hyp standard curve method was employed to determine the collagen content in calipash of the P. sinensis at different time points under stress conditions. The main steps of this method include: sample pretreatment → hydrochloric acid hydrolysis → pH adjustment and volume fixation → colour development reaction → absorbance measurement → standard curve plotting → collagen content calculation.

mRNA expression analysis

Calipash RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol, cDNA synthesis was completed with HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following manufacturer's protocols. RT-PCR amplification was conducted on LightCycler® 96 Pro System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with cycling parameters: 95 °C/120 s, 45 cycles of (95 °C/10s and 58 °C/10s)-72 °C/20s. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S-3. Data normalization employed endogenous GAPDH controls, with ΔΔCt analysis performed.

Oxidative stress response analysis

Calipash samples were homogenized in nine volumes of ice-cold physiological saline, and then centrifuged as above. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathion peroxidase (GSH-Px) in serum and calipash, catalase (CAT) and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) in serum (Jiancheng Bioengineering, Nanjing, China), as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS) in calipash (Kemin Biotechnology, Suzhou, China) were assayed using commercial kits according to the manufacturer's instructions by Multiskan spectrum (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA).

Amino acid composition analysis

Accurately weigh 100 mg of the dried marginal tissue, add 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid for high-temperature hydrolysis for 24 h, take out 0.2 mL of the filtrate and evaporate it under nitrogen, then dissolve it in 0.02 mol/mL hydrochloric acid. After filtration through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, the sample is loaded and analyzed using a fully automatic amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). All data for bioinformatic analysis (PCA analysis, heat map cluster analysis, Kmeans cluster analysis) were homogenized using the online program APT BioCloud (http://cloud.aptbiotech.com).

Proteomics analysis

Quantitative proteomic analysis was performed based on isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ) and collaborated with BGI Co., LTD (Shenzhen, China). Protein extraction involves homogenizing samples with steel beads and SDS L3 buffer, followed by centrifugation, reduction with DTT, alkylation with IAM, acetone precipitation, and final solubilization in SDS-free L3 buffer to obtain the protein solution. Standard proteins were added to wells A1-A10 of a 96-well plate, followed by pure water and Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250. OD595 was measured using a microplate reader to create a standard curve. Protein samples were diluted, mixed with the dye, and OD595 was measured to determine protein concentration based on the standard curve. Quantified protein samples (10 μg) were mixed with loading buffer, heated at 95 °C for 5 min, and centrifuged at 25,000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was loaded onto a 12 % SDS-PAGE gel, electrophoresed at 80 V for 30 min and 120 V for 120 min. Gels were stained and scanned. 100 μg of protein was diluted with 0.5 M TEAB to reduce urea/SDS concentration. Trypsin (1:20 enzyme: protein) was added, vortexed, and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Digested peptides were desalted and lyophilized. iTRAQ reagents were reconstituted in isopropanol. Peptides were dissolved in 0.5 M TEAB and mixed with iTRAQ tags. After 2 h at room temperature, labeled peptides were pooled and lyophilized. Peptides were separated using a Gemini C18 column with a gradient of 5 % to 80 % acetonitrile (pH 9.8) over 40 min. Fractions were collected every 3.15 min, lyophilized, and stored. Lyophilized peptides were reconstituted in 2 % ACN/0.1 % FA, centrifuged, and loaded onto a trap column for desalting. Peptides were separated on a C18 column with a gradient of 5 % to 80 % ACN/0.1 % FA over 60 min. Followed, peptides were ionized via nanoESI and analyzed using a Q-Exactive HF-X in DDA mode. MS1 resolution was 60,000, and MS2 resolution was 15,000. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s. iTRAQ quantification was performed using IQuant software. Peptides and proteins were filtered at 1 % FDR.

Protein GO functional annotation was performed using Blast2GO software. KEGG pathway analysis was conducted using the KAAS (KEGG Automatic Annotation Server). Enriched GO terms and pathways were identified by comparing significantly differentially expressed proteins against the background of all identified proteins using the hypergeometric test. Eukaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOGs) annotation was performed by BLASTing all identified proteins against the KOG database. Protein-protein interaction network analysis based on STRING database, subcellular localization prediction and classification of differentially expressed proteins were performed using WoLF PSORT software.

Statistical analysis

All bar charts were generated using GraphPad Prism‌ (California, USA). Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS (Chicago, USA), and experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard error (Mean ± SE)‌. Significant differences between groups were analyzed using Duncan's multiple comparison test following one-way ANOVA, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Calipash morphological and collagen content

Macroscopic observation revealed that after 3d of A. hydrophila stress, the calipash of P. sinensis began to exhibit significant collapse and atrophy (Fig. 1A & B). By 10d of stress, the atrophy of calipash was most severe and significantly more pronounced compared to other time points (Fig. 1A & B). VG staining, which colors collagen fibers red, demonstrated that the 0-h group exhibited a clear and well-organized tissue structure in the calipash, with evenly distributed fibers and a distinct boundary between the dense collagen fiber layer (★) and the loose collagen fiber layer (▲) (Fig. 1C). As the infection progressed, the boundary between the two layers became blurred. While the fine fiber bundles in the loose collagen fiber layer began to exhibit irregular orientation, thickness, and disorganization (Fig. 1C). Sirius red staining allowed for the observation of the distribution of different collagen types in the marginal rim tissue (Fig. 1D). According to the principles of Sirius red staining, collagen types I are characterized by red or yellow coloration, strong birefringence, and tight arrangement. Overall, as the infection duration increased, the stressed groups exhibited stronger birefringence under polarized light, appearing predominantly orange-yellow or red (Fig. 1D). ImageJ software analysis revealed that compared to the 0 h group, the proportion of type I collagen in the calipash significantly increased at 6 h, 1d, 3d, 6d, and 10d post-infection (Fig. 1E) (P < 0.05). Collagen content in the calipash of turtle following A. hydrophila infection are shown in Fig. 1F. Antioxidant capacity of the P. sinensis following A. hydrophila infection are shown in Fig. 2I-O. The results indicated that serum SOD activity was significantly higher at 1d and 3d post-infection, reaching its peak at 1d post-infection (P < 0.05). Serum GSH-Px activity peaked at 6 h post-infection and was significantly higher than that in the 0 h group, but it was significantly lower than the 0 h group at 10d post-infection (P < 0.05). Serum CAT activity reached its highest level at 6 h post-infection, and at all other time points, CAT activity was significantly lower than that at 6 h post-infection (P < 0.05). The T-AOC in serum was significantly higher in all experimental groups compared to the 0 h group, peaking at 3d post-infection (P < 0.05). The ROS production rate in calipash peaked at 1d post-infection, and it was significantly higher than that in the 0 h group at 6 h, 1d, 3d, and 6d post-infection (P < 0.05).

Calipash amino acid composition

The influence of A. hydrophila infection on calipash amino acid composition was examined by PCA, which showed amino acids were clustered of two groups according to A. hydrophila infected (6 h, 1d, 3d, 6d and 10d group) or uninfected (0 h group) with PC1 and PC2 explaining 55.0 % of the total variation (Fig. 3A). Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was performed and visualized by heat map and, based on the dendrogram, the treatment groups could be grouped into three clusters, cluster 1 (His, Lys, Tyr, Val, Met, Leu, Ala, Asp), cluster 2 (Ile, Phe, Glu, Arg, Cys) and cluster 3 (Thr, Hyp, Ser, Pro, Gly), which meant that the amino acids of calipash in different A. hydrophila infection periods could be distinctly distinguished (Fig. 3B). Further classification analysis showed that with the extension of A. hydrophila infection period, the proportion of essential amino acids (EAAs) in calipash decreased significantly (Fig. 3D), while the proportion of non-essential amino acids (NEAAs) increased significantly (Fig. 3E) (P < 0.05), but the proportion of flavor amino acids was basically not affected (Fig. 3F) (P > 0.05). Importantly, the infection with A. hydrophila significantly affected the content of characteristic amino acids in the collagen of calipash. Compared to the 0 h group, the Hyp content in calipash was significantly increased at 6 h, 1d, and 3d post-infection, reaching its peak at 6 h post-infection (Fig. 3I) (P < 0.05). The glycine (Gly) and proline (Pro) contents in calipash of all experimental groups were significantly higher than those in initial group (0 h) (Fig. 3G&H). Specifically, the Gly content in calipash reached its maximum at 1d post-infection, while the Pro content peaked at 10d post-infection (Fig. 3G&H) (P < 0.05). Overall, gly was the most abundant amino acid in calipash of the P. sinensis (Fig. 3G).

Discussion

In this study, observations through macroscopic examination and histological composite staining revealed that the calipash of healthy P. sinensis is plump, with well-organized collagen layers and uniformly arranged fiber bundles of consistent thickness. However, following infection with A. hydrophila, the calipash exhibited collapse, and the tissue structure became significantly disorganized. The boundary between the dense and loose collagen fiber layers became indistinct, and the fiber bundles were irregularly distributed with varying thicknesses. Similar findings were reported by Zhou et al. (2022), our previous study observed that the collagen fiber layers in the calipash of P. sinensis became loose and disorganized after one week of bacterial stress. In this study, we further explored the calipash changes at different stages of infection. As the infection progressed, the structure of the dense collagen fiber layer gradually loosened. Furthermore, Sirius red staining of the marginal frill tissue allowed for quantitative analysis based on polarized colors (Borges et al., 2007). The calipash was rich in type I collagen, which, under polarized light microscopy, appeared red or orange (Li et al., 2020). In this study, it was found that during the early stages of A. hydrophila infection, the proportion of type I collagen and total collagen content in the calipash increased. However, as the infection duration extended, both the proportion of type I collagen and the total collagen content gradually decreased. These results suggest that under bacterial stress, P. sinensis can modulate the distribution and types of collagen in the calipash tissue to some extent to counteract adverse stimuli. It is precisely these structural changes in the collagen within the calipash during stress that lead to the wrinkling and collapse.

To further elucidate the collagen-related molecular changes in the calipash of P. sinensis following A. hydrophila challenge, the relative mRNA expression levels of several genes closely associated with collagen metabolism were analyzed. These included genes encoding type I collagen (col1a1 and col1a2), genes involved in collagen degradation (mmp1, mmp2, and their inhibitors timp2, timp4), and genes participating in collagen synthesis (p4ha1 and p4ha3). In the early stages after A. hydrophila challenge, the expression levels of col1a1 and col1a2 in the calipash were elevated, which is associated with excessive collagen production. The significant up-regulation of col1a1 and col1a2 expression is accompanied by tissue fibrosis and increased collagen fiber content (Pan et al., 2022). Excessive deposition of type I collagen can disrupt the structure and function of tissues (Giannandrea & Parks, 2014). Prolyl 4-hydroxylase, a key enzyme in the hydroxylation of collagen, promotes collagen maturation and activates pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase, thereby increasing intracellular proline levels and facilitating collagen synthesis (Guo et al., 2019). In this study, the mRNA expression levels of p4ha1 and p4ha3 in the calipash were significantly upregulated during the early infection. It is hypothesized that, in response to stress during the initial infection, the calipash tissue may enhance collagen synthesis by promoting proline metabolism, thereby further facilitating collagen production.

MMPs play crucial roles in both physiological and pathological conditions, including development, tissue regeneration, and wound healing (Murphy & Nagase, 2008). MMP1, known as interstitial collagenase and fibroblast collagenase, is a key member of the MMP family and specifically degrades type I collagen and fibrillar collagen. Under physiological conditions, MMP1 is expressed at low levels; however, its expression is induced in response to pathological conditions such as oxidative stress, inflammatory processes, and tissue remodeling (Alge-Priglinger et al., 2009). Tissue inhibitors of TIMPs are essential regulatory genes in collagen biosynthesis and act as inhibitors of MMPs. TIMPs block the binding of MMPs to their respective substrates, ultimately regulating ECM homeostasis (Cabral-Pacheco et al., 2020). Therefore, the TIMP-MMP system may play a critical role in collagen disruption induced by stress. In this study, the mRNA levels of mmp1, mmp2, timp2, and timp4 in the calipash were significantly elevated during the early stages of infection. Similar findings have been reported in other aquatic organisms. For instance, Zhang et al. (2012) observed a significant increase in the mRNA expression of mmpl2 in the epidermis of amphioxus (Branchiostoma japonicum) following immune stimulation with Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus for 12 h. Xu et al. (2012) also reported an increase in mmp2 expression in the spleen and head kidney tissues of grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) at 4 h post-infection with A. hydrophila, followed by a significant decline in CiMMP2 expression by 1 day post-infection. In another study by Xu et al. (2011), the transcriptional levels of TIMP2b were significantly elevated in the blood, gills, and spleen of grass carp after A. hydrophila challenge. In the early stages of infection in P. sinensis, the expression levels of mmp and timp genes were up-regulated to varying degrees, but declined as the infection progressed. These results indicate that the TIMP-MMP system plays an important role in the response of P. sinensis to bacterial stress. In addition, A. hydrophila secretes a variety of extracellular proteases, some of which may directly degrade collagen. Studies in C. idella have demonstrated that A. hydrophila-secreted metalloprotease elastase (AhE), which is abundant in crude extracellular products (ECPases), can directly degrade complement C3 in the host serum. This mechanism enables the pathogen to evade serum-mediated elimination and exacerbates host tissue damage (Chen et al., 2019). We hypothesize that A. hydrophila may directly secrete collagenase, contributing to the degradation of collagen in the calipash. However, the specific mechanisms require further experimental validation.

The antioxidant systems are crucial for safeguarding the health of aquatic animals, as they reflect the physiological stress responses of fish to nutritional and environmental changes (Kou et al., 2021). In healthy organisms, ROS are scavenged by antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT to maintain homeostasis. However, various forms of stress can alter ROS levels, leading to oxidative stress (Lushchak, 2014). This study also observed a significant increase in ROS levels in the calipash of P. sinensis following A. hydrophila infection. SOD and CAT serve as the first line of defense against cellular oxidation. SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide radicals into H2O2 and O2, while CAT converts H2O2 into harmless water and oxygen (Sheng et al., 2014). Additionally, GSH-Px participates in the detoxification of H2O2 generated by SOD (Xue et al., 2022). Thus, the antioxidant defense system, comprising SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT, maintains redox homeostasis by balancing the production and clearance of free radicals. In this study, the activities of GSH-Px, CAT, and SOD in the serum and calipash were significantly elevated during the early stages of A. hydrophila infection, showing an initial increase followed by a decline. This pattern may be attributed to the production of excessive free radicals in response to stress during the early infection phase, which induces the expression of antioxidant enzymes. As the infection progresses, the accumulation of oxygen free radicals overwhelms the declining activity of antioxidant enzymes, rendering them insufficient to neutralize these radicals. Furthermore, the T-AOC reflects the overall antioxidant capacity of the organism and serves as a comprehensive indicator of the functional status of the antioxidant system (Zhang et al., 2019). The increased T-AOC levels in the serum of P. sinensis after A. hydrophila infection provide a more integrated reflection of the combined antioxidant effects of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems. Therefore, it is likely that the structural changes in the calipash of P. sinensis during infection stress are mediated through oxidative stress responses.

Changes in the amino acid content of an organism can, to some extent, reflect its oxidative stress status and indicate the extent of tissue damage. In the analysis of amino acid composition in the calipash of P. sinensis, it was observed that the proportion of EAAs decreased due to A. hydrophila infection. This phenomenon may be associated with the increased consumption of EAAs. During bacterial infection, the host's immune system is activated, requiring a substantial amount of amino acids for the synthesis of immune-related molecules, such as antibodies, cytokines, and acute-phase proteins. EAAs, including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, serve as critical substrates for protein synthesis and are thus preferentially utilized to meet the demands of the immune response (Li et al., 2007). On the other hand, under oxidative stress conditions, certain essential amino acids, such as sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine and cysteine), may be consumed for the synthesis of antioxidant substances like GSH, leading to a decline in their levels (Wu et al., 2004). Additionally, A. hydrophila may preferentially utilize the host's essential amino acids as a nutrient source for its growth and reproduction, further contributing to the reduction in essential amino acid content in the host tissues (Wang & Quinn, 2010). But the exact mechanism still needs to be further explored and verified. On the contrary, the glycine and proline content in the calipash gradually increased following bacterial stress, which may be associated with the anti-stress response of the turtles. The hypothesized mechanism is that A. hydrophila infection enhances proteolysis in the calipash tissue. Under inflammatory conditions, tissue damage and increased apoptosis/necrosis lead to the release of abundant proteins, such as collagen (which is rich in glycine and proline). Subsequent proteolytic degradation generates free glycine and proline (Singh et al., 2023). During this ECM remodeling process, collagen breakdown, mediated by activated MMPs, likely releases glycine (given that glycine constitutes one out of every three residues in collagen), resulting in localized glycine accumulation (Alge-Priglinger et al., 2009). Transcriptomic and proteomic data support this hypothesis, demonstrating that A. hydrophila infection significantly upregulates MMP expression. Glycine serves as a critical component for GSH synthesis (γ-Glu-Cys-Gly tripeptide). During infection, the burst of ROS necessitates GSH upregulation to counteract oxidative stress, potentially driving glycine mobilization and accumulation (McCarty et al., 2018). GSH acts as both a substrate and electron donor in antioxidant reactions, while GSH-Px functions as a GSH-dependent key enzyme, forming an enzyme-substrate coupling relationship (Margis et al., 2008). In A. hydrophila-infected calipash tissue, GSH-Px activity initially increases but later declines, which may elevate GSH demand and further promote glycine mobilization. However, the precise mechanistic role requires additional experimental validation. Future studies will employ isotopic labeling (e.g., 13C-glycine tracing) to track metabolic flux and MMP interference assays to elucidate these dynamics. Our previous studies have confirmed the stress-protective role of glycine in P. sinensis, where glycine significantly enhances the survival rate of LPS-stressed marginal tissue fibroblasts. Mechanistically, glycine upregulates mRNA expression of: (i) collagen biosynthesis genes, (ii) prolyl hydroxylase, and (iii) collagen degradation-related genes, while simultaneously suppressing ROS generation (Zhou et al., 2023).

Proline is crucial for collagen metabolism and its turnover increases under oxidative stress (Wyse & Netto, 2011). In addition to promoting proline generation through collagen degradation, another potential mechanism involves bacterial infection-induced activation of immune cells (e.g., macrophages, neutrophils), which release pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6). These signaling molecules may upregulate the expression of proline-synthesizing enzymes (e.g., P5CS, pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase), thereby enhancing the conversion of glutamate to proline to meet the increased demands of immune cell proliferation and wound repair processes. Our previous studies have demonstrated that dietary proline supplementation alleviates hibernation stress-induced intestinal inflammation and apoptosis in P. sinensis via modulation of the Nrf2/HO-1/p65 signaling pathway (Luo et al., 2026). Additionally, it has been found that proline may accumulate in organisms when exposed to environmental stress (Alia et al., 2001; Signorelli et al., 2013). Notably, Hyp, a post-translationally modified proline derivative, exhibited marked accumulation in the calipash during initial stress exposure, aligning with prior observations by Zhou et al. (2022). This finding supports our hypothesis that host organisms may activate collagenolytic pathways during bacterial challenge, thereby mobilizing collagen-derived amino acids (notably glycine and proline) for potential immunomodulatory functions.

The ITRAQ technology with proteomic approach, has significantly enhanced the accuracy and efficiency of protein sequencing. This technique has been widely applied in aquatic animal research (Ma et al., 2019). Alderman et al. (2021) conducted a ITRAQ proteomic analysis on the heart of the painted turtle (Chrysemys pictabellii), revealing that cold-induced alterations in mitochondrial structure and functional proteins may serve as a crucial adaptive mechanism for hypoxia tolerance during specific stages of the turtle's life cycle. Yang et al. (2022) reported the first proteomic profile of the spleen in the American bullfrog (Lithobates catesbeianus), providing insights into the immune response to Citrobacter freundii infection. In the P. sinensis, Ge et al. (2024) employed an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic approach to investigate the immune response of the spleen to A. hydrophila infection. They identified 763 and 568 DEP) in susceptible and resistant turtles, respectively. Notably, immune-related proteins such as NF-κB1 were significantly upregulated in the resistant group. Similarly, Liu et al. (2017) conducted a proteomic analysis on liver tissues of healthy and virus-infected (Trionyx sinensis hemorrhagic syndrome virus, TSHSV) turtles, identifying 252 DEPs, of which 118 were up-regulated and 134 were down-regulated. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses provided preliminary insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathological changes post-infection. In the present study, we preliminarily observed that the P. sinensis might regulate collagen metabolism in the calipash in response to external stress. Building on this finding, utilized iTRAQ-based proteomic technology to analyze changes in protein expression in the marginal tissue of P. sinensis following A. hydrophila infection. A total of 517 DEPs were identified, with 227 up-regulated and 290 down-regulated in the experimental group. Notably, immune-related proteins such as Glutathione S-Transferase Omega-1 (GSTO1), Mitochondrial Superoxide Dismutase (SOD2/SOD3), and Glutathione Peroxidase 1 (GPX1) exhibited significant differential expression between the experimental and control groups. These findings suggest that P. sinensis initiated a series of immune responses upon A. hydrophila infection, highlighting the potential molecular mechanisms underlying its immune defense.

Simultaneously, a large number of differential proteins related to collagen metabolism were annotated, such as P4HA1, P3H1, CRTAP, and PLOD1. During collagen synthesis, amino acid residues such as proline, lysine, and glycine on the peptide chain undergo post-translational modifications such as hydroxylation by relevant enzymes, ultimately forming collagen fibers and collagen (Koide, 2005). Among the differential proteins, the hydroxylases P4HA1 and P3H1 catalyze the hydroxylation of proline at corresponding positions in the peptide chain, promoting the translation of the Xaa-Pro-Gly sequence in collagen and other proteins to form Hyp proteins (Rappu et al., 2019). In mouse models, the extracellular matrix and its main component, collagen, fail to deposit in mice lacking the P4HA1 gene, leading to chondrodysplasia in mice, indicating that P4HA1 is a crucial enzyme for collagen deposition (Tolonen et al., 2022). Particularly, P3H1 catalyzes the 3-hydroxylation of specific proline residues in procollagen (e.g., Pro986 in the α1 chain of type I collagen) and forms a complex with CRTAP and PPIB, which participates in collagen folding and quality control. Compared to wild-type mice, P3H1 knockout mice displayed a marked reduction in bone mass and mechanical strength, accompanied by disorganized collagen fibril architecture in fibrous collagen-rich tissues such as bone, tendons, and skin, confirming the essential role of P3H1 in collagen fibrillogenesis (Vranka et al., 2010). CRTAP is required for the 3-hydroxylation of prolyl residues in fibrillar collagens, and CRTAP-deficient mice develop severe chondrodysplasia (Morello et al., 2006). Furthermore, CRTAP and P3H1 cooperatively stabilize collagen fibrils. In zebrafish, CRISPR-mediated knockout of CRTAP and P3H1 resulted in excessive post-translational modification of type I collagen, leading to its aberrant aggregation into irregular fibrils with altered diameters in the extracellular matrix (Tonelli et al., 2020). PLOD1, which catalyzes the hydroxylation of lysyl residues in collagen α-chains, is indispensable for proper collagen assembly and cross-linking. Dysregulated PLOD1 expression has been implicated in various collagen-related disorders (Sadri et al., 2022). Research in zebrafish has also found that PLOD (LH) enzymes are not merely housekeeping enzymes, but play distinct developmental roles in Collagen biosynthesis (Schneider & Granato, 2007). In the present study, the downregulation of PLOD1 in the infected group may lead to abnormal collagen cross-linking, potentially indirectly suppressing COL1A1/COL1A2 expression via ECM signaling pathways, thereby inhibiting collagen synthesis. However, this mechanism requires further validation.

In aquatic animals, the down-regulation of amino acid hydroxylases related to collagen synthesis inhibits collagen synthesis. In the muscle tissue of juvenile turbot (Scophthalmus maximus L.), where P4HA1 promoted the concentration of total collagen (Zhang et al., 2015). An increase in Hyp also up-regulates collagen synthesis-related genes in crucian carp (Carassius auratus), such as COL1A1, COLA2, P4HA1, and TGF-β, which regulate collagen synthesis (Zhou et al., 2023). In addition to Pro and Hyp, increasing the Lys level in feed enhances lysyl hydroxylase expression and increases the content of type I collagen (Tang et al., 2023). The addition of Lys significantly increased the transcriptional levels of genes such as SOD, GPx, and GST in the intestines of grass carp, alleviating stress in grass carp under stress conditions (Li et al., 2016). Therefore, it is speculated that the significant down-regulation of collagen synthesis in the calipash of P. sinensis after bacterial infection may be due to the prioritization of Pro, Hyd, and Lys synthesis for resisting pathogenic bacteria rather than collagen synthesis. This speculation is supported by the up-regulation of numerous immune-related proteins (such as SOD and GST) and proline metabolism-related enzymes. FKBP7 directly regulates intracellular collagen maturation and secretion by catalyzing proline isomerization and facilitating collagen folding. In this study, FKBP7 downregulation may impair collagen synthesis, folding, secretion, and ECM deposition, leading to dysregulated collagen metabolism and subsequent degradation of the calipash in the P. sinensis. However, further experimental validation is still required.

PCOLCE binds to the C-terminal propeptide of type I procollagen, enhancing the activity of the C-terminal procollagen protease, which is essential for collagen deposition (Baicu et al., 2012). The significant down-regulation of COL1A1 in the calipash of P. sinensis after infection validates this function of PCOLCE. The down-regulation of PCOLCE may be a response mechanism of the P. sinensis to pathogenic bacteria, as related studies have shown that low expression of PCOLCE can regulate the cell cycle and promote apoptosis to inhibit disease progression (Ma et al., 2025). COL1A1 is a crucial gene for collagen deposition and promotes collagen synthesis and deposition in yellow croaker (Nibea coibor) (Rong et al., 2022). The down-regulation of COL1A1 in P. sinensis after infection is one of the key factors contributing to pathological changes in the calipash. In addition to COL1A1, a large number of collagen proteins were significantly down-regulated after infection with pathogenic bacteria. COL6A6, a form of type VI collagen, regulates epithelial cell-fibronectin interactions. In disease studies, researchers found that COL6A6 expression was significantly downregulated in individuals with muscle damage (Fitzgerald et al., 2008). COL28A1 participates in cell adhesion and the formation of filaments in the extracellular matrix process, playing an important role in maintaining bone formation and extracellular matrix integrity in chickens (Santos et al., 2022). COL21A1 belongs to the FACIT collagen within the non-fibrillar collagen family and may interact with fibrillar collagens such as types I and III, participating in fibrosis and extracellular matrix assembly (Gil-Cayuela et al., 2016). COL15A1 collagen is widely distributed in tissues and may function in adhering the basement membrane to the underlying connective tissue stroma. Studies in mice have shown that the absence of COL15A1 protein is associated with muscle and microvascular deterioration (Martínez-Nieto et al., 2021). In addition, collagen metabolism is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, such as TGF-β/Smad, integrin, PI3K/Akt, Wnt, and MAPK (Hu et al., 2020). These pathways are enriched in the KEGG pathway analysis of differential proteins. Furthermore, key proteins in related signaling pathways, such as TGFBR1, LTBP1, and Itgb4, are differentially expressed between the control and infected groups, which may reflect the response of P. sinensis to bacterial stress in maintaining collagen homeostasis. Studies in aquatic animals and mammals have demonstrated that the expression of TGF-β or integrin β1 can induce the production of TIMP1 to inhibit extracellular matrix degradation and promote collagen synthesis (Rong et al., 2022). Therefore, it is speculated that P. sinensis may mediate the transmission of related signals from the cell membrane to the nucleus to regulate protein expression, thereby influencing collagen metabolism. TGFBR1 and LTBP1 may enhance collagen synthesis and inhibit degradation through the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway (Wu et al., 2024). Itgb4 may mediate cell-ECM interactions and activate downstream pathways (such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK) to regulate ECM remodeling (Cheng et al., 2024). These proteins collectively help the P. sinensis cope with bacterial stress by modulating collagen metabolism and maintaining tissue integrity.

Conclusion

In summary, our study found that during the initial phase of A. hydrophila infection stress in P. sinensis, the antioxidant indicators in the calipash were elevated, the proportion of type I collagen increased, the Hyp content rose, and the genes related to collagen synthesis and degradation were upregulated, and then gradually declined as the infection progressed. Moreover, histological observations revealed that the collagen layers within the calipash progressively degraded, and the collagen structure underwent atrophic damage during the stress process. Furthermore, proteomic analysis suggested that the P. sinensis may resist pathogenic bacterial infection by remodeling collagen proteins and activating immune and antioxidant systems.
