Surgical intervention of mitral valve aneurysm: a case report

Background

Mitral valve aneurysm (MVA) is a rare structural abnormality characterized by a saccular protrusion of a portion of the mitral valve leaflet, typically the anterior leaflet. In a cohort of patients who underwent TEE, the diagnostic yield was reported to be only 0.2% [1, 2]. MVAs can lead to significant complications, including infective endocarditis (IE), thromboembolism, and mitral regurgitation (MR), and require careful management [1–3]. The preoperative diagnosis of mitral valve aneurysm (MVA) was challenging in this case due to the presence of multiple aneurysmal lesions on the anterior leaflet and the absence of clear saccular findings on echocardiography. This report describes a surgical case of MVA that was initially suspected to be a cardiac tumor on echocardiography.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old asymptomatic male patient underwent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) following a routine health examination that revealed a mildly elevated brain natriuretic peptide level. An approximately 8-mm structure was observed on the anterior mitral leaflet. The patient exhibited no prior medical history of cardiovascular risk factors and had not been receiving any medications. TTE revealed a 14 × 8-mm irregular isoechoic lesion in the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve. The lesion exhibited a broad base and attachment to the anterior mitral leaflet, showing a heterogeneous internal appearance. Furthermore, TTE revealed mild MR along the left atrial septum. Although mild aortic regurgitation (AR) was present, cardiac function was preserved. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed an 11 × 5-mm isoechoic structure in the rough zone of the A2 segment of the anterior mitral leaflet (Fig. 1). The lesion was associated with incomplete coaptation of the mitral valve leaflets and mild MR. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography did not reveal any findings of systemic embolism. Mitral valve surgery was determined to be the appropriate course of action considering the suspicion of a cardiac tumor.

Transesophageal echocardiography findings: (A) A structure is observed that protrudes into the left atrium from the anterior mitral leaflet. (B) Color Doppler imaging reveals mild mitral regurgitation at the same location. (C) Three-dimensional reconstruction reveals two protrusions on the left atrial side of the anterior mitral leaflet (arrows)

A median sternotomy was performed. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via ascending aortic perfusion and venous drainage from the superior and inferior vena cavae. The patient was then cooled to 32 °C. Subsequent ventricular fibrillation necessitated aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegic arrest via antegrade delivery from the aortic root. The mitral valve was approached via right atriotomy. Two nodular lesions were identified on the lateral and medial aspects of the A2 segment of the anterior mitral leaflet. The lesions measured 10 mm on the lateral side and 8 mm on the medial side. The lesion was observed to have an aneurysmal appearance in the valve leaflet, rather than a tumor or vegetation (Fig. 2). No lesions were identified on the posterior mitral leaflet. The lesion involved numerous chordae tendineae of the anterior leaflet, and mitral valve repair by resection was considered difficult. The anterior and posterior leaflets of the mitral valve were completely resected, and the mitral valve was replaced by a bioprosthetic valve.

Intraoperative findings: (A) Two aneurysmal lesions are observed on the anterior mitral leaflet. (B) The resected anterior mitral leaflet (A2) reveals two aneurysmal lesions (arrows), measuring approximately 10 mm (lateral) and 8 mm (medial)

Pathological examination revealed spindle cells sparsely scattered in a background of myxomatous changes at the site of the aneurysm (Fig. 3). The atrialis layer was preserved; however, the spongiosa layer was thickened. Furthermore, a decrease in collagen fiber density was observed in the aneurysm area, and the elastic fiber layer was disrupted. No evidence of neovascularization or inflammatory cell infiltration was found. There were no vegetation and neoplastic findings.

Pathological findings: (A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining reveals myxomatous degeneration in the aneurysm lesion. Inflammatory cell infiltration or granulation tissue formation is not observed. (B) The spongiosa layer is thickened; however, the atrialis layer remains intact. (C) Azan staining reveals decreased collagen fiber density in the aneurysmal portion. (D) Elastica van Gieson staining reveals disruption of the continuity of the elastic fibers in the aneurysmal lesion

Although 4 days of intensive care unit management was required after mitral valve replacement, the patient was discharged without any major complications. The patient has maintained an event-free period of 3 years after the surgery.

Discussion and conclusion

MVA is an uncommon structural abnormality characterized by a saccular protrusion of a portion of the mitral valve leaflet toward the left atrium. The number of reports concerning surgical treatment remains limited [4].

The precise mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of MVA remain unclear [3, 5]. The frequent occurrence of MVAs in the anterior mitral leaflet has prompted extensive investigation into the potential mechanisms underlying its pathogenesis [1, 3]. Although several etiologies have been reported, a significant proportion of patients present with concomitant IE of the aortic valve. The observed association between IE and MVA supports the hypothesis that inflammation initiated in the aortic valve may propagate to the mitral valve, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of MVA [6] Furthermore, the high prevalence of concomitant AR in patients with MVA suggests that the regurgitant jet induces anterior mitral leaflet damage, thereby initiating the pathogenesis of MVA [1–3, 7, 8].

A thorough pathological evaluation revealed localized myxomatous degeneration in the aneurysm, with no indications of postinfection inflammation or granulation tissue formation in this patient. It is hypothesized that regurgitant flow from aortic insufficiency causes damage to the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve, leading to localized inflammation. The application of systolic intraventricular pressure to the weakened area of the leaflet resulted in aneurysm formation [9]. Kim et al. also reported myxomatous degeneration at the aneurysmal site in a patient with MVA without IE [7]. However, MVA is not observed in all patients with AR, suggesting the involvement of other factors.

The diagnosis can be confirmed by echocardiography, which is characterized by a saccular echo-free space protruding on the leaflets; systolic expansion and diastolic collapse of the lesion are observed [1, 3]. However, the poor cystic appearance can be a diagnostic challenge and requires careful differentiation from cardiac tumors, vegetation, mitral prolapse, and chordal rupture. TEE is recommended for a more comprehensive evaluation. In this patient, the MVAs were not typical saccular findings and were located at two sites. Preoperative echocardiographic findings led to the suspicion of a cardiac tumor or vegetation, which prompted surgical intervention.

Various serious complications have been associated with MVA, including acute MR secondary to perforation, IE, and thromboembolism [1–3]. In particular, perforation-induced MR can progress rapidly, resulting in acute heart failure and potentially life-threatening situations. However, there is no a consensus on treatment interventions for MVAs. Although surgical intervention is necessary for patients presenting with complications, prophylactic treatment should be considered for patients with large MVAs or those undergoing concurrent cardiac procedures to reduce the risk of adverse events [3]. In contrast, there are reports showing no significant difference in event occurrence related to aneurysm size, leading to inconsistent conclusions [1]. A small MVA was detected incidentally during a routine health examination. The clinical presentation and early stage of the disease suggest that conservative management is a viable option if the diagnosis is established preoperatively [2, 3].

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of considering MVA in the differential diagnosis of atypical mitral valve lesions and underscores the need for careful evaluation and appropriate intervention.
