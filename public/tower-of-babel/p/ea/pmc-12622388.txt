Mental health outcomes in bariatric surgery patients in Saudi Arabia: A scoping review
Authors: Hessah Alzahrani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This review focuses on the outcomes of post-bariatric surgery mental health among Saudi patients. Due to the high rates of obesity in Saudi Arabia, bariatric surgery has become a popular option for improving one’s physical health; however, the psychological outcomes remain ambiguous. A systematic search of international databases (Web of Science, PubMed, PsycINFO, ScopusArabPsyNet, and Almandumah) found 17 studies (n = 5,155) that had been published during the last 15 years. Most studies employed a cross-sectional design, while the remaining studies employed retrospective and comparative designs. Overall, the findings of the identified studies reported improvements in patient self-esteem, quality of life (QoL), and physical health following surgery; however, many patients experienced psychological impacts, including depression, anxiety, and disordered eating tendencies. Furthermore, variable outcomes were noted, which may be attributed to existing mental health conditions, socio-cultural aspects, and time since surgery. In summary, bariatric surgery resulted in significant physical and psychosocial treatment benefits; however, some patients reported experiencing short-term mental health issues, which is particularly relevant for patient monitoring, the treatment of mental health needs, and the provision of culturally specific support.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a major emerging public health concern in Saudi Arabia[1]: its prevalence has increased rapidly in recent years, and the nation has one of the highest rates of obesity worldwide (particularly among adults). Since 1975, Saudi Arabia has experienced a gradual increase in the number of people diagnosed as overweight or obese. Estimates made between 1990 and 2019 indicated that over 60% of adults and 20%–60% of children and adolescents are affected.[1] Given the mounting obesity rate in Saudi Arabia, bariatric surgery has become the preferred treatment paradigm for obesity-related comorbidities.

In Saudi Arabia, bariatric surgery is widely used to treat obesity, and the nation has experienced the significant increase in the number of patients undergoing the procedure.[2] For the majority of patients, bariatric surgery has significantly improved their mental health outcomes: several studies have reported that patients demonstrate reduced anxiety and depression, and an increase in their overall quality of life (QoL) following surgery.[345] Additionally, the prevalence of postoperative depression was reduced (by between 8 and 74%), and the severity of depressive symptoms decreased (by between 40 and 70%).[3] Additionally, 6 months after surgery, patients reported improved emotional regulation and satisfaction[4]; however, other studies have reported contradictory results. For example, Soroceanu et al.[6] noted that some bariatric surgery patients may experience a decline in their mental health scores despite improvements in their physical health. Brown et al.[7] reported that some patients experienced negative mental health outcomes (such as an increased risk of substance use disorders, depression, suicide, and self-harm). Although bariatric surgery has been demonstrated to have significant positive effects on weight loss and physical comorbidities, its impact on psychological health remains undefined and may vary across different populations.[8] Unrealistic expectations and severe pre-existing psychopathology are two factors that can negatively impact postsurgical psychological outcomes. Additionally, although many patients reported improvements in their eating disorders, anxiety, and depression, there is evidence of an elevated risk of suicide, self-harm, and alcohol use disorder.[9]

The detection of mental health outcomes or issues exacerbated by cultural factors can be facilitated by implementing a systematic review focused on a specific cultural context (such as Saudi Arabia). It should be noted that several studies have identified discrepancies in mental health outcomes following bariatric surgery.

To determine whether these trends are reflected in Saudi Arabia, this research conducted a culturally specific review of several studies that have addressed the impact of gastric sleeve surgery on the mental health of Saudi Arabian patients. This review provides an enhanced understanding of the impact of gastric sleeve surgery and identifies any research gaps that require additional study. Furthermore, this review will enrich the current understanding of this area by providing data that may facilitate the design and implementation of support programs for patients who have undergone gastric sleeve surgery.

METHODOLOGY

Search strategy

A literature search was conducted in full compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework. The search was conducted across several databases (PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar) and covered a specific period (January 2010 to July 2025) to ensure the inclusion of studies published in the last 15 years.

The following search string was used and adapted to each database: (“bariatric surgery” OR “weight loss surgery” OR “sleeve gastrectomy” OR “gastric bypass”) AND (“mental health” OR “psychological” OR “depression” OR “anxiety” OR “quality of life” OR “QoL”) AND (“Saudi Arabia”). The search string was deliberately broad to ensure that studies/journal articles, review articles, and studies with similar search terms would be captured. Within each of the selected databases, Boolean operators and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were employed to maximize sensitivity and optimize specificity. In addition to searching the databases, the authors conducted a manual search of the reference list for included articles and relevant reviews. Furthermore, an additional search was conducted to include grey literature sources (such as the Saudi Digital Library (SDL), ArabPsyNet, Almandumah, and unpublished postgraduate theses from the repositories of Saudi Arabian universities), which produced three additional articles that met the full inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were judged as eligible based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) focused on patients in Saudi Arabia undergoing bariatric surgery, (2) reported at least one mental health outcome (e.g., depression, anxiety, QoL), (3) peer-reviewed or from credible sources of grey literature, and (4) published in either English or Arabic. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) studies without a clear mental health outcome, (2) studies that did not include Saudi participants, (3) studies that did not involve humans, and (4) letters, commentaries, and editorials without original data.

Study selection

The study selection process was conducted per the PRISMA-ScR checklist, and the flow diagram is presented in Figure 1. All the identified records were exported into a spreadsheet, and any duplicates were manually removed. The author screened titles and abstracts for eligibility (based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria) and examined the full texts of the potentially relevant articles to verify their eligibility for inclusion. In cases of uncertainty, studies were re-examined against the research objectives to reduce selection bias.

PRISMA-ScR flow diagram of study selection process

The search of open-access sources resulted in a total of 110 records (Web of Science, PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, ArabPsyNet, Almandumah, grey literature). Following an analysis of the retrieved records, eighteen duplicates were removed, resulting in a total of 92 results to screen. A total of 58 records were excluded at the title/abstract level, which left 34 articles for full-text analysis. Of the 34 full-text articles, 17 were excluded (with reasons for exclusion provided), leaving 17 studies for inclusion in the qualitative synthesis. The study selection was reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR flowchart (see Figure 1).

Quality appraisal

Although quality appraisal is not a requisite for scoping reviews, the methodological quality of the included studies was appraised via the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT, 2018 version) for mixed-methods and observational studies. Quality scores were arranged in Appendix 1 to illustrate the strengths and limitations of each study.

Data extraction and synthesis

A standardized data extraction form was employed to obtain information regarding authors, year of publication, study design, sample size, patient demographic information, type of bariatric procedure, mental health outcomes assessed, measurement tools, key outcomes, and quality scores. Additionally, the type of procedure was extracted and categorized. Data were thematically synthesized to provide information concerning short and long-term mental health outcomes, risk and protective factors, and procedure-specific effects (sleeve gastrectomy vs. gastric bypass).

RESULTS

Characteristics of included studies

The review featured 17 studies conducted in Saudi Arabia that examined psychological outcomes after surgery for weight loss. Most were cross-sectional hospital or clinic-based surveys, though there were several multicenter and community surveys; fewer studies featured retrospective and controlled comparative designs. The contexts of the studies were varied, and included community settings, single-center hospital/clinic cohorts[10111213]; and multicenter samples.[14] The timing of follow-up testing also varied; some studies featured fixed short-term follow-up sessions at 6 months post-surgery,[11] while others included restricted follow-up timelines of ≤36 months,[10] and a few studies included mixed follow-ups ranging from 6 months to >5 years.[1516] One study used a long-term follow-up with mean approximate timing of 4.0 ± 2.6 years across one sleeve cohort,[12] while another multicenter sample analyzed results for surgeries “within the last 5 years.”[16]

Participants across all studies were predominantly adults (≥15 years for almost all studies). On several occasions participants were explicitly designated as adults, though some studies used a broader age range (i.e., 15 to 64 years) and one did not separate out its specific sub-group of adolescents, instead reporting outcomes for all participants across ages. No studies featured a specific adolescent or student cohort, and no specific outcome metrics for pediatric trends were utilized; this means that adolescent data were not necessarily excluded, nor could they be analyzed separately. The procedures used reflected national practice patterns; Laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy (LSG) was the predominant treatment in all cohorts, whereas Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) and Adjustable Gastric Banding (AGB) were less frequently identified.[17]

Psychological measures and instruments

Across studies, psychological morbidity and well-being were measured using a range of reliable measures that included PHQ-9 for depressive symptoms,[10] GAD-7 for anxiety[1819202122] SF-36 (including the mental health/vitality subdomains) for health-related QoL,[1623] and the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale for self-esteem[14] Disorder-specific measures included Night Eating measure for NES.[11] Procedure-oriented or overall outcome measures used included Bariatric analysis and reporting outcome (BAROS),[17] while studies that were focused on women’s sexual health used the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) and related sexual QoL metrics.[13] (A key feature of the evidence base was thus instrument heterogeneity, with the resulting variance compounded by variability in the follow-up windows.

Quantitative prevalence of mental health conditions (a meta-summary)

Across the seventeen selected studies, the point estimates were clustered according to setting and study design. The percentage of patients who reported experiencing depression ranged from 3.8% to 35.0%. The lowest percentage (3.8%) was established by a study conducted by Alqifari et al.[19]; the mid-range (12.4%) was reported by Alghamdi[14]; and the highest recorded scores (30.4% to 35.0%) occurred in hospital/mixed samples and case series investigations conducted others.[1015] The overall rates of anxiety ranged from 9.3% to 40.8%[14]: reported diagnoses of GAD at 9.3%; a hospital-based cross-sectional study established moderate-to-severe anxiety in 33% of patients;[10] and Alamri et al. reported that 40.8% of patients in mixed-window cohorts experienced recurrent anxiety.[15] In a post-operative study conducted by Almuammar et al. Night-Eating Syndrome (NES) was consistently high (33.8%).[11] Notably, several post-operative procedures were associated with additional symptomology: For example, 44.4% of a long-term sleeve cohort reported that they had experienced gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).[12] Beyond this morbidity, there was a general improvement in the Mental SF-36 domains across studies,[16] and 66% of women reported higher sexual satisfaction post-bariatric surgery.[13] Supplementary Figure S1 contains a visual forest plot.

Short-term and long-term mental health outcomes

Surgical outcomes across the 17 Saudi studies were predominantly positive and reflected a variability related to their differing follow-up periods and settings. In the short term (≤12 months), the specific 6-month assessment revealed that night-eating syndrome was more frequently reported by women.[11] A survey (restricted to the time since surgery) revealed that depressive symptoms were elevated during the mid-term period (6–24 months) compared to subsequent time points, whereas anxiety severity was spread across a range of time periods.[19] Conversely, a hospital-based cross-sectional study (limited to participants ≤36 months) reported a substantial prevalence of moderate-severe depression and anxiety.[10]

Mixed window studies (6 months to >5 years) identified cases of recurrent moderate-to-severe depression and anxiety: body-image dissatisfaction and concerns about excess skin emerged as correlates to depressive symptoms, and the majority of participants advocated for psychological counselling.[15] Some longer-term sleeve cohorts reported co-occurring germ and anxiety; however, no formal tests were conducted to establish a correlation.[12] The QoL (SF-36) indices improved over stratified time-since-surgery (<6 months, 6–11 months, 1–5 years, and >5 years).[16] Additionally, a multicenter cohort study (consisting of participants who had undergone surgery in the last 5 years) reported improvements in the Rosenberg self-esteem measure, while a subset endorsed positive screens for depression and general anxiety.[14] Participants who had undergone body contouring (performed ≥6 months post-bariatric surgery) reported that their emotional well-being had improved during the first and second year, and after 2 years.[21] Among post-bariatric women, who were operated on between 2021 and 2022, the majority reported improved sexual satisfaction.[13]

Studies with a 6-month follow-up and footnotes that have a ≤12-month stratum[1119] report the clearest early signals: evidence after 1 year appears to be optimistic; however, it is variable, particularly when post-operative symptoms related to GERD continue to persist. Therefore, most of the remaining studies are best understood as having mixed-window evidence.

Protective and risk factors in the included studies

Several risk and protective factors were identified across the included studies. Body-image dissatisfaction and concerns regarding excess skin were associated with more depressive symptoms, while increased satisfaction had positive correlations with psychological QoL[15] Being female was an identifiable risk factor: women reported elevated levels of depression[1524]; reduced psychological QoL[25]; increased susceptibility to anxiety and depressive symptoms and body image dissatisfaction[15]; higher risk of night eating syndrome.[23] Being married was identified as a protective factor regarding an improved psychological QoL and depressive symptoms[162325] and those with a history of psychiatric issues were identified as risk factors across several studies.[14162324] Clinically, 44.4% of LSG patients had GERD, and anxiety symptoms were reported in the same cohort; however, no formal testing was conducted to establish a direct statistical correlation.[12]

Furthermore, several vitamin and micronutrient deficiencies (such as vitamin D, B12, and iron) were reported among post-operative patients, and there was some suggestion that such deficiencies were linked with affective disturbance; however, no formal analysis was conducted to establish these deficiencies as predictors.[16] Obesity persistence and insufficient weight loss were also correlated with inferior psychological outcomes.[24]

Psychological outcomes by procedure type

LSG was the most frequently performed bariatric procedure, followed by RYGB and AGB. All the included studies reported a correlation between the procedures and postoperative improvements in constructs related to a patient’s QoL and their self-esteem. For example, Al Kadi et al. used the BAROS composite and reported “good” or better ratings in 83.6% of LSG patients, 78.5% of RYGB patients, and 84.4% of AGB patients; the proportion rated “very good” was slightly higher after LSG (30.2%) than RYGB (25.3%) and AGB (18.8%). SF-36 vitality sub-scores were not assessed, and no head-to-head comparisons using standalone psychological instruments were reported between procedures.[17]

In a retrospective cohort, 44.4% of LSG patients had GERD and 34.2% had postoperative anxiety.[¹²] A national survey reported that there were significant associations between type and timing of procedure, and depression severity (χ² = 13.505, P = 0.009), where RYGB had a lower depression score compared to SG. Among 33.8% of night eating syndrome (NES) patients in a multi-procedure cohort, there were no differences in surgical technique.[23] However the same study reported that most of the improvements in sexual outcomes occurred in LSG cohorts: 66% of female patients reported an improvement in their post-operative levels of sexual satisfaction.

DISCUSSION

Across the 17 Saudi studies, consistent early gains in mental-health outcomes such as QoL, body-image satisfaction, and self-esteem were observed after bariatric surgery, though such improvements demonstrated heterogeneous durability thereafter. A parsimonious reading of these outcomes is that rapid weight loss and expectancy effects drive short-term improvements, whereas mid-term delays (~6–24 months) may have allowed the introduction of new stressors such as weight-loss plateaus, excess-skin concerns, and unmet appearance expectations, all of which have been linked to higher depressive-symptom burden in Saudi cohorts, as in, for example, the use of body-image dissatisfaction and excess skin as independent correlates in study.[15]

Compared with studies in Western cohorts using similar instruments and clinic-based sampling, estimates for depression and anxiety appear lower in the Saudi community. The most economical explanations for this are related to differences in sampling frame (community vs clinic), follow-up timing, and cut-off differences; stigma-related under-reporting in Saudi settings may, however, be a plausible secondary contributor. Multiple Saudi studies have documented that stigma reduces disclosure and help-seeking behavior,[2627] which could reduce rates of self-reported symptoms in community surveys. While Western clinic cohorts commonly report double-digit burdens prior to surgery, with only partial improvement at 12 months, residual morbidity persists.[282930] As the Saudi study synthesis did not directly test stigma, this explanation should be treated as plausible rather than proven, and it must be investigated alongside design/timing/threshold effects in any future work.

Sociocultural modifiers specific to the Saudi context—particularly gendered role expectations—may further shape postoperative mental-health outcome. Several Saudi cohorts reported higher postoperative anxiety/depression levels among women.[20222425] The qualitative Saudi evidence offers evidence of strong commitments to traditional gender roles and activities, particularly marriage, childbearing, and family care, with obesity perceived as a barrier to success in these areas; women thus describe shame or distress related to their appearance,[31] which may help explain their greater rates of anxiety and depression. Within this context, appearance-related pressures intensify body-image dissatisfaction as well as after rapid weight loss, heightening excess-skin concerns; these factors have been shown to be tied to residual depressive symptoms in Saudi postoperative samples[15] and similarly noted in western cohorts.[3233] Such pressures also drive demand for body-contouring, with post-contouring improvements in emotional well-being seen in the Saudi sample in this review.[21]

In addition, married patients repeatedly showed better mental QoL and/or lower depressive symptoms in Saudi samples,[162225] aligning with the country’s family-centric, collectivist context where spousal/family support may buffer postoperative stress. This pattern is consistent with international evidence linking partner/social support to better mood and mental HRQoL after bariatric surgery,[3435] though the magnitude of effect may be greater locally due to higher rates of family involvement in daily care and decision-making.

Baseline psychiatric history

In the current review corpus, prior psychiatric history was associated with higher likelihood of occurrence and increased severity of postoperative psychological problems.[10181924] This mirrors broader evidence that pre-existing psychiatric morbidity predicts suboptimal outcomes in such contexts, such as greater symptom burden, higher health-care use/readmissions, and attenuated weight-loss and QoL gains.[3637] Clinically, these findings highlight the need for systematic preoperative screening (e.g. PHQ-9, GAD-7), risk stratification, and integration of psychiatric care into the peri- and postoperative continuum.

Beyond psychosocial factors, physiological mechanisms may play a significant role in postoperative psychological trajectories. International evidence has demonstrated the significance of the gut-brain axis in terms of mediating mood and cognitive factors following gastric surgery.[3839] Fluctuations in the profile of the gut microbiome following radical gastric surgery such as a sleeve gastrectomy or gastric bypass can affect neurotransmitter production, immune signaling, and systemic inflammation, which may all result in mood system modifications. Nutrient deficiencies, particularly with respect to vitamin B12, folate, iron, and vitamin D, are recognized consequences of bariatric procedures, and their relationships with depressive symptoms and tiredness have been well documented.[4041] Some of the Saudi studies examined in this review noted this issue (albeit indirectly), observing that patients experiencing gastrointestinal symptoms or inadequate supplementation tended to report anxiety or lower vitality scores; however, this was observed as a temporal co-occurrence rather than a statistically tested association.[12] Based on this, the adoption of a dual approach that includes measuring psychological impairment and assessing early risks to improve prevention in combination with monitoring gastrointestinal factors may offer a more effective treatment for such issues.

These results emphasize the importance of monitoring gastrointestinal function during post-surgical mental health follow-up care. Investigations concerning ingestion-specific complications such as GERD, dyspepsia, and micronutrient deficiencies should also be conducted alongside psychological follow-ups, as any such complication may exacerbate anxiety, depression, and fatigue via the pathways of physiological dysregulation of the gut–brain axis. Risk reduction can thus be achieved via multidisciplinary collaboration, which may facilitate the implementation of early interventions such as nutritional supplementation and symptomatic relief, with the aim of minimizing risk, and optimizing physical and psychological outcomes for patients.

Against this backdrop, procedure-specific differences—particularly between sleeve gastrectomy and gastric bypass—may further shape psychological outcomes and inform follow-up priorities. The Saudi studies in this review indicate that sleeve gastrectomy and gastric bypass surgeries were the most common surgical procedures used for weight loss, with both surgery types predominantly associated with mental health outcome improvements and beneficial psychological results. While some Western cohorts suggest that gastric bypass may yield slightly greater gains in selected QoL domains (e.g., vitality) compared with sleeve gastrectomy[42]; Saudi studies generally did not focus on procedure-specific evaluation or head-to-head comparisons. Conversely, sleeve gastrectomy participants reported higher rates of GERD and dyspepsia, which co-occurred with higher anxiety symptoms across cohorts; however, no direct statistical association was tested in the relevant reports,[12] though these results were reflected internationally.[4344] These outcomes indicate the need for further discussion concerning the introduction of integrative postoperative care that takes account of both surgical and mental health outcomes.

Implications for practice and future research

The synthesis presents several implications for practice. Firstly, bariatric programs in Saudi Arabia should standardize the use of culturally sensitive mental health screening (e.g. PHQ-9, GAD-7) during pre-and post-surgery consultations. Secondly, to address physical, nutritional, and psychological well-being, multidisciplinary care (consisting of specialists such as psychologists and dieticians) should be provided to all patients. Furthermore, postoperative care should include ongoing psychological screening (such as PHQ-9, GAD-7) in conjunction with regular gastrointestinal follow-ups to monitor for potential complications (such as GERD, dyspepsia, micronutrient deficiency) and ensure appropriate multidisciplinary interventions that address both physical and mental health outcomes. Thirdly, future research should become longitudinal in nature by integrating extended follow-up periods and assessments to evaluate psychological fluctuations over time, while also considering gender and procedure type. Additionally, future studies should consider the use of qualitative research to develop an enhanced understanding of individual patient narratives and how cultural, family, and religious considerations impact mental health outcomes. Finally, adolescent bariatric surgery has been neglected in Saudi Arabia despite increasing levels of childhood obesity; therefore, this review recommends that future research should address the unique developmental and psychosocial challenges presented by these age groups.[45]

Limitations

This review contains some limitations. One important limitation concerns the lack of formal quality assessment of the studies included in this review. This is significant because many of the selected studies were cross-sectional or retrospective with small sample sizes (e.g. n = 72 in study,[46]) which limits their generalizability. Additionally, the follow-up duration of the selected studies was minimal, which limited their ability to measure longer-term psychological outcomes. The heterogeneous nature of the mental health measures, combined with the use of tools that had not been validated in-country, made cross-study comparisons challenging. The fact that this review was completed by only one author could mean that the selection and interpretation choices made could have been biased, which may have influenced the outcome of the review. Finally, certain aspects of Saudi Arabian culture (such as the under-reporting of psychological symptoms) may have impacted the reported prevalence rates.

CONCLUSION

Research in Saudi Arabia has indicated significant QoL and self-esteem gains from bariatric surgery; however, persistent mental health concerns (such as anxiety and depression) remain prevalent. These results are consistent with international evidence, which indicates that for many patients, physical improvement is not a guarantee of psychological recovery. While short-term mental health benefits are common, the results indicate that long-term, multidisciplinary follow-up (including psychological, nutritional, and gastroenterological care) is necessary in supporting physical and mental health outcomes.
