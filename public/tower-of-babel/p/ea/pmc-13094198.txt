Shared molecular signatures between atrial fibrillation and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: an integrated bioinformatic analysis with experimental validation
Authors: Wei Zhou, Qian Tang, Dandan Chen, Jiulin Chen, Linyan Shi, Fuliang Luo, Fei Yan, Yifan Mao, Huimin Lu, Xing Zhou, Zhangrong Chen, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are highly prevalent conditions that significantly impact patient outcomes. While mounting evidence suggests a strong epidemiological and clinical association between AF and COPD, the molecular basis underlying their frequent co-occurrence remains incompletely characterized. Rather than inferring direct disease-specific mechanistic links, this study aimed to identify shared systemic inflammatory and immune-related transcriptional signatures that are detectable across different tissues in AF and COPD.ResultsA total of 208 DEGs were found to be shared between AF and COPD. Functional enrichment analyses indicated that these genes were predominantly associated with inflammatory, immune, and extracellular matrix–related biological processes, including the NOD-like receptor, Influenza A, Phospholipase D, and ECM-receptor interaction pathways. PPI network analysis and external dataset verification highlighted CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 as reproducibly upregulated genes across independent AF and COPD transcriptomic datasets. Their elevated expression was further observed in AF, COPD, and comorbid samples in exploratory validation experiments. No single TF or miRNA was found to simultaneously regulate all three genes, suggesting heterogeneous and context-dependent regulatory patterns rather than a unified regulatory axis. Drug–gene association analysis identified estradiol and methyl methanesulfonate as compounds computationally linked to the shared gene set, without implying biological efficacy or therapeutic applicability.ConclusionThis study delineates shared inflammatory and immune-related transcriptional signatures observed across AF and COPD datasets, highlighting common pathways and candidate genes that may reflect systemic inflammatory states. These findings are hypothesis-generating and primarily reflect shared systemic inflammatory and immune responses rather than disease-specific causal mechanisms, providing a bioinformatic framework for future mechanistic and clinical investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-026-02335-4.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bioinformatics analysis, caspase 1 (CASP1), C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2), interferon-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 5 (IFIT5)

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common type of cardiac arrhythmia [1], affecting approximately 33.5 million individuals globally, representing a critical challenge for public health worldwide [2, 3]. The incidence and prevalence of AF are steadily rising with aging populations and improved chronic disease management, with a marked increase in older adults [4, 5]. Despite extensive research, the molecular mechanisms underlying AF remain incompletely understood. Oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, electrical remodeling, and autonomic imbalance have been recognized as key contributors to its arrhythmogenic substrate [6–9].

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive inflammatory airway disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and irreversible lung damage [10], and ranks as the third leading cause of deaths globally, with 3.72 million deaths in 2021 [11–13]. Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation drive COPD progression and are closely linked to systemic comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer [14].

Increasing evidence indicates a strong epidemiological and pathophysiological association between COPD and AF [15–17], with approximately 13% of patients with AF also suffering from COPD [18]. The coexistence contributes to higher risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, as well as adverse events such as thromboembolism, bleeding, and cardiovascular death [19–21]. A recent multicenter pilot study of patients undergoing typical atrial flutter ablation identified hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, and prior cerebrovascular events as major risk factors for subsequent AF development, highlighting the importance of comorbid conditions in AF pathogenesis [22]. However, in patients undergoing electrical cardioversion for AF, COPD has not been shown to reliably predict recurrence, underscoring the complexity of its clinical impact [23].

The relationship between AF and COPD has been widely explored, yet the precise molecular mechanisms remain elusive, and specific and effective treatments remain lacking. Chemokine signaling, particularly C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2)-related pathways, has been implicated in both AF and cardiovascular disorders [24], suggesting that immune and inflammatory dysregulation may represent a shared systemic background rather than a disease-specific causal mechanism.

Despite increasing clinical recognition, the shared molecular basis linking AF and COPD remains poorly understood. Previous bioinformatic studies have suggested potential overlapping pathways [25]; however, these studies either relied on a single dataset or lacked systematic cross-disease integration and experimental validation. Notably, the AF dataset GSE79768 has been used in prior analyses, but the COPD-specific datasets and independent validations presented here are novel. To address this gap, we applied an integrative, cross-disease transcriptomic analysis to identify shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that are consistently altered in AF and COPD datasets, rather than inferring direct pathogenic mechanisms. We combined the AF dataset (GSE79768) with a COPD dataset (GSE29133) to identify shared DEGs and subsequently validated key genes using independent datasets (GSE41177 for AF and GSE56766 for COPD) and peripheral blood samples. Single-gene discriminative performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis without constructing predictive models, and potential molecular interactions were explored through gene–drug enrichment analysis and docking simulation for hypothesis generation only.

This study aims to define shared inflammatory and immune-related molecular signatures between AF and COPD and to identify reproducible candidate genes associated with their frequent co-occurrence, rather than establishing causal disease mechanisms. Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Gene expression datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) [26]. Using the keywords “atrial fibrillation” and “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,” we systematically screened publicly available datasets and selected GSE79768 (AF) and GSE29133 (COPD) based on relevance, data quality, and completeness of sample annotation.

GSE79768 contains paired left and right atrial appendage tissues obtained from the same individuals, including patients with persistent AF and sinus rhythm controls. To appropriately account for the paired nature of the samples, subject identifiers provided in the original dataset were retained and incorporated into the differential expression analysis using a paired-sample design, explicitly modeling intra-individual correlations between paired atrial tissues. GSE29133 consists of lung tissue samples from COPD patients and matched non-COPD controls. As explicit case–control pairing information was available, subject identifiers were likewise incorporated into the differential expression analysis, and a paired-sample design was applied. Probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols using platform-specific annotation files from GEO platforms GPL570 (for GSE79768 and GSE29133).

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified separately between AF and matched control groups in GSE79768, and between COPD and matched control groups in GSE29133, using the Limma package in R. Genes with an adjusted P value (Padj) < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1.2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. This fold-change threshold was selected to balance biological relevance and statistical stringency in cross-disease transcriptomic analyses and was uniformly applied across datasets to minimize platform-specific bias. Padj values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Differential expression analyses were conducted independently within each dataset prior to overlap analysis, ensuring that tissue-specific and platform-related differences were appropriately controlled. For consistency, the term log2 fold change (log2FC) is used uniformly throughout the manuscript.

To enhance interpretability, DEGs were visualized using the Sangerbox platform (version 3.0, http://vip.sangerbox.com/) [27]. Quantile normalization was applied using default platform settings, and no additional batch correction was required, as datasets were analyzed independently. Visualization modules included volcano plots and heatmaps, with hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance and complete linkage, and z-score normalization applied across rows. All visualization parameters and processed data matrices are documented and available upon reasonable request to ensure reproducibility. Principal component analysis was visually inspected within each dataset to confirm the absence of obvious batch-driven clustering prior to DEG analysis. As analyses were performed independently for each dataset, no cross-dataset batch correction was applied.

Enrichment analysis of common differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Overlapping DEGs between AF and COPD were identified using Venn analysis. Functional enrichment of shared DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler package for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Significant enrichment terms were defined as adjusted p < 0.05. Results were visualized using Sangerbox to highlight biological processes, molecular functions, and pathways potentially linking AF and COPD.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

To explore interactions among shared DEGs, a PPI network was generated using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) [28]. Cytoscape (version 3.10.2) was used for network visualization. Hub genes were identified using the CytoHubba plug-in based on three centrality measures: Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), and Degree. Genes ranking in the top 5% across these metrics were considered candidate hub genes.

Key gene identification

To further examine the reproducibility of expression patterns of the identified hub genes, we conducted analyses using the GSE41177 dataset for AF and the GSE56766 COPD. The final key genes were selected through a two-step process: first, hub genes with high centrality in the PPI network were identified; second, these hub genes were validated across independent datasets to ensure reproducibility. Only CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 met both criteria and were selected as the final key genes. These datasets provided independent supportive evidence for the consistency of our findings. This step was therefore designed to precisely identify the critical genes involved in both AF and COPD and to ensure their biological relevance. The GSE41177 dataset includes 16 AF patient samples and 3 control samples, whereas the GSE56766 dataset contains 137 COPD samples and 69 control samples.

Leveraging the GeneMANIA platform (http://www.genemania.org/) [29], we developed a co-expression network for the identified key genes. GeneMANIA is renowned for its capability to predict and visualize functional associations among gene sets, including co-expression, physical interactions, and genetic correlations, providing valuable insights into their potential biological roles [30]. Notably, CASP1 mediates inflammasome activation, CXCR2 regulates neutrophil chemotaxis, and IFIT5 modulates innate immune responses, which collectively support mechanistic understanding of AF-COPD comorbidity.

Exploratory discriminative analysis of key genes

To avoid misinterpretation as a predictive or diagnostic model, ROC analyses were performed to explore the discriminative performance of individual key genes (CASP1, CXCR2, IFIT5) using independent validation datasets (GSE41177 for AF and GSE56766 for COPD). ROC curves and AUC values were calculated using the pROC package (version 1.17.0.1). These analyses were intended solely as exploratory validation of differential expression at the single-gene level. No multigene model training, calibration, or independent predictive validation was performed.

Transcription factor interaction analysis

TFs are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences, playing a central role in regulating gene expression. To further investigate the interactions between key genes and TFs involved in the pathogenesis of AF and COPD, we utilized the Network Analyst 3.0 tool (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) [31]. This platform offers an interactive environment for analyzing gene-gene, gene-transcription factor, and gene-miRNA interactions, facilitating a deeper understanding of gene regulation in these diseases [32]. This analysis aimed to explore the regulatory influence of transcription factors on key gene expression and their roles within functional pathways. The transcription factor target database was inferred from integrated Chip-X data in the literature [33]. Additionally, Cytoscape was used to visualize the TFs-mRNA regulatory network.

MicroRNA prediction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small, endogenous, non-coding RNA molecules that significantly influence gene regulation by either suppressing the translation of or promoting the degradation of target mRNAs. These regulatory RNAs are pivotal in fine-tuning gene expression, especially in complex physiological and pathological processes. To better understand the intricate mechanisms by which miRNAs regulate mRNAs, we systematically analyzed the interactions and regulatory networks formed between miRNAs and their target mRNAs [34]. We utilized miRTarBase (version 8.0) to study key gene-miRNA interactions, leveraging the database’s extensive collection of experimentally validated miRNA binding sites to uncover potential regulatory mechanisms [35]. By integrating this data, we constructed a network of miRNA-gene interactions. The network was subsequently visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.2), a robust tool for graphical representation and analysis of molecular interaction networks.

Identification of potential drugs

To identify novel potential therapeutic targets and effective drugs, we conducted gene-drug enrichment analysis using the Enrichr platform (https://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/Enrichr/) [36] linked to the Drug SIGnatures DataBase (DSigDB). DSigDB is an open-source drug feature database that includes over 2,000 genes and 17,389 unique compounds [37]. We identified drugs that were strongly associated with the candidate genes. Small molecule structures were sourced from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [38], a publicly accessible and comprehensive database aggregating information from more than 1,000 contributors. It contains an extensive repository of 119 million chemical compounds, 322 million registered substances, and 295 million bioactivity records, making it a valuable resource for chemical and biological research [39]. We obtained PDB-format macromolecular structures for CASP1 (PDB ID: 6F6R), CXCR2 (PDB ID: 4Q3H), and IFIT5 (PDB ID: 4HOR) from the UniProt and RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [40, 41]. These structures were processed in Discovery Studio 2019 software, where water and ligands were removed to isolate their binding sites. After preparing the receptor and ligand, we used the CDOCKER module, which relies on the –CDOKER_ENERGY (kcal/mol) scoring function and default docking parameters [42]. For each compound, the 10 docking poses with the lowest –CDOKER_ENERGY values (kcal/mol) were ranked. Among these, the pose with the lowest energy, indicating the most stable binding interaction, was selected as the optimal pose for further analysis. This approach ensures the selection of the most energetically favorable docking configuration for each compound.

Clinical sample information

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria(Table S1). Clinical blood samples were collected from the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University, with ethical approval granted by the hospital’s Ethics Committee. A total of 24 samples were collected, consisting of 6 healthy controls, 6 patients with AF, 6 with COPD, and 6 with both AF and COPD. Peripheral blood samples were drawn in EDTA tubes. All procedures were conducted in accordance with established standards. Clinical information including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, COPD severity according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) classification, atrial fibrillation (AF) subtype, and medication use (including anticoagulant therapy) was collected at enrollment.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To confirm the transcriptomic findings at the protein level and to establish the clinical relevance of key genes identified through bioinformatic analysis, we performed enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and Western blot experiments. CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 were selected based on their centrality in the PPI network and consistent differential expression across independent datasets. ELISA was used to quantify protein concentrations in peripheral blood, while Western blot provided complementary information on protein expression patterns and allowed assessment of relative abundance across disease states. These experiments were designed to validate the predicted molecular signatures and to ensure that transcript-level changes correspond to protein-level alterations in patients with AF, COPD, or both.

To quantify the protein expression levels of CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were performed on serum samples collected from healthy controls (n = 6), patients with atrial fibrillation (AF, n = 6), patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, n = 6), and patients with both AF and COPD (AF+COPD, n = 6). Commercial ELISA kits specific for human CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions(FANKEW). Briefly, 100 µL of standards or samples were added to pre-coated 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, biotin-labeled antibodies and enzyme-conjugated streptavidin were added sequentially. After further incubation and washing, TMB substrate was added, and the reaction was stopped by sulfuric acid. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the average OD values were used to calculate protein concentrations based on the standard curves.

qRT-PCR

Peripheral blood samples from four groups—AF (n = 6), COPD (n = 6), AF combined with COPD (n = 6), and healthy controls (n = 6) were collected for gene expression analysis using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). RNA was isolated with the RNAeasy™ Blood Total RNA Extraction Kit (R0091M, Beyotime), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the ALL-in-One First-Strand Synthesis MasterMix (EG20117M, Yugong Biotech), adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed on the QuantStudio 6 Flex System (Life Technologies) with Taq SYBR® Green qPCR Premix (Universal). Triplicate analyses ensured data robustness, while beta-actin was used as a normalization control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, where ΔCt values were first obtained by normalization to the internal reference gene, and ΔΔCt values were calculated using the mean ΔCt of the healthy control group as the calibrator. Primer sequences, detailed in Table S2, facilitated high-accuracy quantification, enabling a comprehensive comparison of expression levels under various conditions.

Western blot

As described above, Western blot analysis was performed to complement ELISA by providing semi-quantitative data on protein expression patterns and verifying the presence of CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 in patient samples. This approach allows confirmation that transcript-level differences correspond to actual protein expression changes across AF, COPD, and AF+COPD groups.

To extract protein, 3 volumes of red blood cell lysis buffer (C3702, Beyotime) were added to peripheral blood samples from 6 control cases, 6 AF patients, 6 COPD patients, and 6 AF combined with COPD patients. The mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to ensure effective separation of the components. After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully removed without disturbing the pellet to avoid any contamination or loss of the solid fraction. This step was crucial for maintaining the integrity of the pellet for subsequent analysis. This centrifugation process was repeated 2–3 times. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in PBS, followed by a final centrifugation step at 3000 rpm for 10 min, ensuring the removal of any residual contaminants.Then, high-efficiency RIPA lysis buffer (ROO10, Solarbio) was added at 20 times the volume of tissue sample. The samples were subjected to high-speed centrifugation at 14,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min to ensure effective separation of components. After centrifugation, the clear supernatant was carefully collected, avoiding disruption of the pellet to retain only the liquid phase for subsequent analysis. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (RC0020, Solarbio). SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was performed for Western blotting.After adding 5× protein loading buffer (P1040, Solarbio), the samples were incubated at 100 °C for 7 min. The SDS-PAGE gel was run at a voltage of 80 V for the stacking layer and 120 V for the separating layer to achieve optimal protein resolution. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore) were pre-activated by immersing in methanol for 3 min to enhance protein binding. Proteins were then transferred onto the membranes using a constant current of 400 mA for 0.5 h. Following transfer, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for CASP1 (WL03450, Wanleibio), CXCR2 (bs-1629R, Bioss), IFIT5 (CAP4911, Cohesionbio), and GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech), ensuring specific detection of target proteins. After incubation with the primary antibodies, the membranes were washed thoroughly three times with TBST to remove unbound antibodies. Incubated at room temperature for 60 min with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Following this, excess secondary antibodies were removed by washing the membranes three times with TBST, each wash lasting 10 min to ensure complete removal of unbound reagents. Immunoreactivity was visualized using the SuperSignal™ ECL Reagent (MA0186, Meilunbio). Chemiluminescent signals were captured using a high-sensitivity imaging system (SH-523, Hangzhou Shenhua Technology, China), allowing precise detection of target proteins.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this study were conducted using the advanced software tools GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and SPSS 29.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or number (n), as appropriate. For pairwise comparisons between two groups, the unpaired Student’s t-test was applied. Comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post-hoc multiple-comparison procedures implemented within GraphPad Prism to identify between-group differences when the overall ANOVA was statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the small sample size of the clinical validation cohort (n = 6 per group), statistical analyses were primarily descriptive and intended to support consistency assessment rather than definitive hypothesis testing. No formal assumptions testing or multivariable adjustment for potential clinical confounders was performed. Instead, relevant clinical variables were descriptively reported to provide contextual interpretation of inflammatory gene expression patterns. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted in accordance with standard exploratory research practices.

Results

DEG screening in AF and COPD

We analyzed GEO dataset samples and performed differential expression analysis on the AF and COPD datasets. In the AF dataset (GSE79768), 1857 DEGs were identified(Table S3), and in the COPD dataset (GSE29133), 2214 DEGs were found(Table S4). These DEGs for AF and COPD were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps (Figs. 2 A–D). A Venn diagram was created to display the overlap of DEGs between the two datasets, identifying 208 shared genes (Figs. 2E–F).This reveals a substantial number of common genes between AF and COPD (Table S5).

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the 208 shared DEGs, with GO results (Figs. 3A–C) demonstrating notable enrichment across various functional categories. The biological process (BP) category exhibited significant enrichment in pathways including defense responses to other organisms, defense against viruses, viral response, and inhibition of viral genome replication. In the cellular component (CC) category, the intrinsic component of the membrane, extracellular region part A, and extracellular space were highly represented. Molecular function (MF) analysis identified significant enrichment in peptidase activity specific to L-amino acid peptides, overall peptidase activity, and collagen-binding, emphasizing their roles in enzymatic regulation and structural maintenance. KEGG analysis (Figs. 3D) revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as NOD-like receptor signaling, Influenza A, Phospholipase D signaling, and ECM-receptor interaction. These findings strongly indicate that the common DEGs between AF and COPD are critical in regulating immune responses, viral defense, chemokines, cytokines, and inflammatory processes, which are closely related to the onset and progression of both diseases.

The network, with a minimum interaction score of 0.4, included 192 nodes and 116 edges (Figs. 4A). Three different topological network algorithms in the CytoHubba plugin were employed, leading to the identification of the top 5% of 11 hub genes (Figs. 4C-D). By intersecting the Venn diagram, 10 overlapping genes were identified (Figs. 4E), including IFIT5, IFI16, TRIM22, IFI44L, IRF9, OAS3, CXCL11, BST2, CASP1, and CXCR2.

Using the GeneMANIA database, we conducted a detailed analysis of the co-expression network and functional associations of these genes. Key gene identification

We further validated the expression of the ten hub genes using additional AF and COPD datasets. The analysis revealed significant expression differences for these genes. Analysis of the external atrial fibrillation (AF) dataset GSE41177 revealed that all ten hub genes demonstrated significant differential expression when compared with the normal control group (Fig. 5A). Similarly, in the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) dataset GSE56766, three hub genes exhibited marked expression changes relative to controls (Fig. 5B). By synthesizing the findings from these two datasets, IFIT5, CASP1, and CXCR2 emerged as pivotal genes commonly involved in both AF and COPD, suggesting their potential role in the shared pathological mechanisms underlying these conditions.

Exploratory ROC analysis of key genes in AF and COPD

To explore the potential discriminative ability of the identified key genes, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed for each gene individually. The area under the curve (AUC) was used as a descriptive metric to quantify the ability of gene expression levels to distinguish disease samples from controls, without implying the development of a predictive or diagnostic model. In the AF dataset (GSE79768), IFIT5, CASP1, and CXCR2 exhibited AUC values exceeding 0.80 (Figs. 5C–E), indicating moderate-to-good discriminative ability between AF and control samples. Similarly, in the COPD dataset (GSE29133), these three genes demonstrated AUC values greater than 0.85 (Figs. 5F–H), suggesting consistent discriminative potential in distinguishing COPD samples from controls.

Gene expression in clinical samples

Clinical data were statistically analyzed, and a baseline table summarizing clinical characteristics was constructed (Table 1). In our study cohort, CASP1 expression levels were markedly elevated in AF (P < 0.001), COPD (P = 0.003), and AF+COPD (P = 0.002) patient groups relative to healthy controls (Figs. 6A). Similarly, IFIT5 expression showed significant upregulation in AF (P = 0.006), COPD (P = 0.017), and AF+COPD patients (P = 0.019) compared to controls (Figs. 6B). Likewise, CXCR2 mRNA levels were notably higher in AF (P = 0.029), COPD (P = 0.010), and AF+COPD patients (P = 0.004) than in controls (Figs. 6C). These results should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and are intended to illustrate consistency with transcriptomic findings. Although smoking status, disease severity, AF subtype, and medication use are known to influence inflammatory responses, the small sample size precluded stratified or adjusted analyses. Therefore, the observed expression patterns should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory.

Integrated validation of CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 protein expression

To validate the protein expression of CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5, we conducted ELISA and Western blot analyses on samples from healthy controls, AF, COPD, and AF+COPD patients. Both methods showed that CASP1 expression was significantly increased in AF and further elevated in COPD and AF+COPD groups. Western blot analysis demonstrated a stepwise increase in CASP1 protein levels across disease states (Figs. 6D-F, J). Compared to controls, CASP1 expression was significantly elevated in the AF group (P < 0.05), further increased in the COPD group (P < 0.01), and reached the highest level in the AF+COPD group (p < 0.001). Similarly, CXCR2 expression was significantly upregulated in AF (P < 0.01), COPD (P < 0.05), and AF+COPD (P < 0.05) groups. IFIT5 also showed increased expression in AF (P < 0.05), with a further rise in COPD (P < 0.05) and AF+COPD (P < 0.01) groups compared to controls. These results were further supported by ELISA analysis(Figs. 6G-I). CASP1 concentrations in serum were significantly elevated in AF patients (P < 0.05) and showed further increases in COPD and AF+COPD groups (P < 0.01). CXCR2 levels were mildly increased in AF (P < 0.05) and significantly higher in COPD (P < 0.01). Although CXCR2 levels in the AF+COPD group were lower than those in the COPD group, they remained elevated compared to controls (P < 0.01). IFIT5 concentrations were also increased in AF (P < 0.05), peaked in COPD (P < 0.01), and remained elevated in the AF+COPD group (P < 0.05).

TFs-miRNAs interaction network analysis

An interaction network was constructed between the key genes and TFs, consisting of 53 nodes and 57 edges (Figs. 7A). CASP1, IFIT5, and CXCR2 are regulated by 14, 21, and 22 TFs, respectively. SPI1, STAT4, and SMAD4 regulate both CASP1 and CXCR2. RUNX1, MYB, and GATA3 jointly regulate both IFIT5 and CXCR2, while IRF8 regulates both IFIT5 and CASP1. Using the miRTarBase database, we identified potential miRNA binding sites and constructed a regulatory network linking key genes and their corresponding miRNAs (Table S6). The constructed network comprises 34 nodes and 31 edges, revealing that CASP1 is targeted by 9 miRNAs, CXCR2 by 18 miRNAs, and IFIT5 by 4 miRNAs (Fig. 7B). This highlights the intricate regulatory relationships and the diverse mechanisms through which miRNAs may influence the expression and function of these pivotal genes.

Screening of candidate compounds

From the gene-drug interaction data provided by the Enrichers platform and DSigDB, we discovered estradiol and methyl methanesulfonate as candidate compounds interacting with CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 (Table S7). Another compound, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS), was also retrieved in the analysis; however, MMS is a DNA-alkylating agent and mutagen, not a clinically viable drug, and is included here only for completeness of the computational screening. Molecular docking simulations were conducted to evaluate the binding affinities of the target proteins CASP1 (PDB ID: 6F6R), CXCR2 (PDB ID: 4Q3H), and IFIT5 (PDB ID: 4HOR) with the candidate molecules estradiol (PubChem ID: 5757), while docking of MMS (PubChem ID: 4156) was performed solely for exploratory purposes. This analysis aimed to explore the molecular interactions that may underlie the potential therapeutic effects of estradiol. By quantifying interaction energies, we sought to identify the compound with the strongest affinity and, consequently, the most promising therapeutic potential.

The docking results (Table 2) show the -CDOCKER Interaction Energy (-CIE) scores, where higher values reflect a stronger binding affinity between the active compound and the target protein. All calculated –CDOCKER Interaction Energy (–CIE) values were positive, indicating relatively favorable predicted interaction energies between estradiol and the target proteins under the applied docking conditions. Importantly, these scores represent relative computational affinity rankings rather than evidence of spontaneous binding, biological activity, or therapeutic efficacy. Molecular docking was employed solely as an in silico approach to explore potential protein–ligand interaction patterns and does not constitute experimental validation of binding or functional modulation.

Discussion

The association between AF and COPD is increasingly recognized as bidirectional and strongly correlated [15–17, 25, 43–45]. We explicitly acknowledge that the discovery datasets were derived from different tissues (atrial myocardium and lung), introducing unavoidable biological heterogeneity. Rather than inferring direct molecular causality, and given that systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation are central features of both conditions with shared transcriptional signatures reported across organs and peripheral blood [46, 47], our analysis focused on identifying transcriptional patterns associated with systemic immune activation rather than disease-specific pathogenic mechanisms or direct disease–disease interactions.

Previous bioinformatics studies have reported overlapping molecular targets, such as CDK8, SLC22A15, and TNFRSF17, in AF and COPD [25]. Rather than implying direct disease–disease interactions, these overlaps are more plausibly interpreted as reflecting convergent immune-inflammatory and tissue-remodeling processes commonly activated in chronic cardiovascular and pulmonary disorders. Thus far, however, the identified molecular targets remain unverified through cellular experiments, animal studies, or clinical tissue validation. Given that both AF and COPD are not solely attributed to a single gene, it is likely that multiple genes are involved in both conditions. In this study, we focused on the common DEGs shared by AF and COPD to identify transcriptional signatures associated with systemic immune activation and inflammation across cardiac and pulmonary tissues, without inferring direct causal links between the diseases. Recent clinical studies further support the bidirectional link between AF and COPD. For example, a multicenter pilot study identified hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, and prior cerebrovascular events as major risk factors for AF development in patients undergoing typical atrial flutter ablation, highlighting the interplay of comorbidities [22]. Conversely, COPD has limited predictive value for AF recurrence after cardioversion, emphasizing the complexity of shared mechanisms [23], which may be reflected at the transcriptomic level as overlapping inflammatory signatures rather than direct disease-specific effects.

In this study, GO enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed the pivotal involvement of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway in inflammation. This pathway is increasingly being recognized as a central mechanism in the development of AF, suggesting that targeting this pathway may offer new therapeutic strategies for AF management [48–50]. In AF patients, the NLRP3 inflammasome, which contains the NOD, LRR, and pyrin domains, is significantly activated in atrial cardiomyocytes [51–53]. Recent reviews further highlight that inflammasome activation drives fibrosis and electrical remodeling in cardiac tissue [54]. Beyond inflammasome signaling, accumulating experimental evidence indicates that fibroblast activation, cellular senescence, and profibrotic signaling pathways are central contributors to atrial structural remodeling. Li et al. demonstrated that fibroblast senescence promotes atrial fibrosis through activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway [55]. Jiang et al. demonstrated that xanthohumol markedly attenuated TGF-β1-induced cardiac fibroblast activation via the PTEN–Akt–mTOR axis [56]. In the context of our study, these mechanistic findings are cited to provide biological plausibility for interpreting shared DEGs as reflective of coordinated inflammatory and remodeling programs, rather than as evidence of direct cross-organ molecular causality.

Inflammation is a central factor in the development of COPD, with extensive evidence supporting the involvement of NLRP3 in its pathogenesis [57–60]. KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed key pathways such as Influenza A, Phospholipase D signaling, and ECM-receptor interaction, all of which are significantly involved in the pathogenesis of both AF and COPD. These shared pathways suggest overlapping inflammatory and immune-related signaling landscapes between cardiac and pulmonary tissues, which may contribute to observed comorbidity patterns without establishing direct mechanistic causation [61]. Importantly, recent experimental and translational studies strengthen the biological plausibility of interpreting our shared DEG signature as reflecting coordinated immune-inflammatory responses that affect both heart and lung. Zhu et al. provide evidence that immune cell–driven inflammatory programs and regulated cell-death pathways contribute to cardiac tissue injury and remodeling, linking immune infiltration and necroptosis/metabolic dysfunction to adverse cardiac phenotypes [62]. Complementarily, Certo et al. [63] systematically reviewed the metabolic reprogramming of immune cells in cardiovascular diseases, highlighting the critical intersection of metabolic perturbations, immune activation, and regulated cell death pathways in cardiac pathophysiology. Emerging studies underscore the regulatory layers beyond classical cytokine signaling. Shen et al. [64] experimentally demonstrated that miR-132 modulates the SIRT1/FoxO1 axis in cigarette smoke–induced COPD, where miR-132 deficiency attenuated airway inflammation and epithelial apoptosis. Wang et al. [65] reported that dysregulated miRNA pathways contribute to endothelial inflammatory activation. These findings provide experimental evidence for sirtuin-mediated and miRNA-dependent regulation of inflammation in pulmonary disease and are consistent with our observations of systemic immune activation reflected in shared DEGs between AF and COPD.

Through PPI network analysis, CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 were identified as common key genes. CASP1, a cysteine protease, is activated by multiple inflammasome ligands and is critical for protein processing and the activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-18, which are pivotal in mediating inflammation [66, 67]. Active caspase-1 also triggers the cleavage and activation of Gasdermin D, inducing pyroptosis, a form of lytic cell death [68, 69]. CASP1 is critical in immune responses [70]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, consisting of NLRP3, caspase-1 (cysteine-aspartic acid protease-1), and ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein), triggers inflammation via caspase-1 activation. Our findings extend prior studies by confirming CASP1 expression in clinical AF and COPD tissue samples, providing evidence that CASP1-mediated pyroptosis may link cardiac and pulmonary tissue remodeling. Growing research highlights the pivotal contribution of inflammation to both the initiation and maintenance of AF [52, 53, 71]. Growing evidence suggests that the NLRP3 inflammasome is a key mediator in the persistent airway inflammation that underlies the progression of COPD. Additionally, recent research has revealed that the NLRP3 inflammasome plays a significant role in sustaining airway inflammation and advancing the development of COPD [72–75]. Collectively, these findings support CASP1 as a biologically plausible shared inflammatory marker, consistent with prior experimental evidence, rather than definitive proof of a causal molecular mechanism linking AF and COPD.

CXCR, a chemokine receptor, is a crucial membrane receptor on neutrophils, responsible for regulating neutrophil migration and recruitment [76, 77]. CXCR2, a G-protein-coupled receptor, is predominantly found on neutrophils, lymphocytes, and various non-hematopoietic cells.The interaction of CXCR2 with its ligands governs neutrophil migration to sites of injury and plays a role in disease processes [78]. The CXCL-CXCR2 signaling pathway regulates neutrophil activation in response to infection and tissue injury [79–81]. Recently, neutrophil-mediated fibrosis via CXCR2 signaling has been demonstrated in cardiac remodeling and in cardiovascular disease [82]. In this study, CXCR2 likely reflects neutrophil-driven inflammatory activation common to both AF and COPD, rather than a disease-specific therapeutic target for their comorbidity.

The IFN-induced proteins with tetratricopeptide repeat (IFIT) family, which are also termed classical IFN-stimulated genes, are clustered on chromosome 10 in humans and consist of four members: IFIT1, IFIT2, IFIT3, and IFIT5. These genes are essential for regulating immune responses and maintaining physiological balance [83, 84]. Among them, IFIT5 is a type I interferon-inducible protein that recognizes viral or endogenous double-stranded RNA and potentiates innate immune signaling by interacting with TAK1 and IKK, thereby promoting IKK phosphorylation and NF-κB activation [85, 86]. Furthermore, Jun Huang et al. demonstrated that IFIT5 can induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by suppressing mature miR-99a, consequently enhancing cell migration and invasion [87]. Although direct evidence for IFIT5 in AF is currently limited, recent reports show IFIT5 upregulation in lung endothelial injury, suggesting that IFIT5 may amplify innate immune and signals that contribute to tissue remodeling [88]. Although prior studies suggested a role for IFIT5 in immune modulation, its specific involvement in AF and COPD comorbidity requires further validation, which our study highlights.

While the role of IFIT5 in the pathogenesis of AF is not fully established, its identification in our integrative bioinformatic analysis may partly reflect the presence of other comorbid conditions in the GEO datasets, as the available clinical information does not allow comprehensive assessment of concomitant diseases. Nevertheless, its established functions in innate immunity and inflammation suggest a plausible contribution to AF-COPD comorbidity, which is further supported by our integrative analysis. Transcription factor and miRNA analysis provided only limited additional insight, as no single regulator was found to co-regulate all three hub genes. Although this observation is relatively straightforward, it may still guide future experimental validation of individual gene regulation.

Accurate biomarker discovery is essential for improving the understanding of complex chronic diseases. In this study, we did not aim to construct or validate diagnostic or predictive models. Instead, exploratory analyses were performed to evaluate whether CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5 exhibit discriminative signals within the original discovery datasets. Together, these genes converge on immune-inflammatory pathways, particularly NOD-like receptor and chemokine signaling, supporting the interpretation that systemic inflammation represents a shared molecular landscape underlying AF and COPD, rather than immediate clinical utility.

From a systems-pharmacology perspective, the network-based drug–target–pathway analysis was conducted to contextualize shared DEGs within known bioactive molecular networks, rather than to identify therapeutic agents. Similar pathway-centered strategies have been applied in complex cardiopulmonary disorders to highlight perturbed signaling pathways without implying drug efficacy [89, 90]. Accordingly, the in-silico drug–gene associations and docking results in this study should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, intended to prioritize pathways and molecular contexts for future experimental investigation. Estradiol and methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) emerged from computational screening; however, these findings do not establish therapeutic relevance, as estradiol is a systemic hormone and MMS is a known mutagen.

Molecular docking analysis suggested potential structural compatibility between estradiol and CASP1, CXCR2, and IFIT5; however, predicted binding alone does not demonstrate functional modulation, specificity, or clinical efficacy. Epidemiological and experimental studies have reported associations between estradiol levels and AF risk [91, 92], as well as potential roles of estrogen signaling in inflammatory and oxidative processes relevant to cardiovascular and pulmonary disease [93–96]. Nevertheless, estradiol exerts broad pleiotropic effects, and its biological actions are not confined to the targets examined in this study. Therefore, docking results should be interpreted strictly as supportive computational observations rather than evidence of pharmacological modulation.

Methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) appeared in our computational screening due to its known molecular effects on DNA and cellular stress. However, MMS is a mutagen and is not suitable as a therapeutic agent. Its identification in silico mainly highlights molecular pathways relevant to AF-COPD comorbidity, rather than clinical applicability, and provides a reference point for understanding potential mechanisms. These findings highlight the need for further studies in this area. In summary, the major innovations of this study include: (1) the first cross-disease transcriptomic analysis of AF and COPD, identifying common genetic and signaling features; (2) multi-level supportive validation of key targets using three complementary molecular techniques in clinical samples; and (3) the integration of bioinformatics and pharmacology to propose candidate molecules for further experimental and clinical evaluation, rather than definitive therapeutic recommendations.

While our study advances understanding of AF and COPD’s shared mechanisms, its limitations must be acknowledged. The challenges we faced in this study stemmed from several factors. First, due to the inherent differences between the diseases under investigation, it proved difficult to find suitable publicly available datasets with corresponding tissue samples. Moreover, the insufficient clinical data available in these databases significantly heightens the risk of sample contamination, potentially leading to inaccurate conclusions. This could potentially introduce biases in the analysis, affecting the accuracy and reliability of the results. The limited data availability also underscores the need for more comprehensive and well-curated clinical datasets to improve the robustness of future studies. Second, the small sample size of clinical data remains a notable limitation, which may reduce statistical power and increase the likelihood of correlation bias, potentially influencing the reliability of the conclusions. Additionally, the exploratory validation of protein expression (ELISA and Western blot) in peripheral blood, while supportive, does not directly reflect tissue-specific gene regulation and may be influenced by systemic confounding factors. Future studies with larger, well-characterized clinical cohorts are warranted to validate these findings. The transcriptomic datasets analyzed in this study were derived from different tissues (atrial myocardium for AF and lung tissue for COPD), which may introduce biological heterogeneity. Although large-scale resources such as the GTEx database provide valuable reference expression profiles across multiple tissues, GTEx primarily represents non-diseased samples and lacks disease-specific pathological contexts. Therefore, while GTEx could serve as a complementary reference, it may not fully capture disease-related transcriptional alterations relevant to AF or COPD. Future studies integrating disease-specific multi-tissue datasets may further refine these findings. Finally, due to limitations in laboratory resources and the constraints of available project funding, we are unable to conduct additional in vitro experiments to further validate the efficacy and safety of the potential drugs identified in our study. These limitations underscore the need for future studies with larger datasets, improved clinical data quality, and additional funding for experimental validation.

Conclusion

Our study identifies shared inflammatory and immune-related molecular signatures associated with AF and COPD, providing a hypothesis-generating bioinformatic framework for future experimental validation and translational research. The findings contribute to understanding the systemic inflammatory landscape underlying AF–COPD comorbidity and offer reproducible candidate genes for further mechanistic investigation. Rather than establishing causal mechanisms or therapeutic strategies, this work provides a foundation for future studies aimed at improving biological understanding and guiding targeted experimental exploration of these coexisting conditions .
