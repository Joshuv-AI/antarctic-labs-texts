Assessment of Small Airways Function in Eosinophilic Preserved Ratio Impaired Spirometry
Authors: Tianran Zhou, Hongyu Gao, Xingxing Sun, Jianhua Xu, Hanqing Zhu, Mian He, Wenlan Yang, Jinming Liu, Jian Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPreserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) is an important phenotype of pulmonary function in clinical and public health practice. It is possible for some patients to have chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) at an early stage. At present there is little research on the association of PRISm with type 2 (T2) inflammation biomarkers. The blood eosinophilia and impairment of small airway function in PRISm have not been fully assessed. This study investigated the eosinophilic phenotype in PRISm.MethodsBetween January 2019 and September 2022, a retrospective assessment was conducted in a single pulmonary function unit in China. PRISm was defined as forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ≥ 70% and FEV1 < 80% predicted. Two groups were formed among the PRISm participants: eosinophilic PRISm, blood eosinophil count (BEC) ≥ 150/µL; non-eosinophilic PRISm, BEC < 150/µL. Differences were analyzed between eosinophilic and non-eosinophilic PRISm.ResultsThe study included 313 participants, of whom 135 were assigned to the eosinophilic PRISm group. After adjusting for potential confounders, compared to non-eosinophilic PRISm, eosinophilic PRISm remained correlated with lower natural logarithm (ln) MEF25% predicted (− 0.161 [− 0.267, − 0.054], P = 0.003) and elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) (10.616 [6.384, 14.849], P < 0.001).ConclusionEosinophilic phenotype was common in individuals with PRISm. Compared to participants with non-eosinophilic PRISm, those with eosinophilic PRISm tended to have impaired MEF25% predicted and elevated FeNO.

Introduction

Preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) is a common phenotype of pulmonary function in clinical practice that does not meet the criteria for airflow restriction in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and is manifested by a decrease in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and/or forced vital capacity (FVC), while the FEV1/FVC ≥ 70% [1]. Recently, relevant research has defined the criteria for the diagnosis of PRISm as FEV1 < 80% predicted, while the ratio of FEV1/FVC remained more than 70% [2, 3]. It is worth noting that some patients could progress towards airflow obstruction [4] and PRISm may be an early physiological stage of COPD [5]. It is associated with respiratory symptoms, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and increased mortality [3, 6].

Eosinophilic inflammation is a common and stable phenotype of COPD [7]. Evidence of type 2 inflammation which is associated with increased risk of exacerbations is present in 20–40% of patients with COPD [8–11]. In patients with type 2 inflammation, increased levels of immune cells and cytokines cause elevated eosinophil counts and elevated levels of fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) [10–14]. Eosinophil count has been used as a surrogate for airway eosinophilia in COPD [8, 15]. The is evidence that the presence of eosinophils is associated with disease-related airway remodeling [16]. Previous studies have shown that eosinophil accumulation is associated with decreased lung function [17, 18]. Small airway inflammation is a major risk factor for the development of COPD, which may be related to eosinophil levels [19]. Studies have demonstrated that small airway dysfunction occurs in patients at all stages of COPD and that early identification and treatment could delay disease progression [20]. At present the eosinophilic inflammation and impairment of small airway function in PRISm have not been fully assessed. Whether the eosinophilic phenotype is also present in patients with PRISm and what the lung function status is in these patients are unknown. The correlations between eosinophilic phenotype and the progress from PRISm to COPD still need further research.

The aim of this study is to investigate the eosinophilic phenotype in patients with PRISm and to provide evidence for further investigation of the role of T2 inflammation in airway destruction in PRISm. In this study, we analyze data from the Pulmonary Function Unit of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital affiliated to Tongji University and hypothesize that eosinophilic PRISm is associated with impaired small airway function and elevated FeNO.

Methods

Study Population and Design

From January 2019 to September 2022, subjects who underwent spirometry (Jaeger Toennies spirometer, Germany) after bronchodilator inhalation according to the American Thoracic Society (ATS) [21] in the Pulmonary Function Unit of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital affiliated to Tongji University were included. Inclusion criteria required age > 18 years, spirometry-confirmed PRISm (defined as FEV1/FVC more than or equal to 70% and FEV1% < 80% predicted after bronchodilator inhalation), concurrent FeNO measurements and routine blood tests. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a history of asthma (including positive bronchodilator response, defined as post-bronchodilator FEV1 or FVC increased ≥ 12% and ≥ 200 mL), COPD or other lung diseases; (2) on chemotherapy for malignancy; (3) other systemic diseases causing elevated peripheral blood eosinophil counts or impairment of lung function. Subjects were divided into two groups based on blood eosinophil count (BEC) level: (1) eosinophilic PRISm, BEC ≥ 150/µL; (2) non-eosinophilic PRISm, BEC < 150/µL. The cutoff value of 150 was determined according to previous studies in COPD [22, 23]. Functional indicators for small airways were as follows: maximum expiratory flow rates evaluated at the usual intervals of 50% of exhaled FVC (MEF50), maximum expiratory flow at 25% of FVC left (MEF25), and maximum mid-expiratory flow (MMEF75/25). Impaired small airway function was defined as MEF50 < 65% predicted, MEF25 < 65% predicted, or MMEF75/25 < 65% predicted after bronchodilator inhalation. The cutoff value of 65% was determined according to previous studies on the Chinese population [24, 25]. The GLI (Global Lung Initiative)-Global equations were used as the reference standard.

The endpoint of the study consisted of assessing the correlation between eosinophilic PRISm and impaired small airway function and analyzing the differences of FeNO between eosinophilic and non-eosinophilic PRISm.

Ethical Approval

The Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital affiliated to Tongji University approved this study (L20-236-1). All patients in this study signed informed consent forms. The study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

Data Collection

The electronic medical record system of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital affiliated to Tongji University was used to collect the data, including demographic characteristics, pulmonary function measurement reports, routine blood tests, and comorbidities. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, smoking status, smoking exposure (pack-years), and body mass index (BMI). Never smoked was defined as smoking fewer than 100 cigarettes over the course of a lifetime. Routine blood tests were taken early in the morning on the day of the pulmonary function tests. Comorbidities included diabetes and hypertension. Comorbidities were provided by the patient’s medical record homepage in the electronic medical record system for the diagnosis of the disease, according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 diagnostic code.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). Statistical differences between the groups were compared through t tests (normal distribution), Wilcoxon rank sum tests (skewed distribution), and chi-square tests (categorical variables). The associations between eosinophilic PRISm, impaired lung function, and FeNO were evaluated by regression analysis. Adjusted variables included sex, age, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension). According to the STROBE guidelines [26], unadjusted (model 0) and fully adjusted (model 1) equations were presented simultaneously. SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, USA) was used for all analyses. The statistical significance threshold was set as two-sided P < 0.05.

Patient and Public Involvement

It was not appropriate to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

The small airways function values of participants with eosinophilic PRISm, including MEF50% predicted (45.3% vs. 46.4%) and MMEF75/25% predicted (35.9% vs. 37.8%), were lower than those with non-eosinophilic PRISm. The median of FeNO in the eosinophilic PRISm group was significantly higher than in the non-eosinophilic PRISm group (16.0 ppb vs. 12.0 ppb). No significant differences were found between age and sex in the two groups.

Association Between Eosinophilic PRISm and MEF25

The association between eosinophilic PRISm and MEF25% predicted was evaluated (Table 2). In the non-adjusted model (model 0), participants with eosinophilic PRISm tended to have lower natural logarithm (ln) MEF25% predicted compared to those with non-eosinophilic PRISm. In model 1 (adjusted model), which adjusted for variables including sex, age, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, and comorbidities, eosinophilic PRISm remained correlated with lower ln (MEF25% predicted) (− 0.161 [− 0.267, − 0.054], P = 0.003). The corresponding value remained significant in this fully adjusted model with adjustment of all potential cofounders. Discussion

In this study, we aimed to investigate the eosinophilic phenotype and impairment of small airways function in PRISm. We found that the eosinophilic phenotype, characterized by increased blood eosinophil count and FeNO, was also present in patients with PRISm. The prevalence of eosinophilic PRISm (blood eosinophil counts ≥ 150 cells/μL) was 43.1%. Compared to non-eosinophilic PRISm, eosinophilic PRISm was significantly associated with lower ln (MEF25% predicted) and higher FeNO.

Impaired Small Airway Function and Elevated FeNO in Eosinophilic PRISm

In our study, in the non-adjusted and adjusted models, eosinophilic PRISm was significantly associated with lower ln (MEF25% predicted) and higher FeNO. We therefore hypothesized that eosinophilic PRISm has a higher risk of impaired small airway function compared to non-eosinophilic PRISm.

A small airway was defined a small bronchiole with an inner diameter of less than 2 mm. The narrow lumen, lack of cartilage, and the slow airflow were reasons for its susceptibility to damage from harmful particles and gas. Small airway diseases were widely present in COPD [20]. A pathological study has shown that the loss of small airways precedes emphysema [27]. Early identification of small airway dysfunction and treatment may delay disease progression. Physiological measures for assessing small airway function included spirometry, percentage decrease in FVC during hyperresponsiveness testing (air trapping in peripheral airways), lung volumes measured by body plethysmography, and impulse oscillometry, among others [28]. Spirometry was a common measure for assessing lung ventilation, where MEF50% predicted, MEF25% predicted, and MMEF75/25% predicted were commonly used indicators to evaluate small airway function [20, 27, 29].

Traditional diagnostic criteria for COPD defined FEV1/FVC as < 70% and the early stages of COPD cannot be identified. Studies in recent years have found that PRISm may be an early physiological state of COPD [5]. Despite the decline in FEV1, the ratio of FEV1 to FVC has remained ≥ 70% [30]. The clinical manifestations of PRISm were similar to COPD, which manifested as more dyspnea, shortening of the 6-min walk distance, and increasing percentage of radiographic emphysema [31]. PRISm can transition to COPD of varying severity during follow-up [32]. Twenty-five percent of patients with PRISm in the COPDGene cohort progressed to COPD, compared with 32.6% in the Rotterdam Cohort Study [33]. PRISm might be an important lung function state that identifies individuals at risk of developing COPD [5, 33, 34]. In our study, compared with non-eosinophilic PRISm, eosinophilic PRISm tended to have higher risk of impaired small airway function. Thus, we hypothesized that eosinophilic PRISm was possibly a high-risk subgroup for progression to COPD. However, although we found that eosinophilic PRISm had impaired small airway function, eosinophilic PRISm was significantly associated with lower MEF25% predicted only.

Previous studies showed that blood eosinophil count was positively correlated with FeNO, which was a substitute for airway eosinophilic inflammation and T2 endotype [35, 36]. We found that eosinophilic PRISm had a higher risk of elevated FeNO. This was consistent with previous studies. Our research indicated that the eosinophilic phenotype, characterized by increased blood eosinophil count and FeNO, was also present in patients with PRISm. Furthermore, we found that there was no significant difference in FEV1% and FEV1/FVC between the two groups of PRISm with different levels of blood eosinophil counts, which was consistent with some previous studies [36, 37]. Importantly, future studies should incorporate matched controls to assess PRISm phenotype specificity.

Blood Eosinophil Counts and Lung Function Decline

Eosinophils can be assessed across multiple sample types, including blood, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, and tissue biopsies. While the last of these methods are invasive and typically restricted to tertiary care facilities, blood eosinophils remain the most clinically accessible resource in routine practice [38]. Previous studies have found that elevated blood eosinophil counts can be used as a surrogate marker for airway eosinophilic inflammation [8, 15]. Epidemiological data showed that no matter what the diagnosis of asthma was, the increase of blood eosinophils would lead to the deterioration of lung function [39, 40]. It was worth noting that a study by Zhong et al. found that more than 50% of patients with non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis had recurrent exacerbations after treatment and that small airway dysfunction increased over time [41]. In patients with chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), the decline of lung function was related to the degree of type 2 inflammation. Compared with the control group, MEF25 and MEF50 of non-asthmatic patients with CRSwNP significantly decreased, which was related to the severity of CRSwNP [42]. Little was known about the potential role of eosinophils in the early pathogenesis of COPD [43]. Further research was needed to explore whether the transition from PRISm to COPD was associated with high blood eosinophil counts and PRISm with high blood eosinophil counts could be treated as a high-risk subgroup. Intervention targeting this subgroup might improve clinical symptoms and delay disease progression.

Previous Pathological Findings

There was currently little knowledge of the pathogenesis associated with PRISm. This abnormality may be associated with small airway dysfunction. Pathological changes to emphysema in the small airways, bronchiolitis, and peribronchial lymphatic aggregation were common in patients with PRISm [6].

The increase of blood eosinophils would lead to the deterioration of lung function [39, 40]. This may be related to the role of eosinophils in airway pathophysiological remodeling. There was evidence that the existence of eosinophils was directly related to airway remodeling [16]. Eosinophilic inflammation of airway wall was associated with the thickening degree of reticular basement membrane [44, 45]. As a result of airway remodeling, patients may have irreversible airway obstruction, which may lead to deterioration of lung function, airway thickening, and air trapping [16]. The structural effect of airway remodeling driven by eosinophils has been assumed to explain the cause of persistent airflow obstruction [16, 46–48]. Therefore, recognizing eosinophilic inflammation should be an important part of PRISm management.

Prevalence of Eosinophilic PRISm

There was a lack of research concerning the prevalence of eosinophilic PRISm worldwide and our study provided new information. Since patients with eosinophilic inflammation cannot be identified easily from clinical features and eosinophil counts in sputum are not easy to perform clinically, blood eosinophils are suitable as a measure of eosinophilic inflammation in the airways [37] and could predict the nature of future inflammatory deterioration [7]. Previous studies have shown that up to 40% of patients with COPD have an eosinophilic phenotype, defined as a peripheral blood eosinophil count of approximately 150 to 200 eosinophils per cubic millimeter [10, 22, 43]. In the cohort of patients with bronchiectasis, 49.6% patients had blood eosinophil count ≥ 150 cells/µL [36]. Our study suggested that the eosinophilic phenotype was also common in PRISm. A meta-analysis found median values of 130–140 cells/µL in the healthy population and 160–235 cells/µL in asthma [49]. Our mean value of 169 cells/μL in PRISm suggested that PRISm may be higher than in the general population.

BMI and Smoking Status of Eosinophilic PRISm

In eosinophilic PRISm, compared to non-eosinophilic PRISm, the BMI and the proportion of participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were both significantly higher. This was consistent with previous studies: from an epidemiologic point of view, there might be a positive correlation between blood eosinophil count and BMI. Obesity, metabolic changes, and eosinophilic inflammation may coexist [50, 51]. Moreover, diabetic vasculopathy impairs pulmonary vascular reserve, thus increasing its susceptibility to pathological processes and complications caused by aging, and the combination of diabetes in parenchymal lung disease accelerates the decline in lung function [52]. Nevertheless, we did not find a significant difference in the prevalence of diabetes between eosinophilic and non-eosinophilic PRISm.

Studies have confirmed that blood eosinophil count could be used to identify smokers with airway eosinophilic inflammation, who are high-risk individuals of COPD, thus providing opportunities for early intervention of COPD [40, 49]. In this study, a higher proportion of participants with eosinophilic PRISm were smokers and the mean value of pack-years in eosinophilic PRISm was higher too. However, these differences were not statistically significant. The relationship between smoking and eosinophilic PRISm needs to be validated by further studies in other populations.

Limitations

The study had some limitations. First, despite the lack of pathological evidence, we tested for impaired small airway function solely based on spirometry. Therefore, our findings should only be applied to spirometry-defined impaired small airway function. Future research should employ multimodality assessments—such as plethysmography for air trapping evaluation, impulse oscillometry for airway resistance measurement, and chest CT for structural imaging—to comprehensively characterize small airway pathophysiology. Secondly, eosinophil counts in sputum were not easy to perform clinically, so we used blood eosinophils as a measure of eosinophilic inflammation in the airway. Third, cross-sectional retrospective studies were not able to establish a causal relationship between eosinophilic PRISm and COPD. There was a lack of analysis of longitudinal changes in eosinophilic PRISm to assess whether the transition from PRISm to COPD was associated with the high blood eosinophil counts. Fourth, PRISm can exhibit air trapping, which reduces FVC and thus increases the FEV1/FVC ratio [53]. However, as a result of clinical limitations, lung volumes were not measured using plethysmography in this retrospective study. Prospective studies should incorporate lung volume measurements to clarify the role of gas trapping in PRISm pathogenesis.

Conclusion

In summary, these observations suggested that eosinophilic phenotype was common in individuals with PRISm. Compared to individuals with non-eosinophilic PRISm, those with eosinophilic PRISm tended to have impaired small airway function and elevated FeNO.
