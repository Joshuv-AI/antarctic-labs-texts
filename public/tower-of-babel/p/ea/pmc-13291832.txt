Hemoperfusion with a polymer-based column alters inflammatory responses in lipopolysaccharide-treated horses in vivo
Authors: Kallie J. Hobbs, Yu Ueda, Andre N. V. Le Sueur, Bethanie L Cooper, Megan J. Burke, M. Katie Sheats
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo determine the effect of hemoperfusion with a polymer-based column on systemic cytokine concentrations and neutrophil dysfunction in lipopolysaccharide-treated horses in vivo.Methods6 university-owned horses received 60 ng/kg lipopolysaccharide, IV, as a bolus and then 60 ng/kg, IV, as a constant rate infusion over 1 hour. Endotoxemia was confirmed by clinical signs and neutropenia. In a crossover model that was completed from January 2024 through July 2024, hemoperfusion was performed for 4 hours with either a sham or polymer column. Blood was collected at 5 time points over a 72-hour period for flow cytometry analysis and 14 time points for cytokine multiplex analysis.ResultsThere were significant differences between column treatment and sham treatment, including increased early and late neutrophil apoptosis and improved reactive oxygen species production in response to stimulation at the postfiltration time point. Additionally, 2 of 5 column-treated horses had improved CBC parameters compared to 0 of 5 sham-treated horses. Systemic cytokines were not significantly different between column and sham treatment.ConclusionsThe results of this study provide proof of concept for hemoperfusion with a polymer-based column as a potential treatment to mitigate deleterious lipopolysaccharide-induced immune responses in adult horses.Clinical RelevanceFurther investigation and optimization of hemoperfusion as an adjunctive treatment for sepsis in the horse is warranted. Because there are known differences in lipopolysaccharide infusion and clinical sepsis, further investigation in horses with clinical sepsis is needed.

Severe systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and/or sepsis are leading causes of mortality in equine patients. Immune system dysregulation plays a key role in the pathophysiology of severe SIRS/sepsis. One of the driving forces of this immune dysregulation is an imbalance of cytokines. Cytokines are small protein mediators produced by immune cells that have both pro- and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Irregular cytokine release and/or elimination can cause endothelial dysfunction, leading to downstream effects, such as hypotension, poor organ perfusion, and disseminated intravascular coagulation.1,2

Cytokines also play a key role in the regulation of neutrophil responses to systemic inflammation. In sepsis, neutrophils undergo delayed apoptosis, and the recruitment of neutrophils into tissues contributes to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome.3,4 Additionally, an overproduction of cytokines can result in exuberant release of cell-free DNA (cfDNA), leading to an overproduction of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)5 in combination with immune paralysis despite ongoing systemic insult from bacterial and viral sources. Immune paralysis can lead to diminished neutrophil responses, such as decreased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). This results in inadequate clearance of infectious organisms and can lead to secondary infections.6

In human medicine, hemoperfusion targeting the removal of cytokines has been used as an adjunctive treatment for sepsis, and studies7 have demonstrated improvements in immune function and clinical outcomes. Moreover, several clinical studies8-10 have shown decreased concentrations of cytokines, a reduced need for mechanical ventilation, and a lower occurrence of multiple organ dysfunction in patients treated with hemoperfusion. These studies have primarily utilized 3 different types of columns: polymer, activated carbon, or atraumatic coated beads. Currently, only 2 types of columns are marketed for veterinary use in the US, which contain a polymer-based adsorbent bead (VetResQ) or an activated carbon adsorbent bead (AimaLojic).

Hemoperfusion with a polymer-based column has been previously shown to remove cytokines from equine whole blood ex vivo and to be feasible and safe in healthy adult horses.11,12 Therefore, we hypothesized that hemoperfusion with a polymer column would decrease systemic cytokine concentrations and attenuate neutrophil dysfunction in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated horses in vivo.

Methods

Animals

This study was approved by the North Carolina State IACUC (No. 21-110). Six healthy adult horses of various breeds and ages, owned by North Carolina State University, were selected for inclusion in this study. Horses’ health statuses were evaluated through a physical examination, CBC, and chemistry analysis.

Hemoperfusion

All horses had a 14-French X 25-cm double-lumen temporary hemodialysis catheter (Arrow) placed sterilely in the left or right external jugular vein without complication using standard over-the-wire catheter placement and an additional 10-gauge over-the-needle catheter (Mila International) placed distal to the hemodialysis catheter (Figure 1). The hemodialysis catheter was used as the outlet of blood flow to enter the circuit, whereas the 10-gauge was used as the return catheter for blood entering the horse. Horses were stimulated with 60 ng/kg LPS, IV, from Escherichia coli 055:B5 (Sigma-Aldrich) followed by a 60-ng/kg constant rate infusion for 60 minutes.13-16 Following LPS stimulation, endotoxemia was confirmed by physical examination parameters, such as the development of hyperthermia (> 38.6 °C), tachycardia (> 60 beats/min), tachypnea (> 20 respirations/min), or hyperemic mucus membranes and decreased leukocyte and neutrophil counts prior to the start of filtration. Once endotoxemia was confirmed, horses were administered a 50-U/kg bolus of unfractionated heparin within 10 minutes to obtain a starting activated clotting time (ACT) of 500 to 900 seconds (reference, 198 to 299 s/heparin). Hemoperfusion therapy was initiated using a polymer-based column or sham for 4 hours. During hemoperfusion, horses were maintained on a constant rate infusion of unfractionated heparin at 10 to 30 U/kg/h to maintain ACT values between 500 and 900 seconds. Following a 30-day washout, horses received the opposite treatment. While the hemoperfusion treatment was time based, every effort was made to filter the same blood volume for each animal in column versus sham treatment. Aliquots of whole blood were collected into 5-mL centrifuge tubes throughout filtration or sham at 14 time points (0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300, and 360 minutes and 24, 48, and 72 hours). Blood samples were stored on ice until the plasma was harvested by centrifugation (776 to 1,318 X g) at 4 °C for 10 minutes. Plasma harvesting took place within 15 to 20 minutes of sample collection, and 3-mL aliquots of plasma were immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C for cytokine and cfDNA analysis.

Cytokine analysis

Quantitative analysis of cytokines was measured using an equine-specific 23-cytokine MAP Magnetic Bead Panel (Millipore) per the manufacturer’s protocol using a previously validated method.17 Samples were analyzed in duplicate. Briefly, for the assay, 96-well plates were washed before use using a kit wash buffer. Background (assay buffer), standard, and control wells were loaded onto the plate. Next, 25 μL of each sample and assay buffer was plated in sample wells, and each sample was plated in duplicate. Plates were covered and incubated on an orbital shaker overnight at 4°C in the presence of magnetic beads coated with fluorescently labeled capture antibodies for each analyte. Beads were then washed and incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies, followed by the addition of streptavidin-phycoerythrin. Beads were washed again before resuspension in 150 μL of sheath fluid, and sample analysis using a minimum count of 50 beads/well was used for inclusion in the analysis. Standard curves were analyzed and deemed to be appropriate. The mean of the duplicate samples was used for analysis. The plate was run on a Luminex Bio-Plex 200 Suspension Array Stem (Bio-Rad), and Bio-Plex Manager Software (version 6.2; Bio-Rad) was used for analysis.

Chemistry and CBC count analysis

Biochemistry analysis was performed at baseline, 4 hours after filtration, and 24 hours after filtration using a chemistry analyzer (Stago) housed in the North Carolina State University Clinical Pathology Laboratory. Glucose, urea, creatinine, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium, total protein, albumin, globulin, triglycerides, bilirubin, ALP, AST, GGT, sorbitol dehydrogenase, creatine kinase, sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate were all included in the analysis. All chemistry values were analyzed for statistical significance and deviations from normally accepted ranges.

Flow cytometry analysis

Annexin V and cellular death were quantified using a dead cell apoptosis kit with annexin V (Invitrogen). Initially, 20 mL of whole blood was allowed to settle at room temperature for 45 minutes, and leukocyte-rich plasma was aliquoted into 1-mL centrifuge tubes (Eppendorf; control, annexin V). Tubes were spun at 300 X g at room temperature for 10 minutes in a centrifuge. The supernatant was then decanted, and cells were resuspended in 1,000 μL of annexin binding buffer. The solution (100 μL) was placed into a flow cytometry tube. Then, 5 μL of Alexa Fluor 488 Annexin V (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 μL of 100 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) working solution—prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions—were added to each 100 μL of cell suspension. Cells were incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes. After incubation, 400 μL of binding buffer was added to the tube and gently mixed. Samples were immediately analyzed through use of a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) with a standard lens setup. Quadrants were defined using cells singly stained with annexin V or PI. Populations of live (annexin V−/PI−), apoptotic (annexin V+/PI−), and dead (annexin V+/PI+) neutrophil singlets were quantified based upon at least 10,000 events. Postacquisition data analysis was performed using commercial software (FlowJo, version 10.7.3, Becton Dickinson). For data analysis, the percentages of live, apoptotic, or dead neutrophils were expressed relative to unprimed neutrophils.

Reactive oxygen species were evaluated using a ROS detection assay kit (Cayman Biologics). Initially, 20 mL of blood was allowed to settle at room temperature for 45 minutes, and then leukocyte-rich plasma was aliquoted into 1-mL Eppendorf tubes (negative control, positive control, experimental). Tubes were spun at 300 X g for 5 minutes in a centrifuge. The supernatant was then decanted, and cells were resuspended in 1,000 μL of 1X ROS Label (prepared by kit instructions). Resuspended cells were allowed to incubate at 37 °C for 30 minutes in a cell culture incubator. After incubation, cells were respun and the supernatant decanted. One hundred microliters of ROS detector (dihydrorhodamine 123; manufacturer directions) was added, and the cells were allowed to incubate for 60 minutes before being immediately analyzed by flow cytometry using a flow cytometer (Cytoflex; Beckman Coulter) with a modified Excitation/Emission = 495/529 nm lens set up. For the measurement of induced ROS, cells were stimulated with 240 μL of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 30 minutes prior to the addition of the ROS detector.

Cell-free DNA

The measurement of cfDNA in equine plasma was performed using a commercial kit (DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit; Qiagen) as previously described.18,19 In brief, 250 μL of plasma was added to a proteinase and buffer mixture and then vortexed. Samples were then incubated at 56 °C for 10 minutes. After incubating, > 96% ethanol was added and mixed. The mixture was then pipetted into a DNeasy spin column, placed in a 2-mL collection, and centrifuged at 6,000 X g for 1 minute. This process was repeated an additional 2 times. After the third centrifugation, the spin column was transferred to a new 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tube, and the DNA was eluted by adding 50 μL of Buffer AE (Qiagen) to the center of the spin column membrane. After incubation for 5 minutes, the column was spun a final time, and cfDNA was read from the eluted sample. Concentrations of cfDNA were measured using a compact benchtop fluorometer according to manufacturer instructions (Qubit 4; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentration of DNA in each sample was automatically calculated by the fluorometer algorithm based on fluorescence and dilution ratio. The DNA concentration reported by the instrument was converted to cfDNA concentration in the extracted plasma sample using a previously reported equation.18

Statistical analysis

It is currently unknown what percentage of reduction in cytokines will be of clinical benefit in septic equine patients. Average human cytokine reduction with polymer-based columns is 30% during the course of treatment.7 A statistical a priori power analysis (type II error = 0.2; type I error = 0.05)20 showed that 5 horses were necessary to detect a 30% reduction in cytokine concentrations. Six horses were included to allow for horse attrition. Statistical analysis was performed using commercially available software (Prism, version 10.5; GraphPad Software Inc). All data were tested for normality, and parametric and nonparametric tests were chosen as appropriate, including but not limited to the Student paired t test and repeated-measures 1-way and 2-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparison testing for parametric data and a Wilcoxon pairs signed-rank test, Friedman test, or mixed-model test with Dunn multiple comparison for nonparametric data. Cytokines were evaluated for percentage of removal by using the formula [((old – new)/old) X 100]. A P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Animals

Five horses met the inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table S1). One horse successfully underwent the sham session but had to be removed from the treatment group due to placement of the 10-gauge blood return catheter into the carotid artery. This resulted in a hematoma, and as a result it was elected to remove them from the study due to the requirements for heparinization. All horses were able to maintain an ACT of 500 to 900 seconds during each filtration session without adverse effects. All horses that completed the study had between 1.3 and 2.1 total blood volumes filtered (44.2 to 69.3 L), with flow rates between 250 and 350 mL/min. For each arm of the study, the filtered blood volume was consistent for each individual horse.

Cytokines

Cytokines/chemokines included in the analysis were as follows: IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-17A, IL-18, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, IFN-γ–induced protein 10 (or C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10), granulocyte-stimulating factor, granulocytemonocyte colony-stimulating factor, growth-regulated protein (growth-regulated oncogene or C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1), monocyte chemotactic protein–1 (or C-C motif chemokine ligand [CCL]-2), FGF-2, eotaxin (or CCL11), fractalkine (or C-X3-C motif chemokine ligand 1), and regulated on activation, normal T-cell expressed and secreted (or CCL50). Measured cytokines and chemokines with significant stimulation (2X baseline) in this model included IL-1β, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. Nineteen measured cytokines either failed to stimulate or were below the lower limit of detection. The percentage of decrease in measurable cytokines was not significantly different between treatment groups. The mean percentage of decrease in TNF-α was 73.3% with column treatment and 72.6% with sham treatment. The mean percentage of decrease in IL-1β was 10% with column treatment, whereas IL-1β increased by 4.5% in the sham group. The mean percentage of decrease in IL-10 was 3% with column treatment and increased by 109% with sham treatment. Finally, mean IFN-γconcentrations decreased by 135% with column treatment and 20% with sham treatment.

Biochemistry

Compared to sham, the column treatment group had significant decreases in biochemistry parameters, including triglycerides (median, 87.56; range, 74.40 to 100 g/dL; P = .0182; 100% of horses), potassium (median, 3.5; range, 3.2 to 4.1 mmol/L; P = .0002; 100% of horses), and calcium (median, 9.75; range, 8.6 to 11.0 mg/dL; P = .001; 88% of horses). No other significant increases or decreases were observed (Supplementary Table S2).

Complete blood cell count

With column treatment, all horses returned to normal neutrophil and total WBC counts between 4 and 72 hours. With sham treatment, 3 of 5 horses returned to normal neutrophil counts, and 4 of 5 horses returned to normal WBC counts within 72 hours (length of the study period). There was no significant difference in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio between the treatment groups (Supplementary Table S3).

Apoptosis

At the end of treatment (time point, 4 hours), the fold change in the percentage of early apoptotic cells was significantly different between sham and treatment groups (P = .03; Figure 2). Additionally, during endotoxemia recovery (time point, 72 hours), the fold change in the percentage of late apoptotic cells was significantly different between sham and treatment groups (P = .03; Figure 3). There was no significant difference in the fold change of necrotic cells at any time point compared to the LPS-stimulated baseline. Notably, even though there was no significant difference when fold change was compared over the 72-hour period, there was a 0.53-fold decrease in necrotic cells in the column treatment group and a 7.0-fold increase in necrotic cells in the sham group relative to the LPS-stimulated baseline.

Reactive oxygen species

Column treatment resulted in a lower mean fold change in levels of systemic ROS at all time points (after filtration, 2.26 ± 2.17; 24 hours, 6.75 ± 9.5; 48 hours, 37.9 ± 64.64) compared to the sham treatment (after filtration, 4.92 ± 2.4; 24 hours, 38.17 ± 59; 48 hours, 5.97 ± SD). However, this difference was not statistically significant. Interestingly, there was a significant difference in cellular capacity for induced ROS between sham and treatment, with column treatment resulting in increased PMA-stimulated ROS immediately after treatment (time point, 4 hours; P = .04; Figure 3).

Cell-free DNA

Plasma cfDNA was significantly increased at 1 hour after LPS infusion in both groups. The difference in plasma cfDNA in column-treated versus sham-treated horses was not significantly different (Figure 4).

Discussion

The results of this study provide proof of concept for hemoperfusion as a potential treatment to mitigate deleterious LPS-induced immune responses in adult horses. Column treatment in LPS-stimulated horses showed improved CBC parameters, increased neutrophil capacity for stimulated respiratory burst, and increased early and late neutrophil apoptosis. While systemic cytokines were not significantly different between column and sham treatment, the small sample size with large individual animal variation in cytokine responses to LPS, the relatively low levels of cytokine stimulation, and the relatively short filtration period could have limited these findings and statistical analyses. Additional research is needed to further address the impact of hemoperfusion on systemic cytokines as well as continue to optimize hemoperfusion protocols for adult horses.

The measurable differences in the percentage of decrease in cytokines between column versus sham, but lack of statistical significance in these differences, could be a result of the small number of horses and/or the variation of cytokine responses in individual animals. Though all horses responded to the LPS stimulation, some horses had profound responses, whereas other horses had a weak response to stimulation. This finding is consistent with other reports21,22 as variable cytokine response is noted in the literature as one of the challenging factors with using sepsis models to investigate novel treatments. This individual variation may also be more clinically representative as all of our horses exhibited clinical signs of endotoxemia, which included fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, and neutropenia. Additionally, our cytokine concentrations obtained were similar to reported horses with clinical endotoxemia. Because the column used in this study works through a gradient removal mechanism, horses with a more profound cytokine response demonstrated greater removal compared to horses with a marginal cytokine response, which could have resulted in our data having a nonsignificant trend.23 Another factor could be the need for further optimization of a hemoperfusion prescription24 that is specific for an individual animal and is guided by examination and/or biomarker parameters, such as cytokines or markers of neutrophil activation. These ideas will require further research as currently there are no stall-side measurements for cytokine concentrations in horses, and neutrophil activation markers are not routinely measured in horses with sepsis.

One of the hallmarks of sepsis is severe leukopenia followed by late-phase leukocytosis. In humans, there is a reported increased risk of mortality in patients with sepsis that have an elevated and rising WBC count in the first 7 days.25 In our study, all horses had severe neutropenia coincident with the induction of endotoxemia. With column treatment, all horses returned to normal neutrophil and leukocyte counts within the 72-hour monitoring period. This is in contrast to sham treatment, where only 3 of 5 horses returned to normal neutrophil counts and 4 of 5 returned to normal leukocyte counts within the 72-hour study period. Cytokines/chemokines, leukotrienes, and hormones have been implicated in the modulation of leukocyte responses, with the most significant effect recognized in neutrophils in both humans and horses.26,27 Altered neutrophil migration leads to impaired neutrophil function and decreased innate immune response to infection. In human medicine, there is increasing evidence that the suppression of apoptosis of neutrophils in sepsis leads to accumulation of neutrophils in tissues and organ damage and failure.28,29 There is evidence that neutrophil apoptosis is delayed in horses with inflammatory conditions, such as colitis. Delayed apoptosis is noted as a contributing factor to the development of SIRS in horses with sepsis and eventually organ damage and failure such as seen in humans.30 There is additional evidence that horses with LPS-induced endotoxemia exhibit delayed apoptosis for up to 24 hours after LPS induction.31 Our study results show a significantly higher fold change of cells undergoing apoptosis with column compared to sham treatment at the postfiltration time point and 72-hour time point. Additionally, although not statistically significant, column treatment resulted in a 0.53-fold decrease in necrotic cells, whereas sham treatment had a 7.0-fold increase in necrotic cells. The presence of necrotic neutrophils has been linked as a key pathology in systemic organ failure in humans with sepsis as they lodge in the vessels of organs, leading to thrombosis.32

Reactive oxygen species are an essential part of bacterial clearance and host defense; excessive or dysregulated ROS production can cause oxidative damage to organelles and tissues, further exacerbating the inflammatory cascade.33 Previous studies13,14 have shown that LPS infusion causes increased ROS production in horses. Interestingly, dialysis and hemoperfusion alone have been shown to increase ROS production in people due to bioincompatibility of materials and leukocyte activation.9,34 However, our study found no significant difference in levels of systemic ROS between sham-treated and column-treated groups. This suggests that the column treatment did not independently induce a measurable increase in ROS in these animals. Leukocyte-rich plasma from the immediately postfiltration time point demonstrated an improved capacity to respond to PMA; this suggests that neutrophils from column-treated horses were able to better maintain their ability to mount an immune response, indicating that the treatment did not impair, and potentially even improved, their function. The lack of “hemoperfusion-induced” ROS in our study might be attributed to a balancing effect of removal by the adsorbent material, mitigating any ROS produced through interaction with the column itself while also improving neutrophil health by reducing cytokines. Alternatively, horses may not experience the same bioincompatibility issues as humans during these procedures.

Neutrophil extracellular traps are small webs of cfDNA containing histones, myeloperoxidase, and elastase. Neutrophil extracellular traps are implicated as players in the microcirculatory disturbance that occurs during sepsis. Increased cfDNA, which is an indirect biomarker of NETs, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality in human patients.35,36 Increased cfDNA has also been identified in horses with inflammatory conditions.18 Although NETs are helpful for pathogen clearance, excessive or dysregulated NET formation leads to tissue damage.37 To our knowledge, our study is the first to show that plasma cfDNA is increased in adult horses after LPS induction of endotoxemia. While our results show no significant difference in cfDNA between treatment groups, there was significant variability in cfDNA concentrations in the individual animals, which may have precluded our ability to detect a difference if one existed. At this point, little is known about systemic cfDNA and NETs in horses with clinical sepsis, and further research is needed to better understand the role that NETs play in the pathophysiology of equine sepsis.

Horses with sepsis can experience severe electrolyte derangements. We performed a full serum biochemical analysis to determine whether hemoperfusion would impact any parameters that are commonly altered in patients with clinical sepsis. While we did detect significant differences between column and sham treatment in triglycerides, calcium, and potassium, none of the mean values fell below reference ranges. Interestingly, in this population of horses, albumin and globulin remained stable, which is in contrast to previous reports11 of hemoperfusion in healthy horses. In this study, a mean ACT of 500 to 900 seconds was maintained throughout hemoperfusion without adverse side effects, such as thrombocytopenia, bleeding, and vein thrombi, which can be seen with overheparization.35 This is consistent with prior studies in healthy horses where ACTs of 500 to 900 were observed to be clinically safe.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was small. While we did perform a priori power calculations, the target of a 30% change in cytokines was based on human data, not equine. While we hoped to minimize the impacts of variability in individual horse cytokine production by using a crossover design, we still saw a high degree of inter- and intra-animal variation in cytokine concentrations, which likely impacted our ability to detect significant differences. For our cytokine analysis, we elected for a multiplex assay. While this allowed us to measure a large array of cytokines, a potential limitation of this technique is the possibility of crossreactivity or matrix effects, which can lead to inaccurate quantification of individual cytokine concentrations. Additionally, the filtration capacity of the polymer-based column in horses is currently unknown and could require further optimization. In this study, we used leukocyte-rich plasma, of which neutrophils are the most abundant cells. This means that we cannot definitively say that our ROS production was from neutrophils alone. We chose not to isolate neutrophils as the isolation methods themselves can stimulate cells, and we wanted to determine ROS levels in LPS-treated horses without iatrogenic effects.38 Finally, we selected a uniform filtration time across horses but not a uniform target for filtered blood volume. Because filtration rates had to be adjusted occasionally based on the degree of heparinization and pump pressure parameters, the blood volume filtered was not standardized across all animals. While this was certainly a limitation for comparing group means, it is not a limitation that would be relevant for the clinical application of hemoperfusion. Ideally, in clinical cases of sepsis, horses would receive individual prescriptions for hemoperfusion based on ongoing clinical signs and measurement of sepsis-associated parameters in real time, such as cytokine concentrations or markers of neutrophil activation. Indeed, a recent case study39 describes hemoperfusion of a horse with colitis-induced sepsis in which decreasing cytokine concentrations were associated with an improvement in clinical signs.

In conclusion, the findings of this study support further investigation of hemoperfusion as an adjunctive treatment for sepsis in the horse. The improvements in neutrophil parameters in column-treated versus sham-treated horses provide promising proof-of-concept data. The lack of a significant impact on cytokine concentrations could be due to study design but may also indicate that further optimization of hemoperfusion protocols is needed. As clinical sepsis differs from an induced endotoxemia model, further investigation of hemoperfusion in horses with clinical sepsis is needed.
