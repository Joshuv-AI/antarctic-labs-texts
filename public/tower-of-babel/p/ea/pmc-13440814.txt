Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome with concomitant recurrent subarachnoid hemorrhage and coronary vasospastic angina: A manifestation of systemic vasoconstriction syndrome
Authors: Jun Tsukano, Toshiharu Nomura, Yusuke Yamazaki, Minoru Takahashi, Kouichirou Okamoto, Hiroshi Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is characterized by thunderclap headache and reversible segmental cerebral arterial constriction. Although neurological complications are well recognized, concurrent extracerebral vasoconstriction remains under appreciated.Case Description: A 53-year-old woman presented with sudden chest tightness immediately followed by a thunderclap headache. Initial brain computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging showed no hemorrhage or infarction; however, magnetic resonance angiography demonstrated multifocal cerebral arterial narrowing, consistent with RCVS. During hospitalization, she developed recurrent nonaneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhages (SAHs) on days 2 and 4. No bleeding source was identified on serial angiography. Recurrent episodes of chest tightness prompted coronary angiography with acetylcholine provocation, which revealed severe coronary vasospasm, establishing the diagnosis of vasospastic angina (VSA). Treatment with benidipine and nicorandil was initiated thereafter, and no recurrence of either cardiac or neurological symptoms was observed, with complete resolution of cerebral vasoconstriction on follow-up imaging.Conclusion: This case highlights a rare but clinically important association between RCVS, recurrent nonaneurysmal SAH, and coronary VSA confirmed by pharmacologic provocation. Although each condition is potentially reversible, their coexistence supports the concept of systemic vasoconstriction syndrome and underscores the need for vigilance regarding extracranial vascular involvement in patients with RCVS. Early recognition and targeted vasodilator therapy may be essential to prevent serious neurological and cardiovascular complications.

INTRODUCTION

Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is characterized by the abrupt onset of severe headache, typically thunderclap in nature, accompanied by reversible segmental vasoconstriction of the cerebral arteries. The condition may be complicated by cerebrovascular events, including subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracerebral hemorrhage, and cerebral infarction.[1,3,4,11] In recent years, increasing evidence suggests that RCVS is not limited to the intracranial vasculature. Vasospasm, stenosis, or arterial dissection involving extracranial vessels – including the coronary, carotid, renal, and abdominal arteries – has been reported.[7,9,12-16,21] Here, we report a case of RCVS presenting with chest tightness and thunderclap headache, complicated by recurrent nonaneurysmal SAH and coronary vasospastic angina (VSA). This case supports the concept that RCVS and VSA may represent different clinical manifestations of a systemic vasoconstriction syndrome (SVS). Given that both conditions may lead to fatal outcomes, early recognition and pathophysiology-oriented management are crucial.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 53-year-old woman presented with sudden-onset chest discomfort immediately followed by a severe occipital headache described as a “blow to the head” (numerical rating scale [NRS], 10/10). She had no remarkable family history. Her medical history included childhood asthma, right mastectomy for breast cancer without chemotherapy, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-negative, nonrheumatic scleritis, intermittently treated with prednisolone eye drops for 7 years, with the last use 6 months before presentation. Oral prednisolone had been initiated at 20 mg/day 1.5 years earlier and tapered to 7.5 mg/day over the preceding year. She had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption.

At approximately 11:00 a.m. on day 1, while working at her desk without an identifiable trigger, she developed sudden chest tightness immediately followed by a thunderclap headache. She was transported to our emergency department and admitted approximately 4 h after symptom onset. On admission, her height was 161 cm and her weight was 60 kg. Vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 174/97 mmHg; respiratory rate, 24 breaths/min; and peripheral oxygen saturation, 95% on room air. She was fully alert and oriented, without focal neurological deficits. The headache had partially improved to NRS 4/10, and nausea was absent.

Laboratory studies revealed dyslipidemia (total cholesterol, 190 mg/dL; triglycerides, 218 mg/dL). Creatine kinase (CK, 62 U/L) and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide - 35 pg/mL, levels were within normal limits. Electrocardiography (ECG) and chest radiography showed no abnormalities. Head computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage or acute ischemia. However, magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) revealed poor visualization of the distal right middle cerebral artery (MCA) and segmental narrowing of the right posterior cerebral artery (PCA), without aneurysm or arterial dissection [Figure 1].

Imaging on presentation. (a-c) Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images: No abnormal signal. (d) magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), anterior circulation: Poor visualization of the distal right middle cerebral artery (dotted circle). (e) MRA, posterior circulation: Segmental narrowing of the right posterior cerebral artery (arrowheads).

Based on the presentation of thunderclap headache with early improvement and the absence of SAH on initial imaging, RCVS was suspected, and oral verapamil 120 mg/day was initiated. The headache improved further to NRS 1/10. On hospital day 2, however, the patient developed recurrent chest tightness and severe headache (NRS 8/10). MRI revealed SAH in the anterior interhemispheric fissure, left Sylvian fissure, and left frontal convexity [Figure 2a-c]. MRA showed improved visualization of the right MCA [Figure 2d and e], but CT angiography failed to identify a bleeding source [Figure 2f and g]. RCVS-associated SAH was diagnosed. Verapamil was discontinued due to concerns for hemorrhagic risk, and conservative management with blood pressure control and rest was continued.

(d and e) Magnetic resonance angiography: Improved distal right middle cerebral artery visualization (dotted circle), persistent narrowing of the right posterior cerebral artery. (f and g) Computed tomography angiography: No aneurysm or bleeding source identified.

The headache gradually improved without neurological deterioration. On day 4, follow-up MRI showed decreased SAH, and MRA demonstrated improved visualization of the right PCA [Figure 3a-e]. Later that day, however, the patient developed another sudden severe headache (NRS 9/10), requiring intravenous pentazocine 15 mg. CT revealed a new SAH in the left parietal convexity [Figure 3f]. Headaches recurred predominantly in the evenings over the subsequent days.

Imaging on day 4. (a-c) Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images: reduction in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). (d) Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA): Improved right middle cerebral artery visualization. (e) MRA: Reduced narrowing of the right posterior cerebral artery (arrowheads). (f) Computed tomography: New cortical SAH in the left parietal region (dotted circle).

By day 8, MRI showed further resolution of SAH and improvement in MRA visualization [Figure 4a and b]. MRI on day 25 showed no new lesions, with good intracranial artery visualization on MRA [Figure 4c and d]. Nocturnal headaches improved markedly from day 13 onward, with gradual resolution thereafter. Prednisolone was tapered to 5 mg/day on day 15 and to 3 mg/day by day 23. Episodes of chest tightness recurred intermittently, including on day 23 and day 30, but resolved spontaneously. Laboratory tests, ECG, echocardiography, and Holter monitoring showed no ischemic cardiac changes.

Serial magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). (a and b) MRA on day 8: (a) normalization of middle cerebral artery (MCA) visualization; (b) further improvement of posterior cerebral artery (PCA) narrowing. (c and d) MRA on day 25: normalization of MCA and PCA visualization.

Given the recurrent chest symptoms, coronary angiography was intentionally performed on day 37, after complete resolution of cerebral vasoconstriction, to minimize the risk of recurrent cerebral vasoconstriction, in accordance with the JCS/CVIT/JCC 2023 guideline to confirm the diagnosis of VSA.[8] Baseline angiography revealed no fixed stenosis; however, acetylcholine (ACh) (50 μg) provocation induced diffuse, severe spasm of the left coronary artery with more than 90% luminal narrowing at the most constricted segment [Figure 5]. The spasm resolved promptly after intracoronary nitroglycerin (1 mg) administration. A diagnosis of VSA was made, and treatment with benidipine (8 mg/day) and nicorandil (15 mg/day) was initiated. Thereafter, neither chest symptoms nor headaches recurred. Her headaches had completely disappeared by day 56. Follow-up MRI on day 70 showed no recurrence of SAH or new lesions, and MRA demonstrated complete resolution of cerebral artery narrowing. DISCUSSION

The present case exemplifies a manifestation of RCVS in which the patient experienced an episode of sudden chest tightness, immediately followed by a thunderclap headache in the absence of an apparent trigger. Initial neuroimaging did not reveal any cerebrovascular lesions; however, subsequent imaging demonstrated SAHs coinciding with recurrent headaches. Although initial ECG, ultrasonic cardiogram, and laboratory evaluations were unremarkable and failed to indicate myocardial ischemia, coronary angiography with ACh provocation ultimately confirmed coronary vasospasm, leading to a diagnosis of VSA.

An increasing number of reports have indicated that RCVS may not be confined to intracranial vasculature but can involve extracranial vessels, such as the coronary, carotid, renal, and mesenteric arteries, through mechanisms such as vasospasm, stenosis, or arterial dissection.[7,9,12-16,21] This has led to the conceptualization of reversible SVS,[12] encompassing both RCVS and extracranial vascular involvement as part of a broader, potentially reversible systemic vasculopathic process.

RCVS is a clinicoradiological syndrome characterized by the acute onset of a thunderclap headache and reversible, segmental narrowing of cerebral arteries.[1,3,4,11] Historically described under various terms [Supplementary Table 1], the unified term “RCVS” was proposed by Calabrese et al. in 2007.[1,3,4,20] Diagnostic criteria typically include (1) abrupt-onset severe headache; (2) absence of aneurysmal SAH; (3) segmental cerebral vasoconstriction; (4) normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF); and (5) resolution of vasoconstriction within 12 weeks.[3] According to the 2019 RCVS2 scoring system [Supplementary Table 2], a score ≥5 yields a sensitivity exceeding 90% and a specificity of approximately 99% for the diagnosis of RCVS.[19] In the present case, the score was 6 at admission and increased to 7 following the development of SAH, supporting a confident diagnosis despite the absence of CSF analysis.

Epidemiological data suggest that RCVS is more prevalent in females, with a peak onset between the ages of 40 and 55, although onset in men more commonly occurs in their 30s or 40s.[1] Hemorrhagic complications such as cortical SAH or intraparenchymal hemorrhages often present within the 1st week, while delayed ischemic events, including infarction, typically manifest after the 2nd week.[5,23] Notably, initial neuroimaging may be unremarkable, with hemorrhagic changes only detectable on follow-up imaging. Approximately half of RCVS patients may also develop other cerebrovascular complications, such as ischemic stroke or subdural hematoma.[4] While approximately 37% of RCVS cases are idiopathic, the remaining 63% are associated with identifiable precipitating factors, including corticosteroids [Supplementary Table 3].[4,11,14,16,20,21] In the current case, although the patient had previously received systemic corticosteroids and steroid-containing eye drops, the dose had been tapered before onset, rendering corticosteroid use an unlikely precipitant.

From a pathophysiological standpoint, dysregulation of vascular tone is central to RCVS. Vasoconstriction may initially affect small pial vessels, subsequently progressing centripetally to involve medium and large cerebral arteries.[1,4] This dynamic process can result in heterogeneous vascular changes, with some vessels improving while others deteriorate or newly constrict.[1,4]

Hemorrhagic complications are hypothesized to arise from blood–brain barrier disruption and microvascular fragility, rather than large-vessel vasoconstriction.[1,4] Although recurrent SAH during the course of RCVS is rare, such occurrences have been documented.[17] RCVS itself recurs in 5–10% of cases.[1,6] Therapeutic management is primarily supportive and includes cessation of triggering agents, avoidance of exertion or Valsalva maneuvers, analgesics for headache, anxiolytics or laxatives as needed, and anticonvulsants if seizures arise.[1,4,6] While nimodipine may attenuate thunderclap headache, its efficacy in preventing stroke remains unproven.[6] Verapamil and nicardipine have been employed with variable success. In our case, verapamil was associated with improved MRA findings, although it did not prevent hemorrhagic recurrence.[4-6] Importantly, calcium channel antagonists may increase bleeding risk in patients with fragile vasculature.[10] Corticosteroids are generally not recommended due to the potential for worsening outcomes;[1,4,6,22] however, in the present case, prednisolone was gradually tapered from 7.5 mg/day to 3 mg/day. Chronic headache may persist in over 50% of patients, with depression occurring in up to 26%. Triptans and indomethacin are contraindicated due to their vasoconstrictive properties.[1,6]

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome is a known complication (7–38%) of RCVS, leading to increased morbidity.[4,6,11,20]

Notably, in our patient, chest tightness preceded the thunderclap headache, occurring during sedentary office work. Cardiac evaluations were unremarkable; however, with the recurrence of chest symptoms, coronary angiography with ACh provocation was intentionally performed on day 37, after complete resolution of cerebral vasoconstriction, to minimize the risk of recurrent cerebral vasoconstriction, and this confirmed coronary vasospasm. The ACh coronary spasm provocation test is an established diagnostic method for VSA, with high sensitivity and specificity ranging from 80% to 90%, particularly in patients with recurrent chest symptoms but no objective evidence of myocardial ischemia on ECG or cardiac biomarkers. Accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment are essential for improving the prognosis of patients with VSA.[8] Because the halflife of ACh is extremely short, the provoked coronary spasm typically resolves spontaneously and often does not require nitrate administration. However, the induced spasm may be severe, extensive, or prolonged, potentially leading to critical conditions such as hypotension, cardiogenic shock, or severe arrhythmias, which must be managed promptly, as in our case. Therefore, ACh provocation testing should be performed only after obtaining adequate informed consent and under well-established safety measures in accordance with the current guideline.[8]

VSA typically presents with chest or epigastric discomfort at rest, more commonly in individuals aged 40–70, particularly those with a smoking history. Women may be more affected in certain populations.[8] VSA shares several triggers with RCVS, including serotonergic agents, alcohol, cold exposure, Valsalva maneuvers, and emotional stress.[18]

To date, six cases of concomitant RCVS and coronary vasospasm have been reported, all but one involving women aged 25–57 years (mean age, 43 years).[9,12,13,16] Among these, three presented with both headache and anginal symptoms, while the remainder had headache alone or were in the postpartum period.[9,16] In the two cases where the sequence of symptom onset was described, thunderclap headache occurred after the onset of anginal symptoms. In one case, similar to ours, occipital headache developed within 1 min after the sudden onset of epigastric pain.[12] In the other case, the patient was hospitalized for chest pain and developed thunderclap headache just before discharge, although the interval between chest pain and headache was not specified.[13] Our case represents the first documented instance wherein coronary vasospasm was definitively confirmed through provocation testing in a patient with RCVS. It is noteworthy that half of the previously reported cases were asymptomatic for chest pain, implying that coronary vasospasm may be under-recognized in RCVS.[9,16]

The syndrome of cardiac cephalalgia, wherein exertional headache results from myocardial ischemia, shares overlapping features with RCVS and VSA.[24] Although most cases occur in individuals over 50 with demonstrable coronary stenosis, a minority of cases, particularly in women, have been attributed to coronary vasospasm.[24] These may, in retrospect, represent RCVS-related events. Given the shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction and sympathetic overactivity, both conditions often respond to calcium channel blockers.[12,24] In our patient, no recurrence of chest symptoms or headaches was observed after initiation of benidipine and nicorandil therapy.

Prognostically, most RCVS patients recover fully (≥90% with modified Rankin scale 0–1). Yet complications – especially intracranial hemorrhage or infarction – may leave neurological deficits. In severe or fulminant cases, progression to fatal outcomes is possible.[2,6] In general, the prognosis of VSA is more favorable than that of atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, with 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates of 95%, 90%, and 87%, respectively. However, despite appropriate treatment, recurrence occurs in approximately half of the cases, and it may lead to myocardial infarction, fatal arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death.[18]

In addition, extracranial vascular involvement in RCVS has been reported in the form of internal carotid artery or vertebral artery dissection,[7,14,15] external carotid artery[21] or renal artery stenosis,[7,15] and mesenteric arterial spasm.[12] These findings support the hypothesis that RCVS may, in some cases, represent a systemic vasculopathy – SVS – manifesting in diverse vascular territories either synchronously or asynchronously [Table 1]. It is possible that RCVS and VSA represent different clinical expressions within a shared pathophysiological spectrum.

CONCLUSION

This report describes a rare case of RCVS complicated by nonaneurysmal recurrent SAH and VSA, with the latter confirmed through pharmacologic provocation. While both conditions are typically self-limiting and reversible, they may result in serious complications, including ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, myocardial infarction, or life-threatening arrhythmias and death. The co-occurrence of these syndromes supports the emerging concept of SVS and underscores the importance of comprehensive vascular evaluation in patients with RCVS. Further research is warranted to elucidate shared mechanisms and optimal management strategies for patients with concurrent cerebral and extracranial vasospasm.
