Real-Time Prediction of Oil-Type Gas Emissions in Coal-Oil-Gas Coexistence Mines
Authors: Mingyao Wei, Kang Gao, Haoqi Huangfu, Chunqin Lu, Liyuan Yv
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Oil-type gas disasters are a frequently occurring concern in coal-oil-gas coexistence mines. In order to actively predict the volume of oil-type gas emissions from floor rocks, this study presents an investigative methodology to forecast the geological conditions of floor rocks in front of the roadway face by utilizing the Direct Current (DC) method. The assessment of electrical resistance in rock formations, which is widely employed for identifying geological characteristics, serves as the basis for proposing a geological anomaly index derived from rock resistivity. This index effectively characterizes the stability of rock strata, providing an indirect evaluation of fracture development. As a real-time geological detection index for floor rocks that are 100 m ahead of the roadway face, it enhances predictive capabilities. Moreover, when combined with parameters such as floor rock thickness and permeability, the paper presents simulations of oil-type gas emissions under varying geological conditions. Subsequently, an adaptive optimization of the Back Propagation (BP) neural network is achieved through the Genetic Algorithm Back Propagation Neural Network (GA-BP) model to evaluate the quantity of oil-type gas emissions in roadways. This advanced real-time prediction method is applied in Huangling coal mining to predict the oil-type gas emissions from the floor rocks in the excavation roadway area. The results show consistency with field monitoring outcomes, confirming the accuracy of the predictive model. In conclusion, this advanced real-time prediction technique enables continuous monitoring and real-time forecasting of oil-type gas emissions in front of roadways. This capability facilitates the implementation of specific measures for pre-extraction in gas disaster prevention and control, thereby ensuring the safety of coal mine production. Moreover, the versatility of this advanced real-time prediction method extends to early warnings of rock mass instability-related disasters. Through a comprehensive understanding of subsurface conditions, continuous monitoring of changes, and the application of predictive models, timely actions can be taken to reduce risks and uphold safety standards.

Introduction

During the process of roadway excavation, the reduction of pressure on the floor induces abnormal oil-type gas emissions from the underlying strata, leading to a potential safety hazard with the gas concentration at the working face surpassing safety limits. Thus, early prediction of such oil-type gas emission disasters from the floor is imperative for ensuring coal mine safety. Presently, advanced detection methods in mine roadway excavation encompass drilling and geophysical techniques. Drilling methods, despite their utility, suffer from limitations such as a restricted detection range, prolonged time consumption, and elevated trial-and-error costs, rendering them insufficient for comprehensive oil-type gas layer detection.1,2 In this context, geophysical detection technology emerges as an effective means, playing a pivotal role in safeguarding roadway excavation safety. Various technical methods are employed, including seismic reflection wave methods grounded in rock elastic mechanics, direct current electrical methods based on geological body electrical disparities, transient electromagnetic advanced detection technology, as well as other methodologies like infrared detection, microgravity methods, and electromagnetic radiation methods.3 Among these, seismic wave methods are commonly utilized for detecting geological structures, while electrical prospecting, sensitive to water-bearing geological bodies, stands out as a primary technique for advanced prediction and forecasting in roadway excavation.4−6 The acquisition of geophysical parameters such as the volumetric density of the formation is achieved through the borehole logging tool based on pulsed neutron technology.7 Andrade et al. determined the thickness and density of geological bodies by using the gamma logging method to identify the area of the exploited ore deposit.8 To comprehensively investigate the direct current electrical exploration process, a simulation model of rock layer anomalies using ANSYS was constructed based on the forward method. Results indicated that direct current electrical methods precisely identify the position of geological anomalies.9 On-site studies further validated that the spatial distribution of electric potential effectively reflects the characteristics of geological anomalies.10 However, in strata regions containing oil-type gas, existing physical advanced detection technologies still face challenges in accurately identifying deposits. Achieving advanced prediction of oil-type gas emission disasters remains a formidable task.

In predicting coalbed gas emission disasters, the quantity of gas emissions in the mining and excavation working face is forecasted based on adjacent layer emissions.11 Based on key influencing factors such as the gas content in the coal seam and the output of the coal mining face, a gray gas model is constructed to predict the gas in the working face.12 Geological parameters are widely acknowledged as influencing factors for predicting gas emission quantities in mining working faces. However, current prediction indicators rely on static assessments of emissions disasters using periodically acquired geological indicators. In actual mining operations, the geological parameters of rock layers undergo dynamic changes influenced by mining activities. Real-time detection of geological anomalies, therefore, assumes a crucial role in comprehending subsurface conditions and significantly impacts oil-type gas emissions from the coal seam floor.

In this study, geological anomalies are defined as the variations of the resistivity of the floor rock, and the direct current electrical method is utilized for advanced detection in front of the roadway face. An electrical variation coefficient, which is derived from the resistivity of the floor rock layer, is used as a real-time geological structure indicator for anomaly assessment. Moreover, the emission anomaly evaluation incorporates factors such as the thickness of floor rock strata, permeability, and geological structure parameters. A prediction model of genetic algorithm-optimized backpropagation neural network algorithm (GA-BP) is adopted to predict the quantity of oil-type gas emission. Finally, the integration of advanced electrical resistivity exploration techniques with machine learning enables quantitative prediction of oil-type gas emission in the Huangling mining area. This comprehensive approach aims to improve the accuracy and effectiveness of predicting oil-type gas emissions during coal mine roadway excavation, thereby contributing to enhanced safety measures and disaster prevention in the mining industry.

Advanced Detection of Geological Anomalies

Geological factors play a crucial role in influencing the emission dynamics of oil-type gas. Anomalies in the geological structure, including faults, fractures, or permeable rock layers, act as channels that facilitate the upward migration of gas from the oil-type gas reservoir to the roadway. These pathways establish essential release mechanisms for previously trapped gas. Furthermore, mining operations, notably advanced underground techniques such as longwall mining, possess the potential to perturb the floor rocks under geostress, thereby inducing gas emissions, as illustrated in Figure 1. The extraction process itself becomes a pivotal factor, releasing previously trapped oil-type gas and predisposing the mining area to gas seepage, or, in extreme cases, accidental explosions if requisite safety measures are not diligently implemented.

Advanced Detection Basic Principles

As a primary method of advanced detection, the direct current electrical method has garnered increasing attention, with comprehensive studies addressing various interferences encountered during its application.13 The exploration of these interferences has evolved to a more profound level, characterized by more accurate data collection and inversion calculations based on that data. Consequently, the acquisition of the location of geological anomalies has become notably more reliable. This paper delves into the application of the direct current electrical method for detecting geological structures, beginning with an introduction to the basic principles and methods for processing resistivity data in advanced detection.

Assuming a homogeneous medium, the basic principle of advanced detection involves establishing close electrode contact with the roadway floor rock and configuring measurement lines to connect all electrodes. The power-supplying electrode emits an electric current, and the variations in potential difference formed by the electric field generated by a single electrode are utilized to calculate resistivity. This resistivity acts as a comprehensive indication of the electrical parameters of geological anomalies within a spherical surface.14 In the roadway, a three-pole observation system is utilized for electrode arrangement and data collection, as depicted in Figure 2(a). The 48 current electrodes, inserted into the ground at 1.5 m spacing, contribute to the stability of the spherical electric field generated by the power-supplying electrode. The current electrodes inject a direct current into the ground, and the potential electrodes measure the voltage difference induced by the current flow. The resistivity of subsurface materials dictates the distribution of electrical potential in the ground. Through the analysis of voltage measurements, the resistivity of the subsurface can be accurately estimated.

In this observation system, the power-supplying electrode acts as a point source, creating a stable spherical electric field. The different combinations of power-supplying and measurement electrodes generate multiple detection spheres with varying radii, as illustrated in Figure 2(b). Prediction of Oil-type Gas Emission Quantity

In recent years, there has been a significant surge of interest in utilizing deep learning techniques to address complex challenges posed by collective effects. Numerous successful applications in geological disaster prediction, such as landslides,2,19 debris flow,20 and water inrush,21−24 have demonstrated the efficacy of employing deep learning in scenarios involving similar mechanisms. To enhance the precision of evaluating oil-type gas emission disasters, a training model is proposed that combines Genetic Algorithm (GA) with Backpropagation (BP) neural network techniques.25 Numerical simulation methods are employed to establish a model for oil-type gas migration, creating data samples for quantitative prediction by defining the relationship between factors influencing oil-type gas emission and the corresponding emission quantity.

The model, rooted in the principles of the GA-BP machine learning algorithm, is trained through learning from data samples to predict the quantity of oil-type gas emissions. The predicted quantity of oil-type gas emission serves as an indicator for evaluating oil-type gas emission disasters in terms of emission anomalies.

The collected resistivity data are integrated into the GA-BP model as input parameters, enabling the model to learn and establish relationships between resistivity patterns and the emission of oil-type gas from the coal seam floor. The GA optimization component aids in fine-tuning the model’s parameters, enhancing prediction accuracy and generalization ability.

Introduction to GA-BP Network

The GA-BP network, also known as Genetic Algorithm-Improved Backpropagation Neural Network, comprises two primary components. GA refers to the genetic algorithm, an optimization technique encoding each solution of the problem as an individual. The solutions collectively form a population, with each individual assigned a fitness value based on evaluation. Through operations such as selection, crossover, and mutation, individuals with higher fitness values replace those with lower values, leading to the evolution of the population toward an optimal solution. Consequently, genetic algorithms can be viewed as the continual evolution of a population composed of feasible solutions. BP, or Backpropagation, is the central framework in the learning process of a neural network, consisting of input, hidden, and output layers, as depicted in Figure 4. By inputting learning samples into the input layer nodes, the network calculates output values, compares them with actual values, propagates errors backward to adjust parameters, and iterates through this process until the error reaches a minimum, resulting in a reliable model. However, determining the initial weights and thresholds of the BP neural network poses challenges, prompting the incorporation of the genetic algorithm to enhance computational efficiency and precision.

Schematic diagram of BP neural network.

The global optimization capability of genetic algorithms is utilized to derive optimal initial weights and thresholds for the BP network. The optimized initial weights and thresholds, obtained through the optimization algorithm, serve as the foundational values for the BP neural network. Subsequent training is conducted to circumvent potential entrapment in local minima. The refined BP neural network, enhanced with weights optimized by the genetic algorithm, is not randomly generated but rather acquired through the optimization module of the genetic algorithm.26,27 In this situation, the initial weights and thresholds within the BP algorithm serve as the genetic values of the genetic algorithm individual. The individual’s length corresponds to the number of weights and thresholds in the BP neural network, with each gene representing a weight or threshold. The numerical value on the gene signifies the actual value of the connection weight or threshold in the BP neural network, collectively forming a chromosome in the genetic algorithm. A specific number of chromosomes collectively constitute the initial population for genetic algorithm training. Through iterations involving selection, crossover, and mutation operations, an optimal individual is discerned. This optimal individual is subsequently utilized as the initial set of parameters for training the BP network, as illustrated in Figure 5.25

Simulation Learning

The migration of oil-type gas is affected by various factors, including geological structures, rock properties, and fluid properties, as shown in Figure 6. Oil-type gas migrates from the reservoir to the roadway through permeable pathways, including fractures, faults, or permeable layers within the subsurface. Structural features such as faults, folds, and anticlines can serve as migration pathways for gas. Faults act as conduits for gas migration, while folds and anticlines can trap and accumulate gas, forming natural gas reservoirs. The permeability and porosity of rocks have a significantly impact on the ability of gas to migrate, where highly porous and permeable rocks facilitating faster gas migration, while rocks with low permeability may prevent or slow down the migration.

Quantitative Prediction of Oil-Type Gas Emission

In the initial stage, a qualitative assessment of oil-type gas occurrence in the roadway was carried out by means of statistical methods, analyzing geological data and the status of oil-type gas enrichment near the already mined working faces. The distribution of oil-type gas, based on its occurrence in the floor of the mined working faces, was classified into three levels: no occurrence (0), low occurrence (1), and high occurrence (2). Predrainage is employed prior to mining activities to minimize oil-type gas emission, and the quantity of oil-type gas from drainage boreholes is closely associated with its occurrence. For comparison, the predicted emission rate based on electrical resistivity exploration is presented in Figure 11, with the predrainage data representing the averaged quantity of boreholes.

Prediction and distribution trend of gas emission risk in the roadway.

The predictions for oil-type gas emission, derived from electrical resistivity exploration, exhibit significant variations in different sections along the roadway. The 280 m region displays anomalies in the electrical method due to fault structures. The 1400 m region is identified as a lower-risk area, characterized by a smaller emission quantity of oil-type gas. Conversely, near the 3000 m region, the predicted emission rate is higher, indicating a poorer evaluation of the floor and a heightened emission quantity of oil-type gas.

The statistical analysis results based on geological data provide preliminary qualitative understandings of the occurrence of oil-type gas without providing precise quantitative results. These results are unable to precisely predict the occurrence of oil-type gas. However, the results obtained through the direct current electrical method in this study are consistent with the qualitative findings from statistical methods. This method enables a more refined and accurate understanding of the local anomaly patterns of disasters. For example, at the fault location in the 280 m section of the roadway, the oil-type gas emission disaster indicator is relatively high. In contrast, another measurement point at the 1500 m location indicates a lower risk. Beyond 2000 m, the disaster indicators at the measurement points are generally high, indicating that these areas have an unstable roadway floor, geological structures, and a heightened risk of oil-type gas emission.

Given the notable variations in the results of the roadway detection, a distinct regional pattern in the distribution of oil-type gas disaster is evident. Consequently, an optimized drilling plan is proposed based on this distribution pattern in the roadway floor. The plan involves reducing the number of drill holes in areas with lower oil and gas disaster risk and increasing the number of drill holes in areas beyond 2000 m to enhance safety.

Since the oil-type gas cannot be directly monitored by technical means, the calculation examples in this paper can only be compared with limited data. The concentration of the oil-type gas extracted from the floor strata carried out on the spot is directly related to the amount of oil-type gas emitted. The evaluation index proposed in this paper is also consistent with the law of the field measured data. Therefore, it shows that this method can effectively evaluate the risk of oil-type gas emission and is reasonable.

Results and Discussion

Based on advanced geological electrical differences detected through the electrical method, it is proposed the electrical method variation coefficient as a real-time geological anomaly indicator, particularly aimed at interpreting geological structures associated with oil-type gas outflow disasters. Different from the methods based on static geological parameter, the integration of data from advanced electrical resistivity exploration and the GA-BP model offers a more comprehensive understanding of gas emission patterns. This approach facilitates the real-time tracking of geological anomalies during the excavation process, enabling the identification and mapping of these anomalies using resistivity data. As a result, the impact on gas migration and potential emission areas can be assessed.

Through on-site detection and analysis, accurate predictions for the entire roadway area are achieved. The results demonstrate that the calculated predictions of oil-type gas emission at different locations in the roadway floor consistent with the qualitative evaluation results obtained from the geological data statistical method. However, this method allows for a more refined and accurate understanding of local anomaly patterns in disasters. Utilizing the predictions of oil-type gas emission, it is proposed the development of more precise management strategies for different locations in the roadway, taking into account the varying gas emission disaster scenarios and aiming to optimize the cost of oil-type gas management.

By integrating advanced electrical resistivity exploration techniques with geological parameters-based GA-BP models, a more comprehensive understanding of gas-related disasters can be achieved. The proposed prediction technology for oil-type gas emission serves as a reliable foundation for preventing disasters caused by oil-type gas emissions. Additionally, this quantitative evaluation technology is transferable to disasters resulting from rock instability, such as adjacent gas influx, water inrush from the roof or floor, among others. Therefore, this technology has broad reference significance for ensuring safety in coal mine production.
