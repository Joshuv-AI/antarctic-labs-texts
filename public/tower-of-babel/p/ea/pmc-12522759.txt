Cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the Arabic version of the SARC-F for evaluating sarcopenia in Saudi older adults
Authors: Bader A Alqahtani, Aqeel M Alenazi, Mohammed M Alshehri, Ahmed S Alhowimel, Bashayer A Alharbi, Norah A Alhwoaimel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to translate and cross-culturally adapt the SARC-F to Arabic and to assess the reliability and validity of the scale for use among community-dwelling older adults in Saudi Arabia. Methods: Older adults aged 60 years and above were included. The translation and cross-cultural adaptation process followed standardized guidelines. Test-retest reliability and internal consistency were evaluated over two visits one week apart. Moreover, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of the SARC-F were calculated. The validity of the Arabic version of the SARC-F was assessed against other measurements related to sarcopenia. Results: A total of 182 community-dwelling older adults were enrolled (mean age = 65 ± 5.2 years). The internal consistency with the overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.71. For the test-retest reliability with a one-week interval, the ICC was 0.94. The Arabic SARC-F scale was significantly associated with other measurements related to sarcopenia. The sensitivity of the Arabic SARC-F ranged from 28 to 50%, and the specificity ranged from 81.4 to 82.9%. Conclusion: The SARC-F was successfully translated and adapted into the Arabic language. The Arabic SARC-F showed excellent reliability and adequate validity but revealed low sensitivity with high specificity across different sarcopenia definitions, which indicates that it can detect the absence of sarcopenia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06349-y.Keywords: Saudi, Sarcopenia, SARC-F, Older adults

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive skeletal muscle disorder characterized by a generalized decline in skeletal muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical function [1]. The term ‘sarcopenia’ was first introduced by Rosenberg in 1989 to describe the loss of muscle mass in older adults [2]. In the past three decades, the research evolved from solely evaluating muscle mass to incorporating muscle strength and physical function into a more comprehensive definition of sarcopenia. In 2010, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) defined sarcopenia as a disorder characterized by progressive and generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength with a risk of adverse outcomes such as physical disability, poor quality of life, and death [3]. In 2016, sarcopenia was classified as a disease and assigned the ICD-10- code [4].

Sarcopenia has been associated with several adverse health-related outcomes such as disability, comorbidities, falls, fractures, hospitalizations, and mortality rate, imposing significant healthcare burdens [1, 5–8]. A systematic review and meta-analysis summarizing the consequences of sarcopenia found that sarcopenia is associated with a high risk of cognitive impairment, osteoporosis, falls, fractures, functional decline, and depression [9]. At the economic level, sarcopenia has a considerable impact on the healthcare system. Individuals of sarcopenia have 5-fold higher health care costs compared to hospitalized older adults without sarcopenia [10]. Furthermore, reducing the prevalence of sarcopenia by 10% could result in annual savings of over $1.1 billion [11].

The prevalence of sarcopenia among older adults ranges from 10 to 16%, depending on the criteria of diagnosis and the studied population [9]. However, no accurate estimate has been reported on the prevalence of sarcopenia among older adults in Saudi Arabia. With demographic shifts leading to an aging population, the prevalence of sarcopenia is expected to be high, which presents a significant health challenge among older adults globally and necessitates accurate and timely assessment for effective intervention.

Assessing older adults for sarcopenia is an appropriate step to identify individuals at risk. The SARC-F is a quick, inexpensive, and self-reported screening tool to identify older adults at risk of sarcopenia. It comprises five items reflecting the primary characteristics of sarcopenia: strength, assistance in walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls [12]. SARC-F has moderate to high specificity in accurately identifying sarcopenia and has been recommended by the international sarcopenia guidelines for the screening of sarcopenia [13, 14].

SARC-F was originally developed in English and then translated and validated in several languages, including Chinese [15], Spanish [16], Japanese [17], Turkish [18], French [19], Korean [20], German [21], Greek [22]and Vietnamese [23]. Translation and validation into different languages enhance the accessibility and application of SARC-F. Furthermore, the proxy-reported versions (SARCF by proxy) have been validated among community-dwelling older adults and nursing home residents and showed that SARC-F by proxy showed a similar result compared to the standard SARC-F, which suggests its feasibility to be used among older patients with communication problems [24]. The European Union Geriatric Medicine Society’s Special Interest Group (SIG) has also initiated efforts to guide and help researchers to translate and validate SARC-F in different European languages [25]. However, there is currently no Arabic translation for the SARC-F, which may limit its use in the Arabic-speaking population. Therefore, this study aimed to translate and culturally adapt the SARC-F into the Arabic language and context for its application in Saudi Arabia. We hypothesized that the Arabic version of the SARC-F would show comparable results to the original version, with good reliability and validity.

Methods

Population and study design

A cross-sectional study was carried out in the Riyadh region of Saudi Arabia among community-dwelling individuals aged 60 and older. Participants in this study were recruited between February and May 2024. They had to be at least 60 years old, fluent in Arabic, and able to walk independently. In Saudi Arabia, those 60 years and older are considered older adults, often known as retirement age, which has also been used by the United Nations to describe older adults. Individuals were excluded if they scored less than 26 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [26]had any acute conditions that would hinder their performance, or had a history of disabling disorders such as stroke, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, or other neurological conditions. Before participation in this study, all individuals submitted written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. An International Review Board (IRB) approval (NO.RHPT/024/003) was granted by the ethical committee at Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University.

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation of SARC-F

The process of translation and cross-cultural adaptation followed the Beaton guidelines [27]. Permission was granted from the original developer [12]. The process involved several phases, including (1) translation from English to Arabic, (2) synthesizing the translation, then (3) backward from Arabic to English translation, consensus, (4) establishing through an expert committee, and (5) evaluating the pre-final version. Initially, the translation of the SARC-F from English into Arabic was conducted by two bilingual professionals who were native Arabic speakers. One translator had a basic understanding of the concepts investigated in the SARC-F, while the other translator, who was not a healthcare professional, provided an alternative perspective. This process resulted in the creation of two Arabic versions. In the second phase, an expert committee evaluated these versions and produced a consolidated translated version. Afterward, two additional professional translators, both bilingual who had no prior knowledge of the original scale, performed backward translations into the initial language, producing two separate English versions. An expert committee, including a certified language interpreter, a specialist in geriatrics, as well as a certified geriatric physical therapist, reviewed the backward English version translation to ensure linguistic, logical, and experiential equivalence. Finally, a preapproved version of the SARC-F was assessed for questionnaire clarity and comprehension on a small sample of older adults (n = 20). Outcomes measures

Demographics

All demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, weight, height, and marital status were obtained. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by height in m [2]. A history of chronic disease was obtained through self-reports during the interviews. Chronic diseases included. In addition, we recorded current medications, including pain medications, antidiabetics, antihypertensives, antilipemic agents, anticoagulants, gastroprotective agents, endocrine/thyroid medications, vitamins, and supplements. For the data analysis, the sum of all self-reported chronic conditions and the total number of medications used currently by participants were considered in the analysis.

The SARC-F questionnaire

The SARC-F questionnaire is a cost-effective and easy method to assess the risk of sarcopenia. Strength, Assistance in Walking, rising from a Chair, Climbing Stairs, and Falls are the five components, each with a point value ranging from 0 to 2. Total scores range from 0 to 10, with higher values indicating an increased risk of sarcopenia. A score of 4 or higher suggests a potential risk of sarcopenia [12].

Other measurements

The Arabic version of activity of daily living (ADL)

The Arabic version of activity of daily living (ADL) The ADL is a questionnaire with self-administration intended to measure the overall level of functional activity, including dressing, bathing, going to the toilet, continence, transferring, and feeding. It is a valid and reliable tool for measuring the level of physical function in older adults. Each item was scored from 0 to 6 in which 0 indicates functional dependence while 6 is functional independence. This total score was calculated after adding up the scores for each of the six items, dividing by six, and multiplying by 100 to obtain the total score [28].

The Arabic version of the fall efficacy scale international (FES-I)

A 16-item questionnaire was designed to gauge the level of concern about falling, and it is based on the 10-item Falls Efficacy Scale with six extended items for assessing more difficult functional tasks and social aspects of falling [29, 30]. FES-I was incorporated in this study for its significance in evaluating fear of falling, a significant psychological barrier to mobility and physical performance, that frequently coexists with sarcopenia. For rating the level of concern about falling, each item was rated on a four-point scale ranging from not at all “1”, somewhat “2”, fairly “3”, and very concerned “4”, generating scores ranked 16 having no fear to 64, having a serious fear [30]. The FES-I Arabic version tool is recognized as valid and reliable for evaluating Arabic-speaking older population levels of concern about falling [31].

Definitions of sarcopenia

The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) criteria define low grip strength as less than 27 kg for men and less than 16 kg for women and/or impaired chair-rise time with a cut-off of > 15 s for five rises, and low muscle quantity as less than 7 kg/m² for men and less than 5.5 kg/m² for women, and low physical performance indicated by a gait speed of ≤ 0.8 m/s.3 For the International Working Group on Sarcopenia (IWGS) criteria, they define sarcopenia as low muscle quantity < 7.23 kg/m² for men and < 5.67 kg/m² for women, with a gait speed ≤ 1 m/s, both low muscle quantity and low physical performance for diagnosis [32]. Finally, the Asian Working Group on Sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria, which defines sarcopenia as established low grip strength as < 27 kg for men and < 18 kg for women, low muscle mass as < 7 kg/m² for men and < 5.7 kg/m² for women, and a gait speed of ≤ 1 m/s.35

Clinical sarcopenia diagnosis measurements

Muscle mass

Body composition was assessed using a bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (BIACORPUS RX 4004 M developed by MEDI CAL HealthCare GmbH). To estimate the Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass (ASMM) we used the equation developed by Sergie et al.,: ASMM (kg) = − 3.964 + (0.227 × (height2/Resistance)) + (0.095 × weight) + (1.384 × sex (with male = 1; female = 0)) + (0.064 × Reactance) [33].

Muscle strength

Handgrip strength was measured using a hand dynamometer in kg (JAMAR PLUS+® Sam-mons Preston, Bolingbrook, IL, USA). Two trained physical therapists collected the strength data, and we averaged the three peak force measurements for the dominant hand in kilograms (kg). Throughout the testing, we regularly checked the calibration of the hand dynamometer.

Gait speed test (4-meter)

The 4-meter Gait Speed Test is a simple and reliable assessment tool used to evaluate an individual’s functional mobility. This test involves walking four meters at a normal pace, using an assistive device if needed, having a 1-meter acceleration zone, a four-meter “testing” zone, with a 1-meter deceleration zone, and a timer that begins at the first foot that passes the 4-meter line and stops after the foot passes the end of the 4-meter line. It is calculated as the distance covered by 4 m divided by the time taken and expressed in meters per second.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Stata version 15.1 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX). Means and standard deviations were reported for continuous variables, and percentages and frequencies were used for categorical variables. The normality of the included variables was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For the test-retest reliability, the Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were computed, where values below 0.5 indicate poor reliability, between 0.5 and 0.75 moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.9 good reliability, while estimates greater than 0.90 indicate excellent reliability [34]. The internal consistency of the Arabic version of the SARC-F scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with a value above 0.70 considered acceptable [35] In addition, the Spearman correlation coefficient was performed between the item in the SARC-F with the Arabic version of the SARC-F total score and was used to analyze the validation of the Arabic version of SARC-F total score against other related measurements to sarcopenia as part of the construct validity. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for the Arabic version of the SARC-F to assess the performance of the screening test with the known cutoff score ≥4; these measurements were computed in relation to the following consensus panels criteria EWGSOP2, IWGS, and AWGS, they are expressed as percentages. Finally, the Receiver Operator Characteristics curve (ROC) and Area Under the Curve (AUC) was calculated to establish the best cutoff score for total SARC-F along with sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios that predicted sarcopenia based on each sarcopenia consensus, including EWGSOP2, IWGS, and AWGS. The best cutoff was determined based on the Youden Index, which calculated the highest sum of sensitivity and specificity for the cutoff score. The significance level was set at alpha = 0.05 for all analyses.

During the translation, only minor changes were made to the final Arabic version of the SARC-F scale, which was utilized in the first question to assess strength. To help participants understand the question, it was necessary to convert the unit pounds to kilograms, as kilograms are the units used in Saudi Arabia.

Discussion

The current study aimed to conduct cross-cultural adaptation and translation and validate the Arabic version of the SARC-F to be used among community-dwelling older adults in Saudi Arabia. Our findings revealed that the Arabic version of the SARC-F had acceptable internal consistency, excellent test-retest reliability, and sufficient validity. However, SARC-F showed high specificity, low sensitivity, and high NPV, which may indicate the ability of the scale to rule out sarcopenia in older Saudi adults. The overall prevalence rate of sarcopenia based on SARC-F was 20.3%, with 3% among men and 17% among women, indicating a significant sex difference. In comparison to other criteria, the EWGSOP2 yielded a prevalence of 29.1%, the IWGS was 5.5%, and the AWGS was 10.98%. In a systematic review and meta-analysis across different countries, the prevalence rates of sarcopenia ranged from 1.8 to 66%, depending on the definition used. The prevalence of people at risk of sarcopenia ranged from 2.8 to 84%, according to different SARC-F versions [36]. The variations in sarcopenia prevalence are mostly due to the diverse diagnostic criteria utilized in research, which may focus on different parameters such as muscle mass, strength, and physical performance. Definitions that focus exclusively on muscle mass and physical performance frequently produce lower prevalence rates, such as IWGS, whereas more complete criteria, such as those from the EWGSOP2 and AWGS, take into account muscle strength and physical performance, yielding higher prevalence rates. Furthermore, age, acute and chronic conditions, race, and geographical health status all play a role in the reported prevalence variance.

The reliability of the Arabic version of the SARC-F, as assessed by Cronbach’s alpha with a reported value of 0.71, suggests acceptable internal consistency, which supports the reliability of the tool in assessing sarcopenia risk among older adults in Saudi Arabia. Although this result is lower than the Cronbach’s alpha values seen in other translations, such as French, Turkish, and Greek versions (0.88, 0.80, and 0.82, respectively) [18, 19, 22]. While some validation studies resulted with less internal consistency, such as the Japanese, Spanish, and Italian versions (0.61, 0.64, and 0.65, respectively) [16, 17, 37]. These differences could be attributed to cultural nuances, linguistic structure, or sample characteristics in each population. Further analysis of these factors and their impact on the psychometric properties of the SARC-F across diverse cultural contexts is warranted to ensure its global applicability and clinical utility. Previous studies on SARC-F validations have found a significant correlation between SARC-F items and total scores. In the current study, our results were similar to earlier findings, with questions 2 and 5 (assistance with walking and falls, respectively) indicating the lowest correlations and items 1 and 4 (strength and climbing stairs, respectively) showing the highest correlations, which very similar to those findings in the Portuguese, Polish, and the Spanish versions [16, 38, 39].

Test-retest reliability was evaluated and found to be excellent, with an ICC equal to 0.94 over a one-week interval. These findings were similar to other validation studies, such as in the Polish validation (ICC = 0.923) and Greek version (ICC = 0.91),23,41 but higher than those reported values in the Spanish validation for the Mexican population, the French version, German, and Turkish version (ICC values of 0.80, 0.86, 0.89, 0.78 respectively) [16, 18, 19, 21]. The high-reliability values in the Arabic SARC-F version highlight the robustness of the tool and endorse their use in clinical practice but in circumstances where repeated measures may be used over time. However, the lower values reported from other translations may be attributed to variations in the features of sample populations and assessment methods.

Regarding the clinical validation against three diagnostic criteria, i.e., AWGS, IWGS, and EWGSOP2, our results showed lower sensitivity and higher values of specificity across all three consensus comparisons. In addition, weak positive predictive values and higher negative predictive values, these findings were found in agreement with different SARC-F versions [16, 17, 19, 37–39]. Previous studies used a SARC-F cutoff of ≥ 4 to identify likely sarcopenia, which had good specificity but low to moderate sensitivity [3, 40]. However, using a cut-off point ≥ 1 improved sensitivity while maintaining adequate specificity for the Arabic SARC-F version across all three definitions. Furthermore, our findings indicated that an SARC-F cut-off point ≥ 2 provided the optimum synthesis of sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing likely sarcopenia across all three definitions. Using a lower threshold (i.e.,≥2) is useful for early detection of people who are at risk of sarcopenia.

In the current study, the Arabic version of the SARC-F was associated with the measurements related to sarcopenia. A significant negative correlation was found between SARC-F scores and ADL, grip strength, and gait speed. That implies that a higher risk of sarcopenia is linked to a lower level of these measurements, which agrees with previous reports [16, 21, 22, 38]. Additionally, our results showed a significant positive correlation between the SARC-F scores and FES, and the number of comorbidities. These results were consistent with other SARC-F versions, such as the Greek and Spanish [16, 22]. Although these findings are typically consistent with other research, the strength of the correlation varies, which may be explained by the fact that different cultures have different cultural beliefs, health conditions, and methods of evaluation, all of which have an impact on the kind and strength of relationships seen.

The present study makes a significant contribution by being the first to thoroughly assess the validity and reliability of the SARC-F translated and adapted into Arabic. Moreover, validity analysis was done against the EWGSOP, the IWGS, and the AWGS criteria.

The results from our study involving the use of the validated Arabic SARC-F have great relevance for the underlying issues of risk assessment and management of sarcopenia among aged individuals in Saudi Arabia. Supporting our research on the effectiveness of the Arabic SARC-F, our study results demonstrated that health professionals can detect sarcopenia early. However, given the scale’s low sensitivity and high specificity, the SARC-F can be an effective assessment tool in determining the absence of sarcopenia and who should undergo further assessment of sarcopenia. Furthermore, using a cutoff of ≥ 2 rather than ≥ 4 on the Arabic SARC-F version would prioritize early detection of individuals with sarcopenia.

Despite the statistical significance of the observed relationships between SARC-F scores and measures such as ADL, grip strength, and gait speed, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to their generally modest correlations. Specifically, grip strength exhibited a moderate association, whereas the correlations with ADL and gait speed were comparatively weaker. Several factors might explain these modest correlations. First, instrument-related factors: The SARC-F relies heavily on subjective self-assessment, introducing inherent variability due to individual differences in the perception of functional capacity. This contrasts with more standardized objective measures like grip strength and gait speed. Second, demographic and clinical sample characteristics: Participants in this study were community-dwelling older adults with an average age of around 65 years, a relatively younger cohort that may not yet exhibit significant functional impairments. As a result, correlations might have been weakened, with potentially stronger associations emerging in populations of older or more frail individuals. Lastly, socio-cultural considerations: Cultural attitudes toward aging, independence, and stigma associated with diminished physical capabilities may influence how participants perceive and report their functional limitations. Such culturally influenced perceptions could moderate the observed associations between subjective and objective assessments. Future research should further explore these factors by including a broader age range and recruiting more culturally and geographically diverse populations to more clearly understand these complex relationships.

Limitations

BIA served as our muscle mass measurement tool despite its limitations compared to DXA and CT as gold-standard techniques. BIA represents a practical tool for clinical use but it does not match the precision levels of advanced methods such as DXA or CT. The research participants came exclusively from Saudi Arabia’s specific geographic area. The research method restricts the ability to generalize our findings beyond Saudi Arabian populations and specific geographic locations.

Strengths

Our research represents the initial investigation that translated and culturally adapted and validated the SARC-F questionnaire for Arabic-speaking Saudi Arabian populations. The research fills an essential need for risk assessment tools to detect sarcopenia early in this population. The Arabic SARC-F received validation through multiple established criteria including EWGSOP2, IWGS and AWGS, which enhances the practicality and reliability of our research findings. The Arabic SARC-F demonstrated strong psychometric properties through its Cronbach’s alpha score of 0.71 and its ICC score of 0.94, which supports its clinical and research applications for assessing sarcopenia risk in Saudi older adults. Finally, a cutoff for the Arabic version of the SARC-F scale has been established, which can help clinicians and health professionals to early detect those who are at risk of sarcopenia.

In conclusion, the SARC-F was successfully translated and adapted into the Arabic language. The Arabic SARC-F demonstrated excellent reliability and adequate validity, but revealed low sensitivity with high specificity across different sarcopenia definitions, indicating that it can detect the absence of sarcopenia. The Arabic SARC-F is easy and practical tool that can be used as a screening tool using the suggested cutoffs. Future studies need to focus on examining its predictive capacity for negative health outcomes and validating its efficacy in diverse clinical and community-based Arabic-speaking populations, which includes individuals with various levels of physical function and comorbidities.
