Lab-scale investigation of drag reduction for crude oil by oil-soluble polymers
Authors: Kun Liu, Xiao Xiong, Xiaodong Dai, Yue Liang, Jinxin Gou, Zhensong Cheng, Guoxin Zhang, Yuan Lu, Jianhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the drag reduction performance of oil-soluble polymers, specifically Polyisobutylene (PIB), in crude oil pipelines. The experiments were conducted using a flow-loop system to simulate turbulent flow conditions. The effects of Reynolds number and polymer concentration on drag reduction were analyzed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM). The results indicated that both the Reynolds number and the concentration of PIB significantly influence the drag reduction performance, with the quadratic term of concentration showing a significant effect. The model developed through RSM was found to be significant with a high R-square value, indicating a good fit to the experimental data. The predictive accuracy of the model was further validated through a comparison of experimental and calculated drag reduction rates, which were found to be within ± 10% of each other. The study concludes that PIB is an effective drag reducer for crude oil, and the developed model can be used to optimize the application of PIB in pipeline transportation, potentially leading to increased efficiency and cost savings.Subject terms: Chemistry, Engineering

Introduction

The research on turbulent drag reduction originated in the early 20th century, with scattered and unstructured findings. It wasn’t until World War II that the demand for fluid transportation efficiency in military and industrial sectors spurred Toms and Mysels to conduct in-depth studies on the impact of polymers on friction and pressure drop in pipelines in the 1940s1. Toms’ research led to the “Toms’ effect,” laying the theoretical foundation for turbulent drag reduction, yet the understanding of the drag reduction mechanism remained rudimentary due to limitations in experimental conditions and theoretical frameworks at that time. From the mid-20th century onwards, advancements in fluid mechanics and polymer chemistry propelled drag reduction research. In 1975, Virk’s comprehensive study on drag reduction in water proposed a general relationship for the Fanning friction factor, offering a crucial analytical tool for subsequent research. During this period, studies mainly focused on drag reduction effects in single-phase fluids like water, with relatively limited research on drag reduction in complex fluids such as crude oil2. Entering the 21 st century, growing energy demands and environmental requirements have increased the focus on drag reduction technologies in crude oil pipeline transportation. Researchers now not only examine the drag reduction effects of drag-reducing agents but also delve into the interaction mechanisms between these agents and crude oil properties, pipeline conditions, etc. Moreover, the development of new drag-reducing agents, such as biodegradable surfactants and high-shear-resistant polymers, has expanded the application possibilities of drag reduction technologies3.

Recent research has significantly advanced the development of drag reducers for hydraulic fracturing operations, particularly in unconventional oil and gas reservoirs. Liang4 developed a shear-resistant associative thickener based on hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) for high-salinity environments, demonstrating its effectiveness in drag reduction and sand-carrying capacity. Jin5 investigated the relationship between the molecular structure and performance of HPAM-based high-viscosity friction reducers (HVFRs), highlighting the impact of molecular weight and hydrolysis degree on their drag reduction and proppant transport capabilities. Dai6 synthesized shear-resistant and fast-dissolving drag reducers using polyampholyte copolymers with hydrophobic associations, which showed excellent rheological properties and drag reduction performance. Qi7 introduced a pH-switchable four-tailed ionic liquid surfactant to improve the stability and dissolution of polyacrylamide emulsion drag reducers, enabling rapid release under specific pH conditions. Qin8 explored the synergistic effects of wormlike micelles and polymer-based Fe3O4 nanoparticles in brine, achieving significant drag reduction and enhanced oil recovery. Wang9 developed a nanoemulsion-enhanced high-viscosity friction reducer system for low-permeability carbonate reservoirs, which improved viscosity, drag reduction, and imbibition oil recovery by altering reservoir wettability. These studies collectively advance the development of more efficient and adaptable drag reducers for hydraulic fracturing in diverse reservoir conditions.

The following papers collectively investigate various aspects of drag reduction in crude oil pipeline transportation, focusing on the development and application of different drag-reducing agents to enhance transport efficiency and reduce energy consumption and costs.

Karami and Mowla10 developed a comprehensive model for predicting drag reduction in crude oil pipelines, considering factors like oil flow rate, temperature, pipe diameter, roughness, and drag reducer concentration. Validated with 648 experimental data points, the model showed a mean absolute percentage error of approximately 3.5%. Their earlier work in 201211 also explored the effects of various parameters on pressure drop reduction in crude oil pipelines using drag-reducing agents, providing valuable insights into optimizing pipeline operations. Kumar12 extracted a natural surfactant from Madhuca longifolia and tested it in horizontal pipeline crude oil flow. The results demonstrated significant pressure drop reduction, with drag reduction reaching 74% at 15% water dispersion and 2000 ppm surfactant. This study highlighted the potential of using biodegradable, cost-effective surfactants to improve crude oil flow properties. Cao13 analyzed the impact of oil viscosity, temperature, and drag reducer concentration on drag reduction, proposing a modified prediction equation and a pre-dispersion method for short-distance pipelines. Their work aimed to improve the utilization efficiency of drag reducers in practical applications, particularly in scenarios with rapid loading and unloading at pipeline terminals. Quan14 investigated the effect of high-molecular polymers as drag reducers in single-phase horizontal pipe flow. They found that drag reduction initially increased with polymer concentration up to 5 ppm and was significantly influenced by Reynolds number, oil temperature, and shear. This study contributed to understanding the complex interactions between polymer additives and flow conditions in turbulent pipe flow. Mahmood15 created an oil-soluble polymer from aloe vera, achieving an 82% drag reduction at 600 ppm concentration, with high resistance to mechanical shear forces. Their work showcased the potential of using agricultural waste-derived polymers as effective drag-reducing agents in crude oil transportation.

Since the drag reduction technology can provide huge economic benefits in the petroleum industry, engineers used a method to reduce the cost in the operation of the pipeline system. The application of drag-reducing additives in oil pipelines has demonstrated significant benefits through successful implementations in real-world scenarios16,17. In the Trans Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS), the use of a polymeric drag-reducing additive allowed for increased flow rates without exceeding pressure limits, resulting in a throughput increase of about 300,000 BID (2000 m³/h) since mid-1979. This success was mirrored in laboratory and field tests with Prudhoe Bay crude oil, where studies investigated the effects of various factors on drag reduction and developed a modified time scale hypothesis for predicting drag-reduction levels. These cases highlight the importance of drag reduction in oil pipelines as it offers increased throughput, energy efficiency, cost-effectiveness, operational flexibility, and compatibility with refinery processing, making it a valuable solution for enhancing pipeline performance and efficiency.

From the previous discussions, we can clearly see that the drag reduction by additives is crucial for the oil transportation in the petroleum industry as suggested by several works18–27, so in this work, we introduce four distinct advances. First, we build a response-surface model that couples Reynolds number and polymer concentration into a single algebraic expression, turning scattered observations into a predictive design equation that engineers can deploy without iterative experiments. Second, through systematic rheometry we reconcile conflicting reports by demonstrating that, within the practical concentration window, PIB-crude-oil mixtures remain Newtonian. Third, we translate these bench findings to commercial scale by invoking a constant-friction-factor similarity rule, providing a transparent pathway for upscaling that bypasses costly pilot loops. Finally, we quantify how repeated exposure to high shear erodes drag-reduction efficiency via chain scission, thereby integrating degradation kinetics into the overall design framework.

Experiment

The experiments were carried out in the flow-loop system as illustrated in Fig. 1. A gear pump (KCB5800, Tianjinhaotian, China, with a maximum flow rate of 6 m3/min) was employed to maintain a steady flow rate for the liquid transferred from the storage tank. The polymer solution used for the drag-reduction test was disposal and it would not be reused again; the polymer solution for the degradation test would be recycled in the flow loop, which were shown in the following content. The turbine flowmeter (HLLWQ3016, maximum flowrate 10 m3/min) was used to monitor the flowrate in the flow loop. A differential pressure sensor (RS-YC16E, Renke, China) was used to measure the pressure drop, which had a full-scale measurement range and uncertainty of 1 MPa and 500 Pa (0.05% of the full-scale range), respectively. The distance between two pressure taps is 20 m. The inner diameter and length of the test section in the flow loop are 5 cm and 20 m, the latter one the same as the distance between two pressure taps. A computer was used to record both the flow rate and the pressure drop. The rheological test was performed at the rheometer (MCR102, Anton Paar, Austria).

Schematic of flow loop for drag-reduction and degradation test.

The preparation of the polymer solution for drag reduction in crude oil pipelines is as follows. First, the drag-reducing polymer, Polyisobutylene (PIB, average molecular weight 2,000,000 g/mol) was used and mixed with the crude oil from Shengli oilfield to form a concentrated solution. We first prepared a concentrated polymer solution, 1%, the ratio of the mass of weighed PIB over the mass of crude oil. Then we diluted this concentrated polymer solution to the target concentration. This pre-mixing ensures thorough dispersion of the polymer. The concentrated solution is then diluted by slowly adding it to the bulk crude oil while maintaining appropriate stirring to avoid shear degradation. The mixing continues until the polymer is fully dissolved and uniformly dispersed. The solution’s rheological properties are tested to confirm its effectiveness in drag reduction. When injecting the solution into the pipeline, low-shear pumps are used to prevent polymer chain scission, and the injection rate is adjusted based on monitoring the pipeline’s pressure drop and flow rate. Temperature control during the process is crucial for maintaining solution stability. Compatibility between the polymer solution and the crude oil is also verified to avoid any adverse effects: Compatibility between the PIB solution and the crude oil was verified by observations: after dilution and gentle mixing, the sample was analyzed in the rheometer. A linear Newtonian response and absence of phase separation or precipitates confirmed compatibility, which are shown in the following content; conversely, incompatibility would cause polymer precipitation, flocculation, or viscosity increases that could clog pipelines and affect drag reduction negatively.

Results and discussions

Figure 2 presents the rheological properties of polyisobutylene (PIB) solutions at different concentrations, which is crucial for understanding their performance in practical applications. Figure 2a displays the shear stress changes of PIB solutions at various concentrations (ranging from 0 to 500 ppm) under different shear rates (from 0 to 30 s⁻¹). It can be observed that as the shear rate increases, the shear stress also correspondingly increases, and the shear stress of solutions at different concentrations shows similar trends across all shear rates.

Viscosity of crude oil under different concentrations (a: shear stress vs. shear rate; b: viscosity as a function of concentration).

This linear relationship indicates that within the tested concentration range, PIB solutions exhibit characteristics of Newtonian fluids, where the shear stress is directly proportional to the shear rate, without any shear-thinning or shear-thickening phenomena. This Newtonian fluid characteristic is particularly important for the application of PIB solutions in turbulent drag reduction, as it implies that under different flow conditions, the solution can provide stable drag reduction effects: PIB solution consistently exhibits a predictable and reliable viscosity, without significant fluctuations of the viscosity, which is very critical to the drag reduction since we need to calculate the Reynolds number. If the viscosities under different concentrations varies significantly, the Reynolds number cannot be calculated well, which may cause an unreliable drag-reduction calculation. The error bars for these viscosities are not added since this rheometer has a high measurement accuracy.

Figure 2b illustrates the viscosity (µ) changes of PIB solutions at different concentrations. As concentration increases, the viscosity of the solution significantly increases, showing a clear concentration dependence. Specifically, from 0 ppm to 500 ppm, the viscosity of the solution increases from approximately 150 mPa·s to about 850 mPa·s. This result further confirms the impact of PIB concentration on the rheological behavior of the solution, and as the concentration increases, the fluidity of the solution decreases, manifested as an increase in viscosity. This concentration-dependent viscosity change is significant for the formulation design and performance optimization of PIB in industrial applications, as it allows precise control of the solution’s rheological properties by adjusting the concentration to meet specific application needs.

This increase in viscosity can be attributed to the enhanced interaction of PIB molecules in the solution. At higher concentrations, intermolecular interactions (such as van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, etc.) become more pronounced, leading to increased internal resistance in the solution and thus higher viscosity. This effect is highly desirable in turbulent drag reduction, as it can improve the efficiency of fluid transportation and reduce energy consumption. By adjusting the concentration of PIB, the rheological properties of the solution can be precisely controlled to achieve optimal turbulent drag reduction effects.

The experimental observation that dilute PIB–crude-oil solutions behave as Newtonian fluids up to 500 ppm and shear rates of 30 s⁻¹ does not contradict the literature reports of shear-thinning for PIB. The key differences are the polymer concentration and the shear-rate range. At the low concentrations used here, chain overlap is negligible and the longest relaxation time is shorter than the inverse shear rate, so the elastic contribution to viscosity is minimal. Moreover, the shear rates examined are well below those required to induce coil–stretch transitions and the associated viscosity reduction. Earlier studies showing pronounced shear-thinning employed either higher concentrations (> 1 wt %) or significantly higher shear rates (> 10² s⁻¹). Thus, the Newtonian behavior observed in this study is consistent with the expected onset of non-Newtonian effects at higher concentrations or shear rates28,29.

Moving to the right graph (Fig. 3b), it illustrates the impact of adding polyisobutylene drag-reducing agents at different concentrations (ranging from 100 ppm to 500 ppm) on the relationship between the Fanning friction factor and the Reynolds number. The use of different colors and shapes for the markers helps distinguish between the various concentrations of PIB used in the experiments. A clear trend emerges where the friction factor decreases with an increase in the Reynolds number, like the left graph. However, the significant difference lies in the magnitude of the friction factor; it is substantially lower when PIB is added, indicating the agent’s effectiveness in reducing drag. The reduction in friction factor becomes more pronounced with higher concentrations of PIB, suggesting a direct relationship between the concentration of the drag-reducing agent and its effectiveness. This finding is particularly significant as it provides a practical guideline for optimizing the concentration of PIB to achieve the desired level of drag reduction. The results also highlight the potential of PIB as a drag-reducing agent in various fluid transport applications, where reducing energy consumption and enhancing efficiency are critical.

In Fig. 3b, the friction factor for the 100 ppm PIB solution (green points) shows a minimum at Re = 8000. This behavior can be attributed to the interplay between the elastic effects of the polymer chains and the turbulent flow characteristics. At lower Reynolds numbers, the inertial forces are relatively weak, and the polymer chains do not significantly influence the turbulent structures. As the Reynolds number increases to around 8000, the elastic effects of the polymer chains become more distinct, effectively suppressing the formation and growth of turbulent vortices. This suppression leads to a reduction in the friction factor, resulting in the observed minimum at Re = 8000. Beyond this point, the turbulent intensity increases further, and the polymer chains can no longer fully suppress the turbulent structures, causing the friction factor to rise again.

In summary, Fig. 3 not only confirms the theoretical predictions regarding fluid friction without drag-reducing agents but also demonstrates the substantial benefits of using polyisobutylene as a drag-reducing agent. The findings underscore the importance of concentration in determining the effectiveness of PIB, providing a foundation for further research and application development in the field of drag reduction.

Figure 4 clearly illustrates the drag reduction rate (DR%) of polyisobutylene (PIB) solutions at various concentrations under different Reynolds numbers (Re). It can be observed from the figure that as the Reynolds number increases, the drag reduction rate also increases, indicating that the drag reduction effect of PIB solutions is more significant at higher Reynolds numbers. Additionally, the different colored dots in the graph represent PIB solutions at various concentrations, with green representing 100 ppm, blue representing 200 ppm, cyan representing 300 ppm, magenta representing 400 ppm, and yellow representing 500 ppm. It can be seen that at the same Reynolds number, the higher the concentration of the PIB solution, the higher the drag reduction rate, indicating a positive correlation between the drag reduction effect and the concentration of PIB.

Drag reduction under different conditions.

This phenomenon can be explained by the viscoelastic properties of PIB solutions. As a polymer, PIB solutions exhibit significant viscoelasticity, meaning that the molecules in the solution can resist shear deformation like a viscous fluid and recover to their original state after shear deformation like an elastic body. Under turbulent conditions, the presence of polymer molecules can suppress the growth and propagation of turbulent eddies, thereby reducing the energy loss caused by turbulence and achieving drag reduction, which is support by Virk, who mentioned that turbulent drag reduction by dilute polymer solutions is widely attributed to the viscoelastic response of stretched macromolecules, which imparts an anisotropic elastic stress that damps the quasi-streamwise vortices in the buffer layer. This suppresses the lift-up of low-speed streaks and thereby attenuates the Reynolds shear stress and turbulent kinetic energy production30. With an increase in PIB concentration, the number of polymer molecules in the solution increases, enhancing the viscoelasticity of the solution and thus more effectively suppressing turbulent eddies and improving the drag reduction effect.

Furthermore, the impact of the Reynolds number on the drag reduction effect cannot be overlooked. The Reynolds number is an important parameter that describes the state of fluid flow, reflecting the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces. At higher Reynolds numbers, the intensity of turbulence in the fluid is greater, and the role of polymer molecules in turbulence is more significant, hence the drag reduction effect is more apparent. This is because when Reynolds number rises, turbulence becomes more vigorous and decreases the size of eddy; the faster-moving eddies then interact more strongly with the polymer coils, enabling the viscoelastic stresses to damp the fluctuations and lower the drag31.

Indeed, drag reduction happens due to the suppression of turbulence by PIB. Theoretical analysis shows that PIB suppresses turbulence primarily through its elastic, rather than viscous, response32. Under turbulent strain the polymer coils are stretched on a time scale set by their relaxation time; when the relaxation time exceeds the Kolmogorov time the stored elastic stress acts as a spring-back force that damps vortex stretching in the buffer layer, reducing bursting events and shifting energy away from the dissipative scales. This “elastic barrier” lowers the Reynolds shear stress and hence the friction factor, while the modest viscosity increases play only a secondary role32.

In summary, the drag reduction effect of PIB solutions is jointly influenced by their concentration and the Reynolds number of the fluid, with more significant drag reduction effects observed at higher concentrations and higher Reynolds numbers. This finding is significant for optimizing the application of PIB as a drag-reducing agent, as it provides the possibility of achieving optimal drag reduction effects by adjusting the concentration of PIB and controlling the conditions of fluid flow. This has potential application value for improving the efficiency of fluid transportation, reducing energy consumption, and achieving energy saving and emission reduction in industrial applications.

In the previous section, we demonstrated that PIB is effective as a drag reducer in turbulent flow of crude oil. However, qualitative assessments alone are insufficient for practical applications in the petroleum industry. Quantitative predictions of drag reduction are crucial for optimizing pipeline operations and achieving significant cost savings. To address this need, we employed Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to develop a predictive model that quantitatively estimates drag reduction based on experimental data. This approach allows engineers to make informed decisions without the need for extensive iterative experiments, thereby enhancing the efficiency and reliability of pipeline transport systems.

In this study, the effects of Reynolds number (A) and concentration (B) on the experimental results were analyzed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM). The analysis revealed that the model was significant overall (F = 35.94, p = 0.007), indicating that the combination of Reynolds number and concentration has an important impact on the experimental results.

Specifically, the Reynolds number (A) had a significant effect on the experimental results (F = 23.02, p = 0.0172), suggesting that changes in the Reynolds number have an important linear contribution to the outcomes. Moreover, the effect of concentration (B) was even more significant (F = 137.53, p = 0.0013), indicating that variations in concentration have an extremely important impact on the experimental results. Additionally, the quadratic term of concentration (B²) also showed significance (F = 13.12, p = 0.0362), further demonstrating that the non-linear effect of concentration cannot be ignored in the experimental process.

However, the interaction between Reynolds number and concentration (AB) had no significant effect on the experimental results (F = 0.2745, p = 0.6365), indicating that within the scope of this study, there is no significant synergistic or antagonistic effect between the Reynolds number and concentration. Moreover, the quadratic term of the Reynolds number (A²) also had no significant effect on the experimental results (F = 5.77, p = 0.0957), suggesting that the non-linear effect of the Reynolds number is not evident under the experimental conditions of this study.

Thus, concentration (B) and its quadratic term (B²) are the key factors influencing the experimental results, while the Reynolds number (A) has a significant linear effect, although its non-linear effect and interaction with concentration are not significant in this study. These findings provide an important theoretical basis for optimizing experimental conditions.

As shown in Table 3, the quadratic model already meets the statistical benchmarks and neglect two terms, AB and B², the interaction term of Reynolds number and concentration, and the square of concentration. This is not only based on the statistical results, but also related to the physical meaning. The measured pressure drop is related to two independent variables, i.e., turbulent dissipation rate in the core governed by Re, and viscoelastic suppression in the near-wall layer governed by concentration26. The absence of interaction term AB, becomes self-evident: Re dictates the turbulence far from the wall, while concentration only affects the near-wall regime; these two regions scarcely overlap, so this term can be neglected. Similarly, within the tested Re range (approximately from 5000 to 15000) the core dissipation rate, which is proportional to the pressure drop, rises almost linearly on the log-law scale, and adding a Re² term merely bends an already gentle curve, so its contribution is therefore statistically negligible2.

In this study, to thoroughly investigate the factors affecting the drag reduction performance of PIB in the turbulent flow, we employed Response Surface Methodology (RSM) for statistical analysis. To ensure the reliability of the analysis results and the validity of the model, we conducted a series of hypothesis tests, including normality checks, residual analysis, and comparison between predicted and experimental values. These analyses not only validate the applicability of the model but also provide a solid foundation for subsequent research and applications.

Firstly, we examined the distribution of residuals through a normal probability plot (Fig. 5a). In this plot, the data points are approximately aligned along a straight line, indicating that the residuals are approximately normally distributed, meeting one of the fundamental assumptions of statistical analysis. This result bolsters our confidence in the model’s outcomes, suggesting that the statistical inferences are valid.

The residuals are randomly distributed in the plot, without any apparent pattern or trend, indicating that the order of experiments does not significantly affect the residuals. In summary, through these graphical analyses, we have validated the applicability and effectiveness of the Response Surface Methodology model in analyzing the drag reduction performance of PIB. The model not only meets the basic assumptions of statistical analysis but also demonstrates good predictive power and data fit. These analysis results will provide a solid foundation for further research on the drag reduction performance of PIB in saline solutions and offer valuable references for related engineering applications.

These results not only validate the effectiveness of the model but also provide a solid theoretical foundation for further optimizing the application of PIB and offer strong support for related engineering practices. Therefore, the model in this study can serve as a powerful tool for assessing and optimizing the drag reduction performance of PIB, which is significant for advancing research and applications in this field.

Due to the scale limitations of our lab-scale flow loop (5 cm diameter, 20 m length), we acknowledge that it may not fully capture the complexities of industrial-scale pipelines, such as wall roughness effects, longer distances, and varying diameters. However, to bridge this gap, we employ an empirical scaling approach, i.e., is a constant under the same concentration of drag-reducing agents, which is the most widely-accepted conclusion for the scaling issue for the drag-reducing flow and has been validated as a constant for geometrically similar systems under turbulent flow conditions28.

Using this method, we first determined the drag reduction performance in our small-scale system (diameter 0.05 m, Reynolds number 11,000, concentration 400ppm, friction factor 0.0016 calculated accordingly). By maintaining the constant , we scaled up to an industrial pipeline with diameter 1 m and Reynolds number 400,000 (literature-derived for typical crude oil transport). The scaled friction factor was calculated as friction factor 0.0011 approximately yielding a predicted drag reduction rate of 38% (compared to 79% in our lab system). This deviation suggests the scaling law effectively translates lab results to field conditions, though we recommend future validation with pilot-scale tests to account for roughness and thermal effects.

The observed discrepancy in drag reduction rates between laboratory-scale and industrial-scale pipelines can be largely attributed to the significant differences in pipe diameter and Reynolds number. In our laboratory experiments, a pipe diameter of 0.05 m and a Reynolds number of 11,000 were used, whereas industrial-scale pipelines typically have a diameter of 1 m and a Reynolds number as high as 400,000. These changes in scale lead to marked differences in flow characteristics, which in turn affect the performance of the drag-reducing agent.

The higher Reynolds number in industrial-scale pipelines implies a more intense turbulent flow regime. The increased turbulence intensity and shear stress distribution in larger-diameter pipes can significantly alter the effectiveness of the drag-reducing agent. Specifically, the complex turbulent structures in industrial-scale pipelines may not be as effectively suppressed by the polymer chains of the drag-reducing agent as they are in the laboratory-scale setup. This is because the higher shear rates and more vigorous turbulent eddies in industrial-scale pipelines can cause the polymer chains to stretch and align differently, reducing their ability to dampen turbulent fluctuations and leading to a lower drag reduction rate34.

This study provides a comprehensive, experimentally validated correlation between PIB concentration, Reynolds number, and drag-reduction efficiency, providing an empirical tool that pipeline engineers can apply immediately. Nevertheless, the laboratory-scale flow loop (5 cm diameter, 20 m length) and the narrow range of shear rates explored here mean that wall roughness, thermal gradients, and longer residence times typical of field pipelines have not been considered. In addition, the investigation remains descriptive: although we confirm the importance of viscoelasticity, we do not resolve how stretched PIB chains suppress the growth of quasi-streamwise vortices or modify the energy cascade within the buffer layer. The present data set can serve as a rigorous benchmark for high-resolution particle-image velocimetry (PIV) experiments and for direct numerical simulations (DNS) incorporating finitely extensible nonlinear elastic (FENE) constitutive models. Such mechanistic studies, conducted across broader ranges of pipe diameter, temperature, and oil composition, will ultimately translate our empirical findings into a physics-based design equation for industrial-scale drag-reduction additives.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has successfully demonstrated the effectiveness of Polyisobutylene (PIB) as a drag reducer in crude oil pipeline transportation through a combination of experimental analysis and Response Surface Methodology (RSM). The findings have shown that both the Reynolds number and the concentration of PIB significantly influence drag reduction, with the quadratic effect of concentration also playing a pivotal role. The predictive model developed through RSM has proven to be highly accurate, with errors kept within an acceptable ± 10% range, which is suitable for practical applications. These results are of significant value to the petroleum industry, as they provide insights into optimizing PIB usage for drag reduction, potentially leading to greater efficiency in fluid transport and reduced energy consumption. The model developed in this research can be an asset for engineers and researchers, assisting in the planning and operation of pipeline systems that employ drag-reducing agents. This study not only enhances theoretical understanding of drag reduction mechanisms but also offers practical guidance for improving pipeline performance using PIB. Future research may continue to investigate the long-term stability and environmental implications of PIB and explore its application across different pipeline materials and configurations.
