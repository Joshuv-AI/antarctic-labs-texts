Concomitant Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation in Redo Heart Valve Surgery
Authors: Yoonjin Kang, Dong Jae Han, Jae Woong Choi, Ho Young Hwang, Kyung Hwan Kim, Hong Rae Kim, Joon Bum Kim, Jin Kyoung Kim, Ho Jin Kim, Jae Suk Yoo, Sung-Ho Jung, Jae Won Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough there is general agreement on performing concomitant ablation of atrial fibrillation (AF) during left-sided heart valve surgery in low-risk patients due to its proven long-term clinical benefits, its role in reoperative cases remains debated because of perceived high risks.MethodsThis study included 338 consecutive patients with AF who underwent redo surgery for left-sided valve disease between 2000 and 2015 at 2 tertiary referral centers. Among them, 143 patients underwent concomitant surgical ablation for AF (ablation group), while 195 did not (no-ablation group). To evaluate comparative long-term clinical outcomes between the 2 groups, inverse probability of treatment weighting was applied.ResultsEarly mortality rates were 3.5% (4/143) in the ablation group and 9.2% (18/195) in the no-ablation group (p=0.064). At 5 years, the cumulative incidence of AF recurrence was 12.3%±0.1% in the ablation group and 85.2%±0.1% in the no-ablation group (p<0.001). During follow-up (median, 103 months), the ablation group demonstrated significantly lower risks of death (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.495; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.312–0.784; p=0.003) and thromboembolic events (aHR, 0.212; 95% CI, 0.058–0.771; p=0.019) compared with the no-ablation group.ConclusionsConcomitant AF ablation during redo valve surgery was associated with improved rhythm outcomes, survival, and freedom from thromboembolic events, supporting its consideration as a reasonable option even in this high-risk group.Keywords: Arrhythmia, Redo cardiac surgery, Surgical ablation

Introduction

Although the Cox-maze procedure is well established for achieving higher conversion rates to sinus rhythm and for reducing the risks of mortality and thromboembolism [1-3], concomitant surgical ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) during redo cardiac surgery is performed less frequently. This may be due to the perception that ablation during redo surgery carries increased risks, given the longer cardiopulmonary bypass and cross-clamp times and the higher rates of perioperative morbidities associated with the Cox-maze procedure [4,5]. While some studies have included patients undergoing redo cases [2,6,7], there is limited literature focusing specifically on the safety and efficacy of surgical ablation in isolated redo cardiac surgery populations [8-10]. We previously reported outcomes of concomitant surgical ablation during redo cardiac surgery [9]. However, that study was limited by its small sample size, use of less robust statistical methods, and single-center design. To address these limitations, the present study incorporates a larger and more diverse patient population from 2 high- volume tertiary centers and applies inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to better control for confounding variables. These methodological enhancements allow for a more rigorous evaluation of long-term outcomes and provide new insights that complement and extend our earlier findings.

Accordingly, as an extension of our prior work, this study was conducted to investigate the outcomes of concomitant surgical ablation for AF in patients undergoing redo cardiac surgery for left-sided valvular heart disease.

Methods

Patient enrollment

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by our institutional review board as a minimal-risk retrospective study that did not require individual consent (approval number: H-2103-115-1205; 2024-0014). From January 2000 to December 2015, a total of 338 consecutive patients with AF underwent redo cardiac surgery for left-sided valvular disease at 2 tertiary centers. Patients were excluded if they had active endocarditis, had previously undergone surgical ablation, or had a permanent pacemaker. Of the eligible patients, 143 underwent concomitant surgical ablation for AF (ablation group), while 195 did not (no-ablation group).

AF was classified into paroxysmal, persistent, long-standing persistent, and permanent, according to established guidelines [11]. Surgical ablation was performed in 57 patients with paroxysmal AF, 17 with persistent AF, and 109 with long-standing persistent AF. The decision to perform concomitant ablation was made at the discretion of the surgeon, based on factors such as patient age, number of prior operations, AF duration, and left atrial size.

Operative strategy

The surgical techniques and strategies for the Cox-maze procedure have been previously described [12]. Specifically, the CryoMaze procedure employed lesion sets identical to those of the conventional surgical maze method, although the majority were created using cryoablation with a nitrous oxide-based system. Left atrial lesions included a line from the left superior pulmonary vein to the left atrial appendage (LAA), LAA closure, a box lesion encircling all 4 pulmonary vein ostia, a coronary sinus lesion, and a mitral isthmus line. The right pulmonary vein isolation and LAA lesion were completed with a cryoprobe. Right atrial lesion sets consisted of a line connecting the superior and inferior vena cava orifices, a “T” lesion on the right atrium, lesions extending posteriorly and inferiorly toward the tricuspid annulus, and a lesion involving the right atrial appendage. Both institutions used the same maze lesion set.

In 2000, the Cox-maze III procedure was implemented exclusively with cryoablation. Since 2005, cryoablation has been consistently used, with radiofrequency ablation added in 6 cases at the surgeon’s discretion. From 2015 onward, all surgical ablations have been performed with cryoablation only. All operations were carried out via median sternotomy under moderate hypothermia and cold cardioplegic arrest. The choice of ablation type was determined by the operating surgeon.

Evaluation of early and long-term clinical outcomes

Operative mortality was defined as death occurring within 30 days of surgery or during the same hospitalization. Postoperative low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS) was defined as the need for mechanical or inotropic support to maintain systolic blood pressure above 90 mm Hg after correction of reversible factors. Stroke was defined as a cerebral thromboembolic event.

Final cardiac rhythm was determined from the most recent electrocardiogram (ECG) at follow-up. Postoperative AF included AF, atrial flutter, or a pacing rhythm with underlying AF. Freedom from AF was defined as the absence of AF during follow-up, and AF recurrence was defined as any documented AF episode after the blanking period [13]. Patients with early postoperative or late recurrent AF were treated with antiarrhythmic medications.

Patients were followed in outpatient clinics every 3–6 months. For those who missed visits, follow-up was conducted by telephone. Mortality data, including all-cause and cardiac mortality, were obtained for all patients from Statistics Korea, which maintains a nationwide mortality registry. The primary endpoint was overall survival. Cardiac death was defined as death due to cardiac causes, including sudden unexpected death. Thromboembolic events were defined as those causing death, hospitalization, or permanent injury, excluding transient ischemic attacks. Permanent injury was defined as a thromboembolic complication leading to persistent neurological deficits or functional impairments documented at discharge or thereafter. A bleeding event was defined as major bleeding requiring medical or surgical intervention. The follow-up period concluded on March 31, 2022.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 3.6.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The normality of continuous variables was tested with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Non-normally distributed variables were compared using non-parametric methods. Group comparisons were made using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the Student t-test for continuous variables. Continuous variables without normal distribution were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Results were expressed as median (interquartile range). Survival rates were estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method. Risk factors for all-cause mortality were evaluated using multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. Cumulative incidences of thromboembolic events were estimated with all-cause death as a competing risk, and the Fine-Gray test was used to compare cumulative incidences between groups.

The variables included in the multivariate analysis of early and long-term outcomes were age, sex, body surface area, risk factors (smoking, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, body mass index ≥25.0 kg/m2, history of stroke, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease, dyslipidemia, poor functional class, paroxysmal AF, AF duration, more than 2 prior operations, preoperative left ventricular dysfunction, and severely dilated left atrium), previous cardiac procedures (aortic, mitral, or tricuspid valve surgery, coronary artery bypass grafting, or aortic surgery), and concomitant procedures (mitral, tricuspid, or aortic valve surgery). Impaired left ventricular function was defined as an ejection fraction <50%. The left atrium was defined as severely dilated if its anteroposterior diameter on parasternal long-axis view was ≥60 mm at end systole. Variables with p<0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate models, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Propensity score (PS) analyses were performed to adjust for baseline differences. Preoperative characteristics and concomitant procedures were included in logistic regression models to generate PS values. To balance patients for baseline characteristics, IPTW was applied. All variables with standardized mean difference <0.1 were included. A clustered Cox regression analysis of overall survival was then performed using the IPTW-adjusted cohort.

The median preoperative AF duration was 10 years (Q1–Q3, 3–16). None of the patients had previously undergone catheter ablation. The no-ablation group was significantly older, more frequently had at least 2 prior cardiac operations, and more often had prior aortic and mitral valve procedures. AF duration was longer in the no-ablation group, although there were no significant differences in the proportion of patients with long-standing persistent AF between groups. After IPTW adjustment, none of the preoperative variables differed significantly.

The mean total cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time was 230.8±73.3 minutes, with the ablation group demonstrating a shorter CPB time (219.5±63.8 minutes) compared with the no-ablation group (239.1±78.8 minutes, p=0.012). The mean aortic cross-clamp (ACC) time was 145.5±56.0 minutes, with no significant difference between the ablation (144.6±53.9 minutes) and no-ablation groups (146.2± 57.6 minutes, p=0.786). Concomitant cardiac procedures included mitral (n=304), aortic (n=162), and tricuspid (n=223) valve operations. The no-ablation group more frequently underwent concomitant aortic valve procedures, though the difference was not significant after IPTW adjustment.

Early results

Operative mortality occurred in 23 patients (6.8% overall; 5 [3.5%] in the ablation group and 18 [9.2%] in the no-ablation group). The difference between groups was not statistically significant, even after IPTW adjustment (p= 0.233) (Table 2). The no-ablation group experienced higher rates of postoperative low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS) and acute kidney injury compared with the ablation group; however, these differences were not significant after IPTW adjustment. Postoperative stroke was more frequent in the no-ablation group (9.2%) than in the ablation group (2.1%; p=0.014). No patients required pacemaker implantation during hospitalization in either group.

Long-term survival

Late deaths occurred in 26 patients in the ablation group and 94 patients in the no-ablation group. The 10-year overall survival rates were 83.9% in the ablation group and 55.1% in the no-ablation group. Survival was significantly better in the ablation group (p<0.001) (Fig. 1). After IPTW adjustment, multivariate analysis confirmed that concomitant AF ablation was independently associated with improved overall survival (hazard ratio [HR], 0.495; 95% CI, 0.312–0.784; reference: no-ablation group). Increasing age at the time of operation was independently associated with worse survival (HR, 1.070; 95% CI, 1.045–1.094) (Table 3).

Recurrence of atrial fibrillation

At the final follow-up ECG, sinus rhythm was observed in 88 patients (61.5%) in the ablation group, compared with 20 patients (10.3%) in the no-ablation group receiving optimal medical therapy. The cumulative incidence of AF recurrence was significantly lower in the ablation group (p< 0.001) (Fig. 2). After IPTW adjustment, concomitant ablation remained strongly associated with reduced AF recurrence (HR, 0.168; 95% CI, 0.116–0.245; reference: no-ablation group) (Table 4). In subgroup analysis of the ablation group, age was the only independent predictor of AF recurrence (HR, 1.570; 95% CI, 1.027–1.089; p<0.001) (Table 5).

Thromboembolic events

The incidence of thromboembolic events was higher in the no-ablation group, though the difference was only marginally significant (3 [2.1%] vs. 14 [7.2%]; p=0.063). Of the 3 events in the ablation group, 2 occurred in patients who had undergone left atrial appendage excision or internal obliteration. Prothrombin time remained within therapeutic range in all cases of bleeding or thromboembolism. Bleeding events were attributable to spontaneous hemorrhage of unknown origin, while thromboembolic events occurred without identifiable precipitating factors. The cumulative 5-year incidence of thromboembolic events was 1.5% in the ablation group and 4.2% in the no-ablation group; this difference was not significant (p=0.053) (Fig. 3). However, IPTW-adjusted multivariate analysis demonstrated a significantly lower risk of thromboembolic events in the ablation group (HR, 0.212; 95% CI, 0.058–0.771; reference: no-ablation group) (Table 5).

Discussion

The Cox-maze procedure has been shown to provide excellent long-term efficacy in AF patients with valvular heart disease [14,15]. However, evidence regarding the safety and effectiveness of surgical ablation during redo cardiac surgery for persistent or long-standing persistent AF remains limited [16,17]. As cardiovascular care improves and life expectancy increases, complex operations in high-risk patients, including reoperations, are becoming more common [18]. Thus, it is important to re-evaluate the outcomes of concomitant AF ablation in reoperative cardiac surgery. Only a few studies have reported on the efficacy of the Cox-maze procedure in this setting [8,10]. For example, Kobayashi et al. [8] examined 42 patients undergoing redo mitral valve surgery with or without the Cox-maze procedure. They found no significant difference in chest tube drainage or transfusion between groups; however, the study was outdated and limited to early postoperative outcomes, providing insufficient evidence on long-term efficacy. Similarly, Stulak et al. [10] reported results from 245 patients with prior cardiac surgery who underwent concomitant AF ablation. However, the primary diagnoses in that cohort were congenital conditions such as Ebstein anomaly and Tetralogy of Fallot, which differ in AF pathogenesis compared with valvular disease. Furthermore, the ablation techniques in that study were not standardized. Other studies included only a small proportion of patients with previous cardiac surgery, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions regarding the long-term benefits of ablation in redo cases [2,6].

We previously reported on the impact of concomitant surgical ablation in redo cardiac surgery [9]; however, that work was limited by a relatively small sample size, single- center design, and less rigorous statistical adjustment. The present study addressed these shortcomings by analyzing a larger and more diverse cohort from 2 high-volume tertiary centers and employing IPTW to minimize confounding bias. These methodological improvements provide a more comprehensive and generalizable assessment of long-term outcomes. Building upon our prior findings, this study more thoroughly evaluated the effectiveness of concomitant AF ablation in patients undergoing redo surgery for left-sided valvular heart disease.

Reoperative cardiac surgery is recognized as being associated with increased perioperative morbidity and mortality [19,20]. In our cohort, early clinical outcomes were comparable between the Ablation and no-ablation groups, with the exception of postoperative stroke, which occurred more frequently in the no-ablation group. Beyond perioperative outcomes, our study demonstrated that concomitant AF ablation was associated with improved long-term survival, lower recurrence of AF, and fewer thromboembolic events. These findings suggest that concomitant ablation should be more actively considered in reoperative cases.

Regarding AF recurrence, the cumulative incidence in this study was higher than that reported in studies of patients undergoing primary cardiac surgery [13,16]. Restoration of sinus rhythm is generally less frequent after reoperative surgery [8]. Because sinus rhythm is critical for reducing thromboembolic events, a lower conversion rate could be concerning. Nevertheless, our data demonstrated that surgical ablation was still associated with a reduced incidence of thromboembolic events. Consistent with previous reports that AF increases mortality in the years following surgery [21,22], our findings suggest that maintenance of sinus rhythm contributes to improved long-term survival.

Interestingly, more than 10% of patients in the no-ablation group exhibited sinus rhythm at final follow-up. This may be explained by postoperative initiation of antiarrhythmic therapy in patients who had not previously been on appropriate rhythm control medications. Although not part of the surgical strategy, these medical interventions may have contributed to sinus rhythm restoration in the absence of ablation.

Limitations

The present study had several limitations. First, the study was retrospective in design. Second, AF recurrence was defined as AF detected by ECG, which provides only a snapshot and may underestimate the true recurrence rate. Third, the decision to perform surgical ablation during redo surgery was left to the discretion of the operating surgeon. Although IPTW was applied to adjust for observed confounders, unmeasured variables such as surgical proficiency and case selection may still have contributed to residual bias. The inclusion of patients undergoing various combinations of valve operations may also have acted as an additional confounding factor. Because all surgeons in this study were highly experienced and practiced in high-volume centers, the shorter ACC time observed in the ablation group likely reflected greater surgical complexity in the no-ablation group rather than differences in surgeon skill. In addition, postoperative junctional rhythm may have been misclassified when assessing freedom from AF recurrence, as it can encompass AF with block. This nuance should be considered when interpreting our findings. Furthermore, AF recurrence was analyzed using Kaplan- Meier estimates based on time to first recurrence; this approach is consistent with prior studies, but does not account for competing risks such as death or for multiple recurrent episodes. Thus, our results should be interpreted in light of these methodological constraints, and future investigations using competing risk models or recurrent event analyses may provide additional insights. Finally, although a mixed-effects model could have addressed patient-level variability, it was not applied due to the lack of structured longitudinal rhythm data, which limited the suitability of random effects modeling.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that concomitant AF ablation during redo cardiac surgery for left-sided heart disease was associated with improved overall survival, better maintenance of sinus rhythm, and fewer thromboembolic events. Concomitant surgical ablation should therefore be considered for appropriately selected patients undergoing redo cardiac surgery.
