Mitochondrial DNA common deletion in cancer: Emerging evidence and methodological challenges
Authors: Mun Kay Ho, Joshua W.K. Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The 4977 bp common deletion of the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA-CD) is a large-scale deletion that has been frequently observed in various cancers in humans. The common deletion mutation affects 7 key genes encoding for several complex subunits essential to the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation system. In the last 20 years, the evidence for the presence of mtDNA-CD in cancer has been elusive. This review aims to examine the evidence of the molecular mechanism and prevalence of mtDNA-CD in different cancers, and to discuss methodological challenges in detecting the presence of mtDNA-CD from clinical samples, and the estimation of prevalence in cancers. This is an attempt to perform a comprehensive literature review and evaluation of experimental evidence to determine whether there is sufficient evidence to suggest a link between mtDNA-CD and cancer. Our results suggest there is a lack of evidence across all cancers. Source of potential discrepancies depends on the quality of the experimental methods, statistical analyses and sampling strategy. Emerging evidence indicates the presence of mtDNA-CD may be cell-type- and cancer-specific. With advances in experimental and analytical techniques, such as single cell genomic and transcriptomic sequencing, spatial omics profiling, and long-read sequencing, we expect to see more comprehensive research that can better clarify the role and prevalence of mtDNA-CD in cancer.

Mutations in mtDNA

Characteristics of mtDNA

Eukaryotic cells contain two complete sets of genomes, namely the nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The mitochondria are thought to have originated from an alpha-proteobacterium via endosymbiosis [1]. In humans, the mitochondrial genome is encoded in a 16,569 bp circular DNA. The coding region of the mtDNA contains 37 genes that encode 13 mitochondrial peptides, which are important components of the oxidative phosphorylation system. These peptides form the subunits of complex I, III, IV and V, as well as 22 transfer RNAs (tRNA) and 2 ribosomal RNAs (rRNA) [2]. The RNAs are key components required for mitochondrial protein synthesis.

Within the circular mtDNA, there is a noncoding region, commonly referred to as the displacement loop (D-loop). The D-loop spans 1124 base pairs and covers two hypervariable regions with HV1 at 16,024–16,383 and HV2 at 57–372 [3]. In the D-loop region, promoters for both H and L strands are found and serve as key elements for initiating mtDNA transcription and replication. The D-loop is also a common location for mtDNA mutations.

The double-membrane mitochondrion is an important organelle that regulates oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), generates free radicals, mediates apoptosis, and controls metabolic pathways [4]. Arguably, the most important function of the mitochondria is the OXPHOS system, embedded in the lipid bilayer of the inner mitochondrial membrane. This system produces ATP, the primary energy source for cellular activities. The OXPHOS system mainly consists of complexes I to V, coenzyme Q and cytochrome c, all of which function as electron carriers. Together, these components drive electron and proton transfer, generating a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane that powers ATP synthesis [5]. Only a small portion of the mitochondrial-generated ATP is reserved for the use of the organelle itself; the majority is exported by the adenine nucleotide translocator for cellular functions [6]. Additionally, that leak from the respiratory chain at early stages contributes to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [2].

There are features of mtDNA that differ from nuclear DNA, such as high copy number, high mutation rate and heteroplasmy. Heteroplasmy is a condition in which both wild-type and mutated mitochondrial genomes coexist in the same cell. This is unique to mtDNA, which exists in multiple copies per cell. When cells carrying heteroplasmic mtDNA undergo cell division, mtDNA molecules are randomly segregated into daughter cells. As a result, the proportions of mutant and wild type mtDNA may fluctuate over time, causing genetic drift. This can eventually lead to homoplasmy, where cells contain only mutant or only wild-type mtDNA.

Another distinguishing feature of mtDNA is its high copy number. Since several copies exist per cell, a threshold level of mutant genomes is generally required before cellular function is impaired. The threshold is not a set value but varies depending on tissue type, disease context, and mutation effects. It has also been shown that mutation type influences the threshold. A threshold value of 50–60 % mutated mtDNA has been observed for deletions, whereas point mutations generally require a higher level to cause cellular dysfunction [7]. Therefore, to successfully apply mtDNA as a biomarker in disease progression, it is important to evaluate both genotype–phenotype relationships and heteroplasmy levels in affected tissues [8].

mtDNA is present at high copy numbers within the mitochondria of eukaryotic cells. The mtDNA copy number appears to vary substantially with the tissue or cellular origin [9]. Since mtDNA replication is independent of the nuclear cell cycle, mtDNA copy number cannot be inferred from cell cycle stage [10]. It has been suggested that the copy number of mtDNA is regulated by internal and external cellular conditions, such as oxygen concentration and hormonal influences [4]. Recent research indicates that mtDNA copy number may serve as a prognostic marker in glioma patients [11].

Types of mutations in the mtDNA

A high mutation rate is another notable feature of the mitochondrial genome. The mutation rate of mtDNA is estimated to be 10–200 times higher than that of nuclear DNA [12]. This high mutation rate is presumed to be driven primarily by the absence of histone protection, as mtDNA is compacted into nucleoids [10]. In addition, the limited efficiency of mtDNA proofreading and repair, the absence of introns, and the high concentration of ROS in the inner mitochondrial membrane are thought to further contribute to this elevated mutation rate.

Ju et al. conducted a study to examine the potential effects of mtDNA mutations on oncogenesis and clonal selection. Results from 1675 matched cancer and healthy mtDNA samples across 31 cancer types show that there is no survival benefit for cancer cells carrying mtDNA mutations [13]. Yet, this study was limited by sample size. Furthermore, only point mutations were assessed; structural variants and mtDNA copy number were not included.

During replication, mtDNA may acquire somatic mutations that are not inherited but arise spontaneously. The cause of such spontaneous mutations remains unclear but is thought to involve external factors such as elevated ROS, smoking, and UV exposure. Furthermore, the association of BRCA1/2 gene mutations with breast cancer is well established, yet no clear link has been demonstrated with the mitochondrial genome[14]. Thus, mtDNA mutational mechanisms are proposed to differ substantially from those of nuclear DNA. Moreover, the mtDNA mutations appear to arise predominantly from endogenous mitochondrial processes rather than external environmental exposures [13].

The first study to report physical alterations in mtDNA was published in 1967, when mtDNA from leukemic myeloid cells was observed in oligomeric form using electron microscopy [15]. Most studies in the early stages of mtDNA mutation focused on the identification of somatic point mutations specifically. For instance, a study performed by Abu-Amero et al. identified point mutations in the human thyroid carcinoma cells and concluded that mitochondrial genome mutations play a critical role in early oncogenesis [16]. In all, cancer research concentrating on the study of point mutations indicates that the mutations are frequently found in the D-loop area and are homoplasmic in nature. The D-loop region of the mitochondrial genome is a well-known hotspot for mutations. According to Yu et al., homoplasmic mtDNA mutations emerge during the neoplastic transition stage in cancer cells [4].

According to Krishnan et al., deletions in mtDNA are implicated in sporadic mitochondrial diseases. Furthermore, mtDNA deletions are commonly observed in diseased muscle and central nervous system tissues. Moreover, deletion mutations are often detected in mtDNA from aged tissue and tissues affected by neurological disorders [8]. Deletions in mtDNA can be further classified into small- or large-scale types, with small deletions typically < 1 kb. mtDNA deletions often occur in the primary arc between the OH and OL replication origins, and approximately 85 % are flanked by short repeats [17]. After comparing the mtDNA deletion profile of individuals affected by different diseases such as Parkinson’s disease with those of aged but otherwise healthy individuals, the authors reported similarities in deletion size and in the short repeats flanking the deleted regions [8]. This, in turn, suggests a shared underlying mechanism for mtDNA deletions across diverse pathological contexts.

Common deletion of the mtDNA

Among the mtDNA deletions found in various diseases and cancer types, a 4977 bp deletion occurs frequently between positions 8470 and 13,447 (Fig. 1). This deletion, flanked by two 13 bp direct repeats, is known as a "common deletion" (CD) because it is implicated in a wide variety of sporadic disorders. The 4977 bp long CD has been identified as the causative mutation for Pearson’s syndrome, chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO) and Kearns-Sayre syndrome. Additionally, several studies highlight the role of mtDNA deletion as a biomarker for mtDNA damage, as it accumulates in aged tissue [2]. CD encompasses 12 essential genes encoding even OXPHOS subunits and five tRNAs. The loss of the genomic region results in mitochondrial dysfunction due to impaired energy production and dysregulated ROS generation. This is caused by the complete loss of ND3, ND4, ND4L, COX III, and ATPase6, together with partial loss of ND5 and ATPase8. In contrast to mtDNA point mutations, Wallace and Chalkia found that the CD is heteroplasmic and produces clinical symptoms when the proportion of affected mtDNA exceeds the bioenergetic threshold in metabolic diseases such as mitochondrial encephalopathy (MELAS) [19].

CD was first discovered in 1994 in skin squamous cell carcinoma and renal oncocytic tumours where it was initially linked to cancer pathology [20], [21]. Recent investigations have also detected CD in breast and colorectal cancer samples, shedding light on the association of mtDNA mutation and oncogenesis [2]. In comparison to healthy samples, CD is more abundant in tumour samples. Based on this finding, it is suggested that CD contributes to a selective survival advantage in the clonal expansion of cancer cells. Despite limited understanding of the molecular mechanism by which CD contributes to cancer progression, this discovery emphasises the potential of mtDNA CD as a biomarker for cancer development and progression [4]. For example, through manipulation of the LNCaP cell line, it was demonstrated that mtDNA deletion drives the transition to an androgen-independent prostate cancer phenotype [22]. This emphasises the necessity of gaining a better understanding of the function of mtDNA CD in cancer development and pathogenesis.

To gain insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the mtDNA deletion, Phillips et al. investigated the involvement of slipped-strand replication error and double-strand break (DSB) in CD formation. By simultaneously employing a DNA combining assay to characterise mtDNA replication mechanisms and inducing DSB through adopting mito-TALENs (mitochondrially targeted transcription activator-like effector nucleases), the authors identified a DSB repair mechanism linked to mitochondrial replication. Preliminary findings indicated that the mitochondrial replisome mediates mtDNA break repair in a replication-dependent manner. The results of this study suggest that by inducing DSB using mito-TALENs, replication fork stalling promotes the mispairing of the 13 bp repeats flanking the CD (Fig. 1). This, in turn, exposes extended regions of single-stranded DNA on the heavy strand of mtDNA [23]. This replication-dependent repair pathway currently provides the most compelling model for CD formation in mtDNA [18].

CD is cell-type specific in different cancer types

The role of CD-mtDNA as a biomarker in disease diagnosis and prognosis tracking is well established. In a recent study of coronary artery diseases conducted on an Italian population, CD-mtDNA was found to be a viable biomarker for patients experiencing adverse cardiac events [24]. CD-mtDNA was shown to predict cardiovascular events more sensitively than traditional biomarkers like troponin T and N-terminal pro-BNP. Furthermore, CD-mtDNA was identified as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events. In addition, mtDNA mutations have been linked to illness severity, as reported by Zhang et al., who found a greater proportion of CD-mtDNA in blood samples from individuals with mitochondrial-associated disorders [25]. It is thus thought that CD-mtDNA may serve as biomarkers in cancer progression. This proposal is supported by the notion of discovering mtDNA deletion signatures in most identified tumour and cancer samples (Table 1 and Fig. 2) [26]. Functional effects on mitochondrial biogenesis have been found as a result of such mtDNA alterations; specifically, mutations in the cytochrome C gene may disrupt the normal progression of programmed cell death [27]. With the premise that mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to cancer formation, various studies have investigated the associated molecular pathways. An often-observed biochemical change occurring in cancer cells is the increase in glucose uptake and lactate secretion. Given the reported low respiratory rate in cancer cells, it is hypothesised that cancer cells rely significantly on the aerobic glycolysis pathway to create ATP molecules. However, healthy cells with a fully functional mitochondrial OXPHOS system would not require the employment of an alternate metabolic mechanism. When mitochondrial respiration and ATP generation decline due to accumulated mtDNA damage beyond a critical threshold, cells adopt survival strategies by switching to alternative ATP-producing pathways. This phenomenon has since been named the Warburg effect [28]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that defective OXPHOS systems generate an excessive amount of ROS, facilitating the development of cancer cells [29]. This has been observed in bladder cancer samples, where Cytb deletion induced high levels of ROS and lactate production [30]. The increased cancer growth in the xenograft model demonstrates the relationship between mtDNA damage-induced ROS overproduction and the enhanced Warburg effect in oncogenesis.

First proposed in 1997, the use of synthetic peptide nucleic acids (PNAs) designed to be complementary to human mtDNA carrying the CD was proposed to inhibit the replication of CD-mtDNA [31]. This established the notion of generating personalized treatment by targeting cancer cell energy metabolism, particularly the mitochondrial OXPHOS pathway. Recent research has promoted the inclusion of tumour bioenergetics as a hallmark of cancer [32]. Most previous cancer treatments relied heavily on the development of drugs as targeted therapies, such as imatinib (drug targeting kinase) and Herceptin [32]. However, there are many unintended side effects from clinically prescribed targeted therapy drugs. Thus, research has shifted to the inhibition of components involved in energy generation pathways in cancer cells. Yet, due to the complexities and largely unknown mechanisms behind the dysregulation of the mtDNA OXPHOS system in cancer cells, drug development has faced significant challenges. As a result, it is critical to understand the biological processes that control CD-mtDNA formation and its function in tumour growth.

Breast cancer

A study conducted at Georgetown University Medical Centre [33] used the forward and reverse primer sets (mtF8295 and mtR13738) found no evidence of common deletion (CD) in breast cancer samples. Similar negative findings were reported in Turkish, Indian, and Iraqi patient cohorts despite the use of highly sensitive PCR and nested PCR approaches [40], [41], [44]. These studies suggested that mtDNA-CD in breast cancer may largely represent passenger mutations with minimal role in carcinogenesis.

According to the study conducted by Zhu et al., they identified the 4977 bp mtDNA-CD in both normal and breast cancer samples obtained from matched patient cohorts [35]. Other deletions such as 3938 bp and 4388 bp were less consistent, whereas a 4576 bp deletion was present in 77 % of breast cancer samples but only 13 % of adjacent normal tissue. Importantly, these deletions were absent in healthy controls. A separate study comparing breast cancer, benign breast disease (BBD), and healthy individuals also reported significantly higher mtDNA-CD in blood samples from breast cancer patients, supporting its potential as a circulating biomarker [43]. The reason for the identification in blood samples was postulated to be related to the high levels of oxidative stress in tumour microenvironments. Carcinogenesis is thought to be activated when internal cellular defences are overcome by oxidative stress levels, resulting in the buildup of oxidative damage in the mitochondrial genome, which manifests as CD. When the CD threshold is reached, apoptosis occurs and genetic content is released into the circulation, which the study suggests could be utilised as an effective diagnostic for breast cancer prognosis.

Several studies also reported lower CD levels in tumour samples compared with adjacent tissue, suggesting that CD may be selected against in proliferating cancer cells [34], [36], [37]. Supporting this, cybrid models demonstrated that even low heteroplasmy of CD increased apoptosis sensitivity, consistent with a selective disadvantage in tumour growth. Consequently, CD has generally been considered a passenger rather than a driver mutation in breast cancer [37].

mtDNA-CD was also studied in association with oxidative damage markers. For example, the NQO1 C609T polymorphism, which reduces antioxidant function, was linked to higher CD levels in breast cancer [39]. Estrogen receptor (ER) status was also associated with CD frequency in Vietnamese patients, potentially via ER–p53 interactions that compromise mtDNA genome integrity [42].

Gastric cancer

Maximo et al. first reported that mtDNA-CD was absent in gastric cancer samples [45]. The 4977 bp mtDNA-CD was assessed in 32 tumours and adjacent tissues, and its presence correlated with other mtDNA mutations (5/7 cases with ≥2 mutations). Cells harbouring CD showed increased apoptotic sensitivity, whereas point mutations alone did not alter apoptosis. The authors further suggested an inverse relationship between CD and MSI status, proposing that CD sets an apoptotic threshold, while MSI may act as a secondary driver of dysfunction and cell death [45].

A Chinese cohort reported a high CD frequency in gastric cancers (79.6 %) compared to adjacent healthy tissue (67.6 %) [49]. They linked low MnSOD expression and elevated ROS with higher CD levels, suggesting that reduced antioxidative capacity promotes oxidative damage and CD accumulation.

In contrast, Wu et al. found a low mtDNA-CD frequency in tumours (3 out of 31 gastric cancer samples, 10 %) but higher in adjacent tissues (17 out of 31 adjacent stomach epithelial tissue samples) [46]. No association was observed with D-loop point mutations. Similarly, in an Iranian cohort, CD was present in only 5.6 % of cancers vs. 16.8 % of adjacent tissue [47]. Only 5.6 % (6 out of 107) of gastric cancer samples had CD but was identified in 16.8 % (18 out of 107) adjacent healthy tissue samples. The authors attributed this to a dilution effect from high proliferative pressure in tumours, with no correlation to age, sex, tumour size, or location.

Separately, Kamalidehghan et al. identified a novel 8.9 kb deletion in 12.7 % of gastric cancers (9/71), more frequent in intestinal-type vs. diffuse-type cancers [48]. A correlation was proposed between the patient’s age and the observed occurrence of the 8.9 kb deletion in the gastric cancer sample. In addition to this, the 8.9 kb deletion was found more commonly in intestinal-type gastric cancers when compared against diffuse-type gastric cancer. This suggests distinct deletion signatures across gastric cancer subtypes with potentially different pathogenic mechanisms.

Colorectal cancer

In the Turkish cohort, a study using matched primary colorectal tumour samples with adjacent non-neoplastic tissue samples [40] reported no mtDNA-CD in 25 tumour samples using nested PCR, suggesting rarity and lack of association with colorectal cancer.

However, in a Brazilian cohort, a higher mtDNA-CD frequency was detected in non-tumoural samples [34], attributed to lower selection pressure compared with tumour cells. The results of this study were supported by a subsequent study conducted using the nested PCR method in 104 colorectal cancer patients [50]. Contrary to the age-dependent accumulation model, the 4977 bp mtDNA-CD was found to be more common in patients under the age of 65 (12 out of 48) at 25 % occurrence than those over the age of 25 at 8.9 % (5 out of 56). In addition to this, an inverse correlation was also noted between mtDNA copy number and deletion presence. The authors proposed that CD arises in early cancer through retrograde signalling promoting carcinogenesis, but declines in advanced tumours where intact respiratory function provides survival advantage.

Ethnicity may also influence CD levels. Dimberg et al. reported differences between Swedish and Vietnamese colorectal cancer cohorts [51]. In the colorectal cancer tissue samples from the Swedish cohort, 67.6 % were found to contain the CD mutation (71 out of 105). In contrast to the cancer samples from the Vietnamese cohort with a higher occurrence of 80.7 % (71 out of 88). However, it is noteworthy that despite the difference between the ethnic groups, non-tumoural tissues showed higher CD prevalence (Swedish at 92.4 %, Vietnamese at 92 %), with overall levels significantly higher in Vietnamese patients.

From mesenteric arteries of 27 colorectal tumour resections, a novel 4408 bp mtDNA deletion was identified [73]. This showed oxidative damage in both tumours and neighbouring vessels. The 4977 bp CD was enriched in controls, while the 4408 bp deletion showed no tissue-specific enrichment. The 4977 bp CD was linked to a 13 bp repeat in ATP8 and ND5, absent in the 4408 bp junctions, implying distinct mechanisms. Furthermore, Skonieczna et al. also reported two large-scale deletions (∼bp 3418–3432 and 10,941–10,951) in colorectal cancers [53].

Oesophageal cancer

Early studies reported by Abnet et al. identified a high occurrence of mtDNA-CD in both cancer and adjacent normal tissue. In the 21-patient ESCC cohort, CD mutations were detected in 89 % of tumour samples (17/19) and in 95 % of adjacent normal tissues (19/20) [54]. This high incidence of CD in the ESCC samples led the authors to propose CD alteration as a potential biomarker. Moreover, the lack of CD in the corresponding peripheral blood led to the speculation that external factors are contributing to the increased mutation rate in the oesophageal tissue. In another study, mtDNA-CD was again detected in both tumour and adjacent normal samples, but at a markedly lower frequency. Upadhyay et al. analysed 39 oesophageal cancer samples with matched adjacent normal tissues and peripheral blood [55]. Among these, only two tumour samples harboured the CD mutation, while one adjacent normal sample was positive. This discrepancy with previous studies pointing towards mtDNA-CD as a prognostic biomarker led the authors to hypothesise that the significant difference in detection rates may reflect a population-specific mtDNA genomic alteration. Notably, this study was conducted in a Northern Indian population.

In contrast to this, Keles et al. reported that the mtDNA-CD was absent in ESCC and EA samples [57]. Their study, designed to detect both the 4977 bp CD and a novel 7.4 kb deletion, found neither. As a result, the authors proposed that the large-scale deletions do not play pivotal roles in the prognosis of ESCC and EA.

Using two primer pairs and nested PCR methodology, mtDNA-CD was investigated in 41 ESCC patients from an Indian cohort [58]. A significant 80 % (33/41) of tumour samples harboured the CD mutation. Moreover, no CD alterations were identified in the corresponding healthy blood samples. Another study further reported that higher CD mutation burden correlated with increasing dysplasia severity [56]. However, these findings suggested that while mtDNA-CD may serve as a biomarker for dysplasia severity, it is not indicative of EA presence.

Oral cancer

In a study to establish the association between oral cancer and the habit of chewing betel quid, it was reported that 2 out of 18 tumour samples harboured the mtDNA-CD mutation, while 3 adjacent normal samples from the same cohort contained the mutation [60]. This finding corroborates earlier reports, which suggest a lower frequency of deletion mutations in tumour samples compared to adjacent normal tissues. To further explore the occurrence of CD in oral cancer, real-time PCR analysis was conducted on samples obtained from 12 individuals [61]. The prevalence of CD mutations in precancerous lesions (0.367 %) was higher than in oral cancer samples (0.137 %). To reduce the influence of external factors such as age and genetics, the authors established a baseline CD mutation level in each patient’s peripheral lymphocytes, enabling calculation of the relative fold change in ROS expression. Based on these results, the authors proposed that CD mutations may serve as a biomarker for the early detection of oral precancer, but not necessarily for established oral cancer.

In a separate Indian cohort, mtDNA-CD mutations were identified in a significantly higher proportion of cancer patients (42 out of 50) compared to healthy controls (18 out of 50) [62]. The study suggests an association between CYP2E1 polymorphism and CD mutations, implicating a potential combined role in increasing the risk of oral cancer development.

Conversely, an earlier study by Lee et al. in a Taiwanese population reported a lower occurrence of CD mutations in cancer samples (26 out of 53) compared with adjacent normal tissues (36 out of 53). This discrepancy may be partly due to the novel semi-quantitative PCR method developed by the group, in which DNA samples were subjected to two-fold serial dilution with distilled water before amplification. Despite these findings, the study still supported the hypothesis proposed by Tan et al. [60] regarding betel quid chewing as a key risk factor for oral cancer. A later Indian cohort study also confirmed this pattern, reporting significantly lower levels of CD mutations in oral cancer samples compared to adjacent normal tissues (9.8-fold vs. 10.5-fold, respectively) [63].

Thyroid cancer

An early study conducted by Maximo et al. focused on histologically distinct Hurthle cell carcinoma subtypes in thyroid cancer. Although mtDNA-CD was identified via semi-quantitative PCR in all samples (43 out of 43), they further analysed its presence in each histological type. mtDNA-CD was significantly associated with Hurthle cell carcinoma (P < 0.001) [64]. The mutation occurred more frequently in Hürthle cell follicular carcinomas than in Hurthle cell adenomas and papillary carcinomas (P < 0.0001 and P = 0.0012). In adjacent normal tissue, low-level mtDNA-CD was also detected (12 out of 41). The authors noted that mtDNA changes in Hurthle cell carcinoma mimic mitochondrial proliferation and structural abnormalities seen in mitochondrial encephalomyopathy (MELAS). They hypothesised that mtDNA-CD reflects the D-loop’s susceptibility to deletions, supported by 81.5 % of observed point mutations being transitions. They proposed that CD-induced ATP reduction drives compensatory mitochondrial proliferation. Savagner et al. further suggested that increased uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) expression represents an adaptive response to OXPHOS defects [74]. A Turkish cohort study also identified mtDNA-CD in thyroid cancer [40] with 4/50 tumour samples and 2 adjacent normal samples positive. Notably, CD was only detectable with nested PCR, not conventional PCR.

Conti et al. reported CD in 25 % of thyroid cancer samples (2/8) [75]. However, CD was also observed in 70 % of paired adjacent normal parenchyma, at lower Ct values (30.41 and 28.7, p = 0.05). Mutation frequency also differed by infarction status: 25 % in infarcted tumours (2/8) vs. 73 % in non-infarcted tumours (8/11). The authors speculated that CD may confer a protective effect on tumour cell viability by enhancing oxidative stress tolerance. In agreement with prior studies, they suggested that CD imposes a selective survival disadvantage in rapidly growing cancer cells. Since CD occurred in both tumour and adjacent tissue, it may arise prior to thyroid carcinogenesis.

Hepatocellular carcinoma

Employing an in-house real-time PCR protocol to quantify mtDNA in HCC and hepatocellular nodular hyperplasia (HNH) samples, Shao et al. first reported a novel 4981 bp deletion, a subtype of the 4977 bp common deletion, with breakpoints at positions 8470 and 13,450 bp [67] were proposed. The authors suggested the deletion occurs during hepatocyte transformation and accumulated in mtDNA. No correlation was found with age, cancer stage or HBV infection. In a separate study using nested PCR, the 4977 bp mtDNA-CD was detected in 9.52 % of HCC samples (10 out of 105) [70] and absent in adjacent normal tissue samples, supporting its potential as a clinical HCC biomarker. The authors proposed oxidative damage as a mechanism for CD occurrence.

Early studies found mtDNA-CD in 65.5 % of HCC-adjacent normal samples (36 out of 55) but only in 7/28 HCC samples [65]. Another study reported CD in 28 % of HCC samples and 95 % of adjacent normal tissue, suggesting a selection disadvantage of CD-harbouring cells in proliferative HCC tissue [68]. Gwak et al. similarly reported a lower prevalence of CD in HCC than in non-neoplastic tissues [69].

Follow-up research indicated that mtDNA-CD proportion may be sex-dependent, with lower mean CD in females (0.0035) than males (0.0104) [66]. Additionally, chronic alcohol consumption was associated with increased levels of CD and reduced mtDNA copy number, suggesting ethanol-induced hepatic oxidative stress contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and CD formation.

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma

A novel mtDNA-CD that is 4981 bp in length was first identified in a study conducted in Guangzhou using paired healthy and NPC cancer samples [72]. The increased frequency of mtDNA-CD identified in cancer samples when compared to matched nasopharyngitis samples and white blood cells suggests that cancer cells have a highly dynamic mtDNA genome. Furthermore, it supports the hypothesis that CD accumulates in cancer cells to perpetuate neoplastic transformation. This is further supported by the observation of 3 times more CD found in late-stage NPC (stages III and IV). Additionally, analysis suggests that age is also a contributing factor to accumulated CD in NPC. Furthermore, Pang et al. discovered that CD was abundant in NPC samples from irradiation therapy patients [76]. However, as no biopsy was conducted before the irradiation therapy, it is impossible to ascertain if deletion mutation was present before the irradiation treatment prescribed to the patient.

Methodological challenges in the detection of CD

As discussed in the previous section, the evidence for the presence of mtDNA-CD is quite heterogeneous. We identified and categorised several challenges that limit the empirical detection and quantification of mtDNA-CD from human clinical samples. Sampling limitations

Small sample sizes

Small sample sizes are a common issue faced by multiple studies in the identification of CD in cancer patients [42], [55]. This is partly due to the need for acquiring matching adjacent cancer samples for analysis comparison and acting as an internal control. Without matching cancer and normal samples, it is difficult to determine if the CD mutation signature is specific to either cell type cohort. Nie et al. was faced the issue of insufficient sample size, resulting in being unable to conduct an in-depth subgroup analysis [77].

Ethnic diversity

In the study of breast cancer conducted by Tseng et al., the authors noted a difference in breast cancer diagnosis occurrences in the Taiwanese population compared to the Western population. They described the difference in the C609T polymorphism in the NQO1 enzyme reported in 68.3 % of the Chinese cohort against the 26 % of the Caucasian descent [39]. This difference between the Caucasian population and the Asian population was also observed in the incidence of oral cancer [62]. In their hypothesis of correlation of genetic polymorphism in the CYP2E1 gene, the authors noted that Caucasian populations have notably displayed low levels of CYP2E1 expression with the heterozygous c1c2 genotype. Thus, leading to a significant correlation to oral cancer.

For colorectal cancer, a significantly increased incidence of the mtDNA-CD was reported in the Vietnamese patient cohort than in the Swedish cohort [51]. This is interesting because the authors note that the results identified in the Vietnamese patient population are like the findings previously identified in the Chinese population. However, in research conducted on oesophageal cancer, notable differences were identified when comparing different Asian populations. Upadhyay et al. Reported a significant difference in the number of CD incidences in the Chinese population when compared to a Northern Indian population using oesophageal cancer samples [55]. A similar difference was seen in a subsequent study conducted by Keles et al. in oesophageal carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma [57].

Within-sample heterogeneity

Cancer stages play a vital role in determining mutational signatures. This was proven in a study conducted by Ju et al., in which mutations specific to metastatic tumour clonal samples were identified. Thus, leading to the suggestion that the mtDNA changes are dynamic and will continue to evolve throughout the tumour progression.

Van Trappen et al. reported a similar result in their samples acquired from ovarian cancer patients. The authors reported that taking multiple biopsy samples of the same tumour site from the same patient would result in the identification of various mtDNA mutations. A possible mechanism causing this difference is that two primary tumours arose from the same population of primary cells, or there was an incidence of clonal expansion in the tumour cell population [27]. This study highlights the importance of longitudinal studies in cancer profiling to be able to differentiate between driver and passenger mutations in drug target identification.

Understanding that many types of mutations occur within the mitochondrial genome is thus important in the step of selecting mutations for analysis. It is observed that the aggressive filtering of different mutations can result in a high number of false positive results identifications thus leading to misinterpretation of the biological significance [78]. Furthermore, this argument is supported by biological factors affecting the presence of large-scale deletions. For example, it has been pointed out that smaller genomes have a selectively higher replication advantage. Therefore, small-scale deletions will not appear to accumulate, thus leading to the development of a false sense of mutational selection in the tumour microenvironment [35].

The difficulties in the analysis are further complicated by the observation of a lack of association between point mutations, mtDNA copy number changes and large-scale deletions. This leads to the hypothesis that different mechanisms are driving the occurrences and physiological effects of such mutations on cellular functionality [46].

Furthermore, the decision of selecting different sites for biopsy collection can also impact the observed results. In different studies, various samples have been used as a positive internal control for the discovery of mutations in the cancer sample. This includes obtaining tumour-adjacent normal tissues and matched healthy patient samples. However, in several studies, blood samples were taken and used as healthy controls [34]. Moreover, in a colorectal cancer study conducted by Li et al., samples obtained from mesenteric arteries were taken and used as an internal control for mutation profiling. The authors hypothesised that the blood vessels surrounding the cancer site might play a role in supporting cancer proliferation and growth [52].

Moreover, the cellular integrity of the biopsy samples are also an important factor. Fine needle aspiration is a common procedure used to diagnose thyroid nodules; however, it is also found to cause histopathological changes in thyroid cancer cells [75]. The expected histological differences after the FNA procedure may result in difficulties in diagnosis subsequently. It is suggested that this will lead to complications in the identification of tumour type due to infarction situations. Thus, leading to results that cannot be interpreted in the study of mutation profiling. The accurate identification of biopsy samples histologically is important as the subsequent samples will be used in the identification of CD mutations.

Experimental challenges

Limitations of traditional methods

Maximo et al. first suggested it in their discovery of CD in gastric cancer samples which were previously not found. The authors of the paper suggested the difference in the technical method employed in the identification of CD in their protocol yielded higher sensitivity and specificity, thus contributing to the positive results acquired in their study [45]. This is supported by the results reported by Areal et al., in the study of thyroid cancer in a Turkish cohort, where the results of six samples were found to be associated with CD when the nested PCR method was used. In their early result, conventional PCR was employed, and no CD was discovered within the same samples [40].

Adding on to this argument, it was further proposed that the quantitative real-time PCR procedure has a significantly higher sensitivity than Southern blot analysis in the identification of CD in mtDNA [34]. The dire need for quantifiable identification of CD was also supported by Ye et al. in their suggestion for consolidating the conflicting results reported by distinct groups on the presence of CD in the same cancer type. They suggested that the limitations and inconsistencies of non-quantitative methodologies will hinder the analysis of the cancer samples and thus lead to inconclusive results.

Sample preparation limitations

Aral et al. also further suggested that the results that are seen as inconsistent in similar cancer types can be attributed to the laboratory techniques chosen for sample preparation [40]. For example, the samples might be fresh frozen tissue or formalin-fixed.

In the study conducted by Shieh et al., they proposed that the use of both the laser micro-dissection technique and real-time quantitative PCR methodology would most appropriately result in the identification of CD. According to the results released in their paper, this method allows for reproducible and precise quantitative measurement of the occurrence of CD in formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue samples [61]. By identifying pure cell populations from the patient tissue sample through molecular methods, there is significantly less undesired cell type population in the sample analysed. This is important as a homogenised tissue sample containing multiple cell types or undesired cell types can result in confusing outcomes for downstream analysis. Furthermore, they suggested applying a longitudinal study in CD genotyping to allow for the quantification of the CD occurrences into relative fold change values for better comparison and analysis.

Tissue degradation and contamination

In a study comparing tumour samples against adjacent normal tissue samples, the difference in mtDNA-CD concentrations identified raised the possibility of tissue contamination when obtaining the biopsy [34]. This question was raised because, in subsequent repeated findings, the authors identified lower CD concentration in adjacent normal tissue samples prepared using micro-dissection methods. Similar concerns were raised by several other independent studies [36], [46]. A consensus is observed that micro-dissection of the tumour sample is the current most effective method in reducing tissue contamination. However, further research is needed to establish this.

In addition to this, tissue degradation was suggested as a plausible reason for the identification of CD in tumour samples. A possible strategy in mitigating the effects of tissue degradation is to employ a preliminary check by using PCR to amplify a region not associated with the deletion in the mtDNA genome to ensure no tissue degradation has occurred [35].

Sequencing challenges

Using pre-existing primers on novel deletions

In most research conducted to identify CD in the mitochondrial genome of various cancers, the authors elected to use primers already present and available. This is reasonable within the criteria that the common deletion is expected to be found within the cancer sample or the adjacent normal sample. However, in several studies, novel deletions have been identified, thus making it unreasonable to use already present primers targeted to only specific breakpoints in the known 4977 bp common deletion.

Two novel large-scale deletions were first reported by Zhu et al. in breast cancer samples. The identified 3938 and 4388 bp deletions were found to be of higher prevalence in cancer samples than their matched adjacent normal samples [35]. Following this, another novel deletion was also discovered in colorectal cancer tissue, located around the 3418 - 3432 and the 10,941–10,951 base positions in the mitochondrial genome [53]. Interestingly, there are also subtypes to the 4977 bp CD identified in HCC samples [67]. This novel 4981 bp deletion fragment was identified in the gel electrophoresis result and is located from the 84,710 bp to the 13,450 bp.

In lieu of the known limitations of the current conventional PCR in the identification of large-scale deletions, a novel real-time PCR assay was introduced by Ye et al. in hopes of creating a new protocol with higher sensitivity and increased convenience. Targeting the current issues with the time-consuming semi-quantitative PCR and plateau effects, which affect the quantitative endpoint determination in conventional PCR, this new method was reported to have less possible contamination [37]. Besides that, another possible PCR method for increased identification of CD is the digital PCR (dPCR) method [79]. To overcome the limitations of conventional PCR in quantifying mtDNA deletions of low abundance, dPCR has increased precision to mitigate error and increase sensitivity. However, the efficacy of the dPCR method in CD identification still requires further study.

Analytical challenges

Despite the unknown mechanisms driving the presence of large-scale deletions in the mitochondrial genome, it is undeniable that current studies all agree that mutations in the mtDNA are seen to be cancer-type specific [50]. With increasingly available amounts of publicly available results and data on different cancer types and the mutations found in the mitochondrial DNA, there is a clear need for further analysis into the identification of cancer-specific mutation profiling [2].

Challenges in result interpretation

The mitochondrial genome is prone to mutation occurrences due to its poor repair mechanisms and has a high tolerance towards mutations due to the high copy number compensation. However, it was suggested that the effect of a structural variant is greater than that of a single nucleotide change [45]. Yet, it is the observed protein truncation effect of the mutation that is postulated to cause phenotypic changes in the cell. Therefore, it is important to identify the phenotypic effects that arise from the mutations to identify their deleterious effects in the individual.

Despite this, it is notable that the interpretation of results may remain challenging. One of the main reasons is due to the metabolic effects of CD in the tissue, to cause cellular dysfunction. The effect of such a mutation is highly dependent on the tolerance of the cell towards oxidative stress and the threshold [34]. This leads to the suggestion made by Kim et al. that the interpretation of the mtDNA mutation should be tailored towards its specific effects towards the disease of interest. Subsequently, the importance of the mtDNA mutation should be evaluated based on the physiological effects and functional consequences resulting from the mutated mitochondrial genome [32].

The understanding of the physiological effects of mutations in the mitochondrial genome has been limited due to several factors, such as structural differences in the mtDNA. The D-loop in the mitochondrial genome is a known genomic section prone to mutation. Many studies have identified both structural variation and point mutations occurring commonly in this region, yet the direct functional effects of such mutations have yet to be discovered [32].

Molecular biomarker co-analysis

As not much is understood from the current understanding of the physiological and functional effects of CD in carcinogenesis, it is therefore detrimental to employ different analytical strategies to better quantify and identify the presence and effects of CD in biological processes. This was first performed by Tseng et al., in the use of the NQO1 enzyme as a co-biomarker in the identification of deletions in the mtDNA genome of breast cancer samples. The results showed that there is a significant increase in the presence of the CD mutation in breast cancer samples harbouring the 609 C/T and T/T genotypes when compared against the patient samples with the C/C genotype. By using co-bio markers, the authors were able to come to a conclusion which connects the speculation of increased oxidative stress with the reduction of anti-oxidative functions of the NQO1 enzyme to support the carcinogenesis of breast cancer in the affected patients [39].

Other antioxidant biomarkers were also used in the co-analysis of CD mutation, such as the manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD)expression levels. It was reported in gastric cancer that the co-analysis using MnSOD pathways was able to support the findings that mtDNA-CD is a marker of gastric carcinogenesis. This is purported due to the reduction of anti-oxidative functions of MnSOD, leading to increased oxidative stress and higher reactive oxidative species (ROS) levels and thus resulting in the observed CD levels [49].

Another study in breast cancer also utilised MnSOD as a co-analysis biomarker; the authors reported similar results of oxidative stress and reduced anti-oxidative functions, leading to increased oxidative damage in the cancer cells [43]. By using the MnSOD as a common biomarker for oxidative damage in cancer samples, they were able to conclude that breast carcinogenesis can be detected using CD as an oxidative damage marker.

Other external factors

Common risk factors are often well-established for distinct types of cancers, risk factors such as environmental, physiological and comorbidities can affect the results obtained from mutational profiling. For example, in oral cancer, habits such as cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption are well-known risk factors contributing to mtDNA damage [55]. This is also reported in oesophageal cancer, where the severity of dysplasia can be used to determine the quantity of CD found in the mtDNA genome [56]. Furthermore, it was also shown that long-term heavy alcohol usage in HCC patients will result in an expected increase in mtDNA-CD identified due to the extensive damage [66].

Consolidating the findings from these studies, it is evident that there is a need to collect epidemiological information from patients and further subtypes during CD analysis, as these risk factors are found to directly correlate with the identification of CD. By ignoring the effects of these risk factors and analysing the results from the cohort, we risk missing essential information which might be lost in the homogenised data. This also highlights the importance of increased sample size to allow researchers to confidently conclude population-specific and cancer type-specific inferences about CD occurrence.

It is further notable that the current studies on the identification of mtDNA-CD are conducted primarily on solid tumour cancer types; limited research has been reported on sarcomas. However, it is currently unclear whether this stems from the lack of detection of mtDNA-CD in sarcomas or due to the uncertainty in results resulting from small sample sizes.

Future perspectives

In this review, we discussed the current studies conducted in the field of the identification of large-scale deletions in the mitochondrial genome. The mtDNA-CD is a 4977 bp deletion located in the mitochondrial genome and results in the removal of 7 essential genes. Despite the large number of studies reported on the identification of the mtDNA-CD, the role of the deletion as a biomarker in cancer prognosis remains unclear. We note that there are several potential limitations in the experimental design, sampling method and analytical techniques that possibly contribute to the resulting ambiguous data.

Emerging technologies, such as single-cell RNA-sequencing or long-read sequencing, may be useful in further research into large-scale deletions in the mitochondrial genome. This will potentially reduce the artefacts caused by inaccurate histological annotation of cell type and increase the sequencing coverage to better capture the occurrence of large-scale deletions. Furthermore, it is suggested that multiple biopsy samples should be taken from individual patients to allow for internalised control during data analysis. Additionally, the heterogeneity of the patient sample can be addressed by conducting single-cell RNA-sequencing for cell-type annotation to better analyse the effects of the deletion mutation in each cell type within the tumour microenvironment.

In addition, with the vast number of publicly available scRNA-seq data in databases, it is possible to conduct data mining and re-analysis of known published data. This might reveal a previously missed key insight into the mechanisms of mutations in the mitochondrial genome. Moreover, spatial transcriptomics methods may also be employed for a better understanding of the cell-cell interaction to better depict the mechanisms and occurrence of mtDNA-CD in tumour and adjacent normal cells. In conclusion, further studies are required to clarify the association of CD-mtDNA with cancer diagnosis and prognosis.
