Late recurrence of cervical spine chordoma: illustrative case
Authors: Connor Dolan, Suzanne Powell, Bin S. Teh, Sean Barber
Published: 2025

Chordomas are rare, slow-growing, and locally aggressive bone tumors originating from embryonic remnants of the notochord. They most often appear in the spheno-occipital region of the skull and the sacral area. In adults, about 50% of chordomas are located in the sacrococcygeal region, 35% at the skull base, and the remaining 15% in other areas of the vertebral column.1 The incidence of chordoma is reported at 0.08 per 100,000 persons, with rates of 0.10 per 100,000 in males and 0.06 per 100,000 in females. Systemic chemotherapy is generally ineffective against this tumor, so optimal treatment involves en bloc or wide/marginal resection, at times followed by adjuvant radiotherapy.2,3

The median age at presentation for chordomas is approximately 60 years, although the age range is broad and includes pediatric cases.3,4 Chordomas cause morbidity and mortality primarily through local growth. The clinical presentation varies based on the tumor’s location. Skull base chordomas commonly present with headache, neck pain, and cranial nerve palsies. Upper clival chordomas often present with sixth nerve palsies, resulting from the tumor invading the basilar venous plexus between the periosteal and parietal dural layers, causing a localized compartment syndrome in Dorello’s canal.4 Other cranial neuropathies, such as third and occasionally fourth nerve palsies, can occur with cavernous sinus involvement.5 Brainstem compression can result in symptoms like imbalance or hemiparesis. Lower clival tumors may present with dysphagia due to mass effect or lower cranial nerve involvement.3 In contrast, chordomas in the mobile spine and sacrum often cause back pain or radicular pain, weakness, numbness, and urinary/bowel dysfunction due to compression of the spinal cord or nerve roots. Metastases can occur in chordomas but are unusual at the moment of presentation.4

The prognosis of chordoma is influenced by factors such as tumor location (which affects resectability), the presence of metastases, patient age, and whether dedifferentiation is present. An analysis of 357 spinal chordomas from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results registry reported overall survival rates of 80.5% at 3 years, 68.4% at 5 years, and 39.2% at 10 years. Disease-specific survival rates were higher, at 89.0% at 3 years, 80.9% at 5 years, and 60.1% at 10 years. Overall survival was lower in patients receiving nonsurgical therapy, those with distant metastases, and individuals aged 60 years or older.6 The median survival time for all types of chordomas is approximately 6.3 years. However, for dedifferentiated chordomas of the spine, overall survival is significantly shorter, averaging only about 16 months. Prognosis is more favorable for cases with chondroid histology, as these exhibit low-grade behavior and better long-term outcomes. In contrast, dedifferentiated chordomas, occurring in less than 5% of cases, display features of high-grade spindle cell sarcoma and are associated with an aggressive clinical course.3,7

Chordomas have a recurrence rate of 46%–70%, with the majority of these recurrences (19%–54%) occurring locally.6 Recurrence typically occurs within the first 5–10 years after surgery and is more likely after subtotal/intralesional resection.8–11 However, rare cases of late relapse, occurring up to 10 years after surgery, have been reported, highlighting the importance of long-term follow-up for these patients.

We present the case of a patient with a late recurrence of a spinal chordoma 22 years after initial resection and review the relevant literature on chordoma recurrence rates and management of recurrent disease.

Illustrative Case

A 65-year-old male presented to the emergency department in August 2023 with neck pain and a recent history of multiple mechanical falls at home. He also reported a 1-day history of frequent urination and generalized subjective weakness. Physical examination was notable for weakness in the upper extremities (deltoids: 3/5 bilaterally, biceps: 2–3/5 bilaterally) and a positive Hoffmann’s sign on the left.

The patient’s past medical history included a seizure disorder, thyroid cancer in remission, a stroke in 2008 with residual left lower extremity and left upper extremity weakness, and a history of a cervical spine chordoma treated with resection and proton beam radiotherapy in 2001 with no known interval recurrence since the time of resection. The patient reported ongoing surveillance for local recurrence or metastasis between the time of his initial resection and his later presentation in 2023, but the precise frequency of imaging obtained and the type of imaging obtained is unclear. He had an MRI study of the cervical spine in 2006 without evidence of recurrence, and another MRI study in 2009, which also showed no evidence of recurrence.

The patient was admitted and underwent MRI of the cervical spine with and without contrast, which demonstrated a recurrence of cervical chordoma from C3 to C6 with severe central stenosis and spinal cord compression (Fig. 1).Nuclear medicine bone imaging was negative for metastases or other primary tumors. Surgical intervention was recommended for his cervical spine tumor recurrence followed by postoperative radiotherapy. The patient underwent a C3–6 anterior cervical corpectomy, C2–T2 posterior fusion, C2–4 decompressive laminectomy, and complex plastic surgery wound closure. The surgery proceeded uneventfully. Tumor resection was performed in piecemeal/intralesional fashion due to the circumferential encasement of the spinal cord by tumor, limiting feasibility of redo marginal or en bloc resection.

Tumor removed during surgery was submitted to the pathology team. The intraoperative frozen section was said to be consistent with chordoma. Additional tumor was submitted for permanent histology. Grossly, the specimens were mixed tan-red tissues and tan-white tissues. Microscopically, all three specimens showed features of necrotic bone and tissue mixed with fibrotic tissue. Within the fibrotic tissue of the tumor, a small nest of epithelioid cells with bland nuclei and eosinophilic vacuolated cytoplasm in a myxoid background was noted. Fragments of viable reactive bone and fibrocartilage were also noted. Immunohistochemical staining for brachyury was performed and showed positive nuclear staining in the epithelioid cells, consistent with the chordoma diagnosis. Postoperatively, the patient was neurologically intact at baseline. He was extubated initially but reintubated 3 days later due to increasing shortness of breath. He underwent tracheotomy and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube placement due to dyspnea and dysphagia. He developed a low-grade fever on postoperative day 6 and a subsequent bronchoalveolar lavage was performed, with cultures positive for Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas species. The patient was treated with intravenous antibiotics for 6 days and discharged to a long-term acute care facility after a hospital course of 28 days.

The patient returned for outpatient follow-up in January 2024. He had persistent upper extremity weakness as well as dysphagia and required a PEG tube for nutrition. However, his lower extremity weakness had improved, and he had returned to work. Initial postoperative MRI showed no residual or recurrent tumor. New enhancement was seen within the T2 vertebral body, which was thought to be reactive/inflammatory. This enhancement remained stable to decreased on subsequent MRI. Radiographic and CT imaging demonstrated a small amount of migration of the C7 anterior cervical screws, which was monitored closely with serial imaging and has remained stable (Fig. 3). Postoperative stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) was completed in February 2024. SBRT is used for recurrent cervical chordomas due to a tumor’s relative radioresistance and the need for high-dose customized treatment to ensure local control and minimize toxicity to nearby anatomy. The rationale for SBRT in this setting is supported by its ability to deliver ablative doses with high dose gradients, which is important in the relatively anatomically constrained cervical spine. Dosing strategies for SBRT in recurrent chordoma typically involve single-fraction regimens of 13.5–25.0 Gy or multifractionated regimens of 21–42 Gy in 3–5 fractions.12,13 Higher doses are typically associated with better local control. In the context of reirradiation, attention to spinal cord tolerance is necessary, and cumulative spinal cord dose constraints should be strictly observed. Outcomes with SBRT in recurrent chordomas show local control is typically 75%–87% after 1 year. This declines to 49%–73% at 5 years, with better outcomes for patients without prior radiation therapy.13–17

The decision to use SBRT rather than repeat proton beam radiotherapy in a patient with recurrent cervical chordoma is driven by the need to minimize cumulative spinal cord dose and the associated adverse side effects. It was also important to consider the prior radiation dosage, as well as the tumor’s location and volume when making that decision. SBRT’s ability to offer modifiable dose delivery was advantageous in the reirradiation setting because it allowed for precise targeting of a recurrent tumor in the context of spinal cord radiation dose constraints.13 While there is no strong evidence in the literature for prioritizing treatment with either SBRT or proton beam radiotherapy in the setting of a chordoma recurrence with previous radiation therapy, SBRT allows for effective doses to the tumor while reducing radiation exposure to nearby anatomy.12,18,19

Follow-up MRI of the cervical spine in March 2024 and again in July 2024 demonstrated no evidence of residual or recurrent tumor.

Discussion

Observations

Published case series and meta-analyses on postsurgical outcomes for chordoma suggest that the large majority of patients who undergo macroscopic complete resection, with or without radiation therapy, experience recurrence.9 A recent series on primary, widely resected sacral chordomas reported local recurrence rates of 30%, 46%, and 57% at 5, 10, and 15 years postresection, respectively.20 Stacchiotti et al. published a series of 138 patients with chordoma of the mobile spine and sacrum with reported 5- and 10-year local relapse-free survival (LRFS) rates of 52% and 33%, with a median LRFS of 63 months.9 Factors associated with improved recurrence rates in the literature include smaller tumor size,9 quality of margins (i.e., margins negative for tumor),8,9 and the experience of the center where the index surgery was performed.9 Although very late recurrence of chordoma is rarely reported, previous literature has pointed to recurrences of sacral chordomas at 17, 19, and 30 years,21, 23 as well as the ectopic recurrence of a cervical chordoma at 17 years not involving the primary tumor site.6 These examples are listed in Table 1. Treating recurrent tumors is challenging and often controversial. Previous surgeries can lead to tissue contamination and altered local anatomy, which may increase the risks associated with reoperation. Depending on the previous surgery and radiological interventions, a distinct approach may be required on a case-by-case basis.22 For chordoma in particular, prognosis after local recurrence is unfortunately poor. In a series of mobile spine and sacral chordomas by Stacchiotti et al., 5- and 10-year overall survival rates after repeat resection were 56% and 26%, with LRFS rates of 47% and 31%, respectively.9 In an institutional study by Yang et al. on recurrent sacral chordomas, recurrence after repeat resection occurred in 37.5% of cases, with an average time to recurrence of 26.7 months.22 The recurrence-free survival rates were 69.4% at 3 years and 63.1% at 5 years. Lung metastases developed in 16.7% of patients. There were no other metastases noted. Following surgical treatment of recurrence, the overall survival rates were 67.3% at 5 years and 53.9% at 10 years.22

Lessons

We report the case of a 65-year-old male with a chordoma of the cervical spine that recurred 22 years after initial resection and proton beam radiotherapy. This case is notable for the extended interval between index treatment and recurrence. Our case underscores the importance of ongoing surveillance for recurrent disease in patients with chordoma. Currently, the Chordoma Foundation recommends that chordomas should be imaged via MRI with and without contrast every 6 months for the first 5 years, followed by annual MRI with and without contrast for at least 15 years. This case shows that recurrence can occur later than 15 years. Therefore, this case suggests that after the first 5 years of MRI with and without contrast every 6 months, annual MRI with and without contrast for the duration of the patient’s life may be pragmatic to detect late recurrence.
