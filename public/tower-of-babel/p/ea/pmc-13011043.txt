Outcomes of patients with oral cavity squamous cell carcinomas with perineural invasion treated with adjuvant intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT)
Authors: Kasey R. Cargill, Phillip M. Pifer, John A. Vargo, Uzoma Iheagwara, Seungwon Kim, Mark W. Kubik, Shaum Sridharan, Umamaheswar Duvvuri, Dan P. Zandberg, Zahra R. Kelly, Raja R. Seethala, Jose Zevallos, Robert L. Ferris, Christopher T. Wilke, Yvonne M. Mowery, Heath D. Skinner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Oral cavity cancers are among the most common malignancies worldwide, with an estimated 350,000 new cases each year and steadily increasing incidence and mortality [1], [2], [3]. Oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) accounts for greater than 90% of oral cavity cancer cases, and advanced disease confers high recurrence rates with poor outcomes [3], [4]. The overall five-year survival rate for OCSCC is approximately 60–70%, but differs drastically based on patient characteristics, disease stage, and subsite of disease (oral tongue, gum, floor of mouth, buccal mucosa, retromolar trigone, hard palate, and lip) [2], [3], [5]. Risk factors for OCSCC include tobacco and alcohol use and poor oral hygiene [5].

Patient outcomes are driven by clinical and pathological factors including functional status, age, TNM stage, tumor grade, and depth of invasion [6]. For patients with locally advanced OCSCC, the treatment paradigm is oncologic surgical resection of the primary tumor and neck dissection followed by risk-adapted adjuvant therapy of intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) with or without platinum-based chemotherapy based on adverse pathological features [7], [8], [9].

Patients with OCSCC with “high-risk” pathologic factors (positive surgical margins and/or extranodal extension) benefit from adjuvant chemoradiation to improve locoregional control (LRC), disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS) [7], [10], [11]. Intermediate-risk factors, including perineural invasion (PNI), lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), multiple positive lymph nodes, and pathologically-involved lymph nodes measuring greater than 3 cm, are indications for post-operative RT to improve LRC rates [12]. Previous retrospective analysis of oral cavity cancers treated with surgery and adjuvant IMRT, revealed that LVSI, depth of invasion greater than 1.5 cm, and absence of neck dissection were associated with worse OS [13]. The importance of PNI as a prognostic factor and indication for adjuvant RT remains an area of debate, and pathological criteria for characterizing PNI have not been clearly established. The frequency of reported PNI involvement varies widely, ranging from 5 to 80% of OCSCC cases [4], [14], [15]. When taken at a patient level, multiple intermediate risk factors are a clear indication for post-operative RT. Currently, there is a knowledge gap regarding the strength of PNI as an intermediate predictor of worse outcomes in patients with OCSCC treated with definitive surgery that warrants addition of post-operative RT. In this study, patients with surgically resected OCSCC with or without PNI treated with adjuvant IMRT were examined to determine the association between PNI and locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), relapse-free survival (RFS), and OS.

Materials and methods

Data collection

In this single institution retrospective study, adult patients with OCSCC who underwent surgical resection followed by adjuvant IMRT with or without concurrent chemotherapy at our institution between July 1, 2002 and August 31, 2021 were identified through an institutional Head and Neck SPORE database through an honest broker. T and N category staging was determined using the AJCC 7th edition. This retrospective study protocol was approved by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (#PRO13020306). Patients were excluded if they did not undergo RT, received palliative-intent RT, had metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis, synchronous primaries or had missing RT records. The database was reviewed for demographic information, clinical and pathological factors, and treatment information. Follow-up data including recurrence and vitality status were also extracted.

Statistical analysis

LRFS was defined as time from RT completion to primary tumor and/or regional nodal recurrence or death from any cause. DMFS was defined as time from RT completion until distant disease recurrence or death from any cause. RFS was defined as time from RT completion to locoregional recurrence, distance recurrence, or death from any cause. OS was defined as time from RT completion to death from any cause. Treatment timing was defined as the surgery-to-RT time and is the interval from date of surgical resection to date of RT initiation. Patients who were not known to have died were censored at the time of their last follow-up. Pathologically proven OCSCC within 5 years of IMRT was considered a recurrence, beyond this timeframe any OCSCC was considered a second primary and was excluded from analysis.

LRFS, DMFS, RFS and OS curves were estimated using the using Kaplan-Meier method; outcomes for patients stratified by PNI status were compared by log-rank test. Univariate analysis of predictors for LRFS, DMFS, RFS, and OS was performed using the Cox regression method. Factors significant on univariate analysis were entered into a parsimonious Cox multivariable analysis using forward selection methodology. A propensity score was created using the conditional probability of PNI versus no PNI by the baseline characteristics of sex, age, T category, N category, anatomic subsite, margin status, smoking status, alcohol use, extranodal extension (ENE), LVSI, treatment package time (defined as time from surgery to RT completion), and chemotherapy use. This propensity score was then incorporated as a continuous covariant into a Cox regression analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant in both analyses. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9). Cox regression analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 27).

Results

Patient characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the clinical and pathological characteristics of the 494 patients with OCSCC who met inclusion criteria. Median age at the time of diagnosis was 59 years (interquartile range [IQR] 52–69 years), and 64.8% of the patients were male. The majority of patients had a smoking history (72.9%) and/or alcohol use history (70.2%). With regards to tumor subsite, 40.9% originated from the oral tongue, 21.5% from the gums, 18% from the floor of mouth, and 19.6% from other sites (buccal mucosa, retromolar trigone, hard palate, or lips). With regards to pathological factors, 48.8% of patients had pT4 tumors, 39.3% had LVSI, and 40.3% of patients had ENE. PNI was present in 63.6% of patients (n = 314). The median adjuvant RT dose was 63 Gy (IQR 60–66 Gy). After surgical resection, 60.0% of patients initiated RT within 8 weeks. Discussion

This single institution study represents one of the largest retrospective series of patients with OCSCC treated with surgical resection and adjuvant IMRT. In this cohort, patients with OCSCC exhibiting PNI had lower three-year LRFS, DMFS, RFS and OS compared to patients without PNI. This supports PNI as being a significant negative prognostic factor in patients with OCSCC. These data are consistent with previous studies that have shown PNI as an indicator of worse outcomes after surgical resection, with addition of adjuvant IMRT improving disease-specific survival (DSS) and locoregional control (LRC) [16]. Further, it has been revealed that in patients who developed locoregional recurrence, 85% exhibited PNI, highlighting the importance of PNI as a risk factor for progression [17], [18].

Since PNI is a distinct route of tumor spread, it poses unique challenges for disease control. Although there is moderate consensus that PNI confers increased risk of recurrence, the differing definitions on its pathologic evaluation and the lack of standardization in reporting have made this difficult to assess definitively. In particular, the extent of PNI and involvement of small-caliber and/or large-caliber nerves may carry distinct prognostic significance and influence adjuvant therapy decisions, but these features are not routinely reported, limiting retrospective evaluation. The use of adjuvant IMRT for patients with OCSCC with PNI as the only pathologic risk factor remains controversial, with retrospective series reporting variable benefit of RT for improving oncologic outcomes. One study evaluating 78 patients with early-stage disease (39 with PNI and 39 without PNI) showed no benefit from adjuvant RT [16], while another study of 20 patients with early or locally advanced disease showed that adjuvant IMRT was associated with improving DFS by almost 5 years [19]. In the present study, PNI was associated with inferior LRFS and OS on univariate analysis, but it did not retain independent significance on multivariable analysis. This finding likely reflects a strong association between PNI and other high-risk clinicopathologic features, including advanced disease grade or stage, close or positive margins, extranodal extension, and receipt of concurrent chemotherapy, which may attenuate its independent prognostic effect. The high variability of results among investigations into the role of adjuvant IMRT in the setting of PNI is likely due in part to the relatively small patient populations, heterogeneity in pathologic definitions, and differences in treatment intensification across studies. However, we cannot discount the growing evidence that PNI, at least in part, functions as a marker of aggressive local disease that may adversely impact overall prognosis.

With 494 patients, a major strength of the present study is that it is one of the largest cohorts of patients with OCSCC treated with a homogenous treatment paradigm of surgical resection and adjuvant IMRT. However, the question does remain if adjuvant radiation alone is effective in treating PNI or do these patients benefit from treatment escalation which will be the direction of future investigations. With advancements in radiation therapy, PNI persists as a challenging prognosticator and how to best improve outcomes in these patients is not known. In NRG/RTOG 0920, patient with intermediate risk factors after surgery (including PNI) was randomized to RT with or without cetuximab demonstrated improved DFS but failed to improve OS [20]. In ECOG 3132, patients with Stage III and IV cancer after surgery are being stratified by p53 mutational status and then randomized to radiation with or without cisplatin [21]. A subset analysis of this trial with patients with PNI would help in understanding the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy in these populations. Alternatively, it may be important to investigate whether increasing radiation dose to the PNI area from the normal 60 Gy to 66–70 Gy would improve outcomes in these patients. Future areas of research could include examining tumor biology in patients with and without PNI. Although this study cannot definitively separate the biases inherent in the data, it raises important questions about the underlying biology of tumors with PNI and emphasizes the importance of multi-institutional studies examining these patients.

The major limitation of this study is its retrospective nature, including patients treated over a twenty-year period including only patients who received IMRT in the adjuvant setting. Although this is the largest series of its kind, the relatively small number of events in the subset analyses limits the power of the Cox regression analysis. Additionally, the definition of PNI has evolved over the span of the last two decades, which could affect the outcomes of both this and previous studies evaluating the impact of PNI on prognosis.

In summary, our cohort reveled that the presence of PNI correlated with significantly worse 3-year LRFS, DMFS, RFS, and OS in patients with OCSCC treated with surgical resection and adjuvant IMRT. Similar to previous reports, this study also showed ENE, a high-risk prognostic factor, correlated with worse patient outcomes, thus increasing the validity of these results. While there has been controversy regarding the impact of PNI on prognosis, this large retrospective study highlights that PNI confers worse outcomes for patients with OCSCC even in the context of adjuvant radiotherapy. Therefore, it should be considered a risk factor for disease progression and may necessitate future investigations into combinatorial adjuvant approaches.

Conclusions

In this large single institution study, PNI was a significant negative predictive factor for LRFS, DMFS, RFS, and OS in patients with OCSCC treated with definitive surgery and adjuvant IMRT. To our knowledge this is the largest study of its kind, and these findings can help guide clinical decision making for these patients and stresses the importance of investigate additional treatments in these patients.
