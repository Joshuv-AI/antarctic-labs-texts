Aorto-ostial coronary lesion is a technically challenging anatomic location for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and is associated with difficulties in achieving coaxial guide catheter engagement, accurate stent positioning, and complete lesion coverage.1 Despite advances in stent design and imaging modalities, ostial stenting continues to carry higher rates of geographic miss, malapposition, and restenosis than nonostial lesions.2, 3, 4 In particular, even minor stent protrusion into the aortic root, such as from stent elongation, may preclude optimal guide engagement in subsequent interventions. The management of in-stent restenosis can be challenging because conventional strategies such as balloon angioplasty, drug-coated balloons, or restenting may be limited by the inability to obtain coaxial access through a previously protruding stent. Although wiring through stent struts and performing additional stent deployment (stent crush techniques) have been described, these approaches may not provide durable long-term results and carry risks of stent fracture, malapposition, or recurrent in-stent restenosis given stent cells can only be dilated to a certain dimension.5 In rare cases, percutaneous stent retrieval (stentectomy) may offer a feasible alternative, particularly when stent protrusion into the aortic root significantly compromises catheter engagement and subsequent revascularization. Although stent extraction is most commonly reported in the setting of acute stent loss or maldeployment, intentional removal of chronically implanted aorto-ostial stents has rarely been described.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 In this report, we present a step-by-step method for performing percutaneous coronary stentectomy.Take-Home Messages•Protruding coronary stents can hinder revascularization.•This case demonstrates that percutaneous snare-assisted stentectomy can be performed nearly a decade later to enable successful revascularization; however, potential complications must be carefully considered.

Case Summary

We present the case of a 72-year-old woman with a history of ostial right coronary artery stenting who developed lifestyle-limiting angina refractory to optimal medical therapy. Coronary angiography demonstrated severe in-stent restenosis with a protruding aorto-ostial stent that precluded coaxial guide engagement (Figure 1, Video 1). We elected to attempt percutaneous stentectomy rather than wiring through strut cells (Video 2). Using an 8-F guiding catheter, a 6-F guide-extension catheter, and a 20-mm goose-neck snare in a mother-daughter configuration, the protruding stent was successfully engaged and partially extracted en bloc, fracturing at the ostium (Figure 2, Video 3). This maneuver restored guide catheter access to the right coronary artery (Videos 4 and 5). Lesion preparation with intravascular lithotripsy was performed, followed by intravascular ultrasound–guided deployment of a 3.5- × 28-mm drug-eluting stent with excellent angiographic and intravascular ultrasound results (Figure 3, Videos 6 and 7). A contained wire-induced perforation was managed conservatively with protamine (Video 8). Procedural Steps

The procedure was performed via right femoral arterial access using an 8-F introducer sheath. An 8-F JR4 guiding catheter was advanced to the right coronary sinus and used in conjunction with a 6-F guide-extension catheter to provide enhanced coaxial support in a mother-daughter configuration. A 20-mm goose-neck snare was prepared for stent retrieval.

The first step involved advancing the goose-neck snare through the guide-extension catheter and carefully positioning it around the protruding segment of the previously deployed ostial stent. Once the stent struts were successfully engaged within the snare loop, the guide-extension catheter was advanced over the snare to secure the stent within the narrowed lumen of the extension. This maneuver provided both traction and stability, preventing slippage of the snare from the metallic struts. Gradual traction was then applied, with careful monitoring of hemodynamics and fluoroscopic visualization. During withdrawal, resistance was encountered at the aorto-ostial level, consistent with stent embedding. Additional force was applied in a controlled fashion, resulting in fracture of the stent at the ostium. At this point, the guide catheter, guide extension, snare, and entrapped stent fragment were removed en bloc. The retrieved material was inspected ex vivo to confirm the presence of stent struts.

After retrieval, repeat angiography demonstrated unobstructed engagement of the right coronary ostium. Intravascular imaging confirmed the absence of residual stent material within the aortic lumen and identified stent fracture at the ostium. Lesion preparation was then performed with intravascular lithotripsy to optimize vessel compliance, followed by deployment of a 3.5- × 28-mm drug-eluting stent with precise ostial coverage and postdilatation. Final intravascular ultrasound confirmed optimal stent expansion and apposition with an excellent angiographic result. The procedure was completed without hemodynamic compromise, and the patient remained clinically stable.

Potential Pitfalls

Although percutaneous coronary stentectomy can provide a definitive solution in select cases, the procedure is technically demanding and associated with several potential complications that warrant careful consideration. Stent elongation without fracture may occur if traction is applied but the stent fails to separate at the ostium. This scenario can result in persistent obstruction and continued difficulty with guide catheter engagement. Bailout strategies include advancing a wire through the stent struts to facilitate further PCI with additional stenting, or referral for surgical revascularization. Stent embolization represents another important risk. Dislodged stents may migrate distally into the coronary vasculature or systemically into the peripheral or cerebral circulation. Management depends on the site of embolization: percutaneous retrieval may be attempted in the coronary system, whereas systemic embolization may necessitate collaboration with neurointerventional or vascular specialists for retrieval. Aorto-ostial injury is among the most serious potential complications. Excessive traction can result in coronary ostial perforation or aortic dissection, both of which may necessitate emergent surgical repair. In select cases where guidewire position can be achieved quickly, deployment of a covered stent may provide an effective percutaneous bailout strategy; however, in the absence of wire access, immediate surgical intervention is required. Inability to successfully snare the stent is also possible, particularly when the protruding segment is minimal or unfavorably oriented. If retrieval is unsuccessful, operators may attempt wiring through the stent struts followed by restenting or stent-crushing techniques. Should these approaches fail, surgical revascularization remains the definitive option. In summary, percutaneous stentectomy offers a valuable, although technically complex, revascularization strategy in the setting of aorto-ostial restenosis due to stent protrusion. Awareness of potential complications and familiarity with bailout techniques—including adjunctive percutaneous strategies, surgical revascularization, and, when necessary, neurointerventional collaboration—are essential to optimizing patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that percutaneous stentectomy is a feasible option in selected patients with long-standing, protruding coronary stents that compromise reintervention. With the assistance of snare-based retrieval techniques and guide extension catheters, even chronically implanted stents can be partially or fully removed to restore access and facilitate PCI.
