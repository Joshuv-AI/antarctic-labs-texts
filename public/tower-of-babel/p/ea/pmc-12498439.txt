Evaluation of biomechanical behavior of all-ceramic cantilever resin-bonded fixed dental prostheses: a finite element analysis
Authors: Tri Minh Doan, Yen Hoang Truong, Chau Tran Bao Vu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResin-bonded fixed dental prostheses (RBFDPs) offer a minimally invasive approach while delivering excellent aesthetics and functionality. This study aimed to examine the stress distribution in RBFDPs.MethodsTwo intact natural teeth (#11 and #13) were selected and mounted into a dental model using a silicone mold, with tooth #12 removed to simulate the absence of a maxillary lateral incisor. Four cantilever RBFDP models were created: Model 1, #11 abutment, made of zirconia; Model 2, #13 abutment, made of zirconia; Model 3, #11 abutment, made of lithium disilicate; and Model 4, #13 abutment, made of lithium disilicate. A 200 N occlusal force was applied to the pontic. Finite element analysis (FEA) was performed to quantify the maximum von Mises stress distribution in the framework, cement layer, and periodontal ligament (PDL).ResultsThe maximum von Mises stress in the framework was highest in the zirconia RBFDP with #11 as the abutment (model 1), and lowest in the lithium disilicate RBFDP with #13 as the abutment (model 4). Regarding the cement layer, the maximum von Mises stress was greatest in the lithium disilicate RBFDP with #11 as the abutment (model 3), and lowest in the zirconia RBFDP with #13 as the abutment (model 2).ConclusionsThe canine is a more efficient abutment than the central incisor in terms of maximum von Mises stress in the framework, cement layer, and on the PDL. Regarding the materials used, zirconia showed lower von Mises stress on the cement layer. Therefore, a zirconia cantilever RBFDP with the canine as the abutment is recommended as the preferred choice for all-ceramic cantilever RBFDPs.Keywords: Finite element analysis, Zirconia, Lithium disilicate, Resin-bonded fixed dental prostheses

Background

To restore a missing tooth in the anterior region, clinicians have various options, such as implant-supported restorations, fixed dental bridges, or removable partial dentures [1]. Implant restorations are currently considered the optimal method; however, not all cases are suitable for implants [2]. These include cases involving significant hard or soft tissue deficits, patients under 18 years of age, or those with systemic diseases that contraindicate implant surgery [1, 2]. In these situations, a tooth-supported restoration is necessary as a replacement [1].

Traditional dental bridges are regarded as the most invasive among tooth-supported restorations [3]. In 2002, Edelhoff et al. [3] demonstrated that 63–72% of the tooth structure was lost when preparing for an all-ceramic or metal-ceramic bridge. This could potentially harm the dental pulp, especially in individuals with large pulp chambers [4]. Therefore, resin-bonded fixed dental prostheses (RBFDPs) are a suitable alternative, as they minimize invasiveness while still providing optimal aesthetics and function [5].

Several key factors affecting the clinical success of RBFDPs have been identified, including patient selection, preparation of abutment teeth, framework design, occlusal contacts, and the number of abutment teeth [5, 6]. Clinical studies have shown that adding an abutment tooth increased the debonding rate of the restoration [7–10]. In a study by Kern et al. [7], they found that the 10-year survival rates for ceramic RBFDPs were 73.9% for three-unit configurations and 94.4% for two-unit cantilever configurations. Cantilever RBFDPs showed a higher survival rate than fixed-fixed RBFDPs, which is assumed to be because cantilever RBFDPs avoid the adverse effects of differential movement between two abutment teeth on the cement layer, which can cause fatigue in the cement and lead to restoration debonding [11]. Variations in material properties are considered to influence the functional and clinical effectiveness of RBFDPs [12].

Improper prosthesis design can lead to intense stress on the abutment, which subsequently places strain on the periodontal ligament (PDL) [13]. This intense strain can negatively impact the prognosis of both the abutment and the overall prosthesis [13]. Alternatively, occlusal forces can cause the RBFDP framework to flex, putting stress on the cement layer and leading to framework debonding [13, 14]. As a result, the long-term success of RBFDPs is associated with the stress on the adhesive cement layer and the potential harm to the periodontal tissue [13].

In relation to various materials and prosthesis designs, finite element analysis (FEA) can be used to examine stress distribution in prosthetic frameworks, resin cement layers, and PDL under different conditions [11, 15, 16]. FEA is a technique used to address structural issues with complex geometries subjected to external loads [15, 16]. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have specifically investigated the biomechanical performance of RBFDPs, particularly with respect to different abutment tooth positions and prosthetic materials [13, 17]. This study aims to address that gap by evaluating and comparing stress distribution in two cantilever RBFDP designs made from zirconia and lithium disilicate. The null hypothesis proposes that there is no difference in stress distribution within the framework, cement layer, and PDL between the two cantilever RBFDP designs fabricated from zirconia and lithium disilicate.

Methods

Specimen Preparation

Two extracted natural teeth (#11 and #13), which are intact, free of decay, and without restorations, were collected from the clinic of the Faculty of Odonto-Stomatology at.

the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City. These two teeth were mounted into a dental model using a silicone mold. Tooth #12 was removed to simulate a case of a missing upper lateral incisor, and the remaining teeth were eliminated, retaining only the section of the model containing teeth #11 to #13 (Fig. 1). The specimen was then scanned using a cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) Kavo OP3D machine (KaVo Kerr, Germany), with the following scanning parameters: scanning time of 19 s, a focal point of 0.5 mm, a voltage of 100 kV, a current intensity of 3 mA, and a voxel size of 120 μm. A DICOM file of the model was obtained.

The gypsum model containing extracted natural teeth. A labial view; (B) palatal view

Tooth preparation was performed on teeth #11 and #13. A 0.5 mm thick wing preparation was made on the palatal surfaces, providing a bonding interface on healthy enamel [13, 17, 18]. Chamfer margins of 0.5 mm were created 2 mm below the incisal edges and 1 mm above the cemento-enamel junction. The preparation was finished using a fine diamond bur, followed by polishing with a silicone bur. The model was then scanned using E1scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) to capture a 3D image detailing the tooth preparation.

Two cantilevered RBFDP frameworks were designed based on the obtained 3D image using Exocad software (Exocad GmbH, Germany). The retainers have a thickness of 0.5 mm, corresponding to the preparation on the abutment teeth. The connectors were designed with dimensions of 3 mm in height and 3 mm in width, based on values reported in a previous study [17]. The two frameworks are as follows:

The prosthetic data in STL file format, along with the initial dental model data in DICOM file format, were used to construct the FEA model using SolidWorks software (Dassault Systems SolidWorks Corp., USA). The two geometric frameworks created using SolidWorks were imported into ANSYS (Version 11.0, Ansys Inc., Pennsylvania, USA) (Fig. 2). Each framework was analyzed with two types of materials: zirconia (Y-TZP, Panasonic Healthcare, Ehime, Japan) and lithium disilicate (IPS Empress 2; Ivoclar-Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). Each structural component, including enamel, dentin, resin cement (Panavia F 2.0 TC, Kuraray Co., Tokyo, Japan), prosthesis, periodontal ligament, and alveolar bone was individually delineated using ANSYS software (Version 11.0, Ansys Inc., Pennsylvania, USA). A 120 μm thick cement layer was placed between the prepared tooth surface and the prosthesis. The PDL was defined with a thickness of 0.25 mm around the root surface. The interfaces between the tooth-PDL and PDL-bone were modeled as bonbed contacts. Similarly, the adhesive cement–tooth and adhesive cement–prosthesis interfaces were also modeled as bonded. These assumptions were based on previous FEA studies [17, 19, 20] that applied bonded conditions to focus on stress distribution within the prosthesis, adhesive cement, and PDL, rather than on interfacial debonding behavior. Table 1 displays the characteristics of these biological structures and materials. All materials were considered homogeneous and isotropic [15, 21]. The PDL was assumed to exhibit bilinear elasticity, while a linear elastic behavior was assumed for the other biological structures and dental materials [13, 21].

Mechanical properties

Mesh generation is an important process for dividing a solid geometric model into smaller elements. The smaller the element size, the more elements the model will have, resulting in a finer mesh and higher accuracy. In this study, we performed selective meshing of the model in specific regions, using a moderate number of nodes and elements to optimize the analysis while ensuring accuracy. To determine a suitable element size, a mesh convergence analysis was performed. The prosthesis and cement layer were finely meshed with an element size of 0.12 mm. The periodontal ligament was also finely meshed with an element size of 0.25 mm, while the enamel, dentin and alveolar bone were meshed with medium density using an element size of 1 mm. A load of 200 N was applied at a 450 to the long axis of the pontic, as this value was regarded as the highest biting force for the front teeth [22]. The point of force application was located 2 mm below the incisal edge. Stress levels were evaluated based on the von Mises stress value, which are frequently used in other FEA studies to assess stress distribution in dental materials [15, 19, 23]. A color scale was utilized to effectively highlight areas of stress distribution.

Discussion

In recent times, the retention of RBFDPs has improved thanks to the development of bonding materials and technologies [24]. There are two design types for RBFDPs in the anterior region: two-unit cantilevered RBFDPs and three-unit fixed-fixed RBFDPs [21]. In 2000, Chan et al. [25] compared the clinical effectiveness of RBFDPs and reported a higher survival rate for the cantilevered RBFDPs in a short-term longitudinal follow-up study. Our study highlights the significance of considering dental materials and framework design when planning treatments for patients needing cantilevered RBFDPs. Based on the results, the null hypothesis, which suggested no difference in stress analysis in the framework, cement layer, and PDL of two cantilever RBFDP designs made from zirconia and lithium disilicate, was rejected.

In this study, the sample consists of four three-dimensional FEA models of RBFDPs. The models differ in the design of the cantilever RBFDPs and the type of material, with retainers on either the central incisor or the canine, combined with two types of materials: zirconia and lithium disilicate. The model examined in this study simulates the loss of a lateral incisor in the upper jaw, as this is a common indication for performing RBFDPs [13]. Additionally, the lateral incisor is the second most common tooth to be congenitally missing, following the wisdom tooth [26].

In previous FEA studies in dental biomechanics, von Mises stress had been used to analyzed and compare stress distribution in prosthetic frameworks, adhesive cements and dental supporting structures [15, 19, 23]. While von Mises stress is conventionally applied to assess failure in ductile materials, it has also been extensively utilized in studies involving brittle materials like ceramics and resin cement, as it effectively highlights regions of maximum stress concentration where fatigue failure is prone to initiate [27]. Therefore, von Mises stress was selected for use in this study.

In many studies, zirconia is the most chosen material for all-ceramic RBFDPs [13, 20, 21]. Zirconia overcomes the brittle nature of glass ceramics, but its bonding strength and aesthetics are considered inferior compared to glass-ceramic materials [28]. Recent improvements in the microstructure of zirconia have greatly enhanced its translucency while maintaining its fracture resistance [29, 30]. Lithium disilicate, with advanced material improvements, offers high aesthetics, and its mechanical characteristics are also comparable to those of zirconia [31]. In this study, we chose zirconia and lithium disilicate as materials for RBFDPs. Our design was consistent to that of Keulemans, who also selected two types of ceramics for RBFDPs [20].

When a 200 N force was applied to the pontic, RBFDPs made from materials with higher stiffness experienced greater von Mises stress compared to those made from materials with lower stiffness, as seen in the comparison between zirconia and lithium disilicate. Zirconia, with an elastic modulus of 2.1 × 105 MPa, exhibited a maximum von Mises stress of 158 MPa, whereas lithium disilicate, with an elastic modulus of 9.6 × 104 MPa, showed a maximum von Mises stress of 117.7 MPa. The highest von Mises stress was observed at the connector. It is worth mentioning that in our FE model, the connector was designed with dimensions of 3 mm x 3 mm. Recently, Patel et al. [17] found that a connector with dimensions of 3 mm x 4 mm exhibited significantly lower stress compared to a 3 mm x 3 mm connector.

In cantilevered RBFDPs, the displacement of the abutment determines the stress experienced by the framework. In our study, frameworks using the #11 abutment exhibited more stress than those using the #13 abutment, suggesting that the central incisor underwent greater displacement than the canine. These results are consistent with findings from Uraba et al. [13], where the cantilevered framework experienced greater stress on the central incisor side compared to the canine side.

The bond strength is a key factor influencing the survival of the prosthesis [32]. The stress distribution in the cement layer helps forecast the risk of failure due to prosthesis debonding [23]. In all four models, stress concentration within the cement layer was observed to be focused on the connector edge. This indicates that the first clinically detectable debonding could occur at the connector area. To minimize the risk of debonding in cantilever RBFDPs, incorporating vertical grooves on the abutment tooth could be a viable solution [13, 17]. The stress distribution in the cement layer was higher in the design with #11 as the abutment compared to the design with #13 as the abutment. Therefore, the risk of prosthesis debonding is expected to be lower when a canine is chosen as the abutment tooth.

Regarding the materials for the prosthesis, previous studies have shown that materials with greater elastic moduli lead to reduced stress values at the cement layer [15, 33]. In line with this, the current study demonstrated that the maximum von Mises stress values in the cement layer of the zirconia RBFDP model with #11 as the abutment (model 1) were lower than those of the lithium disilicate RBFDP model with #11 as the abutment (model 3). Similarly, the zirconia RBFDP model with #13 as the abutment (model 2) showed lower stress values than the lithium disilicate RBFDP model with #13 as the abutment (model 4).

Stress accumulation also occurs in the PDL under occlusal loading [17]. In our results, the maximum von Mises stress in the PDL of models 1 and 3, with the central incisor as the abutment, was higher compared to models 2 and 4, where the canine served as the abutment. This difference can be credited to the average PDL volume of the individual abutment teeth [13, 17]. The canine is longer than the central incisor, resulting in a larger PDL surface area [17]. Therefore, when considering the protection of the PDL, the canine may serve as a better choice for an abutment. Our results align with the findings of Uraba et al. [13] and Patel et al. [17].

Furthermore, in this study, we designed the alveolar bone level to be 2 mm from the cemento-enamel junction, corresponding to normal periodontal tissue. Changes in the alveolar bone level have been shown to affect the debonding of the prosthesis, as demonstrated in a study by Sukumoda et al. [34] in 2021. They found that when the alveolar bone level decreased by one-third of the height, the stress on the PDL increased, along with a higher risk of prosthesis debonding [34].

The present study has several limitations. FEA is a simulation and cannot account for all possible variables. Despite the biological structures and dental materials being anisotropic, they were considered isotropic for the aim of this research. Additionally, the latest generations of translucent zirconia were not included as a material for RBFDPs, which could also be a constraint.

Conclusions

In conclusion, when evaluating the maximum von Mises stress in the framework, cement layer, and PDL, the canine proves to be a more effective abutment than the central incisor for a cantilever RBFDP replacing a maxillary lateral incisor. In terms of dental materials, zirconia, which has a higher elastic modulus than lithium disilicate, exhibited lower von Mises stress on the cement layer. Therefore, a zirconia cantilever RBFDP with the canine as the abutment is recommended as the optimal option for all-ceramic cantilever RBFDPs.
