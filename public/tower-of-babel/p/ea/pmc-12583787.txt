Immunometabolic Crosstalk in Adipose Tissue Remodeling: Mechanisms and Therapeutic Perspectives
Authors: Yeonho Son, Cheoljun Choi, Junhyuck Lee, Gyeongran Park, Ki Yong Hong, Woong Sun, Mi-Ock Lee, Yun-Hee Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adipose tissue is a dynamic immunometabolic organ whose cellular heterogeneity and functional plasticity are central to systemic energy balance and metabolic regulation. Disruption of immune-adipocyte interactions is closely linked to the development of obesity and related metabolic disorders. In this review, we summarize current advances in understanding of the immune landscape in adipose tissue, with an emphasis on the distinct roles of immune cell subsets. Recent approaches including global and single-cell transcriptomic analysis, spatial profiling, and lineage tracing have expanded our ability to characterize these populations. We further highlight mechanisms through which immune cells influence adipocyte turnover, lipid handling, and thermogenesis, as well as reciprocal signals from adipocytes such as cytokines, lipid mediators, extracellular vesicles, and nutrient exchange. This bidirectional crosstalk governs adipose tissue remodeling and determines the occurrence of metabolic homeostasis or dysfunction. Finally, we provide perspectives into the ways in which these interactions may guide the identification of novel therapeutic targets for obesity and metabolic disease.

INTRODUCTION

Adipose tissue functions as a dynamic immunometabolic organ, playing essential roles in energy storage, endocrine signaling, and systemic metabolic regulation.1 Beyond its classical division into brown adipose tissue (BAT) and white adipose tissue (WAT), depot-specific features impart distinct metabolic properties and immune microenvironments.2 Each depot contains a complex repertoire of stromal, vascular, and immune cells whose composition and activity are influenced by developmental origin, anatomical location, and environmental cues.3,4 This spatial and cellular heterogeneity underlies the diverse functional outcomes of adipose tissue remodeling in physiology and disease.

Adipose tissue exhibits remarkable plasticity in response to physiological and pathological stimuli, such as nutritional excess, cold exposure, aging, and weight cycling, undergoing structural and functional remodeling.2 Increasing evidence indicates that immune cells are not passive participants in this process but actively coordinate adipocyte turnover, lipid handling, angiogenesis, and thermogenic activation.5,6 Metabolic reprogramming of immune cells enables them to sense and respond to local nutrient, lipid, and oxygen availability, shaping the inflammatory and metabolic milieu.7 Adipocytes influence immune cell phenotypes through lipid mediators, cytokines, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and nutrients, establishing a bidirectional regulatory loop that determines tissue homeostasis or dysfunction.8-11 Disruption of these fine-tuned immune-adipocyte interactions contributes to the chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation characteristic of obesity and related diseases.

Recently, single-cell transcriptomic analysis (single cell RNA-sequencing [scRNA-seq]), spatial profiling, and functional lineage tracing uncovered novel immune cell subsets and depot-specific communication networks.12-14 These advances have deepened the mechanistic understanding of adipose tissue remodeling and revealed new immunometabolic targets for therapeutic intervention. This review synthesizes current knowledge on the cellular and molecular bases of immune-adipocyte crosstalk, key signaling pathways, and translational opportunities for metabolic disease therapy.

MACROPHAGES: GATEKEEPERS OF ADIPOSE TISSUE INTEGRITY

Obesity is a major cause of chronic low-grade inflammation in adipose tissue and is associated with various metabolic diseases, including adipocyte hypertrophy, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hyperlipidemia, and atherosclerosis.15 In lean, visceral adipose tissue (VAT), macrophages are predominantly polarized toward the M2-like phenotype, which plays a critical role in tissue homeostasis.16,17

In obese conditions, macrophage infiltration is markedly elevated, comprising up to 40%–50% of the stromal vascular cell (SVC) population in VAT.18 In contrast to lean adipose tissue enriched in alternatively activated macrophages, obese WAT is dominated by pro-inflammatory macrophages, some of which cluster in crown-like structures (CLSs) around hypertrophic dead/dying adipocytes. These adipocytes, under metabolic stress, release free fatty acids, chemokines, and damage-associated molecular patterns, which activate macrophages and perpetuate local inflammation. The result is chronic, low-grade metaflammation that contributes to systemic insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance.19 While the M1/M2-like polarization model has provided a useful framework to understand macrophage behavior in obesity, recent work underscores that adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) exist along a broad spectrum of activation states, reflecting context-dependent metabolic and environmental cues rather than binary categories.14,20 For example, metabolically activated macrophages acquire a unique lysosomal lipid-catabolizing program distinct from both classical M1/M2-like phenotypes, enabling them to process excess lipids and modulate inflammation.20 Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have further expanded this complexity by identifying novel ATM subpopulations, such as triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 positive (TREM2+) lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs) and transmembrane 4 L six family member 19 positive (TM4SF19+) subpopulations, which emerge specifically under obese conditions.20,21 These findings highlight the diversity and plasticity of ATMs and suggest that their functional role in metabolic disease extends beyond the M1/M2 paradigm. By integrating efferocytosis, lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, and immune regulation, macrophages act as true ‘gatekeepers’ of adipose tissue integrity, buffering metabolic stress or driving chronic inflammation depending on their state.

Lipid-associated macrophages: adaptive and pathological roles in adipose tissue remodeling

TREM2+ macrophages have been identified across multiple metabolic diseases, including obesity, fatty liver disease, and atherosclerosis.20,22-24 In obese adipose tissue, monocyte-derived, metabolically activated macrophages are phenotypically and functionally distinct from tissue resident macrophages. Among these, LAMs represent a specialized TREM2+ subset that expands markedly under metabolic stress.25,26 In WAT, LAMs localize within CLSs surrounding dead or stressed adipocytes, where they facilitate lipid-rich debris clearance and support systemic metabolic homeostasis.

Recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have identified at least two major LAM subpopulations in obese WAT (Fig. 1A): (1) an adaptive subset enriched in lysosomal and lipid-catabolic programs and (2) a maladaptive subset marked by inflammatory and fibrotic signatures.27 Adaptive LAMs express higher levels of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG), lysosomal acid lipase (LIPA), lipoprotein lipase (LPL), and scavenger receptor class B member 2 (SCARB2), reflecting enhanced lipid metabolism and tissue remodeling capacity. In contrast, maladaptive LAMs include inflammation-associated genes such as NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) and nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) and innate immune receptors (toll-like receptor 2 [TLR2] and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 1 [TREM1]) that drive inflammatory signaling and potentiate macrophage activation in response to pathogens. Spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed that, in obesity, maladaptive LAMs are predominantly found as isolated or paired cells, whereas adaptive LAMs tend to cluster within CLSs surrounding transcriptionally inactive adipocytes in obese WAT. Consistent with a protective role, TREM2 deficiency or LAM depletion exacerbates adipocyte hypertrophy, insulin resistance, and systemic glucose intolerance.20,22 However, other reports describe TREM2+ LAMs with pro-inflammatory transcriptional signatures that promote lipid biosynthesis and contribute to insulin resistance.21,23,28,29 Given reports that TREM2 proteolysis in the liver impairs TREM2+ macrophage function,24 we posit that proteolytic cleavage of TREM2 in obese adipose tissue may underlie the functional dichotomy of LAMs by attenuating their beneficial, tissue-remodeling programs. These findings underscore the context-dependent dual nature of LAMs as both beneficial and detrimental regulators of tissue remodeling.

The developmental trajectory of LAMs has recently been clarified by single-cell lineage tracing. A monocytic precursor population termed ‘pre-LAMs’ emerges early during obesity and displays an intermediate transcriptional profile between infiltrating monocytes and mature TREM2+ LAMs (Fig. 1A).26 Pre-LAM clusters express intermediate levels of monocyte (C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1 [Cx3cr1] and lymphocyte antigen 6C2 [Ly6c2]) and LAM markers (galectin 3 [Lgals3], Trem2, and cathepsin L [Ctsl]). Furthermore, pre-LAMs are enriched in ligands such as amyloid beta precursor protein (App), apolipoprotein E (Apoe), and Lpl that engage the LDL receptor related protein 1 (Lrp1) receptor on LAMs, suggesting that they shape the nascent CLS microenvironment. Spatial co-localization of pre-LAMs and LAMs in early obesity supports a coordinated monocyte—pre-LAM—LAM transition that drives chronic inflammation and remodeling in WAT.

In contrast to WAT, LAMs in BAT appear to adopt a more pathological phenotype in obesity and T2DM.25 Metabolically stressed brown adipocytes release EVs enriched in damaged lipids and mitochondria, which are captured by CD36+ LAMs (Fig. 1B). This uptake promotes a foam cell-like phenotype and induces secretion of transforming growth factor β1 (TGFβ1), which stimulates aldehyde dehydrogenase 1A1 (Aldh1a1) expression in brown adipocytes and drives their phenotypic shift toward a white adipocyte-like state. As ALDH1A1 catalyzes the conversion of acetaldehyde to acetate, increased ALDH1A1 expression elevates acetate levels within BAT.30 Elevated acetate has been reported to promote lipogenic gene expression and reduce mitochondrial oxidative activity, facilitating the phenotypic transition of brown adipocytes toward a white adipocyte-like state. TGFβ1 treatment suppresses the expression of brown-fat-related and mitochondrial genes, concomitant with a reduction in Aldh1a1 expression, in primary brown adipocytes. Moreover, co-culture with macrophages exhibiting reduced Tgfβ1 expression failed to repress mitochondrial gene expression in brown adipocytes. While these responses may initially mitigate lipid toxicity, they ultimately impair thermogenic capacity and contribute to BAT dysfunction.

Collectively, these findings position LAMs as central regulators of adipose tissue remodeling, with roles that are adaptive or pathological depending on their ontogeny, spatial context, and tissue environment. Their dual nature highlights the importance of depot-specific and state-specific characterization for considering LAMs as potential therapeutic targets in metabolic disease.

Macrophage lysosomal activity in adipose tissue remodeling

Hypertrophic or dying adipocytes are too large to be internalized by phagocytosis, requiring macrophages in CLSs to adopt specialized clearance strategies. Instead of engulfment, ATMs form a ‘lysosomal synapse’ at the adipocyte surface, fuse lysosomes with the plasma membrane (marked by lysosome associated membrane protein 1/2 [LAMP1/2] exposure), acidify the pericellular space, and secrete catabolic hydrolases such as cathepsins to degrade lipid-rich debris extracellularly.31 Pharmacologic blockade of lysosomal acidification (e.g., bafilomycin) or protease activity (e.g., chloroquine) impairs extracellular lipid hydrolysis and delays adipocyte clearance, demonstrating the functional necessity of this lysosome-dependent pathway.31,32

Our recent work identifies TM4SF19 as a negative regulator of this process: by restraining vacuolar-ATPase assembly, TM4SF19 limits lysosomal acidification in macrophages. Its loss enhances acidification and accelerates clearance of dying adipocytes in vivo, as visualized by two-photon intravital imaging (Fig. 2A).21 We also propose that dampened lysosomal programs bias LAMs toward maladaptive phenotypes, whereas restoration of lysosomal acidification; for example, via TM4SF19 loss-of-function, favors adaptive programs and may permit a state transition from TREM2+ LAM-like to lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 positive (LYVE1+) resident-like macrophages (Fig. 2B). These data highlight the importance of regulated lysosomal function not only for debris removal but also for balancing inflammatory outcomes during adipose tissue remodeling.

Although still an emerging concept in adipose biology, lysosome-dependent extracellular digestion, sometimes described as ‘lysosomal exocytosis-like’ clearance, is well established in other systems. Osteoclasts resorb bone by creating an acidic lysosomal synapse at the bone surface, neutrophils and cytotoxic T cells release lysosomal enzymes at immune synapses, and tumor-associated macrophages exploit lysosomal exocytosis to remodel extracellular matrix (ECM) during cancer invasion.33-36 The parallels suggest that ATMs may employ a conserved lysosomal program adapted to the unique challenge of oversized adipocytes.

Taken together, these findings suggest that lysosome-dependent clearance represents an adaptive mechanism for managing hypertrophic adipocytes, essential for lipid homeostasis and tissue remodeling.21,31,32 Its regulation, through molecules such as TM4SF19, may determine whether this process promotes metabolic balance or contributes to chronic inflammation.21

T CELL SUBSETS AND REGULATORY NETWORKS IN ADIPOSE TISSUE

T cells play a pivotal role in both adipose tissue homeostasis and the development of metabolic disorders.37 Under physiological conditions, diverse T cell subsets contribute to the maintenance of an anti-inflammatory microenvironment within adipose tissue.38 Regulatory T cells (Tregs) accumulate in VAT to maintain systemic metabolic homeostasis by suppressing inflammation and preserving insulin sensitivity through interleukin 10 (IL-10) production.39 Invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells, enriched in adipose depots, contribute to tissue homeostasis by promoting type 2 immunity.40 Adipocytes can directly regulate CD4+ T cell function by releasing lipids that promote T cell proliferation and tissue residency.11

Under pathological conditions such as obesity, the balance shifts toward pro-inflammatory T cell activity.41 CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, T helper type 1 (Th1) cells, and T helper 17 (Th17) cells infiltrate adipose tissue early, often preceding macrophage accumulation, and secrete interferon gamma (IFNγ), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), and interleukin 17A (IL-17A).41,42 These cytokines activate pro-inflammatory macrophages, impair insulin signaling, and establish chronic inflammation.43 Simultaneously, protective subsets such as Tregs and iNKT cells are depleted in obese VAT.39,40

Specialized T cell subsets including gamma delta T cells (γδ T cells) contribute to thermogenic responses by producing IL-17A and TNFα, which promote adaptive thermogenesis in brown/beige adipose tissues through interleukin 33 (IL-33) signaling in progenitor cells.44 Two distinct VAT Treg populations with non-overlapping roles in systemic metabolism were recently described.45 Moreover, pathways such as CD27/CD70 signaling in T helper type 2 (Th2) cells46 and innate programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) checkpoints47 fine-tune the immune balance.

Altogether, these findings position adipose tissue T cells as dynamic coordinators of metabolic health, capable of exerting both protective and pathological effects depending on their subset, activation state, and metabolic context (Table 1). Their plasticity and tissue-specific adaptations indicate them as attractive targets for immunometabolic interventions in obesity and related diseases

Regulatory T cells in metabolic homeostasis and obesity

Adipose-resident Tregs are phenotypically distinct from their lymphoid counterparts, exhibiting high expression of PPARγ and a unique transcriptional signature tailored to the lipid-rich microenvironment.48 Under homeostatic conditions, Tregs suppress effector T cell activation and inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine production, preserving adipocyte function and systemic metabolic health.49 In BAT, Tregs preserve thermogenic function by locally restraining IFNγ-producing lymphocytes and preventing mitochondrial dysfunction.50 VAT Tregs comprise functionally distinct subsets with sex-specific distributions: serum stimulating 2 positive (ST2+) Tregs that are enriched in males and express high levels of PPARγ, GATA binding protein 3 (GATA3), and IL-33 receptor, as well as C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 positive (CXCR3+) Tregs that are more abundant in females and express T-box transcription factor 21 (T-bet) and IFNγ response genes.45 The ST2+ subset specifically depends on PPARγ-mediated transcriptional programming for its tissue accumulation and metabolic adaptations.45 In contrast, CXCR3+ VAT Tregs, induced by Th1-mediated IFNγ, suppress pro-inflammatory responses.45 Recent mechanistic studies reveal that gut microbiota-derived butyrate acts as an endogenous PPARγ agonist, directly binding to PPARγ and promoting ST2+ Treg expansion through enhanced PPARγ-mediated transcriptional activity.51

Treg cells residing in VAT of lean mice and humans directly limit the differentiation of local adipocyte precursors to preserve insulin sensitivity and metabolic homeostasis through oncostatin-M (OSM) signaling as a critical mediator.52 Exogenous OSM blocked precursor differentiation, and neutralizing anti-OSM antibodies restored adipogenesis despite the presence of Treg-derived factors.52

Obesity profoundly alters the abundance, phenotype, and functionality of VAT Tregs.48 Functionally, Treg depletion promotes chronic VAT inflammation, enhances effector T cell activity, and exacerbates systemic insulin resistance.49,53 Studies demonstrate that diet-induced obesity reduces Treg numbers via multiple mechanisms: disruption of local cholesterol homeostasis,54 altered circadian rhythm regulation,55 accumulation of dendritic cells (DCs),56,57 and inflammatory cytokine-mediated depletion.58,59

Sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor 2 (SREBF2)-mediated cholesterol biosynthesis is required for accumulation of ST2-hi VAT Treg but not for that of other tissue Tregs.54 High-fat diet (HFD) feeding disrupts the expression of cholesterol biosynthesis genes in VAT Tregs, impairing their clonal expansion and T cell receptor (TCR) signaling capacity.54 Importantly, restoring cholesterol homeostasis rescues VAT Treg populations in obese mice.54

VAT Tregs exhibit robust upregulation of core circadian clock genes, particularly basic helix-loop-helix ARNT-like 1 (BMAL1).55 BMAL1 deficiency in Tregs leads to constitutive activation, impaired oxidative metabolism, and loss of the characteristic diurnal variation in adipocyte lipolysis, resulting in enhanced adiposity and VAT inflammation during HFD challenge.55

VAT DCs emerge as critical regulators of Treg homeostasis during obesity. Plasmacytoid DCs accumulate in VAT in response to HFD and secrete interferon alfa (IFNα), which selectively suppresses PPARγ+ VAT Treg proliferation and induces apoptosis.56 Obese VAT DCs specifically downregulate IL-33 production in response to saturated fatty acids, reducing their capacity to support Treg differentiation and maintenance.57

Helper T cell subsets and immune balance

Beyond Tregs, helper T cell subsets, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, contribute to the immunological tone of adipose tissue.37 Th1 cells secrete IFNγ, which drives macrophages toward an M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotype.41 This shift amplifies adipose tissue inflammation by upregulating inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and TNFα, impairing insulin signaling in adipocytes and promoting adipocyte hypertrophy.60 In contrast, Th2 cells produce interleukin 4 (IL-4) and interleukin 13 (IL-13), cytokines that support M2-like macrophage activation, stimulate ECM remodeling via arginase-1 induction, and enhance tissue repair processes.61 These Th2-driven pathways contribute to the maintenance of insulin sensitivity and lipid handling in both BAT and WAT.62 Also, resident Th2 cells coordinate remodeling of mesenteric adipose tissue adjacent to intestinal infection by interacting with stromal progenitor cells to promote tissue adaptation and immune defense.63 Notably, obesity skews the Th1/Th2 balance toward a pro-inflammatory state, with reduced Th2 activity and impaired capacity for adipose tissue remodeling.37 CD27/CD70 co-stimulatory signaling pathways regulate programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) expression on fat-resident Th2 cells in an intrinsic manner, which shifts the fate of Th2 toward activation-induced cell death.46 Th17 cells in adipose tissue modulate metabolic homeostasis and inflammation primarily through their signature cytokines.37 IL-17A directly inhibits the differentiation of preadipocytes into mature adipocytes and promotes neutrophil recruitment into adipose depots, amplifying local inflammatory responses.64

OTHER IMMUNE-ADIPOCYTE CROSSTALK IN ADIPOSE TISSUE REMODELING

B cell accumulation and function in adipose tissue

B lymphocytes, traditionally recognized for their role in adaptive immunity and antibody production, have emerged as important modulators of adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic homeostasis.65 Under physiological conditions, although lean adipose tissue contains a relatively small fraction of B cells in SVCs, there are phenotypically distinct subsets of B cells. Regulatory B cells (Bregs) residing in adipose tissue constitutively produce anti-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-10, and B-1 cells secrete natural immunoglobulin M (IgM), which helps maintain homeostasis and prevents excessive inflammation.66-68 However, under metabolic stresses, the B cell compartment undergoes significant quantitative and qualitative changes that exacerbate metabolic dysfunction.

Obese adipose tissue demonstrates increased B cell infiltration, particularly of pro-inflammatory B-2 subsets that secrete pathogenic immunoglobulin G (IgG) and inflammatory cytokines.69,70 B cells are also enriched in fat-associated lymphoid clusters (FALCs) and in CLSs surrounding stressed adipocytes in VAT.71,72 Conversely, during obesity development, Bregs decline in frequency and IL-10 output, weakening their suppression of adipose inflammation.66 Concomitantly, a distinct population of pro-inflammatory B cells, characterized by the transcription factor T-bet, accumulates within VAT.73 These T-bet+ B cells produce pathogenic IgG antibodies and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα and interleukin 6 (IL-6), which amplify local macrophage activation and impair insulin signaling in adipocytes.73 Functional depletion of these B cells via genetic or pharmacologic approaches attenuates adipose inflammation and improves systemic glucose tolerance, underscoring their pathogenic contribution.73 Mechanistically, T-bet+ B cells interact directly with macrophages through Fc receptor engagement and cytokine signaling, creating a feed-forward loop that sustains chronic metaflammation.73

Beyond obesity, B cell accumulation also occurs with aging.74 Specifically, aged adipose B cells (AABs) are a distinct population localized within FALCs.75 AABs expand in VAT via an NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent mechanism and contribute to impaired lipolysis and metabolic dysfunction.75 In sepsis, age-associated B cells infiltrate adipose tissue and aggravate local inflammation by promoting macrophage activation while simultaneously inhibiting adipocyte lipolysis.76 This dual action not only worsens acute inflammatory damage but also limits the mobilization of lipid stores needed for energy balance during systemic infection.76 These observations highlight the broader immunometabolic relevance of B cell activity in adipose tissue, spanning chronic metabolic diseases to acute inflammatory syndromes.

Exhaustion and metabolic regulation of group 2 innate lymphoid cells

Group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) are critical regulators of adipose tissue homeostasis and metabolic function.77 Functionally analogous to Th2 cells but lacking antigen-specific receptors, these cells respond to IL-33 and interleukin 25 (IL-25) stimulation by producing type 2 cytokines, particularly interleukin 5 (IL-5) and IL-13. These cytokines recruit eosinophils and promote alternatively activated M2-like macrophage differentiation in adipose tissue.77,78 In lean adipose tissue, ILC2s contribute to maintenance of metabolic homeostasis by promoting a type 2 immune environment that favors insulin sensitivity and limits chronic inflammation.79 Furthermore, ILC2s directly promote WAT browning through the production of methionine-enkephalin peptides that stimulate uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) expression in adipocytes, enhancing thermogenesis and energy expenditure independent of the adaptive immune system.80 Additionally, ILC2s support local Treg proliferation through inducible T cell costimulator ligand (ICOSL)-inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS) and tumor necrosis factor superfamily member 4 (TNFSF4; OX40L)-tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, member 4 (TNFRSF4; OX40) co-stimulatory pathways, further reinforcing immune tolerance and insulin sensitivity.81

In obesity, ILC2 abundance and function decline significantly (Fig. 3).82 Pro-inflammatory cytokines from M1-like macrophages drive PD-1 upregulation on ILC2s, leading to reduced eosinophil recruitment, impaired M2 polarization, and heightened insulin resistance.59,83 Adipose-resident ILC2s are metabolically tuned to their nutrient-rich microenvironment. The metabolic regulator liver kinase B1 (LKB1) serves as a critical regulator of ILC2 function by maintaining mitochondrial integrity, sustaining oxidative phosphorylation, and restraining aberrant PD-1 expression.84 Disruption of LKB1 signaling in obesity leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive and pathological PD-1 upregulation, ultimately impairing type 2 cytokine production and exacerbating insulin resistance.84

The PD-1-fatty acid binding protein 5 (FABP5) axis has recently been identified as a novel immunometabolic checkpoint.85 PD-1 signaling suppresses FABP5 expression, compromising fatty acid uptake and energy metabolism critical for ILC2 effector functions. Elevated osteopontin (OPN) levels in obese adipose tissue exacerbate this dysfunction by reinforcing PD-1 expression, leading to ILC2 exhaustion.85 This OPN-PD-1-FABP5 pathway appears conserved across species, strengthening its translational relevance.85

Therapeutically, ILC2 function can be restored. Pharmacologic activation of cannabinoid receptor 2 (CB2) promotes ILC2 proliferation and type 2 cytokine production via AKT serine/threonine kinase (AKT), extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs), and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) response element-binding protein (CREB) signaling; re-establishes IL-13-dependent immune balance; and improves glucose tolerance in T2DM models.86 Together, these findings highlight multiple immunometabolic checkpoints in ILC2s, including LKB1 signaling, the PD-1-FABP5 axis, and CB2 activation, as promising targets for therapeutic intervention in obesity and related metabolic diseases.

Emerging immune cell states revealed by single-cell and spatial transcriptomics

Recent advances in scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics have revolutionized our understanding of adipose tissue immunity, revealing cellular heterogeneity and novel immune cell states previously unrecognized in bulk analyses. scRNA-seq demonstrated that innate lymphoid cell (ILC) precursor-like cells act as progenitors for group 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1) and group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3) lineages depending on contextual cues but exhibit minimal association with ILC2 differentiation.87 scRNA-seq also identified two functionally distinct Treg subsets in VAT: CXCR3+ Tregs mediate adenosine-dependent immunosuppression and interact with CXCR3-ligand expressing DCs and macrophages. ST2+ Tregs promote tissue repair through amphiregulin and IL-10 production while regulating interleukin 2 (IL-2) availability through high CD25 expression.45 scRNA-seq revealed distinct iNKT subpopulations: killer cell lectin like receptor G1 positive (KLGR1+) iNKT cells are fat-resident and differentiate into CX3CR1+ cytotoxic cells that eliminate inflamed adipocytes and recruit macrophages via C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5). iNKT17 cells produce amphiregulin to promote the proliferation of progenitors.88

Spatial transcriptomics has further delineated how immune cells and adipocytes organize into functional niches. Stress-associated niches enriched in Jun proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit positive (JUN+)/NFKB inhibitor zeta positive (NFKBIZ+) inflammatory adipocytes and NADPH oxidase 4 positive (NOX4+)/lysyl oxidase positive (LOX+) fibrotic adipocytes expand in obesity but regress with weight loss.27 These analyses demonstrate a positive correlation between adipocyte hypertrophy and stressed/fibrotic gene signatures, alongside an inverse association with homeostatic transcriptional programs. Importantly, HFD-induced obesity drives regional enrichment of TREM2+ LAMs in CLSs, where they display elevated expression of genes linked to phagocytosis, autophagy, and cell death, consistent with their localized role in clearing apoptotic adipocytes and excess lipids.26

Together, these studies underscore the dynamic spectrum of adipose tissue immunity, with discrete and spatially restricted cell states that adapt to depot-specific metabolic and structural demands. This emerging view shifts the paradigm from binary classifications (e.g., M1/M2-like macrophages, Tregs/effector T cells) to a continuum of adaptive states shaped by local metabolic cues and tissue architecture.

BIOCHEMICAL SIGNALING AT THE IMMUNE-ADIPOSE TISSUE INTERFACE

Communication between immune cells and adipose tissue occurs through both direct cell-cell interactions and indirect molecular signaling. At the adipose-immune interface, intercellular communication is orchestrated by a broad spectrum of signaling molecules that allow cells to sense and adapt to environmental cues.

As numerous comprehensive reviews have detailed the role of cytokines and chemokines in metaflammation, we provide only a concise overview, while emphasizing additional, emerging classes of signaling mediators. Communication at the adipose-immune interface occurs not only via classical protein cytokines, but also through lipid-derived mediators,89 metabolites, and EVs.90 These non-canonical mediators have recently been recognized as critical intercellular messengers that extend the signaling repertoire of adipose tissue beyond cytokines alone.

Together, these diverse pathways, including cytokine networks, lipid mediators, metabolites, and EVs, constitute the molecular language by which adipocytes and immune cells dynamically regulate immunometabolic homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, this communication supports adipose plasticity and systemic metabolic health; in obesity, it amplifies inflammatory stress and metabolic dysfunction.

Cytokines as mediators of immune-adipocyte crosstalk

Cytokines are key molecular mediators of immune-adipocyte communication, integrating metabolic and inflammatory signals. Representative, well-studied examples include IL-4, IL-6, IFNγ, and TNFα, each with context-dependent effects on adipose tissue remodeling.

IL-4 is predominantly produced by eosinophils within adipose tissue and plays a crucial role in glucose homeostasis.91 In the obese state, IL-4 levels decline markedly, a change implicated in dysregulation of leptin secretion from adipocytes.92 Functionally, IL-4 promotes M2-like polarization of ATMs, sustaining an anti-inflammatory microenvironment in lean adipose tissue. Beyond immunoregulation, IL-4 regulates systemic metabolism by modulating glucose homeostasis and lipid metabolism through protein kinase A (PKA) signaling. Moreover, IL-4 has been shown to inhibit adipogenesis93 and activates a futile lipid cycle in 3T3–L1 adipocytes. These multifaceted actions highlight IL-4 as a key integrator of immune regulation and metabolic control.

IL-6 has been widely studied but remains controversial. Traditionally viewed as a pro-inflammatory cytokine secreted by M1-like macrophages, IL-6 is also produced by adipocytes, particularly under stress.94 Hypertrophic adipocytes recruit IL-6-producing macrophages via C-C motif chemokines, amplifying inflammation. However, adipocyte-derived IL-6 can paradoxically promote M2-like polarization through signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling and has been linked to improved systemic glucose metabolism in some contexts, possibly via enhanced leptin secretion and secondary glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) release from pancreatic islets.95 These discrepancies likely reflect cellular source-specific effects and the distinction between canonical IL-6 signaling and trans-signaling mediated by ADAM metallopeptidase domain 10/17 (ADAM10/17) metalloproteases, which confer divergent outcomes.96

IFNγ, primarily produced by natural killer (NK) cells, Th1 cells, and CD8+ T cells, promotes pro-inflammatory macrophage activation and reduces Treg abundance, exacerbating adipose tissue inflammation.58,97 In BAT, Tregs counteract IFNγ-driven suppression of thermogenesis by restraining IFNγ-producing lymphocytes.50 Moreover, obesity enhances IFNγ production through IL-12-driven recruitment of ILC1, and elevated IFNγ further promotes pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages, contributing to insulin resistance.98

TNFα, a cytokine derived from both macrophages and adipocytes, impairs insulin signaling and promotes adipocyte lipolysis.99 In addition, TNFα prevents adipogenesis via the β-catenin/T-cell factor 4 (TCF4) pathway in 3T3–L1 adipocytes,100 yet it can also contribute to resolution phases when regulated by maresin (MaR), which is produced in BAT during cold exposure.101

Together, these cytokines illustrate the dual and often paradoxical functions of adipose tissue signaling networks, supporting homeostasis under lean conditions but fueling inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in obesity.

Lipid mediators

Dynamic metabolic alterations in adipose tissue profoundly influence lipolysis, which generates fatty acids that not only serve as energy substrates but also act as precursors for bioactive lipid mediators. Among these, eicosanoids derived from arachidonic acids are one of the major lipid species produced in adipose tissue and regulate diverse aspects of immune and metabolic functions.

Prostaglandins (PGs) represent a major class of eicosanoids with pleiotropic roles. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), released during adipocyte lipolysis and cytosolic phospholipase A2α (cPLA2α) and cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2) activation, enhances macrophage recruitment into adipose tissue (Fig. 4).102 Interestingly, a subset of these recruited macrophages exhibits anti-inflammatory properties, suggesting that PGE2 can skew macrophage polarization toward an alternatively activated phenotype.102 While PGs are classically recognized for their capacity to regulate macrophage pro-inflammatory activation and recruitment in adipose tissue, accumulating evidence indicates that they also orchestrate adipose tissue remodeling and contribute to whole-body metabolic regulation by influencing insulin sensitivity. For instance, deletion of the prostaglandin E2 receptor 3 (EP3) in macrophages exacerbates diet-induced obesity by downregulating secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich (SPARC), a matricellular protein important for ECM remodeling and insulin sensitivity. Mechanistically, PGE2 binding to EP3 normally activates Gi signaling, leading to inhibition of cAMP/PKA and subsequent suppression of Sp1 transcription factor (SP1) phosphorylation. This, in turn, maintains the expression of DNA methyltransferases 1/3a (DNMT1/3a), which regulate the methylation state of the SPARC promoter. In the absence of EP3, SP1 phosphorylation is disinhibited, leading to reduced DNMT1/3a expression and hypermethylation of the SPARC promoter to disrupt adipogenesis and insulin sensitivity via Wnt/β-catenin signaling (Fig. 4).103 Moreover, COX2 derived PGE2 in ATM signals through prostaglandin E2 receptor 4 (EP4) to restrain macrophage proliferation and adipose tissue fibrosis, preserving insulin sensitivity in obese mice.104 Prostacyclin (PGI) has also been shown to support adipogenesis and adipocyte maturation, processes that facilitate healthy adipose expansion and indirectly limit inflammation.105

Beyond arachidonic acid-derived mediators, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) give rise to specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs), including resolvins (RvDs) and MaRs, which exert anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects. In obese or diabetic mouse models, administration of RvD1 markedly reduces macrophage accumulation in WAT and improves insulin sensitivity.106 Both RvD1 and RvD2 suppress monocyte adhesion to adipocytes and inhibit their migration into adipose tissue, limiting CLS formation and inflammation.107 MaRs also play depot-specific roles. Cold exposure induces MaR production in BAT, which contributes to inflammation resolution and helps maintain glucose homeostasis.101 Furthermore, both RvDs and MaRs act as positive allosteric modulators of the EP4 receptor in collaboration with PGE2. These lipid mediators allosterically enhance PGE2-induced activation of both Gαs and Gαi-type G-proteins, leading to pro-phagocytic effects via Gαi-mediated phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) activation and promoting anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving responses via Gs-mediated cAMP signaling.108

Extracellular vesicles

EVs, including exosomes and microvesicles, are increasingly recognized as essential mediators of intercellular communication in adipose tissue. By directly transferring bioactive molecules, such as lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and even organelles, EVs influence both local immune responses and systemic metabolic regulation.

Adipocyte-derived exosomes (AdExos) are enriched in lipids and can be generated via lipase-independent lipolysis.109 These vesicles not only provide a lipid reservoir for tissue-resident macrophages but also induce their differentiation toward ATM-like phenotypes, marked by increased expression of fatty acid binding protein 4 (Fabp4), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (Cxcl1), and Cd209f, reshaping the immune landscape of adipose tissue.109 In addition to lipid cargo, EVs serve as vehicles for inter-organelle transfer: adipocytes can deliver mitochondria to macrophages through EVs, enhancing oxidative metabolism and supporting tissue homeostasis. This process is impaired in obesity, leading to dysfunctional ATMs and reduced metabolic flexibility.110

Beyond adipocyte-macrophage crosstalk, ATMs themselves release EVs that carry ferritin and other iron-binding proteins, enabling them to modulate systemic iron metabolism. This pathway influences not only local redox balance but also whole-body metabolic outcomes.111

Collectively, these findings establish adipose tissue-derived EVs as critical mediators of metabolic crosstalk. They not only regulate macrophage function and systemic energy homeostasis but also provide novel therapeutic and diagnostic opportunities for obesity and related metabolic diseases.

CONCLUSION

Obesity drives profound remodeling of adipose tissue, characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy, local hypoxia, and recruitment of diverse immune cells. Over the past two decades, advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have revealed a rich heterogeneity of immune cell populations within adipose tissue, ranging from macrophage subsets such as LAMs and perivascular macrophages to specialized lymphocyte populations including Tregs, γδ T cells, B cells, and ILC2s. These cells collectively act as central regulators of adipose tissue integrity, linking local tissue stress with systemic metabolic outcomes.

Macrophages emerge as gatekeepers of tissue homeostasis, balancing adaptive clearance functions with pro-inflammatory responses. Enhancing beneficial macrophage programs, such as LAM-mediated lipid handling or lysosome-dependent adipocyte clearance, may help buffer obesity-associated inflammation. Conversely, dysregulated macrophage signaling contributes to chronic metaflammation and systemic insulin resistance, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting macrophage plasticity.

Adaptive and innate lymphocytes exert equally critical influences. Expansion of Tregs, modulation of Th1/Th17 responses, and regulation of B cell-derived inflammatory pathways can restore immune balance in obesity. Likewise, sustaining ILC2 function through pathways such as LKB1, PD-1-FABP5, or CB2 signaling offers new opportunities to preserve type 2 immunity, promote thermogenesis, and maintain metabolic resilience.

Beyond immune cell dynamics, intercellular communication via cytokines, lipid mediators, metabolites, and EVs represents a molecular language that integrates immune and metabolic signals. While cytokines such as TNFα, IL-6, and IFNγ have long been implicated in obesity-associated inflammation, more recent studies highlight the importance of SPMs, EV cargos, and metabolic byproducts in shaping immune responses. These findings expand the repertoire of therapeutic targets beyond classical inflammatory pathways.

Looking forward, several perspectives emerge. First, depot-specific and context-dependent immune cell programs must be systematically defined using integrated single-cell, spatial, and multi-omics approaches. Second, immunometabolic therapies should seek not only to suppress inflammation but also to restore adaptive immune-adipocyte interactions; for instance, by increasing SPMs or enhancing mitochondrial transfer. Third, translation into human systems remains a critical challenge; interventions will require precise targeting of conserved pathways while accounting for human-specific immune heterogeneity.

In conclusion, immune-adipocyte crosstalk is a central driver of adipose tissue remodeling and systemic metabolic health. By unraveling the diversity of immune cell states and the biochemical mediators of their communication, the field is advancing toward a more nuanced and therapeutically actionable understanding of metaflammation. Future research will need to bridge mechanistic insights with translational strategies, ultimately enabling immunometabolic interventions to prevent and treat obesity-associated metabolic disease.
