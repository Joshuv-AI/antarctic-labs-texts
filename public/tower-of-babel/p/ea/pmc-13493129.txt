The Current State of in vitro Diagnostics Evaluation in Sub-Saharan African Countries: Insights from the Advanced Workshop
Authors: Jens Reinhardt, Chancelar Kafere, Albert Maqolo, Goran Abdurrahman, Emmanuel Willie, Aggrey Phiri, Kapunda Masuwa, Frank Laban, Abhimanyu Shivhare, Ivan Emil Mwikarago, Khanyisile Zachia Nkuku, Erick Kitangala, Hafiza Moges, Edina Zebedayo, Joseph Donkor Issachar, Linda Nhukarume, Arinzechukwu Ikemefuna, Saren Shifotoka, Lilian Mutesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough the African Union Development Agency-New Partnership for Africa’s Development (AUDA-NEPAD) has initiated activities to support the establishment of a harmonized regulatory framework for in vitro diagnostics (IVDs), including those for testing blood and blood components, challenges such as limited infrastructure and insufficient technical expertise remain. The Global Health Protection Programme (GHPP) project BloodTrain, which seeks to strengthen regulatory systems for blood and blood products in Africa, addressed this by organizing an advanced workshop focused on IVD technical assessment. Data on national IVD regulatory systems were also collected through a structured questionnaire. Furthermore, a survey was conducted to evaluate both the perceived usefulness of the training and the applicability of the tools provided.MethodsAn advanced training was conducted for personnel from 14 African national regulatory authorities who possessed prior knowledge and experience in the assessment of IVDs. The participants were also granted access to online courses on IVD technical file assessment. The workshop comprised theory and case study exercises by experts from TÜV Rheinland and BloodTrain, complemented by presentations from the participants on their national IVD regulatory systems. These presentations led to the development of a questionnaire to achieve a comparison of these regulatory systems.ResultsIn the survey, the workshop content resulted in a mean score of 4.5 on a 5-point scale. Lesser mean scores on workshop duration and previous knowledge to follow the workshop underline the need for additional training on IVD assessment. The comparative assessment of the regulatory criteria for IVDs collected by a post-workshop questionnaire showed the majority of the participating countries already had some level of regulation on IVDs, but there are still gaps that need to be addressed.ConclusionThe advanced IVD workshop provided well-appreciated training on IVD dossier assessment and also delivered understanding of the heterogenous regulatory landscape in the participating countries. While the questionnaire suggested that in most of the participating countries regulatory systems for IVDs are already in use, the survey painted a differentiated picture with heterogenous preexisting knowledge and the expectation that the trained content will be helpful in the future.

Introduction

A challenge that the African continent is facing is the lack of its own regulatory framework and a harmonized approach for regulating IVDs. A report by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2017 has shed light on this issue and found that 40% of countries in the African region have no regulatory framework for medical devices, 32% have some regulations, and the remaining 28% have no available data [1].

Many parts of the African continent face unique challenges in regulating IVDs and especially in the performance evaluation of the device claims. This is due to two main issues. Firstly, many countries lack comprehensive regulatory systems for IVDs, resulting in the use of devices that may not meet international standards. Secondly, limited resources and infrastructure hinder the implementation of stringent regulatory practices on both the national and continental levels.

In the last years, there have been attempts on the continental level to develop a harmonized regulation of IVDs in Africa, e.g., through the Africa Medical Devices Forum (AMDF), supported by the African Union Development Agency (AUDA-NEPAD) [2]. This is a process requiring multiple stakeholder engagements and has proven to be challenging. However, a regulatory framework specifically designed for Africa does not necessarily need to reinvent the wheel but can benefit from the experiences gained in other parts of the world.

The GHPP BloodTrain is a project that aims to strengthen the regulatory systems for blood and blood products in sub-Saharan African partner countries. The project, which is funded by the German Federal Ministry of Health, provides technical support on the development and implementation of regulatory documents in national regulatory authorities and performs online and on-site workshops on different issues like authorization and licensing of blood establishments, hemovigilance, and assessment of technical documentation for in vitro diagnostics. The overall goal is the provision of safe, efficacious, and quality-assured blood and blood products [3].

Regulatory oversight of associated substances and medical devices, including in vitro diagnostics, is vital in ensuring that patients have access to safe, effective, and quality-assured blood products. Several associated substances and medical devices, including IVDs, are used during transfusion, blood screening, and blood and blood component preparation. The training on technical file assessment of blood-related IVDs addressed some of the gaps observed during the assisted-self-benchmarking activities for blood and associated substances in BloodTrain partner countries.

A benchmarking to pilot the WHO Global Benchmarking Tool + Blood revealed, among other aspects, an absence of requisite technical competencies in NRAs for technical file assessment of IVDs [4]. The above situations informed the here-described training workshop in Africa. In addition to training the participants, this was also used to gather more information on the current status of regulation of IVDs in the participating countries.

Methods

An advanced training workshop was organized for staff from regulatory authorities in Africa which focused on the following aspects: (i) improvement of skills and competencies in IVD technical file assessment, (ii) familiarization with other international specifications for blood screening IVDs, (iii) discussions on reliance in regulation of IVDs for blood screening, and (iv) sharing of best practices and experiences among NRAs in IVD regulation. NRAs nominated staff with background knowledge and experience in technical file assessment of medical devices and IVDs to participate in the workshop. In preparation for the workshop, nominated participants were introduced to the e-learning platform of the BloodTrain for the basic courses in technical file assessment. The workshop was held from 30 July to first August 2024 at Sainte Famille Hotel in Kigali, Rwanda.

During the workshop, presentations on selected topics prepared participants with the requisite knowledge and understanding for practical group exercises in evaluating IVD technical files. Participants were given IVD product dossier materials and related assessment checklists on the following areas: analytical performance characteristics, clinical performance, instructions for use (IFU) studies, and stability studies. BloodTrain staff and the TÜV Rheinland expert coordinated the group presentations and discussions on findings from the assessments. Furthermore, participants gave presentations on the status of the regulatory system for IVDs in their respective countries.

To evaluate the workshop, an anonymous survey was conducted via SoSci Survey to solicit participants’ feedback. The online survey link was shared with all participants at the conclusion of the training workshop.

Based on the presentations that were given by representatives of the 14 countries that participated in the workshop, a more detailed questionnaire was developed to systematically evaluate and compare the status of IVD regulation in these countries after the workshop. The questionnaire (Table 1) was divided into three sections: Regulatory status of IVDs, availability of IVDs in the countries, and regulatory approach in the National Competent Authorities (NCAs). The questionnaire was sent out via email. The questionnaire was sent out via email, with responses collected until the data lock point of September 2024.

Workshop Scope and Program

On the first day of the training workshop, Dr. Reinhardt, head of the BloodTrain project, opened with an overview of the project’s objectives, achievements, and future directions. Representatives from BloodTrain partner countries (Ghana, Nigeria, Tanzania, Zambia, and Zimbabwe) presented summaries of their regulatory systems for IVDs, providing context for regional challenges and progress. Dr. Abdurrahman (TÜV Rheinland) then outlined the European Union’s regulatory framework for IVDs, offering insights into a mature regulatory system as a learning model. Dr. Reinhardt followed with a presentation emphasizing the critical role of IVDs in minimizing the risk of transfusion-transmitted bloodborne infections. The subsequent session, led by Mr. Kafere (BloodTrain), introduced the structure and formats of IVD technical files. Mr. Maqolo (BloodTrain) detailed the Essential Principles of Safety and Performance for IVDs, including requirements for design and manufacturing information, while Ms. Nhukarume (Medicines Control Authority of Zimbabwe) explained risk management principles and key considerations for evaluating a manufacturer’s risk management system within the technical file. The day concluded with Dr. Abdurrahman’s presentation on analytical performance characteristics, followed by a practical group exercise where participants assessed analytical studies from a mock IVD dossier.

The second day began with presentations on regulatory systems from representatives of Botswana, Ethiopia, Kenya, Liberia, and Malawi, further broadening the regional perspective. Dr. Abdurrahman then discussed the evaluation of clinical performance, focusing on diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, which led to a practical exercise, where participants reviewed model clinical studies from IVD dossiers. Mr. Kafere concluded the day with a session on stability studies, covering shelf-life, in-use stability, and shipping stability, followed by a practical exercise to evaluate provided stability study data.

The third day opened with presentations on regulatory systems from Namibia, Rwanda, South Africa, and Uganda. Dr. Reinhardt then covered labeling requirements and IFU, followed by a final practical exercise where participants assessed sample IFUs. The workshop concluded with Mr. Laban’s (ZAMRA) presentation on regulatory history and post-market surveillance activities, summarizing key considerations for ongoing IVD oversight.

Results

Results of Participants’ Evaluation Survey

All 21 participants completed the survey. Figure 1 represents the part of the survey addressing the results for the content of the workshop, including its objectives, the training materials, participants’ prior knowledge, and its applicability to professional work. This resulted in a mean score of 4.5 out of 5, where 1 means “strongly disagree” and 5 means “strongly agree” (data not shown). Some aspects of the survey are explicitly mentioned here because they underline that the workshop was seen as important and helpful (shown in Fig. 1). The participants felt that the workshop was well organized (average rating of 4.8), that it helped understand the underlying concepts (average rating of 4.5), and that the participants felt that the content enhanced their knowledge on technical file assessment for blood screening IVDs (rating 4.6). The content was considered useful for their professional work, and they will be able to apply the knowledge (both rating 4.6). The average rating regarding the duration of the workshop sessions was lower (rating 3.4). This rating was supported by additional written comments received with the survey, expressing preferred longer and more detailed training (data not shown). The average rating on the question, of whether the participants had sufficient previous knowledge to follow the workshop, was also low (3.5). A more detailed analysis of the data showed that 12 participants agreed or strongly agreed and three participants disagreed or strongly disagreed (data not shown). This supports the notion that pre-workshop knowledge on IVD regulation varied between participating countries. The other parts of the survey (performance of the trainers, use of the pre-recorded material, and written comments of the participants) were left out to keep this article focussed. However, the mean score in all measurable areas was always above 4. If participants form the same countries may have given different answers, this would not be seen in the survey because the results provided to the authors were completely anonymized.

Results of the Questionnaire

The questionnaire was divided into three sections: (i) the regulatory status of IVDs in the countries; (ii) the availability of IVDs in the countries; and (iii) the regulatory approach in the countries (Table 1). Concerning the first regulatory section, 12 of the 14 countries already included IVDs in their national medicines law. The two others reported that this is about to be included with the next revision of their medicine law. Eleven countries have a legal framework for IVDs. Of the remaining countries, two declared preparing relevant legislative documents. Nine countries have guidelines for IVDs in place; three are currently drafting guidelines or are awaiting their approval. In 13 of the 14 participating countries, the IVD licensing is a function of the NCA. However, in six countries, this function is also partly performed by other entities, which are mostly national medical laboratory organizations. The question of whether assessors for the technical assessment of IVD documentation are available and adequately trained was answered positively by 11 representatives and negatively by three. However, of the 11, four pointed out that there is additional training needed.

Concerning the second section related to availability of IVDs, 13 of the 14 countries reported the availability of IVD products, and both rapid test devices and semiautomated/automated systems were reported to be in use. Six country representatives confirmed in-country manufacturing of IVDs. However, the questionnaire did not capture which IVDs are manufactured in these countries. Eight countries reported no in-country manufacture. Moreover, all countries reported that IVDs are imported. In eight countries, the IVDs are reported to be registered. Of the other six countries, two reported listing the IVDs and one was just starting the assessment of the first applications.

The third section of the questionnaire focused on the process of IVD registration. Ten countries reported to register IVDs. This includes, in all cases, assessment of technical documents with a full evaluation of the documentation. Eight countries are also performing abridged evaluation. These eight countries are all using the collaborative registration procedure of the WHO (WHO-CRP), which means they rely on the assessment of other NCAs that are considered stringent regulatory agencies by the WHO, e.g., the European Medicines Agency (EMA) [5]. Six countries reported to perform audits, and seven countries perform lab testing on the IVDs. Post-marketing activities are performed in eight countries. However, the nature of reporting activities was not captured. One country declared that adverse event reporting was implemented.

The results of the questionnaire hinted toward inconsistencies in the way IVDs are regulated. For example, in countries No. three, nine, and 10, IVDs are reported not to be registered, although IVDs are included in the national law and the framework and guidelines for their regulation are available. Country No. nine reported that assessors for the technical assessment are not available; however, they also report to perform full evaluations of dossiers, albeit no formal registration. These inconsistencies may be due to understaffing in the national regulatory agencies as well as to a lack of regulatory stringency. In any case, this highlights the need for additional training in the area of IVD regulation in sub-Saharan Africa.

Discussion

The results of the questionnaire (Table 1) show that in the majority of the countries represented in the workshop, some form of IVD regulation exists. However, there are gaps in different aspects of the regulatory system and its implementation, for example, while IVDs are included in the national medicines law in most countries, the registration process or laboratory testing of IVDs is not generally performed. In addition, audits are only performed by a small proportion of the represented countries. This is also consistent with the fact that all countries have imported IVDs in use, but only about half of the countries have in country manufacturing of IVDs. It is possible that the NCAs rely on audits/inspections of the NCAs from the countries or regions where the IVDs are manufactured, but this was not discussed in detail. During the writing of the paper, we also learned that in-country manufacturing of IVD also started in another of the participating countries, showing the dynamic of the situation in Africa.

The 14 countries that were represented in the workshop had been invited based on their participation in the former benchmarking activity [4, 6] as well as former participation in BloodTrain activities. In these countries, the regulatory systems for IVDs show gaps on different levels that need to be addressed in order to strengthen assessment processes of IVDs and to ensure safe and quality-assured IVDs as well as products that rely on IVDs for their safety (e.g., medicines stemming from tested blood donations, i.e., blood products and plasma-derived medicinal products). While the countries represented in the workshop are most likely not representative of the sub-Saharan African countries, they provide a snapshot of the heterogeneity of IVD regulation in African countries. Harmonization in this area is needed, and the African Union Development Agency already addresses that with the formation of the African Medical Devices Forum Technical Committee, which working toward the harmonization of medical devices and diagnostics regulation in Africa [2].

The results of our workshop clearly highlight the need for national regulatory authorities to receive support in relation to IVD product regulations, particularly with regard to training assessors to evaluate technical documentation, conduct audits, and carry out laboratory testing. One possible solution may be to foster the interaction between those agencies that already perform these activities and those who do not yet, either by training activities or by reliance concepts.

This study has important limitations. For example, the questionnaire responses from individual participants may not accurately represent practices in their respective countries as these responses were not verified. Furthermore, respondents may have interpreted the questions differently, meaning the validity of the responses depended heavily on each participant’s understanding of the IVD regulatory system in their country.

Challenges in Sub-Saharan Africa and the Way Forward

Performance evaluation, licensing, and surveillance processes of IVDs are still in infant stages in the sub-Saharan African countries that participated in this workshop. While the legal basis for the regulation of IVDs appears to be present in these countries, it is in most cases not yet fully implemented. While such a regulatory approach needs to be integrated into the legal framework of the countries, it is still possible to learn from other systems, where this implementation is completed. The regulatory approaches for performance evaluation of IVDs that have shown to be successful and considered state-of-the-art practice internationally include regulatory frameworks such as the European In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR), the Unites States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulation of IVDs, recommendations from the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF), and guidelines from the World Health Organization. These regulatory frameworks and their associated guidance documents establish robust performance evaluation standards for IVDs, thereby safeguarding public health.

Therefore, a regulatory framework for IVDs tailored to African needs does not need to be created from scratch; it can draw on successful, state-of-the-art practices from around the world, including the European IVDR, FDA regulations, IMDRF recommendations, and WHO guidelines, all of which set robust standards for IVD performance evaluation to ensure public health protection. While benefitting from experiences gained from other parts of the world, regulatory authorities in Africa could also foster reliance on other jurisdictions’ approval of IVDs. This reliance in its own is a good approach toward a global harmonization. However, in certain cases, complete reliance on other jurisdiction approval without local contextual evaluations may not address region-specific needs such as differences in pathogen prevalence and patient demographics. This process will likely be supported by the AMRH’s initiative to perform a continental listing of medical products, published in the Continental List of Human Medicinal Products with Positive Opinion – Green Book [7] provides “A standardized process under the AMRH for evaluating and approving medicinal products at continental level and supporting translation across African Union member states.” Such an approach for IVDs may help bridge the gaps in the evaluation of IVDs in the African countries. However, there is still need for support for this development both on the national and the continental level.
