Technical and anatomical determinants of ischemic complications in unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysm surgery: The role of temporary arterial occlusion
Authors: Lucas Ribeiro, Vivien Szabo, Jennifer Hernandez, Marine Le Corre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIschemic complications (IC) remain a major source of morbidity following microsurgical treatment of unruptured middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms. The contribution of temporary arterial occlusion (TAO) to ischemic risk remains debated, particularly given potential confounding by anatomical and intraoperative factors.Research questionWhat are the primary determinants of clinically evident IC in unruptured MCA aneurysm surgery, and what is the role of TAO?MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed consecutive patients undergoing clipping of unruptured MCA aneurysms between 2023 and 2025. Clinically evident IC was defined as a new postoperative neurological deficit with corresponding ischemic lesions on MRI in symptomatic patients only. Associations with IC were assessed using Firth's penalized logistic regression.ResultsForty-seven patients were included. TAO was used in 60% of cases and was associated with larger aneurysm size (8.3 ± 2.7 vs 6.8 ± 2.4 mm; p = 0.048). TAO, including repeated and prolonged applications, was not associated with IC (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.06–6.97; p = 0.74). A short pre-aneurysmal M1 segment (OR 30.4; p = 0.034) and intraoperative rupture (OR 17.4; p = 0.031) were associated with IC. All IC occurred in patients with a short M1 segment and were related to perforator injury during dissection or clipping. Complete occlusion was achieved in all cases; morbidity was 4.3%.ConclusionClinically evident ischemic complications are primarily driven by anatomical constraints and clipping strategy rather than TAO. TAO should be considered a technical adjunct rather than a dominant determinant of ischemic risk. These findings warrant validation in larger prospective studies.

Introduction

Ischemic complications remain a major concern in the microsurgical treatment of unruptured MCA aneurysms. Prior studies (Lai et al., 2024; Byoun et al., 2016; Hsu et al., 2016) have reported associations between ischemic risk and patient related characteristics (i.e., age, prior stroke), aneurysm morphology (i.e., size, neck configuration), surgeon experience, or unfavorable anatomical features (i.e., short preaneurysmal M1 segment). However, these associations have been inconsistently reported, reflecting the coexistence of multiple risk factors. TAO (Staarmann et al., 2017; Lavine et al., 1997; Hindman et al., 2010; McDermott et al., 1989) has long been incorporated into microsurgery to facilitate aneurysm dissection, optimize clip placement (Akyüz et al., 2002), and mitigate the risk of intraoperative rupture (Leipzig et al., 2005). Although the safe duration of TAO remains unclear (Doron et al., 2022), it is commonly considered a necessary and generally safe (Staarmann et al., 2017) adjunct in unruptured MCA aneurysm surgery when applied selectively. Nevertheless, ischemic events might be attributed to TAO and understanding the respective contributions of anatomical configuration, technical strategy, or TAO to ischemic risk is crucial. The objective of this study was therefore to identify the main determinants of IC in unruptured MCA aneurysm surgery and to reappraise the role of TAO.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

We included adult patients who underwent clipping of unruptured MCA aneurysms between 2023 and 2025. TAO was applied selectively before aneurysm dissection in cases deemed at high risk of intraoperative rupture, requiring aneurysm remodeling or circumferential dissection, or involving large or calcified aneurysms. Intraoperative variables—including TAO duration and frequency, number of permanent clips, and intraoperative rupture—were prospectively recorded, with additional perioperative data retrieved from medical records. TAO duration and number of occlusion episodes were documented by the neuroanesthesiology team. Among the patients presenting with multiple aneurysms, co-existing non-MCA aneurysms were managed by endovascular treatment prior to the MCA aneurysm surgery, treated during the same craniotomy, or kept under surveillance. In all cases, outcome data, including TAO utilization, clipping strategy, and clinically evident ischemic complications, refer exclusively to the MCA aneurysm treated during the pterional craniotomy. Aneurysm morphology, size, neck diameter, MCA location, and pre-aneurysmal M1 length were assessed on digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Pre-aneurysmal M1 length was categorized as short (≤13.3 mm) or standard (>13.3 mm) (Park et al., 2016). The study was approved by the national ethics committee (IRB00011687), and all data were anonymized in accordance with national data protection regulations.

Outcome assessment & adverse events

Postoperative aneurysm occlusion rate was defined on computed tomography angiography (CTA). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not routinely performed except in case of new postoperative deficit to rule out ischemic complications. Clinically evident ischemic complication was defined as a new postoperative neurological deficit with corresponding ischemic lesion on postoperative MRI. As MRI was performed only in symptomatic patients, this definition necessarily captures symptomatic events and precludes the identification of silent ischemic lesions. Accordingly, all references to “ischemic complication” refer exclusively to clinically evident. Adverse events-including hemorrhagic complications, wound infections, massive intraoperative bleeding or cerebrospinal fluid leakage were recorded.

Surgical approach

All surgeries were performed by two neurosurgeons (senior author M.L.C and L.R) under general anesthesia. Patient's head was secured in a standard cranial stabilization system (Mayfield®). Head position was 20° rotated to contralateral side, curvilinear incision and classical pterional craniotomy were performed in all cases. Surgeries were retractor less and systematic wide opening of the sylvian fissure allowed proper identification of MCA branches and lateral lenticulostriate arteries (LSAs) for safe clip application and atraumatic manipulation of the brain. Preservation of the sylvian venous complex was prioritized; veins were divided only when strictly necessary, mainly on the frontal side. Sylvian fissure was dissected in a distal-to-proximal fashion unless early proximal control was deemed necessary, or to facilitate brain relaxation. Whenever necessary, TAO of the sylvian artery was performed with either straight or curve 5 mm temporary clips. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials and motor evoked potentials, was not routinely employed during the study period for unruptured MCA aneurysm surgery. This institutional practice reflects the standard approach at our center during the study window and is acknowledged as a limitation in the interpretation of TAO safety.

Neuroanesthesiology protocol for unruptured aneurysm surgery

During surgery, a standardized neuroprotection protocol was systematically applied. General anesthesia was induced and maintained using target-controlled infusion of propofol (8–12 μg/mL) and remifentanil (5–8 ng/mL) based on Schnider's and Minto's models, respectively (Marges et al., 2024). Anesthetic depth was adjusted according to intraoperative brain relaxation, as assessed by the neurosurgeon. Osmotherapy with hyperosmolar sodium chloride (0.9%, 0.1–0.15 g/kg) was administered as needed. End-tidal CO2 was maintained between 30 and 35 mmHg. Thiopentone (2 mg/kg IV bolus) was administered immediately prior to each TAO episode as a cerebral metabolic suppressant that maintains neurovascular metabolic coupling (Astrup et al., 1984); EEG-guided burst suppression was not routinely employed, and thiopentone dose was not titrated to EEG endpoints. The oxygen fraction (FiO2) was maintained at 0.40–0.50 during all phases of surgery, including TAO phases, as an intermediate measure to mitigate both brain tissue hypoxia and hyperoxia-evoked capillary constriction (Hirunpattarasilp et al., 2022a, 2022b).Mean arterial pressure was augmented to 90–100 mmHg using norepinephrine infusion during both the clipping phase and the clip-release (reperfusion) phase to optimize collateral perfusion pressure. The clip-release phase was systematically managed so that the duration of clip-release equalized or exceeded the duration of clip-occlusion, in accordance with evidence that reperfusion kinetics contribute substantially to total ischemic burden.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software, version 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Illustration created using BioRender.com. Figure Labs was used to assist in the generation of figures. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, and qualitative data were described using percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. To address small-sample bias and quasi-complete separation due to sparse outcome events, univariate associations were evaluated using Firth's penalized logistic regression, which provides bias-reduced estimates and finite odds ratios. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Intraoperative and surgical details

Aneurysm intra operative rupture occurred in 3 cases (6.1%), all during aneurysm dissection and were easily controlled with TAO, cottonoid pressure at rupture's site and gentle suction without additional morbidity. The mean duration of surgery was 160.8 ± 48.1 min range (80 to 300min). Temporary arterial clipping was required in 59.6% (n = 28) of procedures. The mean total cumulative TAO (Cliptotal) duration was 10.2 min. The maximal continuous TAO (Clipmax) duration was 35.0 min and the maximal Cliptotal duration was 77.0 min. Final clipping for aneurysm exclusion required a single clip in 34.0% of cases (n = 16), two clips in 57.0% (n = 27), and three clips in 9.0% (n = 4) of procedures. Cliptotal exceeding 10 min was accounted in 13/28 (46.4%). Each TAO episode was followed by a clip removal phase (Cliprelease) equal or exceeding the clip application phase duration (Cliprelease ≥ Clipocclusion).

Surgical outcome & factors associated with ischemic risk

Overall procedural morbidity was low (n = 2; 4.3%), with two patients presenting a postoperative neurological deficit due to ischemic stroke. No patient with a concurrent non-MCA surgical aneurysm suffered a clinically evident ischemic complication. Importantly, there were no procedure-related deaths. The mean duration of hospitalization was 6.6 ± 4.4 days (range: 4–25 days) and all but two (95.7%) were discharged home. Immediate postoperative complete occlusion was achieved in all patients (100.0%). There was no adverse event requiring revision surgery, no CSF leakage or massive bleeding requiring blood transfusion. The univariate Firth penalized logistic regression analysis (Table 4) identified two variables significantly associated with clinically evident ischemic complications. A short pre-aneurysmal M1 segment was strongly associated with ischemic risk (OR 30.4, 95% CI 1.31–707.4; p = 0.034), as was intraoperative rupture (OR 17.4, 95% CI 1.30–233.7; p = 0.031). Technical and anatomical features in patients with ischemic complications

Patients with postoperative clinically evident IC exhibited a short pre-aneurysmal M1 segment. A 60-year-old female (Fig. 2A) with a left MCA aneurysm sited on a short M1 pre aneurysmal segment and insular aneurysm's dome projection suffered a perforator occlusion during permanent clip placement. Postoperative MRI showed an anterior/genu internal capsule infarction. TAO was not used and the injury was likely related to the length of the clip despite clear identification of the LSAs origin close to the bifurcation. She quickly improved and was discharged to rehabilitation walking but with a complete right upper limb paralysis. The second case involved a 68-year-old male (Fig. 2C) with a left MCA bifurcation aneurysm arising from an extremely short M1 segment. LSAs originated from both M1 and the M2 superior trunk, with several perforators adherent to the aneurysm dome. Inadvertent sacrifice of a perforator during dissection resulted in postoperative hemiplegia and aphasia, with deep internal capsule infarction on Diffusion Weighted Imaging (DWI). TAO was used to facilitate final clip placement, a small injury to the M2 superior trunk required repair with a 10.0 suture. The Cliptotal was 77.0 min and Clipmax 35.0min. Discussion

In our cohort of surgically treated unruptured MCA aneurysm, TAO was required in nearly 60% of patients and was significantly associated with larger aneurysm size, consistent with contemporary literature (Hendrix et al., 2025; Griessenauer et al., 2014). Despite the need for multiple episodes (maximum: 7) no clinically evident ischemic complication attributable to TAO was observed. Extreme values of TAO occurred in a single patient (Section 3.6, Case 2), in whom the postoperative deficit was likely attributable to perforator injury during dissection rather than to the period of vascular occlusion. While these observations are clinically encouraging, the limited sample size and low event rate preclude any definitive claim of safety. Approximately half of patients had a Cliptotal exceeding 10 min and the mean Cliptotal was significantly longer for large aneurysms (≥10 mm). Overall, we achieved a maximum rate (100%) of immediate postoperative occlusion.

The optimal use of TAO remains poorly defined in the literature, with inconsistent reports despite major advances in intraoperative neuromonitoring and neuroprotective strategies (Staarmann et al., 2017; Lavine et al., 1997). To date, no consensus exists regarding a maximal “safe” duration of TAO (Doron et al., 2022). While brief occlusion is widely considered safe and routinely used by many surgeons, others have largely abandoned TAO altogether (Doron et al., 2022; Charbel et al., 1991). Part of this discrepancy may stem from an incomplete distinction between total cumulative TAO duration (Cliptotal) and the maximal continuous duration of a single occlusion episode (Clipmax), the latter potentially exerting a greater impact on ischemic risk (Tso and Macdonald, 2014). Intermittent short episodes of TAO are generally favored, as prolonged continuous occlusion (>20 min) has been independently associated with stroke risk (Ogilvy et al., 1996). Although TAO has been linked to postoperative complications and longer Cliptotal durations to poorer 6-month outcomes (Yoshikawa et al., 2022), other studies have found no relationship between the number of occlusion episodes and long-term outcome, suggesting that intermittent occlusion strategies may be acceptable (Yoshikawa et al., 2022).

Beyond occlusion duration alone, emerging evidence suggests that the clip-release phase may represent a critical and underappreciated contributor to ischemic burden. Silva et al. (2022) demonstrated delayed and incomplete tissue oxygen recovery during reperfusion, such that cumulative hypoxic exposure during clip release may equal or exceed that of the occlusion phase, particularly in the early minutes following flow restoration. These observations support viewing TAO as a dynamic occlusion–reperfusion process rather than a static time threshold. Moreover, in cases where prolonged occlusion is anticipated, particularly in large or calcified aneurysms with adherent perforators, controlling total TAO duration, optimizing reperfusion intervals, and evaluating alternative revascularization strategies are essential considerations. Sevgi et al. (2025) recently emphasized that controlled clipping durations and systematic reperfusion intervals are key determinants of ischemic outcome, and that bypass procedures should be considered when prolonged or unacceptable occlusion is anticipated; in this context, preoperative superficial temporal artery (STA) preparation should be considered in complex aneurysms where the need for an STA-MCA bypass may arise intraoperatively. Our results are consistent with this framework: the preferential use of short (<10 min), intermittent TAO episodes with Cliprelease ≥ Clipocclusion, combined with a structured neuroanesthesiologic neuroprotection protocol, likely accounts for the absence of TAO-attributable clinically evident ischemic events in our series. Notably, mean Cliptotal exceeded 30 min in large aneurysms (n = 11) which did not lead to clinically evident IC, suggesting potential resilience of the MCA territory to cumulative ischemic burden (Ota et al., 2025).

Finally, penalized univariate analysis identified intraoperative rupture and a short preaneurysmal M1 segment as significantly associated with ischemic risk, consistent with prior outcome-based studies (Hsu et al., 2016; Park et al., 2016; Ribeiro et al., 2024; Yeon et al., 2011a). Although causality cannot be inferred due to sample size limitations and separation bias, these findings likely reflect increased procedural complexity and warrant validation in larger multicenter cohorts.

Technical considerations of TAO application

A safe TAO application comes first with thorough and careful breakdown of patients' anatomy observed on preoperative imagery (i.e., angiogram, morphological imaging). Indeed, MCA anatomy carries great diversity that range from variations in the numbers of vessels to morphological variations (Kashtiara et al., 2024). Clear identification of the aneurysm position on MCA branches is also essential to anticipate technical challenges as certain location are harder to tackle (e.g., proximal MCA aneurysm) (Dashti et al., 2007a). Additionally, the shape of the MCA on axial and coronal planes is of importance in planning the procedure, as certain shape might facilitate proximal control and lead to unusual (e.g., below M1 bed) TAO positioning (Fig. 3). Furthermore, close investigation of aneurysm's dome projection combined with MCA direction helps to predict permanent clip selection (Dashti et al., 2007b). When TAO is considered intraoperatively, confirmation that pre-operative roadmap matches peroperative anatomical findings allows to confidently approach the Clipocclusion phase; the clip is usually placed close to the neck but with sufficient distance so that the head of the clip does not hinder dissection around the aneurysm neck or application of the permanent clip. The final step is marked by confirmation of lateral LSAs position or in the ruling out of the ones that might revolve close or around the selected location for arterial closure.

Lateral LSAs positioning in MCA aneurysm: anatomical variant

Lateral LSAs usually originate from the infero-medial aspect of the M1 segment of the MCA and primarily supply the genu and posterior limb of the internal capsule and lateral parts of putamen and caudate head (Ota and Komiyama, 2021; Marinkovic et al., 2001). The number of lateral LSAs varies widely, ranging from 1 to 14 per hemisphere, with an average of approximately four (Aydin et al., 1996). They may arise from a single large trunk (52%), two large trunks (35%), or as multiple small individual perforators (13%) (Aydin et al., 1996). The distance between the origin of the first lateral LSA on M1 and the internal carotid artery bifurcation was reported to be approximately 9 mm (Ülkü et al., 2024). Despite being angiographically identifiable, LSAs may appear to originate more medially than they truly do due to the recurrent course of lateral perforators along the parent vessel (Fig. 2B). (Aydin et al., 1996) MCA aneurysm located on long-M1 preaneurysmal segment (i.e., >18.5 mm) are typically associated with a low risk of symptomatic perforators injury (Yeon et al., 2011b). However, in specific anatomical configuration, the aneurysm may sit in close relation to perforators, thus exhibiting high risk of injury during aneurysm dissection. Short pre-aneurysmal M1 segment is a meaningful example as lateral LSAs may originate very close to the aneurysm neck, at the neck (Fig. 2D), on the M2 superior trunk (not to be confused with insular branches from the M2 inferior trunk resembling LSAs (Ohgaki et al., 2025)) or on a frontal early cortical branch (Fig. 4). LSAs may be adherent to the aneurysm dome escalating operative complexity and TAO of the short M1 segment may be considered excessively risky, instead a very brief TAO of the internal carotid artery might be performed. Moreover, in aneurysms with insular dome projection (Dashti et al., 2007b) and a short pre-aneurysmal M1 segment, LSAs are highly vulnerable; as illustrated in our patient (Fig. 2A), mandating use of the shortest length of clip possible. In summary, the safe use of TAO during MCA aneurysm surgery relies on detailed preoperative anatomical review, awareness of MCA and perforator variations, and strict adherence to a stepwise clipping and reperfusion strategy. However, given the potential surgical morbidity (Yoshikawa et al., 2022; Ferch et al., 2002) associated with TAO we believe it should be avoided if not strictly necessary. When effectively integrated into the surgical plan — preferably using repeated short episodes (Ferch et al., 2002) (i.e., <10min) and avoiding prolonged TAO (limiting the Clipmax to a minimum) —TAO may facilitate dissection, optimize clip placement, and improve occlusion rates without adversely affecting clinical outcome. Importantly, our findings indicate that ischemic risk is driven primarily by anatomical constraints, intraoperative rupture and permanent clip strategy rather than by TAO itself.

Limitations

The most significant limitation of this study is the absence of routine postoperative MRI. Performed exclusively in patients who developed new postoperative neurological deficits, it introduces a systematic bias that substantially underestimates the true ischemic burden. Future prospective series should mandate routine postoperative DWI to enable accurate ascertainment of the full ischemic spectrum. The low event rate (n = 2 clinically evident ischemic complications) and small sample size limits statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions; these results should be regarded as hypothesis and require validation in larger prospective cohorts. The absence of formal neurocognitive evaluation is a further limitation, as ischemic injury in prefrontal or subcortical regions may produce deficits in executive function or processing speed not captured by standard clinical examination. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized neuropsychological batteries to fully characterize postoperative morbidity. Finally, the absence of routine intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring represents a limitation. Real-time neuromonitoring provides continuous feedback on subcortical perfusion during TAO and enables earlier detection of ischemic events. Its absence may have contributed to delayed recognition of vascular injury during dissection and clip placement and precludes a comprehensive neurophysiological assessment of the impact of TAO duration in this series.

Conclusion

In this series, selectively applied TAO was not associated with clinically evident ischemic complications, even with repeated episodes or prolonged cumulative durations. Surgical risk was primarily driven by anatomical constraints, such as a short pre-aneurysmal M1 segment, intraoperative rupture, and permanent clip strategy rather than by TAO itself. Careful preoperative anatomical assessment, early identification of LSAs, and a stepwise occlusion–reperfusion strategy using short, intermittent clipping episodes with systematic reperfusion phases appear critical to minimizing ischemic burden. When integrated into a disciplined microsurgical and neuroanesthesiologic framework, TAO facilitates safe dissection and precise clip placement while maintaining excellent occlusion rates without compromising neurological outcome in unruptured MCA aneurysm surgery. These findings require validation in larger prospective cohorts with routine postoperative MRI and systematic neuropsychological assessment.
