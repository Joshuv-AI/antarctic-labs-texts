Enzymatic colorimetric encoding-based digital medicine for pancreatic cancer diagnosis
Authors: Dongsheng Mao, Chenbin Liu, Runchi Zhang, Zhiyuan Ma, Liang Wu, Mingjin Zhu, Xiaochen Tang, Wen Chen, Jie Deng, Hongquan Gou, Xiong Dun, Jingqi Chen, Zhaocheng Liu, Wenxing Li, Fenyong Sun, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digital medicine leverages digital biomarkers by algebraically integrating multiple biomarkers to reflect disease status. Colorimetric analysis offers an intuitive readout, but colorimetric-based digital medicine remains underexplored. Here we show an Enzymatic Colorimetric Encoding-based Digital Medicine platform (EnCODE). By harnessing enzyme-catalyzed multicolor encoding in tandem with the programmability of DNA technology, EnCODE converts multidimensional miRNA information into recognizable optical signals. We demonstrate that these signals are decodable and can be interpreted by visual inspection or spectral analysis, facilitating dimensionality reduction and visualization of disease states. Additionally, EnCODE integrates a continuous weighting mechanism that enables accurate mapping of digital biomarkers. In a cohort of 163 pancreatic cancer clinical samples, EnCODE achieves 96% detection sensitivity and 90% overall accuracy—comparable to the 96% sensitivity and 91% overall accuracy with conventional molecular diagnostic methods. We increase data density through three-dimensional color encoding and hyperspectral imaging-based analysis, enabling an intuitive color-coded molecular readout.

Introduction

Biomarker detection plays a critical role in diagnosing diseases, guiding medication, and monitoring disease progression1–3. With the development of omics technologies, an increasing number of biomarkers have been discovered and it has been proven that the joint analysis of multiple biomarkers often yields better results4–9. To harness diverse biomarker data, digital medicine has emerged, leveraging digital biomarkers to quantify and mathematically integrate multiple targets through weighted algebraic operations10. However, current multi-target assays require separate reaction tubes, increasing costs, operational complexity, and inter-well variability11,12. Integrating the concept of digital medicine with the programmability of DNA technology enables single-tube multiplex biomarker detection, addressing these limitations while maintaining analytical rigor13–15.

Current solutions predominantly rely on fluorescence signals, using signal type or intensity to determine the positive or negative status of the sample16–19. For instance, Han et al. developed a molecular classifier using fluorescence signals for lung cancer diagnosis via serum microRNA (miRNA)20, while Randolph Lopez et al. designed a molecular classifier to distinguish viral and bacterial respiratory infections21. Although these schemes are all based on DNA technology and have strong designability, they still have limitations, including but not limited to: (1) only non-continuous multiplicative molecular calculations are possible; (2) the output signal is confined to a one-dimensional space, which inevitably imposes high demands on experimental systems to accurately reproduce digital medical models; (3) signal analysis must be performed under specific conditions, making it difficult to directly observe. In contrast, enzymatic colorimetric detection enables direct visual interpretation22,23, with inherent signal amplification and continuous color mixing, thus comprehensively reflecting the different expression states of various biomarkers24–26. Although colorimetric coding remains underexplored in digital medicine, the establishment of a comprehensive theoretical system for colorimetric coding would provide digital medicine with a powerful solution.

To address this, we develop EnCODE, an enzymatic colorimetric encoding-based digital medicine platform that utilizes DNA technology to convert multidimensional biomarker signals into two-dimensional colorimetric outputs for dimensionality reduction and visualization. Unlike fluorescence-based strategies limited to one-dimensional signal outputs, EnCODE harnesses two-dimensional colorimetric encoding to achieve higher information density without specialized optical equipment. We demonstrate that this two-dimensional colorimetric space offers greater combinatorial diversity, detectable via both visual inspection and spectral analysis. In addition, reverse signal decoding validates the system’s mathematical rigor, laying a foundation for enzymatic colorimetric theory. EnCODE integrates continuous weighting, aligning with digital medicine principles. We apply this approach to pancreatic cancer diagnosis, enabling precise multi-miRNA serum profiling for enhanced diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, integration of three or more colors allows high-dimensional encoding, supporting more precise disease detection or multi-disease classification in a single test. EnCODE integrates hyperspectral imaging to merge rapid visual screening with high-density spectral data capture, enabling real-time in situ detection27,28. This innovation couples enzymatic colorimetric encoding with quantitative decoding, enabling multiplex biomarker integration with instrument-light readout and scalable workflows. These features support a feasible route toward decentralized deployment, particularly when paired with standardized imaging, internal calibration, and automated analysis. The development of related technologies will be beneficial for further promoting the progress of colorimetric coding, multi-omics analysis, digital medicine, POCT, and other fields.

Results

Construction and Diagnostic Application of EnCODE

In the enzymatic colorimetric encoding system, we selected horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and alkaline phosphatase (AP) with their substrates ABTS (green) and pNPP (yellow), respectively, to establish a two-dimensional colorimetric scheme. The final presentation consisted of two colorimetric dimensions: green and yellow (Fig. 1a). Since the colorimetric encoding includes two different aspects: color proportion encoding and color concentration encoding, a single colorimetric code can reflect a variety of information (Fig. 1b). To enable high-density information encoding and user-friendly detection of the enzymatic colorimetric encoding system for digital medicine and disease diagnostics, we established EnCODE for multidimensional miRNA computation (Fig. 1d). Using rolling circle amplification (RCA)29 and colorimetric enzymes with unique DNA barcodes, we linked color signals to miRNA type and concentration data. By leveraging the specificity of template DNA for target miRNA and the specificity of DNA barcodes for RCA amplification products, we successfully converted complex miRNA information into identifiable color signals in a specific and efficient manner. Additionally, to assign miRNA weights, we designed DNA templates with positive and negative variants, where positive templates proportion determined miRNA weight (Fig. 1e, f). By assigning different weights and color signals to different miRNA, we achieved a multidimensional miRNA molecular computing based on colorimetric encoding, compressing multi-dimensional disease biomarker signals into two-dimensional color signals for dimensionality reduction and visualization.

Investigating the Encoding-Decoding Paradigm of EnCODE

Colorimetric signals, rich in information, can be easily encoded and decoded for multi-dimensional biomarkers detection. Colorimetric encoding refers to the changes in visible color and absorption spectra when colors are mixed in varying proportions or concentrations (Fig. 2a). To validate this capability, we explored whether the colorimetric encoding adhered to well-defined mathematical relationships to ensure the accuracy of encoding and decoding. Using green (HRP/ABTS) and yellow (AP/pNPP) solutions as initial components, we mixed them in different ratios. The absorption spectrum of the mixed solution shifted predictably (Fig. 2b). Further analysis of the characteristic absorption peaks of the green initial solution (415 nm and 730 nm) and the yellow initial solution (400 nm) showed that the absorbance of the mixed solution was linearly related to the proportion of the initial solutions (Fig. 2c). The absorption spectrum of the mixed solution equals the sum of the absorption spectra of the initial solutions multiplied by their proportions, within the experimentally validated linear range, confirming a mathematically rigorous encoding system. (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Similarly, RGB values of the mixed solution varied linearly with the proportion of the initial solutions (Fig. 2d), and matched the sum of the RGB values of the initial solutions scaled by their proportions, within the unsaturated RGB acquisition regime (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 1b). Direct color mixing for encoding poses practical challenges. To enhance the applicability of colorimetric encoding, we controlled colorimetric encoding through the concentration of colorimetric enzymes and tested its enzyme responsiveness (Fig. 2g). First, systematic optimization identified robust reaction conditions (2 mM ABTS, 2 mM pNPP, 0.5 mM H₂O₂, and a 30 min reaction), ensuring reliable and reproducible signal generation for subsequent encoding. (Supplementary Fig. 2a–e). Additionally, we validated the enzymatic colorimetric performance of AP and HRP, confirming a well-defined mathematical mapping between enzyme concentration and colorimetric output. Both enzymes showed stable catalytic behavior under the selected reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Across the validated dynamic range, enzyme concentration exhibited positive linear relationships with both absorbance and RGB features, supporting robust enzyme-driven color modulation (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i). For the spectral readout, linear quantification was maintained from 68 pM to 2 nM, whereas for the RGB-based readout, the linear range was approximately 294 pM to 2 nM. For lower-abundance samples, assay sensitivity can be improved by moderately extending the chromogenic development time. Extending the color-development reaction to 1 h improved low-end sensitivity, reducing the spectral LOD to 5.54 pM (HRP–ABTS) and 1.6 pM (AP–pNPP), and the RGB-based LOD to 8.87 pM (HRP–ABTS) and 8.81 pM (AP–pNPP) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Building on the feasibility and predictability of enzymatic colorimetric encoding, we directly mixed AP and HRP at varying concentrations to generate colorimetric encoding. Intuitively, visual color modulation was observed as enzyme types and concentrations varied (Fig. 2h). Further analysis of the absorption spectrum showed that the absorption spectra of the mixed solution matched the sum of the spectra from single enzyme solutions (each at the enzyme concentration present in the mixture) minus the blank substrate (Fig. 2i, j), with four representative absorption spectra shown in Fig. 2i. RGB values exhibited analogous relationships (Fig. 2k, l). These quantitative additivity relationships, validated within the experimentally defined linear regime, established the foundation for accurate encoding and decoding of enzymatic colorimetric signals. This additivity was further confirmed for mixed-enzyme reactions at ultralow concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Implementation of Multiplex miRNA Detection with EnCODE

We propose a potential application for enzymatic colorimetric encoding. In the field of precision medicine, the collective expression pattern of multiple miRNAs in blood has been considered a more robust and valuable biomarker for tumor detection and prognosis prediction compared to a single miRNA marker30,31. To analyze these complex miRNA combinations efficiently and accurately, we designed EnCODE, a computational platform leveraging enzymatic colorimetric encoding and DNA technology (Fig. 3a). EnCODE consists of four steps. First, target miRNAs act as linker primers, circularizing template DNA via SplintR Ligase. The template DNA is designed with functional binding domains, including a target miRNA-specific binding region, a DNA barcode area, and a capture binding region. Subsequently, miRNA-circular template DNA complexes are immobilized on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plates via coated capture sequences. Then, the capture strand serves as the amplification primer, and the circular template DNA acts as the template for RCA to amplify signals. Subsequently, colorimetric enzymes with specific DNA barcode bind to the RCA-amplified sequences, converting target miRNA signals into colorimetric enzyme signals. Finally, the enzymatic colorimetric reaction generates a visible encoded color reflecting the collective expression levels of the target miRNA panel. By decoding the color, complex target miRNA contents can be extracted from the color result (Fig. 3b). To validate the EnCODE platform, we employed miRNAs as both linker and amplification primers for RCA signal validation. We found that using miRNA primers enabled efficient template DNA circularization (Supplementary Fig. 5a). RCA exhibited high sensitivity to miRNA concentration (5 pM detection limit) and reaction time (5-minute amplification) (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d), with high orthogonality between miRNA primers and templates (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Also, cross-linking enzymes to DNA barcodes via NHS-PEG4-N3/DBCO click chemistry achieved efficient conjugation. The conjugation yield was approximately 70%, and each enzyme was modified with 1-3 DNA molecules. Enzyme activity remained stable, with 78% of the activity of native HRP and 84% of native AP, confirming that the conjugation process did not significantly affect enzymatic performance (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results validate the chemical integrity and metrological stability of the enzyme-DNA conjugates used in EnCODE. To further estimate the enzyme loading per RCA product, we compared colorimetric outputs obtained by directly capturing enzyme–DNA conjugates on the plate-immobilized capture strand (without RCA) versus capturing the same conjugates via RCA products after a 30-min amplification. The RCA-enabled workflow increased signal intensity by ~28-fold for HRP–DNA and ~26-fold for AP–DNA, which suggests average loadings of ~28 HRP and ~26 AP molecules per RCA product under the working conditions (Supplementary Fig. 7). These results support efficient signal amplification while maintaining a quantitatively stable miRNA-to-enzyme signal transfer. After solving the technical issues related to platform construction, platform feasibility was further confirmed using fluorescent DNA barcodes. On the ELISA plate, the results showed a proportional relationship between fluorescence intensity and miRNA content (Supplementary Fig. 8a). On the magnetic bead platform, the results demonstrated precise dual-target detection (Supplementary Fig. 8b-c). These verifications provided critical technical support for miRNA colorimetric encoding detection platform construction.

We optimized the concentration of key components in the EnCODE assay. The capture oligonucleotide was immobilized at the manufacturer-recommended saturating concentration to ensure maximal surface coverage and minimize variability. The template strand concentration was selected at 50 nM, which met two criteria: ensuring efficient circularization while enabling complete capture by the immobilized probes. For the enzyme–DNA conjugates, we titrated 0–100 nM and selected 100 nM to ensure near-saturating signal generation and robust readout. (Supplementary Fig. 9).

After constructing the EnCODE platform, we validated its detection performance using two miRNA targets. The system successfully translated distinct miRNA expression patterns into unique colorimetric codes (Fig. 3c). EnCODE demonstrated a detection limit in the pM range, with both absorbance and RGB values exhibiting a high linear correlation (R² > 0.98) with target concentrations, confirming precise miRNA-to-enzyme signal mapping (Fig. 3d). Additionally, encoded results could be decoded using spectral or colorimetric methods for accurate miRNA quantification (Fig. 3e). To assess the platform’s ability to handle sequence variations, we introduced base mismatches between target miRNAs and template DNA. EnCODE successfully distinguished between matched and mismatched sequences, confirming its robustness in detecting miRNAs even with sequence homology within the same family (Fig. 3f). These results collectively demonstrate the feasibility, reliability, and clinical diagnostic potential of the EnCODE platform for miRNA detection.

However, in actual clinical practice, the miRNA combinations that can effectively reflect disease conditions often involve multiple markers beyond two. To address this, we expanded the detection throughput of the EnCODE and allow the final color to reflect the overall expression of the miRNA combination. Four simulated miRNA targets (miR-150, miR-636, miR-145, miR-223) were constructed for multiplex validation. Since the detection of multiple targets using a dual-enzyme system requires mapping different targets to the same enzyme, we verified colorimetric consistency across targets (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). Orthogonality between miRNAs and template DNA ensured specific colorimetric reactions only when targets matched their corresponding templates (Fig. 3g). Adding non-target miRNAs or templates to the reaction system did not perturb colorimetric signals, confirming the platform’s high specificity and stability (Fig. 3h). These results validate EnCODE’s multiplex detection capability.

We implemented molecular computation for multi-miRNA detection. Based on disease prediction models, miRNA markers with similar expression trends in disease states were encoded with identical colorimetric enzymes, while divergent trends were assigned distinct enzymes, enabling arithmetic operations (addition and subtraction) in digital medicine. This strategy compresses multidimensional miRNA data into interpretable two-dimensional color maps, where colors intuitively reflect both miRNA expression dynamics and disease prediction score. Direct classification of samples is achieved through standardized spectral decoding or quantitative color-feature analysis (Fig. 3i).

To validate the direct detection of disease prediction scores by EnCODE, we hypothesized a model: Score = miR-150 + miR-636 − (miR-145 + miR-223). By modulating miRNA concentrations, we validated the molecular computational performance of EnCODE (Fig. 3j). Consistent color outputs were observed when the prediction scores of the samples are consistent, confirming EnCODE’s capability to accurately reflect the overall miRNA expression profile (Fig. 3j, k). Decoding via the logarithmic ratio of the absorbance at 400 nm (the characteristic peak wavelength of the AP colorimetric product) to 730 nm (the characteristic peak wavelength of the HRP colorimetric product) revealed a linear correlation with model scores, enabling effective sample classification (Fig. 3l). Visual classification using Hue values further validated diagnostic accuracy, while Hue value is a visual property used to indicate the perception of a color, independent of saturation and brightness (Fig. 3m). To quantify the variability of subjective visual interpretation, three blinded raters independently classified representative EnCODE outputs spanning strong/weak positive and strong/weak negative cases, showing substantial inter-rater agreement (accuracy = 94.4%; Supplementary Table 1). This further supports the consistency of visual readout when complemented by standardized imaging and objective decoding. To further assess robustness, we evaluated inter-batch reproducibility and examined the impact of pre-extraction room-temperature holding time. Both intra-plate and inter-batch variations remained low, and the decoded scores showed no appreciable drift within the tested handling-time window (Supplementary Fig. 10). A validation set with randomized miRNA concentrations demonstrated that both spectral and color features could accurately classify samples based on model scores (Fig. 3n). The above results proved that through standardized spectral decoding or quantitative color-feature analysis, the disease prediction model scores can be directly reflected by EnCODE, enabling sample classification.

Realization of miRNA Weighted Calculation with EnCODE

In the context of multi-disease biomarkers reflecting disease information, different biomarkers often have different weighted contributions, so basic arithmetic operations (addition/subtraction) clearly lack the precision required for digital medicine32,33. To enable precise clinical detection, we introduced weighted calculation via multiplication into EnCODE (Fig. 4a). Three weighting schemes were designed (Fig. 4b). (i) Signal-to-non-signal template ratio control: Two template DNA strands (signal strand Tem1 with DNA barcode domain vs. non-signal Tem0 without barcode domain) adjust weighting coefficients through their proportional mixing. (ii) DNA barcode copy number modulation: Weighting is dictated by the number of barcode domains on the template DNA. All DNA templates have the same number of bases. (iii) Competitive binding weighting: Varying ratios of enzyme-conjugated DNA barcode and unlabeled DNA barcode control signal output. All schemes achieved effective weighting (Fig. 4c). However, scheme (ii) supports only discrete weighting, whereas scheme (iii) showed a weaker target concentration–response, potentially due to the unlabeled DNA barcodes—experiencing less steric hindrance—can more effectively compete with RCA products for binding. To validate the weighted calculation approach, we applied variable weights to miR-150 and miR-223 at concentrations determined in a simulated disease prediction model (Score = W1×miR-150 − W2×miR-223) (Fig. 4d). Both spectral features and visual colorimetry accurately reflected model scores and enabled sample classification, confirming the practicality of the weighting scheme (Fig. 4e, f).

To validate complex multi-miRNA weighting, we proposed a simulated disease prediction model, Score = 0.5×miR-150 + 0.5×miR-636 − (0.75×miR-145 + 0.25×miR-223), mimicking real-world clinical scenarios (Fig. 4g). Randomized miRNA concentrations testing demonstrated that both spectral features and visual colorimetry could effectively reflect the model score, as well as provide a good classification of the samples (Fig. 4h). In summary, EnCODE’s integration of weighted computation enables refined digital medical analysis, fulfilling clinical diagnostic demands with enhanced precision.

Clinical Diagnosis of Pancreatic Cancer with EnCODE

To validate EnCODE in actual clinical settings, we tested the platform with clinical blood samples and attempted to construct a pancreatic cancer prediction model. Following the established workflow, total miRNA was extracted from the serum samples, pre-amplified, and analyzed via EnCODE-based colorimetric detection. The results distinguished between cancer and normal samples through spectral analysis or visual readouts (Fig. 5a). To address low miRNA concentrations ( ≤ pM) in biological samples, we implemented the linear-after-the-exponential-polymerase chain reaction (LATE-PCR) technique, which employs excess primers and limiting primers during the PCR amplification stage following miRNA reverse transcription34,35. The exponential phase of amplification can be switched to a linear phase shortly after the limiting primers are exhausted. This feature enables near-linear amplification of miRNAs relative to their logarithmic initial concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 12a). To further assess metrological stability in a workflow-relevant setting, we benchmarked EnCODE using miRNA samples processed through the same pre-amplification step as in the clinical workflow. Across the tested concentration series, EnCODE readouts closely followed the input miRNA levels and showed strong agreement with qRT-PCR quantification, supporting reliable quantitative transfer from miRNA abundance to enzymatic output (Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). In parallel, both the HRP–ABTS and AP–pNPP systems exhibited robust linear relationships between miRNA concentration and colorimetric features at the levels of absorbance and RGB values, further supporting a stable miRNA-to-enzyme signal mapping (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e).

To evaluate robustness under clinically relevant conditions, we assessed common pre-analytical interferences and therapeutic agents using aliquots from the same-source blood sample. We tested anticoagulants/coagulants, representative pancreatic cancer drugs (5-Fluorouridine, Irinotecan, Oxaliplatin, and Gemcitabine), and simulated matrix interferences (lipemia, high protein, and hemolysis) by adding equal volumes of Intralipid, albumin, or red-blood-cell lysate, respectively. Anticoagulants/coagulants, drugs, and lipemic/high-protein conditions showed no appreciable impact on EnCODE readouts, whereas hemolysis caused a modest upward shift consistent with erythrocyte miRNA release (Supplementary Fig. 14a–c). Notably, EnCODE readouts followed the same directional trends as qRT-PCR on the matched samples, supporting that these sample conditions do not materially bias miRNA quantification (Supplementary Fig. 14d–f). Based on these findings, we concluded that hemolysis should be avoided in sample collection, as it can impact the miRNA detection accuracy, whereas other common interferences had minimal effects. In addition, we systematically assessed the effects of storage conditions (4 °C, −20 °C, −80 °C) and storage duration (0–72 h) on the accuracy of miRNA detection. Our results showed that miRNA signals remained stable when samples were stored at −20 °C for up to 1 day or at −80 °C for up to 3 days, with no significant deviation from fresh controls (Supplementary Fig. 15). These findings provide clear guidance on sample storage conditions for reliable EnCODE analysis.

After determining the clinical testing workflow, we applied EnCODE to pancreatic cancer diagnosis and prediction. We attempted to construct a pancreatic cancer diagnosis prediction model. Using the GSE211692 dataset (including serum miRNA expression levels from healthy controls and pancreatic cancer patients) and the GSE163031 dataset (included pancreatic tissue miRNA expression levels from normal and cancerous samples) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, we identified three upregulated and two downregulated miRNAs consistently expressed across both sample types (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 16a). Subsequently, a pancreatic cancer diagnosis model was constructed via binary logistic regression: Score = 0.43×miR-154-5p + 0.57×miR-629-5p + 0.41×miR-99a-5p – (0.09×miR-5006-5p + 0.51×miR-575). Notably, initial miRNA concentrations were log10-transformed to make the concentration data compatible with the LATE-PCR amplification mechanism. The model demonstrated strong discrimination between pancreatic cancer and healthy samples in public datasets (Fig. 5d) and performed robustly across serum (GSE106817, GSE211692) and tissue (GSE163031) cohorts (Fig. 5e).

Prior studies have reported miR-629 upregulation in pancreatic cancer and its contribution to proliferation and invasion, potentially through negative regulation of FOXO337. miR-99a-5p has been implicated in PDAC progression through the NOTCH2/Notch signaling pathway and has been proposed as a candidate prognostic/diagnostic biomarker in multiple cohorts38–40. Although the miR-154 family is frequently reported as tumor-suppressive in several malignancies, dysregulation can be context-dependent across tumor types41,42. miR-575 has also been reported as a potential biomarker for pancreatic cancer detection43,44. Finally, miR-5006-5p is relatively less studied in cancer, but emerging evidence suggests its involvement in tumor-related processes (e.g., via lncRNA-associated mechanisms)45.

Finally, we evaluated EnCODE using a clinical serum cohort comprising pancreatic cancer (n = 63), benign pancreatic disease controls (pancreatitis, n = 50), and healthy controls (n = 50) (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Table 4). EnCODE generated directly interpretable prediction scores and enabled robust classification of pancreatic cancer versus controls, achieving an AUC (area under the ROC curve) of 0.9586 (95% CI: 0.9310–0.9862), with 96.83% sensitivity (95% CI: 89.14–99.44%) and 86.00% specificity (95% CI: 77.86–91.47%). Importantly, EnCODE provides effective discrimination from benign pancreatic disease (pancreatitis) (Fig. 5g–j). In parallel, qRT-PCR profiling of the same miRNA panel recapitulated the expression differences in clinical samples and showed comparable discrimination performance, supporting the analytical consistency between EnCODE and conventional PCR-based workflows (Supplementary Fig. 16b–d). To further assess generalizability, we tested an independent external validation cohort collected at two additional medical centers (n = 50; pancreatic cancer n = 20, pancreatitis n = 15, healthy n = 15), which yielded consistent classification accuracy (Supplementary Table 5, Fig. 5k, l).

Construction of Multidimensional EnCODE Platform

We achieved multidimensional biomarker detection through two-dimensional colorimetric encoding. To enhance information density without increasing the analytical complexity, we developed a three-dimensional colorimetric encoding system by integrating a third color into the existing scheme, achieving precise diagnosis of disease subtypes and differentiating among multiple diseases (Fig. 6a). We introduced red, a color with high discriminatory power generated by HRP-catalyzed oxidation of Amplex Red with H2O2, exhibiting a characteristic absorption peak at 570 nm (Fig. 6c). Analysis of the three original solutions (red, yellow, and green) revealed that their concentrations primarily modulated distinct RGB channels. Specifically, the yellow solution predominantly affected the B value, the red solution primarily influenced the G value, while the green solution affected all RGB values in a balanced way. We directly mixed the three original solutions (red, yellow, and green) at varying concentrations to validate the feasibility of three-dimensional colorimetric encoding. Encoded colors exhibited unique visual hues based on the original solution concentrations and compositions, with RGB trends mirroring the original solutions’ contributions (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 17b). Furthermore, three-dimensional chromaticity encoding maintained the mathematical characteristics of two-dimensional system. The RGB values of the mixed solution equal the sum of the RGB values from three individual enzyme solutions (each at the enzyme concentration present in the mixture) minus twice the blank substrate (Fig. 6d). This relationship is also satisfied by the absorption spectra, enabling constituent quantification via their unique spectral fingerprints (Fig. 6c, e, Supplementary Fig. 17c). Therefore, decoding of encoded solutions — identifying original solution types and concentrations — is achieved through spectral or colorimetric methods (Fig. 6f). Collectively, these characteristics validate the feasibility of three-dimensional colorimetric encoding, demonstrating that encoded colors preserve compositional information through spectral/visual signatures, thereby unlocking rich biochemical data.

To further enhance clinical practicality, we implemented hyperspectral imaging as a key component for rapid and intuitive diagnosis27. Hyperspectral imaging is an advanced imaging technique that combines imaging and spectral analysis, capturing reflectance data across hundreds of wavelengths (visible to mid-infrared), enabling detailed analysis of an object’s composition, structure, and state in both spatial and spectral domains46–48. This high-dimensional data confers a unique advantage on hyperspectral imaging in analyzing multidimensional color-coded solutions. In absorption spectrum analysis, due to its characteristic of single-hole detection, only a portion of absorbance information can be obtained to improve detection speed. Hyperspectral imaging, on the other hand, simultaneously acquires full image and spectral data for multiple samples within 3–5 min per shot, functioning akin to Polaroid-style instant photography, significantly increasing the detection throughput and speed without compromising information density. Its vast information content lays the groundwork for decoding higher-dimensional colorimetric encoding, and has immense potential for subsequent computer-assisted intelligent decoding28. Portable hyperspectral imaging cameras also can be integrated with portable devices (e.g., smartphones) to enable true real-time in situ detection (Fig. 6g). The reflectance spectra of the red, yellow, and green solutions reveal a high degree of consistency with their respective absorption spectra (Fig. 6c, h). Also, consistent with the absorption spectrum, increasing the concentration of a raw solution in the mixture induces the reflectance spectrum shifts that align with the characteristic reflectance profile of the raw solution (Fig. 6i). This suggests that the reflectance spectrum possesses the same performance as the absorption spectrum, and also has the ability to encode and decode, capable of releasing the rich information contained in the spectrum.

Discussion

In summary, we established a comprehensive theoretical system for colorimetric coding and designed an enzymatic colorimetric encoding-based digital medicine platform (EnCODE), which meets the need for efficient and accurate analysis and computation of complex biomarker combinations by transcending the limitations of fluorescence-based frameworks. The innovative system encodes and integrates multi-biomarker information through controlled color mixing. Spectral detection enables quantitative decoding of the information encoded in colors, while visual color readout provides an intuitive and instrument-light option for rapid assessment. Also, EnCODE achieves the continuous weighted operations and the arithmetic operations of multiple biomarkers through a one-tube reaction, enabling multidimensional joint analysis for disease diagnosis. It is noteworthy that by compressing multidimensional biomarker signals into two-dimensional color codes, EnCODE stores denser disease information than one-dimensional fluorescence signals. Integration of three or more colors allows high-dimensional encoding, supporting more precise disease detection or multi-disease classification in a single test. Hyperspectral imaging bridges rapid visual analysis with spectral depth, enabling rapid, high-density analysis of multiple samples27. This makes it capable of high-dimensional color decoding, paving the way for AI-assisted intelligent analysis and smartphone-integrated real-time detection across multiple scenarios. In particular, because EnCODE yields a stable, static colorimetric output, smartphone integration is technically straightforward and does not require specialized optics or time-resolved measurements. A practical workflow can be realized with standardized illumination and fixed imaging geometry (e.g., a simple holder plus diffuse LED lighting), allowing reliable well-level RGB extraction and on-device decoding via a lightweight app. Robustness can be further improved by incorporating internal color references for plate-level normalization and automated QC checks (focus, exposure, and illumination uniformity). In future iterations, computer-vision–assisted capture (auto-segmentation, perspective correction, and real-time guidance) could enable rapid, real-time readout and decoding within seconds.

Using 200 μl serum within 7 h, we demonstrated EnCODE’s capability for pancreatic cancer diagnosis. Compared with commercial rapid NAAT/RT-PCR POCT platforms, EnCODE offers a unique colorimetric-based technology with a focus on multiplex information density, flexible marker integration, and plate-based batch throughput. This technology is particularly advantageous for batch screening and multi-marker risk scoring scenarios. In summary, this platform may provide a foundation for low-cost, non-invasive early cancer screening. The development of related technologies will also be conducive to further development in the fields of colorimetric encoding, multi-omics analysis, digital medicine, and POCT.

While this study focused on miRNA analysis, the enzymatic colorimetric encoding framework exhibits broad adaptability across diverse molecular detection scenarios25,26. The modular design of EnCODE allows seamless extension to other nucleic acid targets (e.g., circulating tumor DNA, mRNA) by reconfiguring sequence-specific DNA templates. Beyond nucleic acids, the integration of antibody-DNA conjugates could further unlock cross-omics detection capabilities49. For instance, coupling protein-specific antibodies would enable simultaneous quantification of protein biomarkers (e.g., cytokines, tumor antigens) and nucleic acids within a unified colorimetric workflow, thereby providing a holistic view of disease mechanisms. Such versatility positions EnCODE as a universal platform for multi-analyte profiling, with potential applications spanning cancer subtyping, infectious disease differentiation, and dynamic treatment response monitoring. Future iterations could harness this scalability to integrate metabolomic or epigenetic markers, ultimately advancing precision medicine through multi-omics convergence.

Validation of Optimal Enzyme Reaction Conditions

The exploration of optimal reaction conditions for HRP involved incubating various concentrations of ABTS and H2O2 with 2 nM HRP at room temperature to initiate the reaction. The reaction kinetics and HRP activity were evaluated by monitoring the change in absorbance at a wavelength of 400 nm.

Concurrently, to investigate the optimal conditions for AP, different concentrations of pNPP were combined with 2 nM AP at room temperature to initiate the reaction. The reaction kinetics and AP activity were assessed by monitoring the change in absorbance at a wavelength of 400 nm.

HRP and AP were stored at 4 °C in PBS containing 50% (v/v) glycerol. The reaction buffer consisted of 500 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM MgCl₂, 0.1 mM ZnCl₂, and 100 mM KCl. Diluted enzyme solutions were stable at 4 °C for up to 1 week.

Enzymatic Color Reaction and Color Mixing of the Color Development Solution

First, prepare a color substrate mixture by combining 2 mM pNPP, 2 mM ABTS, and 0.5 mM H2O2. Next, add 2 nM HRP to this mixture and allow the reaction to proceed at room temperature for 24 h, until the color stabilizes, yielding a green solution. Subsequently, introduce 2 nM AP into the color substrate mixture and again incubate at room temperature for 24 h, until the color stabilizes, producing a yellow solution. Mix the yellow and green solutions in various proportions. Utilize a multi-mode microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland) to measure the absorption spectrum of the resulting mixtures within a 96-well plate. Capture photographs of the solutions and analyze the average RGB values of the colors using Photoshop software for further evaluation.

Enzyme-Catalyzed Colorimetric Reaction

Initially, prepare a chromogenic substrate mixture by combining 2 mM pNPP, 2 mM ABTS, and 0.5 mM H2O2. The enzyme-catalyzed colorimetric reaction is realized by precisely adjusting the concentrations of AP and HRP. After allowing the reaction to proceed for 30 min at room temperature, utilize a multi-mode microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland) to measure the absorption spectrum of the resulting mixtures within a 96-well plate. Furthermore, capture photographs of the solution and employ Photoshop software to analyze the average RGB values of the solution. All assays were conducted under controlled ambient conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% relative humidity). For image acquisition, a fixed-distance holder was used to standardize the camera-to-plate geometry, together with uniform diffuse illumination (LED ring light or backlight lightbox) to reduce shadows and spatial inhomogeneity. Each run included standardized reference wells for plate-to-plate calibration.

Circularization of Template DNA

The template DNAs used in this study were custom-synthesized and purified by Huzhou Hippo Biotech (Huzhou, China) and Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Purification was performed by HPLC, and mass spectrometry confirmed a single dominant peak.

Initially, combine 10 μM of linear template DNA with 20 μM of the target. Subsequently, subject this mixture to heat annealing by incubating it at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by a gradual cooling to 12 °C at a rate of 1 °C per minute to form a linear DNA template-target heterodimer. Next, incubate the 2 μM linear template DNA-primer heterodimer with 0.4 U/μL SplintR Ligase in 1× SplintR Ligase buffer at 16 °C for a minimum of 1 h to yield a circular template DNA-primer heterodimer. To confirm the successful circularization of the template DNA, treat the system with 0.2 U/μL of exonuclease I and 2 U/μL of exonuclease III in 1× appropriate buffer at 37 °C for 1 h to completely remove the primers and linear template. Verify the results through polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE).

Verification of Rolling Circle Amplification

In a 1× phi29 DNA polymerase buffer, combine a specific concentration of primers with 1 μM of circular template, 0.4 mM dNTP mixture, and 0.2 U/μL phi29 DNA polymerase. Incubate the mixture at 30 °C for a predetermined duration. The rolling circle amplification products are confirmed through agarose gel electrophoresis.

Preparation of Enzyme-DNA Barcode Conjugates

Initially, a 30 μM solution of enzyme (AP or HRP) was combined with 1.5 mM of the bifunctional crosslinker NHS-PEG4-N3 in 1× PBS buffer. The mixture was gently stirred for 1 h at 300 rpm and 25 °C to convert the amino groups of the enzyme into azide groups. Once the reaction was complete, the enzyme was purified using a 30 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube and washed 6 times with 1× PBS buffer (pH 7.4) to remove excess NHS-PEG4-N3. Subsequently, the azide-modified enzyme was mixed with DBCO-modified DNA barcodes at a concentration ratio of 10 μM:100 μM and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h to produce the enzyme-DNA barcode conjugates. To eliminate excess DBCO-DNA, the product was filtered again using a 30 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube and washed with 1× PBS buffer (pH 7.2). Finally, The conjugated enzymes were then stored in PBS containing 50% glycerol at −20 °C for future use.

All conjugates were verified for successful enzyme-DNA conjugation using SDS-PAGE to confirm the expected molecular weight shift. Enzyme activity was assessed using HRP or AP activity assays to ensure that the conjugates retained sufficient enzymatic activity.

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

A 15% PAGE was selected for assessing the preparation of the circular template. Initially, the samples were mixed with 5× DNA loading buffer at a 4:1 volume ratio. Subsequently, 10 μL of the prepared mixture was loaded onto the 15% PAGE for electrophoresis, using 1× TBE buffer. The gel electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V for 20 min, followed by a voltage increase to 120 V for an additional 45 min. Following electrophoresis, the gel was imaged and analyzed using the Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR imaging system.

Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

The efficiency of Rolling Circle Amplification (RCA) was evaluated through 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Prior to loading, all samples were mixed with 5× DNA loading buffer at a 4:1 volume ratio. Subsequently, 6 μL of the prepared mixture was loaded into the 2% agarose gel. In 1× TAE buffer, the gel electrophoresis was conducted at 120 V for 40 min. Following electrophoresis, the agarose gel was imaged and analyzed using the Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR imaging system.

SDS-PAGE

To verify the preparation of enzyme-primer conjugates, 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis was employed. Typically, samples containing 10 μM enzyme were mixed with 5× loading buffer in a 4:1 volume ratio and heated at 100 °C for 10 min to denature the enzyme. A 10 μL aliquot of the mixture was loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel, with a pre-stained protein standard (6.5–270 kDa) serving as a reference marker. The gel was run at 80 V for 20 min, followed by an additional 60 min at 120 V to complete the electrophoresis. The gel was then placed in a microwave and heated for 3 min, repeated twice. Subsequently, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. After washing with H₂O, imaging and analysis of the samples were conducted using the Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR gel imaging system and ImageJ software.

Application of Enzymatic Colorimetric Coding Detection Process for Target miRNA

First, the circularization of the template DNA is achieved by preparing a 100 μL reaction mixture that includes 1× SplintR Ligase buffer, miRNA samples, 50 nM of the corresponding template DNA, and 0.4 U/μL of SplintR Ligase. This mixture is incubated at 25 °C for 30 min to form the circular template DNA-target heterodimer.

Concurrently, the capture strand is immobilized on a streptavidin-coated 96-well enzyme plate by adding 200 µL of 10 μM 5’-biotinylated capture strand to each well and incubating at room temperature for 2 h. The wells are washed three times with PBS to eliminate excess capture strand, ensuring complete immobilization.

The circular template DNA-target complex is then incubated at room temperature for 30 min within the wells coated with the capture strand, followed by three washes. Subsequently, rolling circle amplification (RCA) is performed by mixing 0.4 mM dNTP, 0.2 U/μL phi29 DNA polymerase, 100 nM HRP-DNA barcode 1, and 100 nM AP-DNA barcode 2 in 200 μL of 1× phi29 DNA polymerase buffer in the microplate wells and incubating at 30 °C for 30 min.

Lastly, an enzyme-catalyzed colorimetric reaction is conducted by adding 200 μL of the color development solution, which consists of 5 mM pNPP, 5 mM ABTS, and 0.5 mM H₂O₂ mixed in 1× PBS, to the enzyme plate. The reaction is allowed to proceed at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance measurements are taken using a multi-mode microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland), and the color development results are photographed and analyzed. All assays were conducted under controlled ambient conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% relative humidity). For image acquisition, a fixed-distance holder was used to standardize the camera-to-plate geometry, together with uniform diffuse illumination (LED ring light or backlight lightbox) to reduce shadows and spatial inhomogeneity.

By incorporating various template DNAs corresponding to multiple miRNA targets, multiplex detection of these targets is enabled. Through the inclusion of blank template DNAs corresponding to the miRNA targets in appropriate proportions, weighted detection of the targets is achieved.

For calibration and robustness evaluation, a panel of score-referenced standard samples spanning the intended score range was processed in parallel with each assay plate. These standards were measured in triplicate and served as internal controls to correct for run-to-run variations in enzyme activity and optical readouts, enabling consistent transformation of colorimetric signals into decoded scores.

Simulated Fluorescence Validation of Target Detection

First, fluorescence validation of miRNA detection is performed on an enzyme-linked plate. In the RCA process described, a fluorescence-labeled DNA barcode is substituted for the enzyme-DNA barcode polymer. After 1 h of RCA, the microplate is subjected to fluorescence imaging and analysis using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Next, fluorescence validation for the dual detection of miRNA targets is carried out on magnetic beads. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads are incubated with 5’ biotin-modified capture DNA at concentrations of 4 mg/mL and 10 μM at room temperature for 2 h. Following this, the beads are washed three times with PBS to remove any excess capture strands. After the magnetic beads are coated with different capture DNA, they are mixed in equal proportions. Then, the two miRNA targets are mixed with their respective template DNA for circularization. The resulting circular template DNA-target polymer product is mixed with the capture DNA-modified magnetic beads. This mixture is incubated at room temperature for 0.5 h and then washed three times with 1× PBS. Finally, the magnetic beads are mixed with 0.4 mM dNTPs, 0.2 U/μL phi29 DNA polymerase, 100 nM FAM-labeled DNA barcode 1, and 100 nM Cy3-labeled DNA barcode 2 in a total volume of 200 μL of 1× phi29 DNA polymerase buffer, and rolling circle amplification is performed for 1 h. The samples are analyzed through fluorescence imaging using an LSM 900 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

The Construction and Validation of the Pancreatic Cancer Prediction Model

The construction and validation of the pancreatic cancer prediction model was conducted using miRNA expression datasets GSE21169250, GSE163031, and GSE10681751 downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE211692 dataset included 851 serum samples from pancreatic cancer patients and 1972 serum samples from healthy individuals. The GSE163031 dataset comprised 25 tissue samples from pancreatic cancer patients and 13 samples from non-cancerous pancreas. The GSE106817 dataset consisted of 115 serum samples from pancreatic cancer patients and 2759 serum samples from healthy individuals.

The LIMMA package from the Bioconductor project in R software52 was utilized to identify differentially expressed miRNAs. Given our objective to analyze serum miRNAs for subsequent detection, we aimed to find miRNAs with greater expression differences in serum samples. The cutoff for differentially expressed miRNAs in serum samples from the GSE211692 dataset was set as log2 fold change (FC) ≥ 3.5 or log2 fold change (FC) ≤ −2.5 and an error discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, while in the tissue samples from the GSE163031 dataset, the cutoff was set as |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥ 1 and an FDR < 0.05. Overlapping miRNA expression profiles between the GSE211692 and GSE163031 datasets were retained for further study.

Binary logistic regression was used to construct a probability model for pancreatic cancer using the selected miRNAs as predictors. Prior to model fitting, miRNA concentrations were log10-transformed to reduce skewness and improve numerical stability across the wide dynamic range introduced by the pre-amplification step. Regression coefficients were estimated by maximum likelihood using the standard iterative algorithm implemented in SPSS (Version 20). A parsimonious model was derived using forward stepwise selection in SPSS (Forward: Wald), with candidate variables sequentially entered according to prespecified significance criteria (entry p < 0.05). Model discrimination was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and summarized by the area under the curve (AUC). The final score was reported as a linear combination of the retained miRNAs with their estimated coefficients and directions of effect.

miRNA Extraction in Serum Samples

All methods were performed in accordance with the approved guidelines. Unless otherwise specified, miRNAs in serum samples were extracted using column-based the miRcute Serum/Plasma miRNA Extraction Kit (TianGen Biotech, Beijing, China). All samples were purified according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the eluted RNA was stored in nucleic acid-free water at −80 °C until required.

miRNA RT-qPCR Quantification

Following the extraction of miRNAs from serum samples, unless otherwise specified, the miRNAs were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio, Japan). Subsequently, quantitative PCR (qPCR) detection was performed using Real Time PCR kits from TaKaRa Bio (Japan). All samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Pre-amplification of Serum miRNA for Enzymatic Colorimetric Coding Detection

Pre-amplification of serum miRNAs using LATE-PCR (Linear-After-The-Exponential PCR) was performed prior to enzymatic colorimetric coding detection. Following the reverse transcription of miRNAs into cDNA, the 2X TaqMan fast qPCR master mix from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) was used for the LATE-PCR amplification. The concentrations of the forward and reverse primers were adjusted to be excess and limiting, respectively. The excess primer was set at 2 μM, and the limiting primer at 50 nM. The limiting primer was derived from the universal primers provided in the one-step miRNA reverse transcription kit from TaKaRa Bio (Japan). All other steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Overview of the EnCODE Assay Workflow

The entire EnCODE assay, from sample preparation to final colorimetric readout, is completed within 7 h with the following breakdown:

Serum/Plasma miRNA Extraction: Serum/plasma samples are processed using the miRcute Serum/Plasma miRNA Extraction Kit (TianGen Biotech, Beijing, China). This step takes approximately 1 h Using a refrigerated high-speed centrifuge (Eppendorf 5424), 24 serum/plasma samples can be processed per batch. Extracted miRNAs are stored in nuclease-free water at −80 °C until use.

miRNA Pre-amplification: miRNAs are reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio, Japan), followed by LATE-PCR amplification using the 2X TaqMan Fast qPCR Master Mix (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). This step requires 30–40 min for reverse transcription and 1–1.5 h for PCR amplification. The sample processing capacity per batch is limited to the capacity of the PCR machine, typically 96 samples per batch.

Circularization of DNA Templates: Using the pre-amplified miRNA product as a primer, DNA templates are circularized via SplintR ligase. This step takes approximately 30–40 min, with no theoretical limit to the number of samples per batch.

Capture Probe Coating: The capture probe is immobilized onto a streptavidin-coated enzyme plate in 2 h, which can be done in parallel with steps 1, 2, and 3.

Template Capture: Circularized DNA templates are captured by incubating the miRNA-pre-amplified DNA-template complex with the streptavidin-coated enzyme plate. This step takes 30 min. The batch processing capacity is theoretically unlimited.

Rolling Circle Amplification and Signal Encoding: Phi29 polymerase is used for amplification of the circular template, while simultaneously introducing DNA-enzyme complexes for signal encoding. This step takes 30 min and can handle an unlimited number of samples per batch.

Enzyme-Catalyzed Colorimetric Reaction: Substrates for the enzyme-catalyzed reaction are added, producing detectable colorimetric signals. This step requires 30 min. While theoretically unlimited in terms of sample processing per batch, for reducing batch-to-batch variation, it is recommended to process no more than 96 samples per batch.

Signal Detection: Signal detection takes 3–5 min, with the capacity to process 96 samples per batch.

The total hands-on processing time is approximately 5–6 h. With additional error allowances for calculation and operational setup, the full process from clinical sample to result output can be completed in approximately 6–7 h. All steps in the assay can be automated with robotic arms, reducing processing time and minimizing batch-to-batch variation.

Three-dimensional Colorimetric Coding and Hyperspectral Imaging Detection

In a mixture solution containing 5 mM ABTS and 0.5 mM H2O2, HRP (2 nM) was added and fully catalyzed until the color became stable, resulting in a green solution. Similarly, in a mixture solution containing 5 mM Amplex Red and 0.5 mM H2O2, HRP (2 nM) was added and fully catalyzed until the color became stable, resulting in a red solution. In a solution containing 5 mM pNPP, AP (2 nM) was added and fully catalyzed until the color became stable, resulting in a YELLOW solution. The three basic color solutions were mixed in a series of different volumes and adjusted to maintain a final solution volume of 200 μL. The absorption spectra of the final solutions were measured in a 96-well microplate reader and the solutions were photographed. The average RGB values of the solutions were analyzed using Photoshop software.

Additionally, the three-dimensional Colorimetric coding results were photographed using a hyperspectral camera (Specim IQ, Finland), and the reflectance spectra were analyzed using ENVI software (v 5.6).

Statistics & Reproducibility

Unless otherwise specified, data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.2.1. All data were statistically analyzed using Two-tailed Student’s t-test, and are presented as the mean ± s.d.
