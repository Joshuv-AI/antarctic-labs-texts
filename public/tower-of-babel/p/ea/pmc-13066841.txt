Conventional ultrasound combined with contrast-enhanced ultrasound for differentiating benign and malignant thyroid nodules
Authors: Lijuan Luo, Jing Wen, Haisong Duan, Chaonan Deng, Yan Cai, Hongqing Ma, Yuyu Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound (US) is the primary modality used to evaluate thyroid nodules (TNs). However, the US features of a portion of benign TNs may suggest malignancy, leading to decreased diagnostic efficacy. Previous studies have reported that contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has good efficacy in determining the malignancy of TNs. However, guidelines for thyroid CEUS remain lacking. The purpose of this study was to conduct single-factor and multifactor analysis to assess the value conventional US combined with CEUS in diagnosing TNs.MethodsIn this study, a cohort of 871 patients (345 males and 526 females) with 1,039 TNs was enrolled, and TNs were classified into a benign or malignant group based on the pathological results. TN echogenicity, echo level, aspect ratio, margin regularity and smoothness, extrinsic invasion, presence and degree of calcification, acoustic halo, lesion boundary, enhancement direction, level and distribution, peripheral circular enhancement, and TN texture after enhancement were included by single-factor analysis. A binary logistic regression model was constructed, with statistically significant indices serving as the independent variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and forest plots were used to evaluate the model effectiveness in differentiating benign and malignant TNs.ResultsThe US and CEUS characteristics of benign and malignant TNs were different. An aspect ratio ≥1, poorly defined boundaries on enhancement, and hypoenhancement were risk factors for malignant TNs (area under the ROC curve =0.891; 95% confidence interval: 0.868–0.913; P<0.001). The specificity, sensitivity, and Youden index of the model in diagnosing malignant TNs were 76.4%, 88.0%, and 0.643, respectively.ConclusionsAn aspect ratio ≥1, unclear boundaries after enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, centripetal enhancement, hypoenhancement, and stiffness of the TN after enhancement were identified as indicators of malignant TNs.Keywords: Two-dimensional ultrasound (2D US), contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), thyroid nodule (TN)

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Observatory, there were 586,000 new cases of thyroid cancer (TC) in 2020 (1). Conventional ultrasound (US) possesses certain advantages in examining thyroid nodules (TNs), including good safety, no radiation, and the provision of real-time dynamic imaging (2). Globally, with the widespread use of US, the detection rate of TC has significantly risen, raising concerns of overdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of other thyroid diseases (3). Inflammatory nodules, nodular goiter necrosis stage, zombie nodules, and other benign lesions may present similar low-echo and blurred boundary manifestations as those of malignant nodules, which may contribute to a relatively low specificity in US-based diagnosis (4). Previous studies indicate that contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has good efficacy in determining the malignancy of TNs, with a sensitivity of 0.5–0.87 and a specificity of 0.65–0.83 (5,6). However, no guidelines for thyroid CEUS have been formulated thus far, and the value of CEUS for diagnosing TNs remains to be definitively assessed.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the accuracy of US and CEUS in assessing benign and malignant TNs. Due to the differences in vascular distribution between TNs and the surrounding normal thyroid tissue, secondary-harmonic imaging in CEUS can effectively distinguish TNs based on their contrast imaging features. During the CEUS imaging process, benign TNs have a soft texture and are similar to the normal thyroid tissue. They move slightly with normal breathing, while malignant TNs are stiff and remain in the same position, being still during normal breathing. This study is the first to demonstrate that the texture of enhanced TNs is an effective indicator for distinguishing between benign and malignant TNs. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1509/rc).

Methods

Study cohort

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (ethics approval No. [2021]321). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The study cohort included 871 patients (345 males and 526 females; mean age 45±11.5 years; age range, 22–74 years) with 1,039 TNs who had undergone thyroid US and CEUS with pathological analysis of TN specimens between June 2021 and December 3, 2023, at The Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University.

The inclusion criteria were (I) age ≥18 years; (II) no US-guided fine-needle aspiration of TNs within the 2 weeks prior to the CEUS procedure; and (III) diagnosis confirmed by postoperative histopathologic analysis. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) allergy to sulfur hexafluoride or other components of contrast media, (II) heart diseases (e.g., non-ST elevation myocardial infarction, ST elevation myocardial infarction, effort angina, variant angina, preinfarction angina, postinfarction angina, mixed angina, reduced ejection fraction heart failure, malignant arrhythmias, and right-to-left shunt heart diseases), tetralogy of Fallot, severe pulmonary heart disease (pulmonary hypertension >90 mmHg), adult respiratory distress syndrome, and refractory hypertension, and (III) pregnancy/lactation. Based on the pathologic findings of TNs, the participants were assigned to a benign or malignant group.

Examination equipment

Examinations were conducted with the LOGIQ E11 system equipped with a high-frequency probe (L2-9 or M6-15; GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) and the Resona R9 system equipped with a high-frequency probe (L9-3U or L14-3WU; Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China).

For routine US examinations, the patient was instructed to lie supine and breathe calmly, with the neck and shoulders supported with a pillow to fully expose the anterior and lateral sides of the neck. After the target TN was determined, the location of the TN, maximum diameter in longitudinal and transverse sections, internal echoes, echo level, anteroposterior-to-transverse diameter ratio (A/T), margins, relationship with the capsule, absence or presence of microcalcifications, presence of acoustic halos, and blood flow were recorded.

For CEUS examinations, the maximum plane of the target TN was selected, and the transducer was switched to the harmonic CEUS mode (mechanical index 0.05–0.08). Sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles (1.5–2.0 mL; SonoVue; Bracco S.p.A., Milan, Italy) were rapidly pushed through a peripheral vein. The patient was asked to breathe quietly and not swallow. The timing and storage keys were activated, and the changes in the images of the TNs after enhanced imaging for more than 1 minute were observed. The following CEUS indices were recorded: (I) lesion boundary; (II) enhancement direction: (i) centripetal enhancement, (ii) diffuse enhancement, or (iii) centrifugal enhancement; (III) enhancement level (hyper-, iso-, or hypoenhancement); (IV) distribution of enhancement intensity; (V) circular enhancement around the lesion (with or without circular enhancement); (VI) lesion enhancement time and lesion regression time; and (VII) texture after enhancement (stiff or soft). After the injection of contrast agent, the TNs and normal thyroid tissues moved slightly along with normal breathing and were found to be of soft texture. If the TN did not move with breathing but remained stationary, this indicated a hard texture.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for Windows was used for statistical analyses. The chi-squared test was applied for univariate analysis, and if the minimum expected value was not met, the Fisher’s exact test was conducted. With pathological results of the malignant group and the benign group serving as the gold standard, a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Bonferroni method was used for pairwise comparisons for each categorical variable in the chi-square test, with an adjusted P value <0.017 being considered statistically significant. With pathological results serving as the dependent variable and significantly different US features as the independent variable, a binary logistic regression model was established. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the diagnostic value of conventional US and CEUS features for benign and malignant TNs. GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) was applied to generate forest plots to visualize the logistic regression results.

Results

The mean age of patients was 45±11.5 years. Among the 764 cases of malignant nodules, 531 cases had solid echoes, 198 cases had predominantly solid echoes, and 35 cases had predominantly cystic echoes. There were 83 hyperechoic cases, 552 hypoechoic cases, and 129 markedly hypoechoic cases, 266 cases with an aspect ratio <1, 498 cases with an aspect ratio ≥1, 446 cases with nonblurred margins, 18 cases with blurred margins, 589 cases with irregular edges, 175 cases with regular edges, 125 cases with microcalcification, 582 cases without microcalcification, 57 cases with large calcification, 605 cases without edge invasion, 159 cases with edge invasion, 707 cases with silent halos, 57 cases with audible halos, 185 cases with a diameter >10 mm, and 579 cases with a diameter ≤10 mm.

Among the 275 benign nodules, 211 cases had solid echoes, 47 cases were predominantly solid echoes, and 17 cases were predominantly cystic echoes. There were 15 hyperechoic cases, 222 hypoechoic cases, and 38 markedly hypoechoic cases. There were 122 cases with an aspect ratio <1, 153 cases with an aspect ratio ≥1, 148 cases with nonblurred margins, 127 cases with blurred margins, 229 cases with irregular edges, 46 cases with regular edges, 41 cases with microcalcification, 195 cases without microcalcification, 39 cases with large calcification, 219 cases without edge invasion, 56 cases with edge invasion, 244 cases with silent halos, 31 cases with audible halos, 60 cases with a diameter >10 mm, and 215 cases with a diameter ≤10 mm.

Among the 764 cases of malignant nodules, the lesion boundaries were clear in 264 cases and obscure in 500 cases on CEUS. The direction of enhanced blood perfusion within the lesion was centripetal enhancement in 449 cases, centrifugal enhancement in 25 cases, and diffuse enhancement in 290 cases. There were 6 cases with no enhancement of the lesion enhancement level, 223 cases with hyperenhancement, 17 cases with isoenhancement, 518 cases with hypoenhancement, 79 cases with homogeneous enhancement, and 685 cases with heterogeneous enhancement. There were 52 cases of annular enhancement around the lesion and 712 cases without an enhancement ring. The enhancement time of the lesion was synchronized with that of the surrounding thyroid tissue in 652 cases, earlier than that of the surrounding thyroid tissue in 17 cases, and later than that of the surrounding thyroid tissue in 95 cases. The regression time of the lesion was synchronous with that of the surrounding thyroid tissue in 273 cases, earlier than that in 305 cases, and later than that in 186 cases. After enhancement, 97 cases had soft nodules and 667 cases had stiff nodules.

Among the 275 benign nodules, 136 cases had clear lesion boundaries and 139 cases had unclear boundaries on CEUS. The direction of enhanced blood perfusion within the lesion was centripetal enhancement in 76 cases, centrifugal enhancement in 60 cases, and diffuse enhancement in 139 cases. There were 33 cases with no enhancement of the lesion enhancement level, 105 cases with hyperenhancement, 49 cases with isoenhancement, 88 cases with hypoenhancement, 69 cases with homogeneous enhancement, and 206 cases with heterogeneous enhancement. There were 24 cases of annular enhancement around the lesion and 251 cases without an enhancement ring. The enhancement time of the lesion was synchronized with that of the peripheral thyroid tissue in 232 cases, earlier than that of the peripheral thyroid tissue in 1 case, and later than that of the peripheral thyroid tissue in 42 cases. The regression time of the lesion was synchronous with that of the surrounding thyroid tissue in 98 cases, earlier than that in 99 cases, and later than that in 78 cases. After enhancement, 206 cases had soft nodules and 69 cases had stiff nodules.

Comparison of conventional US imaging characteristics between benign and malignant TNs

Differences in the nine indices described above were cross-analyzed via the chi-squared test. The results indicated no significant difference in the diagnosis of benign and malignant TNs based on the presence of an acoustic halo, maximum tumor diameter, blurred/nonblurred margins, or extrinsic invasion (P>0.05). However, there was a significant difference in diagnosis of benign and malignant TNs based on the echo (χ2=9.192; P<0.05), echo level (χ2=9.406; P<0.05), aspect (A/T) ratio (χ2=7.877; P<0.05), regular/irregular margins (χ2=4.721; P<0.05), and calcification (χ2=10.904; P<0.05). Multiple comparisons were conducted via the chi-squared test with Bonferroni correction. At the α=0.05 level, there was a statistical difference between the number of benign TNs with solid echoes and predominantly solid echoes and the number of malignant TNs with solid echoes (P<0.017) but not between solid echoes and predominantly cystic echoes or between solid echoes and predominantly solid echoes (Table 1).

Discussion

Due to the incidence of TC increasing over the past three decades, it currently represents a major healthcare issue across the world. In the United States, there were 52,890 new cases of TC in 2020, making it the eleventh most common cancer, and in China, there were approximately 90,000 new cases in 2015, making it the ninth most common cancer. Overall, with an annualized incidence of 20.1%, TC is among the most prevalent malignant tumors (1). The increased detection and incidence rates of TC have been attributed to a greater awareness among endocrinologists and the growing popularity of neck ultrasonography (7). However, overdiagnosis may be the reason for the rise in incidence of malignant thyroid tumors, which may lead to overtreatment. In 2014, respective articles in the New England Journal of Medicine and the Lancet reported a noticeable increase in the incidence of TC in Korea, described as a “tsunami” of TC. Although not as extreme as this surge in Korea, the incidence of TC has been rapidly growing in the United States, France, Australia, and Canada (8,9). Early detection and diagnosis are key to achieving good treatment outcomes. However, the increase in diagnoses has not led to decreased mortality from TC, and the avoidance of overdiagnosis of TNs has become particularly challenging.

US is the preferred modality for evaluating TNs. However, the US features of certain benign TNs are suggestive of malignancy, which reduces diagnostic efficacy (10). Maddaloni et al. (11) reported that two-dimensional US provides little benefit in excluding the risk of suspected malignancy, as chromatin concentration is not only found in malignant tumors but also exists in benign thyroid diseases, such as nodular goiter and Hashimoto thyroiditis. When cystic changes occur, the internal components of the cancerous nodules are also altered. Moreover, this complex background can also affect the two-dimensional manifestations such as the internal echo and boundary of the nodules. These phenomena can increase the difficulty and risk of two-dimensional US in the differential diagnosis of benign and malignant TNs. Vascularity is a key feature of malignancy. Malignant thyroid tumors are hypovascular tumors (7), but small vessels and slow blood flow within TNs can also lead to failure of color Doppler imaging (12). In contrast, CEUS can provide more information related to the vessels (13). Moreover, color Doppler can only display blood vessels larger than 0.5 µm, while CEUS can provide information on small blood vessels and low-speed blood flow. Finally, color Doppler primarily shows the speed and path of blood flow, while CEUS can provide perfusion information on blood flow. However, there is yet no unified standard for the indicators of CEUS. Several studies have reported the favorable specificity and sensitivity of CEUS for the diagnosis of TC, suggesting that US combined with qualitative CEUS indicators may be valuable for determining TN malignancy (14).

Aspect ratio ≥1 on US images as an indicator of malignant TNs

According to the Chinese Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System, a TN with any cross-section with an anteroposterior diameter greater than or equal to the transverse diameter on US imaging is suggestive of malignancy (15). Benign TNs grow parallel to the normal tissue plane, whereas malignant TNs grow longitudinally in a centrifugal pattern through the normal thyroid tissue plane (16,17). In our study, an aspect ratio ≥1 was predictive of malignancy, in agreement with the findings of a previous study (14). This can be attributed to the fact that cells anterior and posterior to a cancerous TN appear to be in the early stage of cell division, whereas cells on the left and right are relatively quiescent. This difference in cell division leads to a tendency for the tumor to grow longitudinally (18). However, Cai et al. (19) reported no significant difference between single-section and double-section aspect ratios ≥1 in predicting malignancy. Moreover, we only assessed an aspect ratio ≥1 in our study, and thus, in future work, we plan to assess the values of both single-section and double-section aspect ratios for differentiating benign and malignant TNs.

Risk factors for TC

The CEUS-based risk factors for TC identified in this study included poorly defined boundaries, heterogeneous enhancement, centripetal enhancement, hypoenhancement, and stiffness of TNs, which is partially in agreement with the findings of a previous study (8).

Malignant TNs exhibit infiltrative growth, lack of clear boundaries with surrounding tissues, disrupted continuity or absence of the peripheral capsule, and unclear blood supply, especially when the tumor infiltration margin is small or obscure (20,21). However, conventional US features overlap somewhat between malignant and benign TNs (22), and it is occasionally difficult to determine the margins of TNs, especially when the image quality is poor (23-25). CEUS has high specificity and sensitivity in imaging the boundary between the lesion and surrounding normal thyroid gland, and thus the detection of unclear nodular margins can improve the diagnostic efficacy for malignant TNs (26). The majority of malignant TNs have predominantly centripetal enhancement, which is due to the division of neovascularization in malignant TNs into central and peripheral regions. Vessels in the central region are sparse or have significant fibrosis, while the peripheral region has relatively dense blood vessels. The difference in the distribution of blood vessels between the central and peripheral regions results in centripetal enhancement (27). However, centripetal enhancement is also observed in benign TNs (28). In this study, 76 benign TNs showed centripetal enhancement, which is consistent with a previous study (29). Factors influencing the centripetal perfusion mode of benign TNs may include the following: (I) various stages of development of nodular goiter, such as colloid storage, fibrous tissue encapsulating lobules or follicles, hemorrhage, scar formation, necrosis, and calcification (30); and (II) the lack of neovascularization due to invasion by inflammatory cells, the presence of normal glands in areas of inflammation, and the prolonged alternating processes of regression and hyperplasia within the nodule, which can lead to changes in vascularity (31). The vascular distribution of malignant TNs is heterogeneous. Intracancerous necrosis, cancer emboli, arteriovenous fistula formation, and fibrosis result in heterogeneous enhancement and hypoenhancement of malignant TNs (31-33).

The stiff texture of the TN on CEUS was the best-performing feature for predicting malignant TNs, with an odds ratio (OR) of 22.400, indicating that the risk of malignant tumors increased by 22 times with poor nodular mobility on enhancement when all other independent variables were constant, a finding which has not been previously reported. Malignant TNs grow faster than do benign TNs and have a higher degree of follicular destruction. Moreover, malignant TNs are characterized by the longitudinal arrangement of cells; fewer parenchymal cells, glial components, and intranodular vessels; and a greater abundance of interstitial components, fibers, and calcified psammoma bodies. Therefore, the risk of malignant TNs is positively correlated with stiffness (34-36). Accordingly, benign TNs have a soft texture with normal mobility, while malignant TNs are harder with poor mobility. However, TN texture varies according to location. As a result, isthmus TNs with a fixed location are easily misdiagnosed as malignant.

CEUS characteristics of the different pathologic types of TNs

Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC)

Most thyroid tumors originate from follicular epithelial cells (nearly half). PTC is rich in lymphatic vessels but has relatively less vascularity, and the majority exhibit hypoenhancement on CEUS (37). In our study, 27 PTCs (among 27 participants) were hyperenhanced, which was mainly because active vascular status resulted in hyperenhancement (38). He et al. (39) found that hyperenhanced malignant TNs were more prone to lymphatic metastasis. However, lymph node metastasis was not examined in our study. Hence, further investigation is needed to evaluate the diagnostic value of varying degrees of enhancement for suspected malignant TNs and lymph node metastasis. In addition, the CEUS features of different pathological subtypes were not analyzed, but these might have influenced the results.

TNs combined with Hashimoto thyroiditis and subacute thyroiditis

The principal pathological findings of subacute thyroiditis are follicular destruction and glial overflow with interstitial fibrosis (40). In this study, 12 cases (among 10 patients) of subacute thyroiditis were misdiagnosed as malignant due to heterogeneous hypoenhancement, which was due to invasion of inflammatory cells of a normal thyroid gland, lack of neovascularization, and normal glands present in the area of inflammation, especially those with rapid progression of inflammation, resulting in small or absent glands (41). Risk factors for subacute thyroiditis include a history of viral infection and thyroid pain. The main pathological changes of Hashimoto thyroiditis are lymphocytic infiltration with lymphoid follicle formation, transformation of follicular epithelial cells into eosinophils, reduction of hyperchromatic glial cells in the cavity, and infiltration of interstitial plasma cells and diffuse lymphocytes with extensive proliferation of fibrotic tissue (42). In our study, the presence of heterogeneous hypoenhancement led to misdiagnosis of malignancy in 15 cases of Hashimoto thyroiditis, mostly due to the heterogeneous formation of extensive fibrosis in the advanced stages. In three cases of Hashimoto thyroiditis, the TNs exhibited isoenhancement from chronic inflammation due to the formation of lymphoid follicles and the invasion of plasma cells and lymphocytes. However, Hashimoto thyroiditis is associated with the isoenhancement of peripheral gland formation but not tumor neovascularization or inflammatory edema (43). Elevated levels of antithyroid antibodies and ultrasonographic recognition of intrathyroidal hypovolemia may be diagnostic indicators of Hashimoto thyroiditis (44).

Nodular goiter and adenoma

Nodular goiter is pathologically characterized by alternating hyperplasia and regression, which leads to the proliferation of fibrotic tissue that encompasses glial-filled follicles (45). Thyroid adenomas are characterized by the tendency to grow expansively, which slightly pushes the veins and arteries to the periphery of the tumor, and the formation of new capillaries (46). Previous studies indicate that adenomas generally display diffuse homogeneous hyperenhancement on CEUS. In our study, there were 9 cases of adenomas and 72 cases of nodular goiters with heterogeneous centripetal hypoenhancement that were misdiagnosed as malignant TNs; moreover, the pathological findings were all associated with proliferation of fibrotic tissue, scarring, or calcification. This may be explained by the fact that nodular goiters and adenomas with interstitial collagenization, calcification, and crystallization show punctate hyperechoes, which can easily be interpreted as microcalcification by conventional US and interfere with the assessment of the thyroid gland by US. Additionally, the bulk of these TNs are benign and degenerative and primarily caused by hemorrhage, degeneration, infarction, and fibrosis. During hematoma absorption, the degenerated TNs gradually become irregular and shrink, suggesting malignancy on CEUS (47).

Limitations

This study involved certain limitations that should be addressed. First, there was obvious selection bias due to the population in the analysis consisting exclusively of patients who underwent thyroidectomy. Second, most malignant TNs were papillary carcinomas, with few cases of follicular carcinoma and no cases of medullary or undifferentiated carcinoma. Moreover, benign TNs were predominantly of the nodular goiter type, and the findings are not representative of all pathologic types of TNs. Finally, quantitative CEUS was not performed.

Conclusions

An aspect ratio ≥1 on conventional US images was found to be a valuable indicator for differentiating between benign and malignant TNs. An obscure boundary on CEUS, heterogeneous enhancement, centripetal enhancement, hypoenhancement, and stiffness of the TN on enhancement may suggest the malignancy of TNs.
