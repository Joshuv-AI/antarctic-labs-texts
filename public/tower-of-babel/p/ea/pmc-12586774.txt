Conditional survival prediction in elderly patients with hormone receptor positive locally advanced breast cancer using a competing risks model
Authors: Jun Zhang, Yunyun Zhao, Wenhui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeConditional survival (CS) analysis reveals dynamic changes in long-term survival, offering cancer survivors more accurate prognosis. We aimed to develop a prognostic model for elderly patients with hormone receptor-positive locally advanced breast cancer (HR+ LABC) based on CS.MethodsData for patients aged > 65 years with HR+ LABC were obtained from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database (2010–2021, n = 8,450) and randomly divided into training (n = 5,915, 70%) and validation (n = 2,535, 30%) sets. CS analysis assessed changes in 10-year survival over time. Multivariable Cox regression identified prognostic factors for breast cancer-specific mortality (BCSM, defined as death due to breast cancer) and overall mortality (OM, defined as death from any cause) for model development.ResultsFor the entire cohort, 10-year OM and 10-year BCSM were 58.7% (95% CI 56.8%-60.4%) and 28.0% (95% CI 26.6%-29.4%), respectively. Both OM and BCSM improved over time according to CS analysis. Competing risks regression identified seven independent factors associated with BCSM: age, T stage, N stage, histological grade, HR status, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Ten prognostic factors were identified for OM, with additional factors including marital status, race, and surgery (all p < 0.05). The CS models demonstrated strong concordance between predicted and observed outcomes in both training and validation sets. The median time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) for the 10-year period remained above 0.73 for both models.ConclusionsThis study investigated the CS patterns in elderly patients with HR-positive LABC and develop personalized CS prediction models. These models may assist in patient counseling, guide clinical decision-making, and support healthcare resource allocation during follow-up. However, several limitations remain before clinical application, including the absence of important clinical variables, limited applicability to non-surgical patients, and the lack of prospective validation. Introduction

Hormone receptor-positive (HR+) status is a common subtype of breast cancer among elderly women, characterized by its generally favorable prognosis when treated with endocrine therapies [1, 2]. However, the elderly population presents unique clinical challenges due to age-related comorbidities, decreased functional reserve, and varying responses to standard treatments, especially in patients with locally advanced disease [3–6]. Therefore, they tend to be more concerned with “how many additional years can I live from now?” rather than “how many years can I live from diagnosis”. Traditional prognostic methods typically provide static survival estimates calculated from the time of diagnosis, regardless of how long a patient has already survived. This limits their utility in long-term survivors, whose risk of mortality may change significantly over time [7].

Conditional survival (CS) analysis provides a more refined prognostic tool by estimating survival probabilities contingent upon patients having survived a specific period after diagnosis [7–9]. This approach is particularly relevant for elderly patients with HR-positive locally advanced breast cancer (LABC), as it incorporates the time already survived after diagnosis and treatment into the survival estimation process [10–12]. Incorporating competing risks, such as non-cancer-related mortality, further enhances the accuracy of survival predictions in this population, where the likelihood of dying from causes other than breast cancer increases with age [3, 4].

This study aims to develop a real-time survival prognostic model for elderly patients with HR+ LABC using CS analysis, which dynamically updates survival estimates based on the number of years already survived. By including the time already survived after diagnosis and treatment, this model will provide more precise survival estimates, addressing the specific concerns of elderly patients regarding their future life expectancy. To further aid clinical decision-making and individualized patient care, we will also create a CS-nomogram. This nomogram will serve as a practical tool for oncologists, enabling them to offer personalized survival predictions based on both the patient’s current survival status and the competing risks they face, ultimately helping to optimize treatment strategies and improve quality of life in this vulnerable population.

Methods and materials

Patient and variable selection

We obtained Elderly HR+ LABC data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) dataset for the years 2010–2021 (site code: breast; Histology code: 8500/3; Age > 65 years; Gender: female; TNM stage: IIB-IIIC). The SEER database covers approximately 47.9% of the U.S. population, providing a substantial amount of data for analysis, which ensures the robustness of the results and models. Since this study is retrospective and all patient data have been anonymized, the institutional ethics committee waived the requirement for ethical review and individual informed consent. Additionally, we excluded cases that were not confirmed by histopathology, those where breast cancer was not the first primary malignancy, cases with distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, those that did not undergo surgery, and those with missing essential variables. Finally, the selected variables were randomly divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. The detailed selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Patient selection flowchart

The variables used in this study included age, marital status, race, T and N Staging (American Joint Committee on Cancer, 8th Edition), histologic grade, molecular subtype, type of surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. HR status was further categorized into three groups: estrogen receptor positive only, progesterone receptor positive only, and either one of the two. The primary outcomes of interest were overall mortality (OM) and breast cancer-specific mortality (BCSM), defined as death from any cause and death due to breast cancer, respectively [13].

Statistical analysis

BCSM was the primary endpoint of the study, defined as the interval from breast cancer diagnosis to death due to breast cancer. OM was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause. The cumulative incidence function was utilized to describe the occurrence rate of BCSM, with non-BCSM events considered as competing risks for BCSM. Univariable and multivariable analyses were conducted using Fine and Gray's competing risks regression to examine the association between variables and BCSM. OM was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses performed to assess associations between variables and OM.

To assess the dynamic changes in established mortality rates (e.g., 10-year mortality) over time, CS formulas were applied [7, 8]. These are defined as C-OM(y|x) = OM(y + x)/OM(x) and C-BCSM = BCSM(y + x)/BCSM(x), where C-OM and C-BCSM represent conditional-OM and -BCSM, respectively. Here, x denotes the number of years the patient has already been under observation, and y represents the additional years the patient survives. For example, the 10-year C-OM on a patient having been under observation for 3 years would be calculated as C-OM(7|3) = OM(10)/OM(3), where OM(10) and OM(3) are the 10-year and 3-year OM rates, respectively. The study also calculated the cumulative mortality rates, C-BCSM(1|x) and C-OM(1|x), for the following year, for each additional year of patient survival, where x represents the observation period (ranging from 1 to 9 years). Additionally, annual hazard rates were used to assess yearly mortality, which can be instrumental in interpreting and reinforcing the conclusions conveyed by survival curves [14, 15]. Notably, this dynamic prognostic model updated survival estimates based on the duration already survived, rather than incorporating time-dependent covariates that might evolve during the disease course, such as recurrence or metastasis.

For model construction, the study utilized a nomogram due to its ability to visually calculate individualized risk. Specifically, multivariable regression modeling was performed using variables associated with BCSM and OM, identified through Fine and Gray's competing risks regression and Cox regression, respectively. The results were then visualized using a nomogram. The nomogram assigns a score to each variable and calculates the patient's mortality risk. By integrating CS method into this predictive risk model, the nomogram allows for a more accurate estimation of individualized patient risk, further enabling the development of a C-OM nomogram and a C-BCSM nomogram.

The study employed a training set for the aforementioned modeling process and evaluated the model using a validation set. The evaluation primarily included calibration curves based on 1000 bootstrap resamples to assess the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes—the closer the curve is to the 45° line, the better the calibration performance. To further quantify calibration performance, we additionally computed time-dependent Brier scores. The Brier score measures the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, with lower values indicating better predictive accuracy. A Brier score between 0.1 and 0.25 is generally considered to reflect good calibration performance. Time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) estimates were used to assess the model’s discriminative ability and stability, confirming that the model performance did not deteriorate over time. Data analysis was conducted using R (version 4.4.0). In two-tailed tests, a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

After screening (Fig. 1), a total of 8450 elderly patients with HR+ LABC were included in the study, with 5915 patients (70%) in the training set and 2535 patients (30%) in the validation set. The average age of patients in the entire cohort was 74.0 ± 7.1 years, with the majority presenting at stage IIIA (n = 4124, 48.8%) and being ER & PR positive (n = 6738, 79.7%). Most patients underwent modified radical mastectomy (n = 5828, 69.0%), with 59.1% (n = 4992) receiving radiotherapy. The proportion of patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy was 21.6% (n = 1822), and 36.9% (n = 3120) received adjuvant therapy postoperatively. There were no significant correlations between the variables in the training and validation sets, as detailed in Table 1.

Conditional survival analysis

The median follow-up time for the cohort was 42 months (IQR, 20–75 months). During the follow-up period, a total of 2,921 patients (34.6%) died, with 1467 deaths (17.4%) attributed to breast cancer. The 10-year OM for the patients was 58.7% (95% CI 56.8%-60.4%), and the 10-year BCSM was 28.0% (95% CI 26.6%-29.4%).

CS analysis revealed that both real-time OM and BCSM gradually improved over time (Fig. 2A and B). Specifically, the 10-year C-BCSM risk decreased progressively after 1 to 9 years of survival, from 28.0% to 26.6%, 23.8%, 20.9%, 17.9%, 14.8%, 11.8%, 9.3%, 6.9%, and 3.1% (Fig. 2C). Similarly, the 10-year C-OM risk declined from 58.7% at initial diagnosis to 56.5%, 52.6%, 48.2%, 42.8%, 37.3%, 30.8%, 24.0%, 17.8%, and 8.9% (Fig. 2D). We also observed that the decreasing trend in both mortality risks was nonlinear. The decline was relatively modest (with a lower slope) during the first two years of follow-up, after which it transitioned into a more linear downward trend (Fig. 2C and D). The annual mortality risk curves reinforced this pattern, showing that BCSM was higher than non-BCSM in the first three years after diagnosis. Specifically, the mortality rate peaked in the second-year post-diagnosis, then gradually declined, becoming equivalent to non-BCSM at years 3–4, and subsequently falling below non-BCSM (Fig. 2E and F).

Prognostic factor analysis and model development

The Fine and Gray competing risks regression analysis identified seven independent factors associated with BCSM: age, T stage, N stage, histological grade, HR status, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Similarly, Cox regression analysis identified ten independent prognostic factors associated with OM: age, marital status, race, T stage, N stage, histological grade, HR status, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Detailed results are presented in the forest plots (Fig. 3A and B).

Based on these selected variables, multivariable nomograms were developed (Fig. 4A and B). After inputting patient characteristics, the nomogram quantifies the contribution of each variable to the risks of OM and BCSM, assigning a score for each factor. These scores are then summed to generate an individualized total risk score, corresponding to the patient's risk of OM or BCSM. Additionally, to facilitate real-time prediction, the personalized CS axis was included for both 10-year OM and 10-year BCSM. Users can dynamically update the predictions based on the number of years the patient has already survived by referring to the corresponding axis on the nomogram.

Model evaluation and validation

The models were evaluated in both the training and validation sets. Calibration curves showed that the predictions for OM and BCSM were quite accurate, with the curves closely aligning with the 45° line, indicating that the model predictions were very close to the actual outcomes (Fig. 5A–D). In addition, time-dependent Brier scores demonstrated good overall calibration performance. For the CS-nomogram predicting BCSM, the median Brier score across years 1–10 was 0.11 (IQR, 0.08–0.13) in the training set and 0.11 (IQR, 0.07–0.13) in the validation set. For OM, the CS-nomogram yielded median Brier scores of 0.15 (IQR, 0.12–0.16) in the training set and 0.15 (IQR, 0.12–0.16) in the validation set, indicating satisfactory accuracy over time (supplementary Fig. 1).

Model Evaluation. A–D Calibration curves for breast cancer-specific mortality (BCSM) and overall mortality (OM) models in the training and validation sets; E–F Time-dependent area under the curves (AUCs) for BCSM and OM models in the training and validation sets

The time-dependent AUC curves demonstrated the model’s stability and discriminatory ability over time. First, the models predicting OM and BCSM in elderly patients with HR+ locally advanced breast cancer performed very stably in both the training and validation sets, with relatively flat AUC curves, suggesting that the models did not deteriorate over time. Second, although the AUC values showed slight variations over the 10-year period, they consistently remained above 0.7. Specifically, the CS-nomogram for BCSM had a median AUC of 0.739 (range: 0.702–0.837) in the training set (Fig. 5E) and a median AUC of 0.730 (range: 0.705–0.796) in the validation set (Fig. 5F). The CS-nomogram for OM had a median AUC of 0.754 (range: 0.747–0.795) in the training set (Fig. 5G) and a median AUC of 0.751 (range: 0.733–0.794) in the validation set (Fig. 5H).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that CS analysis offers a more dynamic and accurate prediction of survival compared to traditional static method. Our results identified a decreasing trend in 10 years real-time OM and BCSM as follow-up time increased. Breast cancer-related mortality rises during the first two years of follow-up and then gradually declines, underscoring the importance of time-adjusted survival estimates in elderly HR+ LABC patients. This study provides an individualized assessment of long-term prognostic outcomes using a CS model that incorporates competing risks, offering real-time updated survival information to potentially guide the development of more effective follow-up strategies.

Elderly patients with HR-positive LABC present significant clinical challenges due to their distinct characteristics and the complexity of their management. While HR-positive breast cancer generally has a favorable prognosis, the advanced stage of disease combined with advanced patient age complicates treatment strategies and negatively impacts survival outcomes [1, 2, 16, 17]. Age-related comorbidities, reduced physiological reserves, and variability in treatment responses further exacerbate these challenges [3, 4, 18]. Additionally, the lack of prospective data specifically addressing elderly breast cancer patients limits the applicability of management strategies, underscoring the critical need for tailored approaches [3, 11]. Despite these obstacles, advancements in treatment have allowed many elderly patients with HR-positive LABC to achieve long-term survival [5, 6, 19, 20]. However, when survivors seek accurate information regarding their prognosis during follow-up, traditional survival analysis may be inadequate, as it often fails to account for the evolving nature of survival probabilities over time [3, 10].

CS analysis provides a valuable method to quantify changes in prognosis over time for clinicians and patients. One of the primary concerns for cancer survivors is their risk of dying from the malignancy [21]. In the analysis of annual mortality risk, a critical time point was identified where the risk of death from other causes surpasses that from breast cancer (Fig. 2C and D). We suggest that during the first 3 years of follow-up, greater emphasis should be placed on the risk of BCSM. In the 4th and 5th years, the risk of death from other causes becomes comparable to BCSM, and beyond this period, as the BCSM risk gradually declines, the focus of follow-up should shift towards managing mortality from other related causes. In this context, clinicians should prioritize the management of comorbidities and specific health assessments in long-term survivors. Given that the follow-up needs and clinical guidance for long-term survivors differ from those of short-term survivors [21, 22], it is essential to establish a balanced approach to monitoring that addresses these unique requirements.

Our results observed that the cumulative mortality rates of elderly patients with HR-positive LABC decreased over time. Despite the relatively advanced stage, the 10-year real-time OM and breast BCSM decreased with each additional year of survival for patients with HR-positive breast cancer. Some studies have suggested that the negative impact of certain adverse factors in patients diminishes over time [10, 23]. Zamboni et al. proposed that changes in real-time survival are a result of selection effect, where the prognosis of lower-risk survivors improves after the early mortality of high-risk patients [23]. Additionally, we observed that the decline in BCSM was nonlinear in both OM and BCSM, with a relatively gradual decrease during the first two years of follow-up (with a smaller slope, Fig. 2E and F), followed by a relatively faster and more stable decline. This is consistent with the aforementioned annual mortality risk assessment, suggesting that the first two years of follow-up may represent a high-risk period for breast cancer-related mortality.

To achieve accurate predictions, individualized characteristics must be considered, highlighting the importance of multivariable predictive models. A nomogram is considered a valuable tool for personalized prediction [24, 25]. The study selected robust independent prognostic factors, previously validated in other studies, for modeling [10, 11, 26]. By calculating individualized parameters, the model can accurately predict a patient’s OM and BCSM rates, thereby providing more precise, time-updated conditional mortality risks. For example, a patient initially diagnosed with a predicted 10-year BCSM of 51.5% (risk score: 300) could, after surviving 4 years, be informed that her updated 10-year BCSM is now 33.0%, a reduction of 17.5%. This represents an encouraging outcome which may help to alleviate patient anxiety and improve adherence to cancer follow-up recommendations [10]. Additionally, our predictions are highly accurate and do not deteriorate over time. CS analysis calculates estimates based on the subset of patients who survive each year [25], which may lead to smaller sample sizes over time and potentially decrease prediction accuracy. However, the sample size provided by SEER is sufficient to support the study's requirements.

The study has some limitations that need to be clarified. First, as a retrospective study, it is unavoidably subject to selection bias. Second, the SEER database lacks some treatment-related variables, such as endocrine therapy usage, chemotherapy regimens and cycles, radiation doses, and toxicity-related mortality from chemotherapy or radiotherapy. These missing variables may influence prognostic prediction, particularly in elderly patients with HR-positive LABC, and their importance should be further investigated in future studies to enhance the model performance. Third, because patients who did not undergo surgery were excluded, the study cohort may be biased toward fitter individuals. This selection bias limits the generalizability of the model to frail or inoperable elderly patients. Fourth, although the SEER database provides a large sample size for analysis, prospective external validation is still needed to confirm the generalizability of our findings. Finally, as new therapies emerge, the CS-nomogram will need to be updated accordingly in the future.

Conclusion

This study describes the conditional survival patterns of elderly patients with HR-positive LABC, demonstrating a gradual decline in 10-year OM and BCSM among long-term survivors over time. The developed conditional OM and BCSM models enable individualized, time-updated survival estimates. These tools may facilitate patient counseling, clinical decision-making, and inform healthcare resource planning during follow-up. However, limitations remain prior to broad clinical adoption, including the need to incorporate additional clinically relevant variables and to improve applicability to non-surgical populations. P Prospective validation and refinement in independent cohorts are warranted.
