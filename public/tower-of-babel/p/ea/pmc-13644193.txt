Epidemiology, microbiological profile, and antimicrobial resistance patterns of culture-proven neonatal sepsis in a tertiary NICU
Authors: Volkan Bayar, Münevver Kaynak-Türkmen, Ayşe Anık, Abdullah Barış Akcan, Soner Sertan Kara, Murat Telli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeonatal sepsis remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among preterm and low-birth-weight infants requiring prolonged neonatal intensive care. This study aimed to describe the local epidemiology, microbiological profile, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and frequency of fulfillment of the European Medicines Agency (EMA) sepsis criteria among culture-proven neonatal sepsis episodes in a tertiary neonatal intensive care unit. The study was not designed to assess the diagnostic or predictive accuracy of the EMA criteria.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included neonates with positive blood cultures admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit of Aydın Adnan Menderes University between January 2014 and December 2019. Infants with major congenital anomalies, contaminated cultures, or incomplete records were excluded. Demographic, clinical, microbiological, and laboratory data were analyzed, together with antimicrobial susceptibility results and EMA sepsis criteria.ResultsAmong 5,515 hospitalized neonates, 190 infants with 212 culture-proven sepsis episodes were included. Of these episodes, 183 (86.3%) were late-onset neonatal sepsis, 8 (3.8%) were early-onset neonatal sepsis, and 21 (9.9%) were very late-onset neonatal sepsis. Among included infants, mean gestational age was 31.13 ± 4.54 weeks, and mean birth weight was 1613.61 ± 812.62 g. Gram-positive organisms predominated (73.1%), with coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) being the most frequent pathogens (64.6%); Staphylococcus epidermidis alone accounted for 35.8% of all episodes. Gram-negative organisms represented 23.1% of isolates, mainly Klebsiella pneumoniae (7.5%), and fungi accounted for 3.8%. Methicillin resistance was highly prevalent among CoNS, whereas all Gram-positive isolates remained susceptible to vancomycin. Among Gram-negative isolates, 7 of 16 K. pneumoniae strains were extended-spectrum beta-lactamase positive, and 2 isolates were meropenem resistant. EMA-positive sepsis criteria were fulfilled in 83.5% of episodes. Sepsis-related mortality was 7.9%. Episode-based mortality was higher in Gram-negative than Gram-positive infections.ConclusionCulture-proven neonatal sepsis in our NICU predominantly affected preterm infants and was mainly late-onset. CoNS were the leading pathogens, while Gram-negative organisms contributed substantially to severe and fatal infections. Continuous local surveillance and NICU-specific empirical antibiotic strategies are essential.Keywords: Neonatal sepsis, Newborn infant, Intensive care units, Microbial sensitivity tests, Drug resistance

Introduction

Neonatal sepsis remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide despite major advances in neonatology, mechanical ventilation, and the overall care of premature infants. Although these developments have improved survival rates among preterm newborns, they have also been accompanied by an increase in associated comorbidities. Neonatal sepsis is an important systemic syndrome characterized by hemodynamic alterations and various clinical manifestations of bacterial, viral, or fungal origin, and it continues to be a major cause of both morbidity and mortality [1]. Because the clinical manifestations of sepsis are often nonspecific and may overlap with many noninfectious conditions encountered during the neonatal period, early diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment may be challenging. Therefore, combined evaluation of clinical and laboratory findings is essential for accurate diagnosis.

The incidence of neonatal sepsis varies according to country-level development, birth weight, gestational age, and the denominator used for estimation. In developed countries, the overall incidence of neonatal sepsis has been reported to range from 1 to 8.1 per 1,000 live births. For EONS, Stoll et al. reported an incidence of 0.57 per 1,000 live births among infants weighing > 2,500 g, whereas the rate reached 10.96% among very-low-birth-weight infants weighing 401–1,500 g [2]. These figures are derived from different birth-weight-specific populations and therefore should be interpreted as indicators of increasing risk with decreasing birth weight rather than as directly comparable population-level incidence estimates. Similarly, the incidence of LONS is strongly influenced by birth weight and has been reported to be 51.2% among infants weighing 500–750 g, 15%–25% among those weighing < 1,500 g, and 1.6% among those weighing > 2,500 g [3].

Studies from Türkiye have reported that the incidence of LONS ranges between 6.4% and 14.1%, while mortality rates vary widely from 0% to 75% [4, 5]. Encouragingly, mortality rates have declined from approximately 30%–40% to 5%–10% over time [6]. In a previous study conducted in our neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) between 2004 and 2008, the overall frequency of sepsis was found to be 9.6%, and the rate of culture-proven sepsis was 5% [6]. In that cohort, 82% of the infants were premature, and the mortality rate was reported as 13.3% [7]. These findings indicate that neonatal sepsis remains a substantial clinical burden, particularly in premature and low-birth-weight infants, and continues to be an important challenge in neonatal intensive care practice.

Early diagnosis of neonatal sepsis and prompt initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy are of vital importance. The gold standard for diagnosis is the detection of growth in blood culture. However, several factors that may negatively influence diagnostic accuracy should be taken into consideration, including maternal antibiotic use before delivery, initiation of antibiotics in the infant before blood culture sampling, and insufficient blood volume obtained for culture [8, 9]. Therefore, the present study was designed as a descriptive single-center analysis of culture-proven neonatal sepsis in a tertiary NICU. The primary purpose was to characterize the local epidemiology, causative microorganisms, and antimicrobial resistance patterns to support unit-specific empirical treatment and infection-control strategies. A secondary purpose was to describe how frequently culture-proven sepsis episodes fulfilled the EMA neonatal sepsis criteria, without assessing their diagnostic or predictive accuracy.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective study included neonates hospitalized in the NICU of the Department of Pediatrics, Aydın Adnan Menderes University, between January 2014 and December 2019 who had positive blood culture results. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Aydın Adnan Menderes University Faculty of Medicine before study initiation (IRB Number:2020/207). Clinical trial number: not applicable. The study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki II declaration and received written informed consent from the parents or legal guardians of all participants. Patient files were retrieved from the hospital archives, and discharge summaries and laboratory data were reviewed through the hospital electronic medical record system (MIA-MED).

Definitions

Gestational age was defined in completed weeks of gestation, based on obstetric dating using the first day of the last menstrual period and/or early obstetric ultrasonography when available. Preterm birth was defined as birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation according to the World Health Organization definition [10]. Prolonged rupture of membranes was defined as membrane rupture lasting longer than 18 h [11]. Clinical chorioamnionitis was defined as maternal body temperature ≥ 38 °C accompanied by at least one of the following findings: maternal leukocyte count > 15,000 cells/mm³, maternal tachycardia > 100 beats/min, fetal tachycardia > 160 beats/min, uterine tenderness, or foul-smelling amniotic fluid [12]. According to birth weight for gestational age, infants were categorized as appropriate for gestational age (AGA, 10th–90th percentile), small for gestational age (SGA, < 10th percentile), or large for gestational age (LGA, > 90th percentile) using sex- and gestational-age-specific neonatal growth references [13]. Based on absolute birth weight, infants were further classified as low birth weight (LBW, 1500–2499 g), very low birth weight (VLBW, 1000–1499 g), and extremely low birth weight (ELBW, < 1000 g) [10].

Proven sepsis was defined as the presence of clinical and laboratory findings consistent with sepsis together with pathogen growth in blood culture, whereas clinical sepsis was defined as compatible clinical and laboratory findings without pathogen isolation. Suspected sepsis was defined as the presence of risk factors and/or clinical findings suggestive of sepsis, regardless of culture positivity [14]. Repeated positive blood cultures obtained during a single clinical episode were considered a single sepsis attack. According to the time of onset, sepsis diagnosed within the first 3 days of life was classified as EONS, between days 4 and 30 as LONS, and after day 30 as very late-onset neonatal sepsis [14]. Sepsis-related mortality was defined as death occurring during treatment for a culture-proven sepsis episode or within 14 days of the positive blood culture, when the clinical course was considered attributable to sepsis or sepsis-associated organ dysfunction. Deaths unrelated to the sepsis episode or occurring later during NICU hospitalization after clinical resolution were not classified as sepsis-related mortality. Blood culture growth was considered contamination when there were no clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of sepsis and no modification of antibiotic therapy or initiation of new antimicrobial treatment during follow-up [15]. For CoNS isolates, true bloodstream infection was distinguished from contamination using a combined clinical, laboratory, microbiological, and therapeutic assessment. CoNS growth was considered clinically significant when it was accompanied by signs compatible with sepsis, abnormal laboratory findings such as elevated C-reactive protein and/or procalcitonin, leukocyte abnormality, increased immature-to-total neutrophil ratio, thrombocytopenia, or metabolic acidosis, and when the treating neonatologist initiated, continued, or modified antimicrobial therapy accordingly. Classification as true CoNS infection required concordance between microbiological growth and the clinical course. Isolated CoNS growth without clinical deterioration, inflammatory response, or therapeutic consequence was classified as contamination and excluded. In infants with repeated positive blood cultures yielding the same CoNS species during the same clinical episode, the isolates were considered to represent a single sepsis episode.

According to the 2010 EMA criteria, infants with at least two positive clinical and two positive laboratory findings were considered EMA-positive [16]. The EMA criteria were not applied prospectively during clinical care. For the purpose of this retrospective study, EMA criterion fulfillment was assessed post hoc by reviewing medical records, nursing observation forms, laboratory results, and electronic hospital data from the corresponding sepsis episode. The assessment was performed after identification of culture-proven sepsis episodes; therefore, the evaluation was not blinded to blood culture positivity. Clinical EMA criteria included temperature instability, cardiovascular findings, respiratory findings, gastrointestinal findings, nonspecific clinical findings, and skin or subcutaneous lesions documented during the sepsis episode. Laboratory EMA criteria were considered positive using the following thresholds: CRP > 15 mg/L, procalcitonin ≥ 2 ng/mL, immature-to-total neutrophil ratio > 0.2, serum lactate > 2 mmol/L, thrombocytopenia < 100,000/mm³, leukocytosis or leukopenia according to neonatal reference ranges, metabolic acidosis, and glucose intolerance. Infants with at least two clinical and two laboratory criteria were classified as EMA-positive. Because EMA criteria were applied retrospectively and only among culture-proven episodes, the EMA-positive rate should be interpreted as the frequency of criterion fulfillment rather than as a measure of diagnostic accuracy.

Data collection and study variables

The evaluated neonatal variables included gestational age, birth weight, sex, date of birth, time of hospital admission, length of hospital stay, onset time of sepsis, potential neonatal risk factors, sepsis episode count, and mortality. Gestational age was further grouped as < 28 weeks, 28–32 weeks, 32–37 weeks, and ≥ 37 weeks. Maternal variables included age, chronic diseases, urinary tract infection during pregnancy, prolonged rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, and multiple pregnancy. Peripheral blood culture results and concomitant urine, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and tracheal aspirate culture results were obtained from the microbiology laboratory database. Bloodstream isolates, annual distribution of concomitant culture growth, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and the number of sepsis episodes were analyzed. These variables were structured to allow evaluation of yearly sepsis burden, neonatal and maternal demographic characteristics, EMA-based clinical and laboratory findings, microbiological distribution, associated culture positivity, and mortality findings.

Exclusion criteria

Neonates with congenital anomalies, including meningomyelocele, anal atresia, duodenal atresia, and diaphragmatic hernia, were excluded. Infants whose blood culture growth was considered contamination, whose medical records were unavailable, or whose data were incomplete were also excluded from the study.

Microbiological procedures

In our unit, blood cultures were obtained at the bedside under strict sterile conditions by inoculating 1 mL of peripheral venous blood directly into pediatric blood culture bottles (BD BACTEC Peds Plus). CSF samples were obtained by lumbar puncture under sterile conditions, and urine cultures were collected by catheterization after appropriate antiseptic preparation. Blood cultures were processed using fully automated systems (Bactec 9120 and Bactec Fx; Becton Dickinson, USA). Bottles yielding a positive signal underwent Gram staining and were subcultured onto appropriate media, including sheep blood agar and eosin methylene blue agar. Bacterial identification was performed using conventional methods in combination with the fully automated Phoenix system (Becton Dickinson, USA). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was carried out using both the disk diffusion method and the Phoenix automated system. Antimicrobial susceptibility results were interpreted according to the EUCAST clinical breakpoint tables valid for the corresponding study period [17]. For descriptive resistance analyses, isolates reported as intermediate by the laboratory were grouped together with resistant isolates.

Concomitant cultures were obtained selectively according to predefined clinical suspicion criteria rather than routinely in all infants. Urine culture was obtained when urinary tract infection was suspected on the basis of unexplained clinical deterioration, temperature instability, feeding intolerance, abnormal urinalysis when available, or elevated inflammatory markers without an obvious alternative focus. Cerebrospinal fluid culture was obtained when meningitis was suspected, particularly in infants with lethargy, seizures, apnea, bulging fontanelle, marked inflammatory marker elevation, or persistent clinical instability. Deep tracheal aspirate culture was obtained in intubated infants when lower respiratory tract infection or ventilator-associated infection was suspected, based on increased ventilatory or oxygen requirement, worsening respiratory secretions, new or progressive radiographic findings, and elevated inflammatory markers. Wound culture was obtained when local signs of infection such as erythema, swelling, discharge, or wound dehiscence were present. Concordant growth was defined as isolation of the same microorganism from the concomitant culture and blood culture during the same sepsis episode. Because standardized prospective diagnostic criteria for each focal infection were not applied, concomitant culture results were interpreted descriptively and were not considered definitive evidence of site-specific infection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were presented as number and percentage. Normality of distribution was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values together with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two independent groups, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were low. Subgroup analyses were performed to compare survivors and non-survivors, early-onset and late-onset neonatal sepsis episodes, and EMA-positive and EMA-negative sepsis episodes. These comparisons included demographic characteristics, pathogen distribution, EMA criterion fulfillment, and selected clinical and laboratory severity indicators. Fisher’s exact test was preferred for categorical comparisons involving small subgroups, particularly early-onset neonatal sepsis episodes. Additional mortality analyses were performed according to pathogen group, sepsis type, selected pathogens, and antimicrobial resistance profiles. Mortality rates were calculated as the number of sepsis-related deaths divided by the total number of culture-proven sepsis episodes in each category. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient inclusion and sepsis episodes

During the study period, a total of 5,515 neonates were hospitalized in the NICU. Review of microbiological records showed growth in 484 blood cultures obtained from 393 infants. Of these 393 infants, 81 were excluded because of incomplete data, 52 because of major congenital anomalies, and 70 because blood culture growth was considered contamination. After applying the exclusion criteria, 190 infants were included in the study. In these 190 infants, a total of 212 culture-proven sepsis episodes were identified. Thus, the final analysis was performed at the level of clinically significant culture-proven sepsis episodes rather than at the level of all positive blood culture bottles. Positive blood cultures that represented repeated growth within the same clinical episode, contamination, incomplete records, or excluded congenital anomaly cases were not included as separate infection episodes.

Overall, 70 of 484 positive blood cultures were classified as contamination, corresponding to a contamination rate of 14.5% among positive blood cultures. These cultures were excluded from the final analysis using predefined clinical, laboratory, and therapeutic criteria to reduce misclassification of contaminants as true bloodstream infections. When evaluated by year, the proportion of blood culture positivity among hospitalized neonates ranged from 7.8% to 10.2%, while the contamination rate among positive blood cultures ranged from 8.9% to 18.3%. The highest blood culture positivity rate was observed in 2019 (10.2%), whereas the highest contamination rate was observed in 2015 (18.3%).

The neonatal and maternal characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of the 190 infants, 113 (59.5%) were male and 77 (40.5%) were female. Mean birth weight was 1613.61 ± 812.62 g (range, 485–4410 g), and mean gestational age was 31.13 ± 4.54 weeks (range, 22–41 weeks). Among the infants, 42 (22.1%) were extremely low birth weight, 70 (36.8%) were very low birth weight, 46 (24.2%) were low birth weight, and 32 (16.8%) had normal birth weight. Baseline neonatal and maternal characteristics of the study population

Most infants were appropriate for gestational age (83.2%), and cesarean delivery was the predominant mode of birth (78.4%). Twenty-two infants (11.5%) had a 5-minute Apgar score < 7. The mean age at admission was 1.52 ± 4.68 days (range, 0–35 days), and the mean NICU stay was 43.22 ± 31.41 days (range, 4-196 days). During sepsis episodes, 200 infants (94.3%) received total parenteral nutrition, 179 (84.4%) received breast milk, 62 (29.2%) received breast milk substitutes, 78 (36.7%) had a catheter history, and 9 (4.2%) had a history of surgery. Sepsis-related mortality occurred in 15 infants (7.9%).

The mean maternal age was 29.64 ± 6.67 years (range, 16–59 years). Maternal age was ≤ 18 years in 9 cases (4.7%) and > 35 years in 35 cases (18.4%). Chronic disease was present in 72 mothers (37.9%), urinary tract infection during pregnancy in 12 (6.3%), multiple pregnancy in 12 (6.3%), prolonged rupture of membranes in 17 (8.9%), and chorioamnionitis in 11 (5.8%).

Distribution of sepsis episodes and EMA criteria

A total of 212 sepsis episodes were documented in 190 infants. Single, double, and triple sepsis episodes were observed in 171 (90.0%), 16 (8.4%), and 3 (1.6%) infants, respectively. According to onset time, 8 episodes (3.8%) were classified as early-onset neonatal sepsis, 183 (86.3%) as late-onset neonatal sepsis, and 21 (9.9%) as very late-onset neonatal sepsis.

Evaluation according to the EMA sepsis criteria showed that 177 episodes (83.5%) met the definition of EMA-positive sepsis, with at least two clinical and two laboratory criteria present. Among the clinical criteria, respiratory findings were the most common category (82.1%), followed by cardiovascular findings (67.0%) and nonspecific findings (62.3%). The most frequent individual clinical findings were increased oxygen requirement (73.1%), increased need for ventilatory support (65.1%), and hypotonia (58.0%). Among laboratory findings, elevated inflammatory markers were most common; procalcitonin elevation was present in 76.9% and CRP elevation in 68.4% of episodes. An increased immature-to-total neutrophil ratio was observed in 71.2%, and metabolic acidosis in 58.5%. Detailed EMA-related findings are presented in Table 2.

Microbiological findings

Overall, Gram-positive bacteria were isolated in 155 episodes (73.1%), Gram-negative bacteria in 49 episodes (23.1%), and fungi in 8 episodes (3.8%). Gram-positive organisms predominated throughout the study period.

The most frequently isolated pathogen was coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS, including Staphylococcus epidermidis), identified in 137 episodes (64.6%). S. epidermidis alone accounted for 76 episodes (35.8%). All CoNS episodes included in the final analysis fulfilled the predefined criteria for clinically significant infection, including compatible clinical and laboratory findings and corresponding antimicrobial treatment decisions; isolates judged to represent contamination were excluded before analysis. The second most common pathogen group was Klebsiella spp. (23 episodes, 10.8%), followed by Serratia spp. (10 episodes, 4.7%) and Candida spp. (8 episodes, 3.8%). Among Gram-negative isolates, Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most common species (16 episodes, 7.5%), followed by Serratia marcescens (8 episodes, 3.8%) and Klebsiella oxytoca (7 episodes, 3.3%). All fungal isolates were Candida species, and Candida albicans accounted for 62.5% of fungal episodes.

When analyzed by sepsis classification, CoNS remained the predominant pathogen in all categories, particularly in LONS. Detailed microbiological distribution is shown in Table 3.

Concomitant culture results and antimicrobial susceptibility

Concomitant cultures were obtained selectively according to clinical suspicion criteria rather than routinely in all sepsis episodes. Therefore, the results were interpreted descriptively as possible concurrent infectious foci. Urine cultures were obtained in 110 of 212 sepsis episodes, and only 1 episode showed growth of the same organism as the blood culture. Lumbar puncture was performed in 30 episodes, and CSF culture yielded the same organism in 4 episodes. Thus, lumbar puncture was performed in 14.2% of culture-proven sepsis episodes. Because CSF examination was not performed routinely in all episodes, the observed CSF culture positivity should be interpreted descriptively and does not exclude the possibility of unrecognized meningitis among infants who did not undergo lumbar puncture. Deep tracheal aspirate cultures were obtained in 10 episodes, and concordant growth was found in 1 case. Wound cultures were obtained in 5 episodes, with concordant growth in 2 cases. Antibiogram data were unavailable for 9 blood culture isolates, 6 from 2014 to 3 from 2015. Antifungal susceptibility results for fungal isolates could not be retrieved; therefore, antifungal resistance patterns could not be analyzed for the 8 fungal sepsis episodes. Among fungal sepsis episodes, empirical antifungal therapy was initiated in all cases. Fluconazole was used as the initial antifungal agent in 5 episodes, whereas liposomal amphotericin B was used in 3 episodes. Treatment escalation or modification was required in 2 episodes because of persistent clinical instability or insufficient laboratory improvement, and both were switched from fluconazole to liposomal amphotericin B. Because susceptibility data were unavailable, antifungal treatment modifications were based on clinical and laboratory response rather than susceptibility-guided decision-making. Among Gram-positive isolates, 31 were methicillin-susceptible and 124 were methicillin-resistant. Of the methicillin-resistant isolates, 2 were methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and 122 were methicillin-resistant CoNS; 67 of the latter were S. epidermidis. All 155 Gram-positive isolates were susceptible to vancomycin.

Among all CoNS isolates (including S. epidermidis), resistance rates were 89.1% for methicillin, 56.2% for clindamycin, 15.3% for tetracycline, and 5.9% for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Teicoplanin resistance was detected in 10.9% of CoNS isolates. Among S. epidermidis isolates specifically, resistance rates were 88.2% for methicillin, 59.2% for clindamycin, 13.2% for teicoplanin, 7.9% for tetracycline, and 3.9% for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

Among K. pneumoniae isolates, 7 of 16 were extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-positive, whereas all K. oxytoca isolates were ESBL-negative. Overall, ESBL production was detected in 7 of 49 Gram-negative isolates (14.3%), all of which were K. pneumoniae. Of the 49 Gram-negative sepsis episodes, 47 isolates were susceptible to meropenem and 2 were resistant; these resistant isolates were Burkholderia cepacia and Acinetobacter baumannii. No pan-resistant Enterobacteriaceae were detected. Gram-negative susceptibility data are summarized in Table 5.

Mortality findings

Mortality was further evaluated according to pathogen group, sepsis type, selected pathogens, and antimicrobial resistance profiles. Mortality was higher among Gram-negative infections than Gram-positive infections (16.3% [8/49] vs. 3.9% [6/155], p = 0.006), while fungal infections had a mortality rate of 12.5% (1/8). According to sepsis type, mortality was 12.5% in EONS (1/8), 6.6% in LONS (12/183), and 9.5% in VLONS (2/21), without a statistically significant difference. Among selected pathogens, mortality was higher in Klebsiella spp. (21.7%, 5/23) and Serratia spp. (30.0%, 3/10), whereas CoNS had a lower mortality rate (2.2%, 3/137). Regarding antimicrobial resistance profiles, ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae and meropenem-resistant GNB showed numerically high mortality rates, but these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of episodes. Discussion

In the present study, culture-proven neonatal sepsis was predominantly a disease of prematurity and intensive care exposure, with LONS accounting for the vast majority of episodes. This pattern is consistent with contemporary NICU epidemiology, in which LONS is far more common than early-onset sepsis among preterm and very-low-birth-weight (VLBW) infants because of prolonged hospitalization, immature host defenses, central vascular access, parenteral nutrition, and repeated invasive procedures [18–21]. In our cohort, the mean gestational age and birth weight were low, and most episodes occurred in infants requiring prolonged NICU care, which strongly supports the interpretation that nosocomial and device-associated factors played a major role in the observed sepsis burden [18–21].

Another important finding was the marked predominance of Gram-positive pathogens, particularly CoNS, among bloodstream isolates. This result is highly compatible with previous neonatal sepsis studies showing that CoNS remains the leading cause of LONS in NICUs, especially in preterm and VLBW infants [18, 19, 22]. CoNS, and especially Staphylococcus epidermidis, are well adapted to the NICU environment because they colonize the skin, adhere to catheter surfaces, and form biofilms, thereby facilitating persistent bloodstream infection in infants with central venous access and prolonged supportive care [18, 22]. Our finding that S. epidermidis represented a large proportion of all episodes is therefore not surprising, but rather reflects the microbiological ecology described in NICU-based cohorts from different countries [18, 19, 22]. At the same time, the clinical importance of CoNS should not be underestimated simply because of its relatively lower virulence. Prior work suggests that while CoNS sepsis may be less lethal than invasive Gram-negative infection, it is still associated with prolonged hospitalization, repeated antibiotic exposure, and substantial resource utilization [22].

Although Gram-negative organisms were less frequent than Gram-positive bacteria in our cohort, they were clinically very important because they accounted for a substantial proportion of severe and fatal infections. Klebsiella spp. was the leading Gram-negative pathogen group in our study, followed by Serratia spp., and Gram-negative organisms constituted the majority of fatal episodes. This observation agrees with prior reports showing that Gram-negative neonatal sepsis is often associated with greater hemodynamic instability, more rapid clinical deterioration, and higher mortality than Gram-positive sepsis [19–21, 23]. In particular, recent multicenter and national studies have continued to identify Klebsiella spp. as a major NICU pathogen, especially in units with high antimicrobial exposure and prolonged hospitalization of preterm infants [20, 21, 24]. Therefore, while CoNS dominated numerically in our study, Gram-negative organisms likely contributed disproportionately to adverse outcomes, making them critically important when designing empirical treatment protocols and infection control strategies [19–21, 23, 24].

The fungal burden in our study was relatively low, with Candida spp. accounting for a small minority of all episodes. Even so, candidemia remains clinically meaningful in NICU populations because fungal sepsis generally occurs in the most vulnerable infants, particularly those with extreme prematurity, prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, central line use, and total parenteral nutrition [18, 20, 21]. The low proportion of fungal episodes in our cohort may reflect improvements in infection prevention, catheter care, and antimicrobial stewardship over time, but it should not lead to complacency. Recent neonatal studies still describe fungal sepsis as an important cause of morbidity and occasional mortality in VLBW populations, even when absolute numbers are lower than those of bacterial sepsis [20, 21]. Thus, the detection of Candida bloodstream infection in our unit continues to support careful surveillance in infants with prolonged intensive care exposure.

Our antimicrobial susceptibility data also deserve particular emphasis. Methicillin resistance was very common among CoNS isolates, whereas vancomycin susceptibility was preserved in all Gram-positive isolates. Notably, teicoplanin resistance was detected in 10.9% of CoNS isolates and 13.2% of S. epidermidis isolates. This finding is clinically relevant because teicoplanin may be used as an alternative glycopeptide to vancomycin in some NICU settings. The presence of teicoplanin resistance despite preserved vancomycin susceptibility suggests that teicoplanin should not be assumed to provide equivalent coverage for methicillin-resistant CoNS in our unit. Therefore, when teicoplanin is considered for empirical or targeted therapy, treatment decisions should be guided by local susceptibility data and isolate-specific antibiogram results whenever available. This pattern is strongly aligned with the published literature, where methicillin-resistant CoNS is commonly reported in neonatal LONS, while vancomycin remains broadly active against these organisms [18, 22, 24]. Clinically, this creates a familiar stewardship dilemma: local ecology may justify glycopeptide coverage in selected high-risk LONS cases, but routine and excessive empiric vancomycin use may further increase selective pressure and contribute to future resistance problems [18, 22]. In our unit, these findings support a risk-stratified empirical treatment approach rather than routine glycopeptide use for all suspected sepsis episodes. Selective vancomycin use in the presence of high methicillin resistance should be understood as a risk-stratified strategy. Empirical glycopeptide coverage may be justified in high-risk LONS episodes, particularly in extremely preterm or very-low-birth-weight infants with central venous catheter exposure, prolonged hospitalization, total parenteral nutrition, previous broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, recurrent sepsis, or marked clinical deterioration. Conversely, routine empirical vancomycin use may be avoided in clinically stable infants without central catheter exposure or major risk factors, with early reassessment after culture results and clinical response. De-escalation or discontinuation should be performed once microbiological data and the clinical course do not support methicillin-resistant Gram-positive bloodstream infection. The study by Cantey et al. is especially relevant here, as it suggests that CoNS sepsis, although frequent, may not carry the same mortality burden as Gram-negative sepsis, which supports a more targeted rather than indiscriminate approach to vancomycin use [22]. Therefore, our findings reinforce the need for local antibiogram-guided empirical regimens, regular review of unit-specific first-line protocols, and early de-escalation once microbiological results are available.

The Gram-negative resistance profile in our cohort is also clinically significant. ESBL production was detected in 7 of 49 Gram-negative isolates (14.3%), all of which were Klebsiella pneumoniae, and two Gram-negative isolates were resistant to meropenem. Although pan-resistant Enterobacteriaceae were not detected, these results indicate that multidrug resistance has already emerged as a meaningful problem in our NICU. Similar concerns have been highlighted in recent neonatal sepsis studies, including reports from Türkiye and other tertiary settings, where ESBL-producing Enterobacterales and occasional carbapenem-resistant isolates complicate empirical treatment choices [23–25]. Clinically, these data support a local antibiogram-guided and risk-stratified empirical approach rather than routine carbapenem use for all suspected LONS episodes. A non-carbapenem beta-lactam–based Gram-negative regimen may be appropriate for clinically stable infants without previous ESBL colonization or infection, recent broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, or severe clinical deterioration. Conversely, empirical carbapenem coverage may be considered in critically ill infants or in those with previous ESBL colonization/infection, recent broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, or strong suspicion of resistant Gram-negative sepsis. In all cases, therapy should be reassessed and de-escalated promptly once culture and susceptibility results are available.

The overall sepsis-related mortality rate in our cohort was 7.9%, which is clinically important even though it falls within the lower range of rates reported in some NICU-based cohorts. Mortality clustered particularly among Gram-negative and fungal infections, while all fatal Gram-positive episodes occurred in infants with extreme prematurity and very low gestational age. This is biologically plausible and consistent with the broader literature. Large cohort studies have shown that mortality in neonatal sepsis is shaped not only by the pathogen itself but also by host vulnerability, especially gestational age, birth weight, and the intensity of supportive care required [19–21, 23]. Norwegian and other recent data further suggest that culture-positive LONS attributable mortality is higher in Gram-negative than in Gram-positive infections, which fits well with our own mortality distribution [23]. Therefore, the mortality findings of our study support the concept that both organism-related virulence and infant-related frailty determine outcome in neonatal sepsis. When mortality was calculated within pathogen groups, Gram-negative infections showed a substantially higher mortality rate than Gram-positive infections. This supports the clinical observation that Gram-negative pathogens may contribute disproportionately to fatal neonatal sepsis despite being less frequent overall. However, mortality estimates for specific pathogens and resistance phenotypes should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of fatal episodes.

The subgroup analyses further support the clinical relevance of pathogen distribution. Mortality was associated with lower gestational age, lower birth weight, metabolic acidosis, thrombocytopenia, cardiovascular involvement, and Gram-negative infection. These findings are consistent with the concept that neonatal sepsis outcomes are shaped by both host vulnerability and pathogen virulence. In addition, the comparison between EONS and LONS showed a predominance of CoNS in LONS, whereas Gram-negative organisms were relatively more frequent in EONS. However, the small number of EONS episodes limits the robustness of this comparison.

Another notable result was the relatively high proportion of episodes fulfilling the EMA sepsis criteria. In our cohort, most culture-proven episodes met the requirement of at least two clinical and two laboratory criteria. This finding should be interpreted descriptively, because the present study included only culture-proven neonatal sepsis episodes and did not include a culture-negative comparison group. Therefore, diagnostic accuracy measures such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value could not be calculated. The multicenter study by Tüzün et al. demonstrated that EMA criteria have limited sensitivity and only moderate overall diagnostic accuracy for proven neonatal sepsis, meaning that they are insufficient as a standalone diagnostic standard [26]. Accordingly, our results do not establish the predictive or diagnostic performance of the EMA criteria; rather, they describe the frequency with which these criteria were fulfilled among confirmed neonatal sepsis episodes. Microbiological confirmation and clinical judgment therefore remain central in the diagnosis of neonatal sepsis. In this context, the EMA criteria may be useful primarily for standardizing clinical assessment and research documentation, rather than as an independently validated diagnostic tool. The comparison of EMA-positive and EMA-negative episodes showed that EMA-positive episodes were associated with more frequent clinical and laboratory severity indicators, but not with a statistically significant difference in mortality or pathogen distribution. However, because EMA status was assessed retrospectively among culture-proven cases only, these comparisons should be interpreted as descriptive severity associations rather than diagnostic accuracy measures.

Another important methodological point is the low lumbar puncture rate in our cohort. Lumbar puncture was performed in only 30 of 212 culture-proven sepsis episodes, reflecting the selective rather than routine use of CSF sampling in this retrospective NICU cohort. In clinical practice, lumbar puncture may be deferred or omitted in very preterm or clinically unstable infants because of respiratory compromise, hemodynamic instability, thrombocytopenia, or concern that the procedure may worsen clinical status. In addition, some infants may have already received empirical antibiotics before CSF sampling could be obtained. Therefore, the low lumbar puncture rate may have led to underdiagnosis of concomitant bacterial meningitis. The CSF culture findings in this study should consequently be interpreted as descriptive results among tested infants, rather than as a reliable estimate of meningitis frequency among all culture-proven sepsis episodes.

Because the present study analyzed data from 2014 to 2019, the reported pathogen distribution and antimicrobial resistance patterns should be interpreted as period-specific findings rather than as a direct representation of the current NICU microbiological ecology. Changes in infection-control practices, catheter care, antimicrobial stewardship policies, patient population, and laboratory methods over time may have altered both pathogen distribution and resistance profiles. Therefore, although our findings provide an important baseline for local epidemiology and historical comparison, current empirical treatment decisions should be guided by recent unit-specific surveillance data and regularly updated antibiograms.

Taken together, our study supports three major messages. First, LONS remains the dominant form of neonatal sepsis in tertiary NICUs and is largely concentrated in preterm and low-birth-weight infants [18–21]. Second, CoNS continues to dominate numerically, but Gram-negative organisms, particularly Klebsiella spp., may account for a disproportionate share of severe and fatal disease [19–21, 23, 24]. Third, local resistance surveillance is essential because empirical treatment choices must respond to the actual microbiology of each NICU rather than to historical expectations alone [23–25]. These observations underline the ongoing importance of strict hand hygiene, optimal catheter care, early removal of invasive devices when possible, stewardship-guided antibiotic policies, and periodic reevaluation of empirical treatment protocols.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design limits generalizability and makes the dataset vulnerable to missing documentation and incomplete retrieval of some clinical variables. Second, only culture-proven sepsis episodes were included; therefore, the full burden of culture-negative but clinically significant neonatal sepsis was not captured. Third, some cases were excluded because of incomplete records, and antibiogram data were unavailable for a small number of isolates. Fourth, antifungal susceptibility results could not be retrieved, limiting interpretation of fungal resistance patterns. In addition, the small number of fungal sepsis episodes limits evaluation of antifungal treatment patterns, treatment escalation, and their association with clinical outcomes. Because susceptibility-guided antifungal management could not be assessed, the impact of possible antifungal resistance on outcomes remains unclear. Fifth, because the study covered a six-year period, temporal changes in NICU practice, infection control measures, catheter use, antimicrobial stewardship, and laboratory workflows may have influenced both pathogen distribution and resistance patterns. Finally, although predefined clinical, laboratory, and therapeutic criteria were used to distinguish true CoNS bloodstream infection from contamination, interpretation of CoNS isolates remains challenging in neonatal cohorts. In addition, the proportion of excluded positive blood cultures, particularly those classified as contamination, may have influenced the observed microbiological distribution. Although these exclusions were necessary to avoid overestimating true sepsis episodes, the relatively high contamination rate should be considered when interpreting the predominance of CoNS and the overall epidemiological distribution of pathogens. Because multiple positive blood cultures were not available or required in all episodes owing to the retrospective design and neonatal blood sampling limitations, some degree of misclassification cannot be completely excluded. Mortality analyses according to specific pathogens and antimicrobial resistance phenotypes were limited by the small number of fatal episodes in each subgroup; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory. In addition, EMA criterion fulfillment was assessed retrospectively after identification of culture-proven sepsis episodes and was not blinded to culture results; therefore, the 83.5% EMA-positive rate should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of diagnostic validity. In addition, lumbar puncture was performed selectively and in only a minority of sepsis episodes. Therefore, the frequency of CSF culture positivity in this study should not be interpreted as the true incidence of meningitis, and concomitant bacterial meningitis may have been underdiagnosed, particularly among clinically unstable preterm infants in whom CSF sampling was deferred.

Conclusion

In conclusion, culture-proven neonatal sepsis in our NICU was predominantly a late-onset disease affecting preterm and low-birth-weight infants. CoNS, particularly Staphylococcus epidermidis, was the leading pathogen, whereas Klebsiella spp. was the most important Gram-negative group and contributed substantially to the resistant pathogen burden. Methicillin resistance among CoNS was very high, but vancomycin activity was preserved; meanwhile, ESBL production and occasional carbapenem resistance among Gram-negative isolates remain important concerns. These findings provide a period-specific baseline for local neonatal sepsis epidemiology and support continued microbiological surveillance, strong infection prevention practices, and NICU-specific empirical antibiotic strategies guided by updated resistance data rather than by fixed or historical protocols alone.
