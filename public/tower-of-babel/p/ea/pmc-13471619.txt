Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay: friend or foe in cancer biology?
Authors: Yuhui Wang, Chenghai He, Chengyan Chen, Xin Ma, Xiyao Gao, Guodong Li, Tangliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) is a highly conserved RNA surveillance mechanism in eukaryotic cells. It plays a key role in safeguarding the accuracy of gene expression by eliminating mRNAs with premature termination codons (PTCs) in the cellular transcriptome. Furthermore, NMD fine-tunes gene expression by regulating the stability of numerous transcripts that harbor NMD-inducing features other than PTC. Emerging evidence has established NMD factors as key regulators in diverse biological processes, including embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and tumor biology. This review focuses on the dual roles of NMD in tumorigenesis, cancer therapy, and their underlying mechanisms. NMD can promote tumorigenesis and progression by degrading PTC-containing mRNA variants arising from mutated tumor suppressor genes or by suppressing neoantigen expression. Conversely, NMD can exert tumor-suppressive effects by eliminating aberrant transcripts of certain oncogenes. The function of NMD in tumor dynamics is highly dependent on multiple factors, including the tumor’s genetic background, the expression and mutation status of NMD factors, alternative splicing coupled to NMD (AS-NMD), and the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, we summarize cancer therapeutic strategies targeting NMD, especially NMD inhibitors (e.g., NMDI-1, NMDI-14, and 5-azacytidine). The synergistic potentials of combining NMD inhibition with nonsense mutation readthrough therapy, immune checkpoint blockade, and chemotherapy in cancer therapy are summarized. Finally, we provide perspectives on future research directions, emphasizing that a deeper understanding of the context-specific mechanisms of NMD in different tumors is crucial for developing precise anti-cancer therapies.Keywords: Premature termination codon, mRNA decay, NMD inhibition, Neoantigen, Cancer therapy

Introduction

During evolution, eukaryotic cells have developed a series of mechanisms to ensure the accuracy of gene expression. Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), which recognizes and degrades mRNAs containing a premature termination codon (PTC), is one of the RNA quality control mechanisms. PTCs can arise from genetic mutations in genes’ exons (e.g., nonsense mutations, frameshift mutations), or from physiological and aberrant pre-mRNA processing [1]. By degrading these PTC-containing mRNAs, NMD protects cells from the potential toxic effects of the translated C-terminal truncated proteins. Beyond the mRNA surveillance, NMD actively regulates eukaryotic gene expression by fine-tuning 3%-10% of the cellular transcriptome. Consequently, NMD plays an important role in many physiological processes, including embryonic development, cellular stress responses, and immune responses [2, 3]. Deficits in the NMD machinery have been implicated in several human pathologies, including neurodevelopmental disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and autoimmunity [4, 5].

Cancers, originating from normal cells across various tissues, pose a leading threat to human health and lifespan. Numerous mechanistic hypotheses regarding tumor initiation, maintenance, metastasis, and therapy have been proposed [6, 7]. Recently, NMD has been included in the scenario of cancer biology [8, 9]. In this review, we first outline the core NMD components and NMD mechanisms. Then, we focus on the functions and potential mechanisms of NMD perturbations in tumors. Lastly, we discuss therapeutic strategies that inhibit NMD in cancer cells, as well as combinations of NMD inhibitors with other therapies, including immunotherapy and chemotherapy.

The NMD pathway: NMD components and working models

Since the discovery in 1979, components of the NMD pathway have been found in multiple model species, including Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila melanogaster, and Homo sapiens. The core NMD factors include the up-frameshift (UPF) proteins (UPF1, UPF2, and UPF3B) and the suppressor with morphological effects on genitalia (SMG) proteins (SMG1, SMG5, SMG6, SMG7, SMG8, and SMG9) [10]. So far, over 20 proteins have been identified as functioning in mammalian NMD [8]. These factors orchestrate the degradation of PTC-containing mRNA transcripts, as well as other NMD targets with specific NMD-inducing features such as upstream open reading frames (uORFs) and long 3’-untranslated regions (3’-UTRs) [11].

The NMD pathway appears to operate in a linear model. However, in vertebrate species, including the mouse and the human, at least two major models have been proposed to explain how mRNAs are recognized as NMD targets, and subsequently degraded: the exon junction complex (EJC)-dependent model and the EJC-independent model [12]. The EJC-dependent model is the canonical NMD model, which primarily degrades PTC-containing mRNAs. The EJC is a large protein complex, typically composed of eIF4A3, MAGOH, and RBM8A [13]. As the structural scaffold protein of the EJC complex, eIF4A3 anchors MAGOH and RBM8A to mRNA and nucleates the assembly of auxiliary EJC factors, such as CASC3 [13]. During the processing of pre-mRNAs by splicing regulators, EJCs are deposited approximately 20–24 nucleotides upstream of every exon-exon junction in mRNAs [14–17]. In the mRNA translation step, EJCs are dissociated from the mRNAs by translating ribosomes [18, 19]. If an mRNA contains a PTC located more than 50–55 nucleotides upstream of an exon-exon junction (known as the ‘50–55 nucleotide rule’) [20, 21], ribosomes stop protein translation at the PTC and thus fail to displace the downstream EJCs from the mRNA [22]. The EJC-associated mRNA interacts with certain NMD factors, thereby initiating NMD [9]. When the translating ribosome stalls at a PTC, the SURF complex (Fig. 1A), consisting of the SMG1 complex (SMG1, SMG8, and SMG9), UPF1, eukaryotic release factors (eRFs) 1 and 3, is formed [23]. The RNA helicase DEAH box polypeptide 34 (DHX34) is also involved in NMD, functioning as a scaffold for UPF1 and SMG1 [24]. UPF1 interacts with EJC-bound UPF2 and UPF3B, leading to the formation of the decay-inducing (DECID) complex [23] (Fig. 1A). The interaction of UPF1 with UPF2/3B causes a structural rearrangement of UPF1, allowing SMG1 to phosphorylate UPF1 [25, 26]. Notably, the EJC-dependent NMD mechanism is not limited to EJCs deposited on the linear mRNAs. Recently, Boo et al. reported that circular RNAs (circRNAs) generated by back-splicing are bound with EJCs. Through sequence complementarity between circRNAs and the 3’-UTR of target mRNAs, these circRNA-bound EJCs can be spatially positioned near the termination codon, thereby inducing EJC-dependent NMD—a process termed circRNA-induced NMD (circNMD) [27].

Compared to the EJC-dependent model, the mechanism of the EJC-independent model (also known as the faux 3’ UTR model) remains less understood. In the EJC-independent model, instead of following the ‘50–55 nucleotide rule’, NMD target transcripts are mostly determined by their long 3’-UTRs [28]. Therefore, EJC-independent NMD is also referred to as the long 3’-UTR-mediated NMD. When the 3’-UTR of an mRNA is critically long, the cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein 1 (PABPC1) cannot interact with eRF3 (Fig. 1B), which is important for normal translation termination. This facilitates the interaction between eRF3 and UPF1 [28]. This length-based model has been refined by recent evidence from nanopore-based RNA sequencing. Karousis et al. showed that NMD-sensitive mRNAs harbor more exons in their 3’-UTRs. However, the length of 3’-UTR in an mRNA does not strongly correlate with NMD sensitivity [29]. In the EJC-independent model, UPF1, SMG1, UPF2, and UPF3B are still required for the NMD process. However, UPF2 and UPF3B are proposed to contact UPF1 by diffusion, rather than via interaction with the EJC [30]. Recent studies have expanded the scope of EJC-independent NMD by revealing that UPF1 can act as a core sensor to recognize poorly translated coding sequences, including long ORFs, uORFs, and ORF-like 3’-UTRs—a mechanism termed ORF-mediated decay (OMD) [31].

Although it processes multiple NMD-independent functions, UPF1 is widely considered the core NMD factor. The intensive phosphorylation of UPF1 by SMG1, a member of the PI3K kinase family, is a prerequisite for the NMD target mRNA degradation. Currently, the degradation of target mRNAs is proposed to proceed through two major, potentially coordinated routes: SMG6-mediated and SMG5/SMG7-mediated degradation branches [32, 33] (Fig. 1C). SMG6, as an endoribonuclease in NMD, cleaves target mRNA near the PTC with its PIN domain (PilT N-terminal domain) to generate two RNA degrading intermediates [34, 35]. Although SMG5 has a similar PIN domain in its C-terminus, SMG5 does not have endoribonuclease activity due to the absence of several conserved aspartic acid residues (Asp). SMG5 and SMG7 could form a heterodimer that is known to recruit the decapping proteins and the deadenylation complex (CCR4-NOT) to remove the cap-binding complex and the poly(A) tail, respectively. Finally, mRNA degradation is completed by 5′-3′ exonuclease XRN1 and the exosome complex [36, 37]. However, several lines of experimental evidence refined this model, revealing that SMG6-mediated degradation and SMG5/SMG7-mediated degradation are tightly correlated rather than fully independent [38, 39]. Phosphorylated UPF1 is postulated to recruit SMG5-SMG7 and consequently lead to the activation of SMG6 [38, 39]. SMG5’s endoribonuclease-inactive PIN domain could physically interact with SMG6 and contribute to the formation of a “composite SMG5-SMG6 PIN domain” to fully activate the endocleavage of NMD-targeted mRNAs [40–42]. Intriguingly, SMG5 deficiency causes stronger NMD inhibition than SMG6 null or SMG7 null; thus, the contribution of SMG5/SMG6/SMG7 in mediating NMD target degradation remains to be fully determined.

Besides the mechanisms described above, NMD can proceed through alternative branches, including UPF2-independent and UPF3B-independent routes [43, 44]. Up to now, the ‘50–55 nucleotide rule’ governing the NMD target definition can explain only half of the observed variance in NMD efficiency in human cells. However, still around one fourth of the variance in NMD efficiency does not fit to any of the known NMD models [45]. Thus, additional NMD rules in recognizing and degrading mRNA targets of NMD machinery need to be uncovered.

Roles and mechanisms of NMD in tumorigenesis

Cancer cells originate from normal cells in human tissues. Genetic mutations, epigenetic modifications, and metabolic rewiring could impact the cellular transcriptome and facilitate the switch from “normal” cells to a “tumor” state [6, 46]. Thus, as a key housekeeping mechanism, NMD could actively shape the transcriptome at every step from tumor initiation, tumor establishment, metastasis, to even tumor therapy.

Existing evidence indicates that NMD is a double-edged sword for tumorigenesis. Under specific conditions, NMD can either facilitate or suppress tumor development and progression [8, 9]. Therefore, the role of NMD in tumor biology is complex and highly context-dependent. This complexity is increasingly appreciated as studies reveal that NMD itself is far from a static, uniform pathway. At the single-cell level, NMD efficiency exhibits striking heterogeneity, with some cells efficiently degrading PTC-containing transcripts while others almost completely evade NMD, a phenomenon linked to differential levels of SMG1 and phosphorylated UPF1 [47]. Large-scale analyses of human normal and tumor tissues have demonstrated that NMD efficiency differs significantly across tissue types, tumors, and individuals, driven in part by genetic alterations and rare inherited germline variants [48]. Furthermore, NMD target specificity is highly cell type- and factor-dependent; the core factor UPF2 and the branch factor UPF3B share only approximately 30% of their target transcripts [49]. Collectively, these findings underscore that NMD functions as a flexible, finely tuned RNA surveillance network rather than a rigid pathway, and its roles in cancer should be understood within this multifaceted regulatory landscape.

Links between NMD factors and cancer

Expression of NMD factors in various types of tumors

TCGA pan-cancer data demonstrate that major factors in the NMD pathway (UPF1, UPF2, UPF3B, SMG1, SMG5, SMG6, and SMG7) exhibit frequent amplifications across various tumor types, including breast invasive carcinoma, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma, and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma [50]. Moreover, these genes show significant co-occurrence of amplifications and mutations, and such co-alterations in the NMD pathway are associated with increased NMD efficiency and decreased cytolytic immune infiltration. For example, NMD core factor UPF1 is highly expressed in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) tissues [51]. SMG9 is upregulated (Fig. 2A) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, consequently promoting the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and facilitating HCC progression [52]. In addition, colorectal cancer (CRC) samples with microsatellite instability (MSI) showed higher expression of UPF1, UPF2, SMG1, SMG6, and SMG7 compared to their microsatellite-stable CRCs [53]. The upregulation of these NMD factors may facilitate the degradation of higher levels of potentially toxic PTC-bearing mRNAs, maintaining the survival of CRC cells with MSI.

In other cases, reduced expression of NMD factors and compromised NMD activity are observed in several tumor cell types. For instance, Chang et al. showed that UPF1 is downregulated in HCC, and low UPF1 expression is associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, UPF1 downregulation reduces NMD efficiency and increases Smad7 transcript levels, subsequently downregulating the TGF-β signaling pathway [54]. Cao et al. reported that UPF1 expression is reduced in lung adenocarcinoma tissues compared to normal lung tissues. Inhibition of NMD increases EMT by targeting the TGF-β signaling pathway, thus promoting lung adenocarcinoma progression [55].

Collectively, these studies reveal differential expression patterns of NMD factors across various tumor types: some are elevated, while others are reduced. One possibility to explain the discrepancy in NMD factor expression across different cancer types is that NMD targets exhibit tissue- or cell-type-specificity [3, 47, 49]. However, the NMD-independent functions of these NMD factors may be another plausible mechanism.

Genetic mutations of NMD factors across cancers

The altered expression of NMD factors in tumors could be a consequence of abnormalities in DNA transcription, mRNA translation, and protein stability regulation with post-translational modification. Genetic mutations in exons or introns of genes encoding key NMD factors, leading to NMD inhibition, are found in several specific tumor types.

In a genomic study of pancreatic adenosquamous carcinoma (PASC), a rare and aggressive subtype of pancreatic cancer, Liu et al. discovered that UPF1 is mutated in 18 of 23 PASC samples scanned. These mutations clustered in two distinct regions and were distributed equally in exons and introns of UPF1, leading to aberrant UPF1 splicing and subsequent NMD inhibition. These UPF1 mutations cause accumulation of NMD substrate mRNAs, including an alternatively spliced p53 transcript, which encodes a protein with dominant-negative activity and may facilitate tumorigenesis [56]. Furthermore, Lu et al. from the same group demonstrated that UPF1 somatic mutations reduce NMD activity in pulmonary inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors (IMT), leading to the upregulation of several NMD mRNA targets, including ATF3, ATF4, and PYCR1. Notably, one of these NMD targets is MAP3K14 (Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 14, also known as NIK), which encodes a potent activator of the NF-κB signaling pathway, contributing to immune infiltration related to IMT [57]. These two studies establish a hypothesis that UPF1-NMD suppresses tumorigenesis. However, when Polaski et al. introduced two UPF1 mutations identified in PASC patients into human 293T and mouse pancreatic cancer (KPC) cells, they found that these UPF1 mutations had no detectable impact on UPF1 splicing, UPF1 protein levels, or NMD activity. Furthermore, these two UPF1 mutations have no effect on pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, and the acquisition of adenosquamous characteristics [58]. Polaski et al. further showed that 45% of UPF1 variants previously found in PASC samples correspond to known human population polymorphisms, suggesting their potential germline origins rather than tumor-driving alterations. Thus, a causal role for UPF1 mutations in tumor formation has not been conclusively established.

To date, whether mutations in NMD factors act as oncogenic driver events or represent secondary effects remains unclear, as UPF1 is the only NMD factor found mutated in human tumor samples of PASCs and IMTs. Given that UPF1 plays multiple roles in DNA and RNA metabolism beyond NMD, it remains unclear whether the UPF1 mutant tumors observed in human patients are primarily driven by NMD inhibition. Of note, no existing conventional knockout or conditional knockout mouse model with NMD deficiency has developed tumors. Thus, loss of NMD may not be sufficient to drive tumorigenesis. Rather than acting as an initiating event, NMD deficiency caused by mutations of NMD factors may function as a permissive mechanism that enables cancer cell viability and tumor progression by cooperating with additional oncogenic inducers. These possibilities would be consistent with its involvement in ER stress and, more generally, the integrated stress response (ISR), triggered by NMD inhibition, which is notably important for tumor growth, invasion, and therapy resistance [59, 60]. Thus, establishing a direct link between loss-of-function of NMD factors and malignancies requires further evidence.

NMD modulates tumorigenesis via targeting tumor-suppressive and oncogenic mRNAs

Genetic mutations in tumor suppressors are frequently found in cancers. Intriguingly, tumor suppressor genes contain more nonsense mutations than oncogenes [61]. These tumor suppressors include BRCA1/2, p53, RB, and WT1 [62–66]. Analysis of lymphoblastoid cell lines from hereditary breast and ovarian cancer families revealed that approximately 80% of mutated BRCA1 alleles generate PTC+ RNA transcripts, which are further recognized and eliminated by NMD. In these lymphoblastoid cells, around 1.5- to 5-fold reductions of BRCA1 PTC+ transcripts were found [62].

In cancers, NMD-inducing mutations in tumor suppressor genes tend to arise in heterozygosity [61]. NMD may facilitate cancer development when only one allele of a tumor suppressor gene is mutated and generates PTC+ transcripts. According to a cancer data analysis, NMD can inactivate tumor suppressor genes through three principal routes. First, when a tumor suppressor gene experiences both NMD and heterozygous deletion, the “two-hit” combination results in complete biallelic inactivation of the gene. Second, in haploinsufficient tumor suppressor genes, where a single functional copy is insufficient to prevent tumorigenesis, tumors positively select for NMD-inducing mutations to eliminate the expression from the remaining allele. Lastly, in rare cases, tumors may select for mutations that escape NMD. The resulting stable transcripts may produce truncated proteins that exert a dominant-negative effect, disrupting the function of the wild-type protein [45].

In addition to NMD-eliciting mutations found within tumor suppressor genes, other PTC-introducing mutations found in genes involved in RNA stabilization, chromatin modifications, and DNA repair were targeted by NMD in hypermutated tumors [67]. In hypermutated stomach adenocarcinomas (STADs), NMD-eliciting mutations were enriched in two genes related to translation initiation, LARP4B and EIF5B, as well as in the tumor suppressor PTEN [67]. Among 54 hypermutated STADs, 12 (22%) harbored NMD-inducing mutations in PTEN [67]. While the presence of these NMD-eliciting mutations suggests a potential association with tumor development, the causal contribution of these NMD-related changes remains to be functionally validated. Notably, these mutations may play a permissive role in enabling the survival of hypermutated cancer cells.

NMD may inhibit tumor progression by degrading transcripts encoding oncoproteins (Fig. 2B). The tumor-suppressive role of NMD is supported by an unbiased screening approach, which identified approximately 750 NMD-targeted mRNAs—many of which are enriched in oncogenic pathways [68]. NMD-targeted transcripts include potent oncogenes such as CCNE1 (a key cell-cycle regulator whose overexpression drives uncontrolled proliferation and genomic instability, and is strongly associated with high-grade ovarian and breast cancers), FGFR2 (an RTK whose aberrant activation via gene fusion, amplification, or activating mutations drives oncogenic signaling, most notably in cholangiocarcinoma and gastric cancer), and SOX9 (a transcription factor that promotes tumorigenesis, cell proliferation, and therapy resistance across diverse malignancies). By degrading these oncogenic transcripts, NMD likely constrains their pro-tumorigenic potential.

To summarize, NMD could promote tumorigenesis when genetic mutations favor oncogenic transformation, or suppress tumor formation by degrading gene transcripts with oncogenic potential.

NMD affects tumor formation through AS-NMD

Although the direct link between mutations of NMD factors and tumorigenesis remains contentious, NMD may actively contribute to transcriptome maintenance in cancer cells—particularly through regulating alternative splicing (AS) coupled to NMD (AS-NMD). Alternative splicing of pre-mRNAs is an important post-transcriptional mechanism that produces multiple protein isoforms from a single gene locus. It is estimated that more than one-third of AS events in humans generate PTC-containing mRNAs, which can be recognized and degraded by the NMD machinery [69] (Fig. 2C). Exon inclusion/skipping and intron inclusion are common AS events, which may generate PTC-containing mRNAs [11]. Thus, deletions of NMD factors, such as UPF1 and SMG5, confirm the enrichment of AS-generated PTC+ mRNA transcripts in mammalian cells [70].

Abnormalities in AS, mediated mainly by splicing regulators, are often associated with tumor occurrence, progression, and metastasis [71, 72]. Thus, in tumor cells originating from mutations of splicing regulators, NMD has the potential to degrade those PTC-containing mRNAs generated by the aberrant splicing process, and thereby influence tumor cell fate. In patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the splicing factor SRSF2 is occasionally mutated at the Pro95 residue [73, 74]. Rahman et al. showed that SRSF2 Pro95 hotspot mutations enhance the deposition of EJCs downstream of the PTC, which facilitates the association of major NMD factors to elicit mRNA decay [74]. The EZH2 transcript encodes the catalytic subunit of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), acting as a tumor suppressor for myeloid malignancies [75]. In SRSF2-Pro95mut cells, SRSF2-Pro95mut promotes inclusion of the EZH2 poison exon, resulting in the generation of EZH2 PTC+ isoforms that are subsequently degraded by NMD. This loss of full-length EZH2 protein is proposed to impair its tumor-suppressive activity [74]. Thus, active NMD may contribute to tumor formation in SRSF2-Pro95mut cells.

In contrast to the enhanced NMD activity observed in SRSF2-Pro95mut cells, mutations in other spliceosome components exhibit a distinct relationship with NMD. Beyond SRSF2, the core U2 spliceosome components SF3B1 and U2AF1 are frequently mutated in MDS, AML, chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), and solid tumors [76]. Cancer-associated mutations (e.g., SF3B1K700E and U2AF1S34F) cause widespread AS abnormalities, generating numerous PTC-containing transcripts that would normally be cleared by NMD. However, these mutations attenuate global NMD activity, leading to impaired clearance of aberrant mRNAs and contributing to transcriptome dyshomeostasis in tumor cells. Thus, although the direction of NMD regulation differs, both SRSF2 and SF3B1/U2AF1 mutations converge on AS-NMD dysregulation to promote tumorigenesis through distinct mechanisms.

AS-NMD has also been implicated in suppressing tumor formation. For example, a high level of DCUN1D5 is strongly associated with breast cancer tumorigenesis and progression. Skipping of DCUN1D5 exon 4 introduces a PTC in the mature DCUN1D5 mRNA, leading to the generation of an unstable transcript that is degraded by NMD. This AS-NMD process is proposed to be an important mechanism that regulates total DCUN1D5 expression. In the highly metastatic breast cancer subtypes, including triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and HER2-positive breast cancer, DCUN1D5 exon 4 skipping is reduced. Consequently, the total DCUN1D5 protein is elevated, which correlates with poor overall and relapse-free survival in breast cancer patients [77].

In addition to PTC-containing mRNAs generated by splicing abnormalities in exons and introns of genes, recent studies have uncovered another splicing-related mechanism that impacts oncogene expression. Variances in 3’UTR produced by alternative splicing could influence gene expression. Chan and colleagues mapped the pan-cancer landscape of 3’-UTR splicing events (3USPs) and found that such events are widespread, upregulated in tumors, and enriched in transcripts from oncogenes, including CHEK1, MAPK1, THUMPD1, WDR55, and CTNNB1 [78]. Importantly, in tumors from liver and colon tissues, 3’-UTR splicing of the transcripts from the aforementioned genes does not trigger NMD [78, 79]. In colorectal cancer, such splicing events are enriched in the genes of the WNT signaling pathway, and 3USPs are autoregulated by WNT activity. This mechanism is exemplified by CTNNB1, whose 3’-UTR is over-spliced in approximately 40% of tumor samples across ten cancer types and serves as a robust prognostic indicator in HCC [78]. Furthermore, Riley et al. demonstrated that 3’-UTR splicing modulates oncogene transcript stability via both EJC-dependent and EJC-independent mechanisms [79].

In sum, the roles of AS-NMD in tumorigenesis are diverse and highly context-dependent. Under normal conditions, AS-NMD is thought to promote transcriptome homeostasis and may help constrain tumorigenesis. However, in specific contexts, such as mutations in splicing factors (e.g., SRSF2), NMD may degrade PTC-containing transcripts of tumor suppressors, thereby reducing their expression and potentially promoting cancer progression. While AS-NMD has been shown to modulate the expression of cancer-related genes, direct causal evidence for its role in tumor initiation or progression remains poorly understood. Further studies using in vivo models and functional genetic assays are necessary to establish whether AS-NMD of certain genes represents a driver mechanism or a downstream consequence in tumor biology.

NMD diminishes tumor immunogenicity by suppressing the neoantigen expression

Neoantigens are tumor-specific polypeptides that can be recognized by the immune system as immunogenic ‘non-self’ entities [80, 81]. Tumor-associated neoantigens are generated via several mechanisms, including genetic mutations, dysregulated RNA splicing events (such as exon inclusion, exon skipping, and intron retention), and even aberrant post-translational protein modifications [80, 82]. Specifically, dysregulated RNA splicing can induce frameshifts, leading to the generation of mRNA transcripts harboring PTCs. If these transcripts are not degraded, they may give rise to neoantigens that can be presented on the cell surface via MHC I or MHC II complexes and further recognized by T cells. Thus, neoantigens specifically produced by tumor cells can be harnessed as a target of immunotherapy [80].

The NMD pathway, however, acts as a critical cellular checkpoint by eliminating such PTC-containing mRNAs, thereby reducing neoantigen production and diminishing tumor immunogenicity [83]. In an NMD-proficient background, many frameshift indels (fs-indels) that introduce PTCs are efficiently degraded (Fig. 2D), limiting their immunogenic potentials [8]. This is corroborated by the finding that siRNA-mediated knockdown of UPF1 in MDA-MB-231 and SUM159PT cell lines led to a modest but significant induction of numerous novel (truncated) proteins [51]. Nevertheless, NMD is not always completely efficient. Some fs-indels may escape NMD, thereby increasing neoantigen expression and triggering anti-tumor immunity. Analysis of the TCGA skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) cohort reveals that fs-indels predicted to escape NMD (e.g., those in the penultimate exon ≤ 50 bp from the 3’ EJC or the last exon) are significantly depleted. By contrast, fs-indels are more likely to arise in middle exons, where NMD efficiently degrades PTC-containing mRNAs [84]. These results suggest that fs-indels prone to NMD escape are negatively selected (i.e., eliminated by the anti-tumor immune system due to their high immunogenicity).

Tumor microenvironment suppresses NMD in cancer cells

The tumor microenvironment is characterized by nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and other cellular stresses [85]. For survival, tumor cells need to adapt to these unfavorable conditions. Notably, nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and ER stress are all known conditions to inhibit NMD. For example, amino acid starvation has been reported to suppress NMD in HeLa cells, evidenced by the increased steady-state abundance of a nonsense mutation-containing β-globin reporter and the stabilization of endogenous NMD targets that promote amino acid homeostasis [86]. Similarly, hypoxia could inhibit NMD activity in U2OS cells, as evidenced by the stabilization of NMD-sensitive mRNA transcripts (e.g., DNAJB2, ARFRP1, MAP3K14, and UPP1) and a PTC-containing NMD reporter [87]. In addition, Usuki et al. demonstrated that ER stress induced by thapsigargin suppresses NMD in C2C12 myoblasts, characterized by the upregulation of PTC-harboring non-protein coding Snhg1 transcript, diminished UPF1 phosphorylation, and reduced protein levels of UPF1, eIF4A3, and SMG6 [88, 89].

NMD inhibition is considered an adaptive mechanism for tumor cells in response to these stress conditions from the tumor microenvironment. This stress-induced suppression of NMD leads to the upregulation of specific target transcripts that can promote tumor cell proliferation in a hostile microenvironment (Fig. 2E). For instance, the suppression of NMD by tumor microenvironmental stresses stabilizes SLC7A11 mRNA, which encodes a subunit of the xCT amino-acid transport system. Thus, NMD suppression protects tumor cells against oxidative stress by enhancing the expression of SLC7A11 [90]. Mechanistically, environmental stresses inhibit NMD via eIF2α phosphorylation and the mTOR signaling pathway [91].

NMD-related cancer therapies

NMD could promote tumor formation and metastasis, while it may also act as a tumor-suppressive mechanism. Therefore, NMD suppression or activation can be used in cancer treatment, depending on NMD’s roles in different contexts. Furthermore, the combination of NMD modulation with other cancer therapeutic approaches may bring more benefits when treating certain types of cancers.

NMD activation in cancer therapy

In cancers where NMD exerts tumor-suppressive effects, NMD-stimulatory agents represent a promising therapeutic strategy (Fig. 3A). However, developing reagents that enhance NMD activity remains highly challenging. Tranilast, an analog of a tryptophan metabolite, is one of the very few, if not the only, NMD activators identified to date [92]. Xu et al. performed validation experiments in human SH-SY5Y cells expressing C9orf72 dipeptide repeats (DPRs) by utilizing a β-globin-based NMD reporter, and revealed that among the four candidate compounds tested (tranilast, niflumic acid, febuxostat, and nitazoxanide), only tranilast functioned as an NMD activator [93]. It induced a dose-dependent degradation of the NMD reporter without affecting the expression of proline-arginine (PR), thereby confirming its potent NMD-enhancing activity.

Tranilast is an anti-allergic and anti-inflammatory drug approved in several Asian countries for the treatment of allergic conditions and bronchial asthma, with favorable safety and patient compliance [94]. To date, it has no clinical approval for cancer, but robust preclinical evidence supports its translational potential in cancer treatment. Tranilast has shown efficacy in models of lung, esophageal, gastric, colorectal, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers, both as a monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapeutic agents [95–100]. Mechanistically, in these studies, tranilast has been reported to suppress tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and invasion through NMD-independent mechanisms, for example, by targeting a spectrum of oncogenic signaling and immune regulatory pathways, such as TGF-β, NF-κB, PI3K, and MAPK signaling [94]. Thus, whether the anti-tumor effects are associated with tranilast’s capacity to activate NMD remains to be determined.

Given the limited arsenal of NMD-activating agents, the path to clinical application of NMD-promoting therapies for cancer treatment will take considerable time and further research.

NMD inhibition in cancer therapy

Inhibition of NMD by various chemical molecules is a well-developed strategy in cancer therapy at the cellular level. Recently, various NMD inhibitors, including NMD-selective inhibitors (such as NMDI-1 and NMDI-14) and NMD non-selective inhibitors (such as 5-azacytidine and amlexanox), have been reported to be effective in killing various types of tumor cells through distinct mechanisms (Table 1).

NMDI-1 could decrease the UPF1-SMG5 interaction and stabilize the hyperphosphorylated state of UPF1, thus inhibiting NMD [101]. NMDI-1 treatment effectively inhibited NMD in mouse embryonic fibroblasts and human cells. Similarly, NMDI-14 is a specific inhibitor of NMD factor SMG7 that can disrupt the interaction between SMG7 and UPF1 [102]. Studies have revealed that NMDI-1 and NMDI-14 have limited toxicity in human cells. 5-azacytidine is a clinically licensed drug used to treat leukemia and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) [103]. As a cytidine analogue, 5-azacytidine can insert into DNA and inhibit DNA methyltransferase (DNMT), thereby blocking DNA methylation [104]. This process allows the transcriptional enhancement of genes previously silenced by methylation [105]. Bhuvanagiri et al. attempted to explore the mechanism by which 5-azacytidine suppresses NMD and revealed that the inhibitory effect depends on the upregulation of the MYC protein [103]. Another NMD non-selective inhibitor is amlexanox, which is approved for the treatment of asthma and mouth ulcers [106]. Amlexanox may suppress NMD at a late stage, stabilizing PTC-containing mRNAs and inducing the expression of full-length proteins [107]. Furthermore, amlexanox can enhance PTC readthrough [107, 108]. Bokhari et al. found that amlexanox treatment might be an approach to treating CRC with MSI. NMD suppression using amlexanox decreases cell proliferation of MSI CRC cells in vitro and induces anti-tumor effects in vivo. In addition to chemical molecules with the capacity to inhibit NMD, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting transcripts of individual NMD factors could be used to repress the expression of NMD components, thereby inhibiting NMD. In mouse models of hemophilia and mdx caused by nonsense mutations, ASO targeting Upf3b (Upf3b-ASO) treatment reduces NMD activity with minimal impact on the normal transcriptome, stabilizing the PTC-containing coagulation factor IX mRNA and dystrophin mRNA, respectively [109]. Thus, identification of NMD factor-specific ASOs with minimal in vivo toxicity could be an alternative NMD-inhibitory strategy in cancer therapy.

In addition to the aforementioned well-characterized molecules with NMD-inhibitory functions, chemicals that inhibit the kinases responsible for UPF1 phosphorylation have emerged as a new toolbox for future cancer therapy. AKT1 is a serine-threonine protein kinase. Palma et al. used a chemical inhibitor library to screen for protein kinases that modulate NMD activity and revealed that AKT1 inhibition impairs NMD. AKT1 is recruited to EJC-associated mRNAs by UPF3B, and phosphorylates UPF1 to facilitate the NMD process [110]. Additionally, Cho et al. found that AKT can functionally substitute UPF2 in an alternative EJC that contains CASC3 and phosphorylate UPF1 at Threonine 151 residue, thereby promoting NMD [111]. Given that NMD inhibition is a promising strategy for eliminating cancer cells, AKT1 inhibitors could be explored in tumors with elevated expression of NMD factors or as part of combination strategies with NMD modulators to enhance anti-cancer efficacy.

Despite the promising preclinical efficacy of NMD inhibitory strategies in eliminating various tumor cell types, the clinical translation of these NMD inhibitors has encountered a major hurdle, as NMD is an essential housekeeping pathway in normal cells. Although complete loss of NMD is indispensable for cell viability and homeostasis of multiple organs, an 80% depletion of UPF1 or UPF2 can inhibit NMD without impairing cell survival or proliferation, implying that NMD inhibitors may possess a viable therapeutic window [102]. However, a key unresolved question is the magnitude of global NMD inhibition required to achieve therapeutic efficacy, which warrants further investigation. In addition, the aforementioned chemicals could have bioactive functions beyond NMD inhibition. Thus, future efforts should prioritize the development of NMD-specific inhibitors or NMD branch-specific inhibitors that disrupt the oncogenic functions of NMD while preserving its essential cellular roles, thus widening the therapeutic window.

NMD therapy in combination with other approaches

Given the limited efficacy of single-drug approaches in cancer treatment, NMD inhibitors can be combined with PTC readthrough therapy (also known as nonsense suppression therapy), immunotherapy, or chemotherapy to enhance killing effects in cancer cells.

NMD inhibitors coupled with readthrough drugs in cancer therapy

Suppressing NMD activity by various NMD inhibitors could facilitate the stabilization of PTC-containing mRNAs, including transcripts encoding tumor suppressor proteins or neoantigens in cancer cells. Thus, these PTC-containing mRNAs, in theory, could be translated into C-terminally truncated proteins. However, in order to override the possible dysfunctional characteristics of the truncated proteins translated from PTC-containing mRNAs (Fig. 3B), PTC readthrough drugs in combination with NMD inhibitors are necessary to induce the PTC readthrough and production of full-length proteins [112] (Fig. 3C). Readthrough of PTC can be mediated by various pharmacological agents, including aminoglycosides and non-aminoglycoside compounds [113]. In addition, several other approaches have been developed to induce PTC readthrough, including suppressor tRNA, pseudouridylation, RNA editing, and CRISPR technology [114, 115]. Notably, certain agents (e.g., amlexanox) possess both NMD inhibitory and PTC readthrough activities, representing a class of dual-function compounds that directly combine these two therapeutic modalities.

Aminoglycosides, a class of low-molecular-weight antibiotics characterized by a common 2-deoxystreptamine core linked to amino sugars, are the first and most extensively studied agents to induce PTC readthrough [116]. The pioneering discovery that aminoglycosides G418 and paromomycin could induce PTC readthrough in mammalian cells (COS-7) was made by Burke and Mogg in 1985 [117]. Following this discovery, several other aminoglycosides, including gentamicin, kanamycin, and arbekacin, have been shown to exhibit varying degrees of readthrough efficacy in various in vitro and in vivo models. Among them, gentamicin is the only aminoglycoside that has advanced to clinical trials for PTC readthrough, which have been conducted in several genetic disorders, including cystic fibrosis (CF), Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), hemophilia, and genodermatosis [118].

Non-aminoglycoside compounds are another group of small molecules that induce PTC readthrough and are structurally unrelated to aminoglycosides. The representative non-aminoglycoside drugs include ataluren, negamycin, and azithromycin. The most prominent example from this drug group is ataluren, commonly referred to as PTC124 or Translarna [119]. In 2007, a high-throughput screen of approximately 800,000 low-molecular-weight compounds identified ataluren as a potent readthrough inducer [120]. Based on its promising readthrough efficacy in preclinical studies and favorable safety profile, ataluren was advanced to human clinical trials for conditions such as CF, DMD, Dravet syndrome (DS), CDKL5 deficiency syndrome (CDD), and aniridia [118]. In 2014, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) granted conditional approval of the drug for ambulatory nonsense mutation DMD (nmDMD) patients aged 5 years and above. However, subsequent confirmatory trials failed to meet primary endpoints, leading to the formal withdrawal of its marketing authorization in the European Union in March 2025, a decision that remains controversial among some DMD expert clinicians [121, 122].

The withdrawal of ataluren illustrates a broader challenge: readthrough therapy alone has yet to achieve consistent clinical success. As highlighted by Spelier et al., most readthrough compounds fail to demonstrate efficacy in clinical trials [123], underscoring the need for combination strategies such as concurrent NMD inhibition. Suppression of NMD has been shown to enhance readthrough therapy and increase the expression of mutant mRNAs in genetic diseases, including DMD and CF [124, 125]. For example, McHugh et al. established a G542X-Cftr mouse model—the most prevalent CF-causing nonsense mutation—and used it to screen drug combinations in primary mouse cells. They observed a synergistic effect between the NMD inhibitor SMG1i and the readthrough agents G418, gentamicin, and paromomycin via CFTR functional analysis [124]. Furthermore, treatment by NMD inhibition strategy coupled with readthrough therapy has been studied in cancer. For instance, in the small-cell lung cancer cell line N417, the p53 tumor suppressor gene is inactivated by one heterozygous deletion of the p53 allele and a PTC mutation at codon 298 on the remaining p53 allele, resulting in NMD-mediated degradation of the mutant transcript. Martin et al. revealed that the combination of NMDI-14 (which stabilizes mutated PTC+ p53 mRNA) and G418 (which promotes ribosomal readthrough of the PTC) can restore the full-length p53 protein and subsequently lead to the death of N417 cancer cells [102]. In a recent study, researchers identified that combining PTC readthrough therapy (G418) with NMD inhibition (siUPF1) restored full-length neurofibromin protein expression in Neurofibromatosis type 1 [126].

Combined application of NMD inhibition and immunotherapy

Immunotherapy based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is probably one of the most significant advancements in cancer treatment over the past few years [127]. Tumors with high neoantigen levels are associated with favorable responses to ICI therapy [128]. As mentioned previously, NMD reduces the expression of neoantigens generated by fs-indels; thus, efficient NMD effectively masks their expression in cancer cells. NMD-escape may upregulate neoantigens. In a pan-cancer cohort analysis, Lindeboom et al. identified that tumors with frameshift mutations that evade NMD are associated with elevated immunoreactivity, suggesting better responses to immunotherapy [4]. In fact, they found that the number of NMD-escape frameshifts in ICI-therapy responders was significantly higher than in nonresponders. In addition, Litchfield et al. demonstrated a significant positive correlation between NMD-escape mutation count and the clinical benefit of ICI therapy [84]. Furthermore, the NMD-escape mutation count is positively associated with ICI clinical benefit, even in low tumor-mutation-burden (TMB) cancers [84]. TMB is known as a promising predictive biomarker of ICI efficacy [129, 130]. Compared to a model that considers only TMB status, a 5.86% increase in ICI-therapy sensitivity was observed in a model that considers both TMB and NMD-escape frameshifts [4]. These studies suggested that NMD-escape mutation count, which can be used independently or in combination with TMB, may be a potential predictor of ICI therapy response. Therefore, ICI therapy might be more effective for tumors carrying neoantigens produced by transcripts harboring frameshift-induced PTCs that evade NMD.

Inhibition of NMD can increase neoantigen expression and enhance tumor immunogenicity (Fig. 3D). For instance, Pastor et al. found that NMD suppression leads to the expression of neoantigens and their immune-mediated rejection in tumor cells, and to decreased tumor growth in subcutaneous and metastatic tumor models [131]. Additionally, Becker et al. showed that NMD suppression by 5-azacytidine stabilizes frameshifted mRNAs and enhances HLA class I-mediated presentation of frameshift-derived neoepitopes in MSI CRCs [132]. Notably, Zhu et al. reported that inhibition of UPF1 R433 methylation impairs NMD, leading to enhanced tumor immunogenicity and consequently increased anti-PD-1 responsiveness in CRCs [133]. Consistently, Wang et al. demonstrated that inhibiting NMD (using siSMG5 or NMDI-14) reprogrammed the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating innate immune cells and activating CD8⁺ T cells, thereby potentiating the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (anti-PD-1 antibody) therapy in CRCs [134]. Collectively, all these studies highlight that anti-cancer immunotherapy could be enhanced with NMD inhibitor treatments. Thus, the combination of NMD inhibition and immunotherapy emerges as a particularly promising therapeutic strategy for cancers.

In addition to directly inhibiting NMD to stabilize PTC-containing transcripts, emerging evidence suggests that pharmacological modulation of RNA splicing represents a complementary strategy to further expand the repertoire of tumor neoantigens. Splicing-modulatory small molecules, such as indisulam and MS023, have been shown to induce splicing alterations, leading to the generation of immunogenic neoantigens [82]. Notably, these compounds also downregulate SMG1, thereby impairing NMD activity and stabilizing otherwise degraded neoantigen-encoding transcripts [135]. This dual effect—inhibition of NMD and induction of splicing alterations—positions splicing modulation as a promising adjunct to NMD inhibition in cancer immunotherapy. Furthermore, targeting splicing factors such as SLU7 has been shown to induce both splicing aberrations and NMD inhibition, resulting in enhanced tumor immunogenicity and increased sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade [136]. Collectively, these findings support the rationale for combining splicing perturbation with NMD inhibition as a novel strategy to maximize neoantigen production and potentiate antitumor immune responses.

NMD inhibition combined with chemotherapy in cancer cells

NMD inhibition therapy has the potential to be combined with chemotherapy to eliminate cancer cells. Popp et al. demonstrated that combined exposure of the human cervical cancer cell line HeLa to the NMD inhibitor NMDI-1 and the chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin can lead to increased cell death [137]. Separately, UPF1 was found to facilitate chemoresistance to oxaliplatin in CRC by regulating TOP2A activity and maintaining the stemness of cancer stem cells [138]. These studies collectively suggest that NMD inhibitors can enhance the efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents in cancer therapy.

Perspectives

NMD is a vital pathway that controls mRNA quality and regulates gene expression [28, 139]. NMD is widely involved in multiple physiological and pathological processes, from organ development to tissue homeostasis [2, 3, 5]. Due to the functional complexity of individual NMD molecules (See Table 2), the mechanisms and biological functions of NMD in normal cells remain to be precisely elucidated. Recent findings have enlightened an important role of diverse NMD factors in tumorigenesis. However, the roles of NMD per se in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis are complicated and far from being firmly established [8, 9]. One caveat preventing a firm establishment of NMD in cancer biology is that the majority of NMD factors participate in multiple mRNA-decay-independent functions, including genomic stability control, telomere maintenance, and autophagy, which are strongly associated with cancers [3, 140, 141]. Altered expression of these NMD factors in tumors is often interpreted as reflecting global NMD activity, yet it remains unclear whether the observed effects are truly NMD-mediated or arise from their NMD-independent roles. Disentangling these contributions will be essential to precisely define NMD per se in tumorigenesis.

Whether NMD acts as a friend or foe in tumor biology seems to largely depend on the cellular origins and molecular etiologies of tumors. On the one hand, NMD can facilitate tumor formation and progression through various mechanisms, including the degradation of PTC-harboring transcripts encoding tumor suppressors or neoantigens. On the other hand, NMD may suppress tumor development by eliminating PTC-containing mRNAs that encode oncoproteins. Notably, the complexity of NMD function is further underscored by substantial variability in its efficiency. As proposed by Nguyen et al., inter-individual variability in NMD efficiency provides a plausible explanation for the divergent clinical manifestations and therapeutic responses in patients carrying the same PTC mutation [142]. Given the dual-edged role of NMD in cancer and its extensive variability across tissues and individuals, future precision treatment strategies should take into account the patient’s baseline NMD status, thereby paving the way for truly personalized cancer therapy.

In the context of cancer therapeutic strategies with NMD activity modulation, investigations into NMD inhibitors far outnumber those on NMD activators. This disparity reflects not only the considerable technical hurdles in developing NMD activators, but also the better-defined clinical translational prospects of NMD inhibition—particularly by enhancing tumor immunogenicity and synergizing with immune checkpoint blockade. In light of the NMD efficacy variability across different cell/tumor types, cancer therapies that modulate NMD activity could benefit strongly from a better understanding of NMD mechanisms across cell/tumor types and from the development of chemical reagents that specifically target NMD and exert minimal in vivo toxicity.
