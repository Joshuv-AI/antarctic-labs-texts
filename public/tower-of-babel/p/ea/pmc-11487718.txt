Preoperative visit-care for transcatheter aortic valve replacement: a review
Authors: JunYang Tan, GuanXing Wei, Fang Ma, Han Yan, XiTing Wang, QiuLan Hu, Wei Wei, MingFang Yang, YangJuan Bai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe aim of this review is to evaluate and summarize the evidence for preoperative visit-care of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and to provide evidence-based support for clinical intervention.DesignThe review presents an evidence summary report, following the standard of the Fudan University Center for Evidence-based Nursing.MethodsLiterature related to preoperative visit-care for transcatheter aortic valve replacement have been retrieved based on the “6S” pyramid model of evidence. The types of literature included systematic reviews, expert consensus, evidence summary, and guidelines.Data sourcesUp To Date, Cochrane Library, Joanna Briggs Institute, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, Medlive, American Heart Association, Registered Nurses Association of Ontario, Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network, European Society of Cardiology, American College of Cardiology, PubMed, CINAHL, Wanfang database, VIP database, Chinese biomedical literature database, CNKI. The search period is limited to the time when each database was established until February 2024.ResultsA total of 18 articles were included in this review, consisting of 1 systematic review, 1 evidence summary, 3 guidelines and 13 expert consensuses. This review summarized 30 pieces of the best evidence for preoperative visit-care for transcatheter aortic valve replacement, encompassing three evidence themes: multidisciplinary team collaboration, preoperative assessment, and preoperative education.ConclusionThe study has established an ideal foundation of evidence for preoperative visit-care in TAVR. However, throughout the particular application process, it was crucial to assess the feasibility and relevance of the evidence in clinical practice by taking into account elements such as the application setting, recommendations from experts with diverse expertise, and the preferences of the patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04241-y.Keywords: Transcatheter aortic valve replacement, Preoperative visit-care, Evidence-based nursing

Introduction

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVR), also known as Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI), is a leading minimally invasive interventional operation to treat patients with aortic stenosis [1]. In 2002, the world’s first TAVR operation was successfully performed by Alan Cribier in France [2]. TAVR has shown significant advancements over the last 10 years and has emerged as a highly effective therapeutic option for patients suffering from severe symptomatic aortic valve stenosis (AS) [3, 4]. It offers the benefits of reduced injury, faster recuperation, and early enhancement in quality of life [3]. Nevertheless, while choosing TAVR as a therapeutic strategy, there are two crucial matters of concern. It is advisable to use caution when suggesting TAVR as a treatment option for individuals under 65 years old. According to the average life expectancy of the American population, a 60-year-old patient can expect to live for a further 21 to 24 years. This is longer than the lifespan of two bioprosthetic valves [4, 5]. The decision of whether to undergo TAVR is primarily based on age and predicted longevity [6, 7]. In contrast, for patients who are older or have significant comorbidities, TAVR would be more preferable [5, 8]. Prior to TAVR for young individuals at low risk, it is imperative to perform a thorough preoperative assessment and obtain compelling evidence that demonstrates comparable long-term outcomes between TAVR and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) [9, 10]. Another thing to note is that conduction block risk occurs following TAVR, necessitating a permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) [11]. The incidence of PPI within 30 days following TAVR ranges from 6.6 to 17.4% [6]. This is significantly associated with the mortality rate and readmission rate of patients [12]. Abnormal electrocardiogram and operation-related factors have been confirmed as significant predictors for PPI following TAVR. These factors include preoperative conduction abnormalities with right bundle branch block being particularly relevant, the development of new left bundle branch block (LBBB), the depth at which the valve is implanted, and the specific type of valve used [11, 13]. Although the progress of TAVR technology has expanded its use in more challenging aortic anatomies [14]. However, the anatomy of bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) still poses technical challenges. Situations such as paravalvular leakage and asymmetric expansion of the implanted valve may occur and are prone to lead to suboptimal results of TAVR (e.g., high pacemaker rate) [3]. Therefore, current guidelines do not recommend implementing TAVR in patients with BAV [15]. As with patients with BAV, the management of patients with unicuspid aortic valve (UAV) is also challenging. Because most patients are relatively young and usually have associated aortic pathologies [14]. Currently, only a few case reports have described the use of TAVR in patients with UAV [16]. In addition to these factors, the majority of older patients who will undergo TAVR treatment have multiple co-morbidities. Furthermore, the expenses related to TAVR are substantial, resulting in high levels of anxiety, fear, and expectations among patients and their families [17]. The prevalence of preoperative anxiety can significantly impact the administration of anaesthesia and the overall outcome of the final surgery [18, 19].

Therefore, prior to considering TAVR as a treatment option for patients with AS, thorough examinations and evaluations must be conducted to ascertain if the minimally invasive treatment is the appropriate selection for the specific patient. Detailed pre-procedural evaluation is one of the key steps to the success of TAVR, and adequate preoperative evaluation can help foresee possible procedure-related complications [20]. The study by Perry et al. shows that a safe and accurate preoperative evaluation of cardiovascular anatomical structure, physiological function, and pathophysiology may mean the difference between success and catastrophic failure in procedure [21]. In addition, appropriate patient risk stratification is crucial for making the best therapy strategy [22]. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score and Euroscore II, as the most commonly used risk scores, are often used for risk stratification of patients undergoing valvular procedures [22, 23]. The ESII and STS scores are considered to be associated with the risk of death among patients in hospital [24]. The preoperative visit-care is equally important for both patients and healthcare professionals in preparing for surgery. A preoperative visit-care enables healthcare providers to assess the expected benefits and surgical risks associated with TAVR [25], and assist patients in making appropriate preparations for the operation. The main factor contributing to anxiety and psychological pressure for patients is the lack of sufficient surgical preparation [26]. Patients who possess both physical and mental readiness for surgery generally experience a smoother operation, as they are more likely to be in a state of relaxation [27]. The preoperative visit- care was considered to be a potential strategy to enhance surgical preparation for inpatients and their families [26]. The nursing practice process throughout the surgery time involves the integration of medical, social, psychological, and other knowledge and abilities into patient care [28]. Performing a structured preoperative visit-care allows for a thorough evaluation of patients’ physical and psychological health before surgery, as well as their preparedness for the procedure [29]. At the same time, it has the potential to foster effective nurse-patient communication, enhance patients’ comprehension of pertinent surgical details, alleviate preoperative fears and anxieties among TAVR patients, and ensures the smooth progress of the surgery [30, 31]. Health education related to diseases can facilitate patients’ management of their own illnesses and is beneficial to the quality of life of patients [32]. The preoperative visit-care had an important significance for the patient undergoing TAVR.

Currently, there is a lack of uniformity in the preoperative visit-care for TAVR in different institutions. Most of these practices are based on nurse experience summaries and lack evidence-based support and standardization [33–35]. An evidence summary is a review of available evidence for one or one set of research questions related to a particular topic [36]. This review sought to provide evidence-based support for clinical nursing practice by methodically exploring, extracting, and summarizing both domestic and international literature relevant to preoperative visit-care of transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Methods/methodology

The review adhered to the guidelines for evidence summary reports established by the evidence-based Nursing Center of Fudan University [37]. The report requirements encompass several key components, including the identification of the problem, the retrieval and screening of relevant literature, the evaluation of the literature, the summary and grading of evidence, and the development of practice recommendations.

Problem establishment

This review established an evidence-based problem based on the PIPOST principle as follows [38]. P (population) denotes the group of patients who will undergo TAVR; I (intervention) represents interventions related to preoperative visit-car; P (professional) refers to cardiovascular ward nurses, and doctors in the TAVR surgery team; O (outcome) refers to anxiety, nervousness, satisfaction; S (Setting) denotes the Department of Cardiology in a tertiary Grade A hospital; T (Type) means guidelines, evidence summary, systematic review, and expert consensus.

Evidence retrieval

The following databases were searched based on the “6S” pyramid model of evidence-based medicine: Up To Date, Cochrane Library, Joanna Briggs Institute, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Medlive, American Heart Association (AHA), European Society of Cardiology (ESC), Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network(SIGN), Registered Nurses Association of Ontario(RNAO), American College of Cardiology (ACC), PubMed, CINAHL, Wanfang database, VIP database, CNKI database, Chinese biomedical literature database. The search period was restricted from the inception of the database until February 2024.

The search strategy used in databases consisted of the following terms: (“aortic stenosis” OR “Aortic Valve Stenoses” OR “transcatheter aortic valve replacement” OR “Heart Valve Prosthesis Implantation” OR “Stenos*s, Aortic Valve” OR “Valve Stenos*s, Aortic” OR “Stenos*s, Aortic” OR “Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation” OR “Implantation, Heart Valve Prosthesis”) AND (“Preoperative Care” OR “Care, Preoperative” OR “Preoperative Procedure” OR “Procedure, Preoperative” OR “Procedures, Preoperative” OR “Preoperative Procedures” OR “preoperative interview” OR “preoperative visit” OR “preoperative education” OR “patient education” OR “preoperative teaching”) AND (“consensus” OR “Guide” OR “Manua” OR “Guild” OR “System* review” OR “mate-analysis”). The search formula and wildcard characters were altered flexibly by combining topic terms and free words, in accordance with the specific requirements of each database.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: ① Patients undergoing TAVR surgery, aged ≥ 18 years; ② The study focused on nursing interventions related to preoperative visit-care for TAVR; ③ The literature included guidelines, evidence summary, systematic review and expert consensus; ④ Only the latest versions of updated or revised clinical guidelines were considered; ⑤ the language of the literature had to be either Chinese or English.

Exclusion criteria: ① Articles with insufficient information or limited access to the complete text; ② Literature of poor quality following examination.

Literature screening

The literature retrieved was imported into Endnote and any duplicated literature was eliminated. Two proficient researchers conducted a thorough examination of the literature separately. The literature was initially vetted by reviewing titles, abstracts, and keywords. Afterwards, the remaining literature was thoroughly read and screened. When encountering any disagreement on the inclusion of literature during the screening process, authors would consult with a third expert in evidence-based nursing to ascertain the literature’s status.

Quality evaluation

Quality evaluation of expert consensus, systematic evaluation and evidence summary

The appropriate assessment instruments provided by the Australian JBI evidence-based Healthcare Centre (version 2016) were utilized to evaluate the system assessment and expert consensus. The evaluation tool of systematic review consisted of 11 items, while the evaluation tool for expert consensus comprised 6 items. The evidence summary employed the CASE checklist for evaluation, comprising 10 items [39]. Two researchers, who had received systematic training, independently undertook the examination of papers. In the event of any disagreements, a third expert was engaged in the deliberation.

Quality evaluation of the guidelines

The quality of the guidelines was assessed using the 2017 updated British Clinical Guidelines Research and Evaluation tool, specifically the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) [40]. This tool consists of 6 dimensions, 23 items, and 2 overall evaluation items. Four researchers who underwent systematic training independently reviewed the guidelines. Standardized scores were computed using the scoring criteria, where higher scores corresponded to superior quality guidelines.

Evidence summary and grading

The evidence was extracted and summarized independently by two trained researchers. In the event of a disagreement over the evidence extraction and summary, the research team engaged in collective discussion and ultimately reached a consensus. This study utilized the evidence pre-classification system from the 2014 JBI intervention study to assess the evidence, employing a rating scheme ranging from Level 1 to Level 5. The evidence was evaluated by criteria and expert consensus, which involved tracing its source literature and assessing the evidence based on the categories of study conducted in the original literature.

Discussion

Enhancing interdisciplinary collaboration and team expertise

TAVR is intricate and carries significant risks. It necessitates the collaboration of several medical experts, including cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, anesthesiologists, imaging specialists, and other multidisciplinary professionals. Therefore, it is imperative to build a multidisciplinary heart team (MDHT) to ensure the procedure’s success and safety [50]. According to international guideline [48], it is recommended that the MDHT of TAVR should consist of various specialists including cardiovascular internists, imaging assessment physicians, valve disease interventionists, cardiovascular surgeons, anesthesiologists, nursing teams, rehabilitation management teams, and other relevant professionals and technical personnel. As TAVR technology continues to advance, the safety of surgery is increasing significantly. Nevertheless, when compared to conventional interventional surgery, TAVR remains a procedure that carries significant risks and is intricate in nature. Clinical and anatomical factors are important elements in establishing the appropriate treatment methods [4]. Martinsson et al. [59] discovered a correlation between age and the mortality rate in low-risk patients. In intermediate-risk and high-risk patients, comorbidities, frailty status, as well as anatomical and procedural factors play a more significant role than chronological age. Whereas chronological age is of great significance in low-risk patients. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that although the data from the Nordic Aortic Valve Intervention trial has alleviated eased people’s concerns regarding the mid-term durability of TAVR valves within an 8-year span, highly prolonged follow-up studies (> 10 years) are still necessary to ascertain whether TAVR can ultimately be utilized for all young severe AS patients with low risk levels [60]. Therefore, prior to the surgery, it is crucial to engage in multidisciplinary collaboration to analyze these intricate factors so as to ascertain whether minimally invasive surgery is suitable for the patient.

There is a pronounced learning curve in TAVR. Ensuring the safe implementation of TAVR necessitates the consistent functioning of the team and comprehensive training for its members [50]. Nurses who conduct preoperative visit-care must possess a comprehensive understanding of patients’ preoperative status, as well as a mastery of the specific procedures involved in TAVR operations and disease-related knowledge [33]. This study highlights the need for the nursing team to have higher professional standards. Specifically, the team should prioritize selecting specialist nurses who have undergone systematic training and had at least 3 years of experience in cardiovascular-related work.

Special attention should be paid to the patient’s physical condition and surgical risk in preoperative evaluation

The 2017 European Valve Management guideline [61] suggests that TAVR should be considered for patients with AS who have severe symptoms and are not suitable for surgery or for those who have a higher risk of complications from procedure. The survey revealed that the average age of patients undergoing TAVR is over 75 years. Additionally, 62% of these patients have preoperative heart function classified as NYHA class III-IV [62]. Furthermore, these patients are frequently affected by multiple comorbidities including cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, chronic pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, and renal insufficiency [63]. Hence, it is imperative to conduct a thorough evaluation of patients and enhance preoperative examinations in order to accurately assess the preoperative state of patients during the preoperative visit-care. During the assessment process, factors that may increase surgical risk can be identified, such as left ventricular (LV) mass and hypertrophy, complete atrioventricular heart block, malignant tumours, etc [49, 51, 55, 56]. Preexisting right bundle branch block (RBBB) is regarded as the most reliable predictive indicator for PPI following TAVR [64]. A study conducted by Sugiyama et al. revealed that the incidence of new occurrences of PPI following TAVR in patients with RBBB amounted to 33.3% [64]. Performing a preoperative electrocardiogram assessment can assist in identifying RBBB, enabling us to make necessary preparations in advance.

According to previous studies, approximately half of recipients of TAVR have some degree of coronary artery disease (CAD) because they have common pathophysiology and risk factors [65]. The study by Faroux et al. indicates that CAD and its severity seem likely to be markers of high surgical risk for TAVR [66]. Untreated significant coronary heart disease will increase postoperative mortality [67]. Therefore, a professional heart team is needed to conduct a thorough preoperative assessment and discussion to determine how to manage valvular disease and CAD, taking into account the patient’s preferences, surgical risks, and anatomical limitations. When evaluating patients for TAVR, the lifetime risk of requiring coronary revascularization for the patient, the ability to access the coronary arteries percutaneously after TAVR, and the possibility of undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery in the future should also be considered [68, 69].

In order to achieve thorough preoperative evaluation of TAVR patients and early detection of any possible post-implantation complications, Multimodal imaging evaluation is the key [21]. Among them, Echocardiography and computed tomography (CT) play complementary roles throughout the entire phase of patient evaluation [70]. Echocardiography is the preferred imaging examination method for the initial diagnosis and classification of AS. It plays an indispensable role in the assessment of valve morphology and hemodynamics [70]. Compared with traditional surgical aortic valve replacement, there is a lack of direct visualization of the valve and annulus during the TAVR process. CT can provide detailed anatomical features of the aortic valve, aortic valve annulus, left ventricular outflow tract, and coronary ostia, and is the basis for TAVR planning [71]. Clinical care team members must have a basic understanding of preoperative imaging techniques that can be used to optimize the care of TAVR patients.

Preoperative frailty evaluation is a straightforward and essential component of standard preoperative screening. Frailty is considered to be a state of increased susceptibility to stressors such as trauma or illness, leading to poor homeostasis and increased risk of complications and sequelae [72]. Previous study has indicated that the prevalence of frailty in patients undergoing thoracic surgery might reach up to 70% [73]. Frailty in patients following thoracic surgery is linked to a heightened likelihood of postoperative complications and elevated mortality rate [72]. The study conducted by Lin HS et al. indicates that frail patients tend to recover slowly or incompletely after surgery [73]. Frailty should be considered while evaluating patients for surgical suitability and developing prehabilitation and rehabilitation strategies.

Strengthen preoperative health education and increase patient compliance

Meng et al. found that the majority of patients who underwent TAVR were elderly, possessed limited understanding about the disease, inadequate self-management skills, and exhibited poor adherence to treatment [62]. In addition, the life quality of post-TAVR patients is lower than the national norm [62]. Cardiac rehabilitation can promote the clinical outcome of postoperative patients, facilitating their reintegration into society and resumption of their normal activities [74]. Prior to surgery, it is recommended to provide patients with rehabilitation education in order to enhance their adherence to postoperative cardiac rehabilitation. It is also important to provide patients and their families with information regarding TAVR and the necessary measures for coordinating the surgery, in order to assist in alleviating the stress and worry experienced by patients. The study conducted by Karakayali et al. has demonstrated that providing health education to patients yields positive results in improving the quality of life of these patients and reduces the frequency of their repeat hospitalizations [32]. The PARTNER2A and SURTAVI tests showed that the incidence of cerebrovascular ischemic events 30 days after TAVR was about 5%, while the occurrence of severe bleeding episodes was about 10–12% [75, 76]. The recommendation is to develop customized anticoagulation strategies for patients based on individual consultations within a multidisciplinary team, taking into account the patient’s specific circumstances. Additionally, nurses should improve health education for patients regarding lifelong adherence to anticoagulant therapy, with the goal of decreasing the occurrence of cerebrovascular ischemic and bleeding episodes.

The complexity of the cardiac surgery process poses very high requirements for preoperative patient health education [77]. Due to the invasiveness and significant risks of the surgery, it is essential to ensure that TAVR recipients have a comprehensive understanding of the reasons for the surgery, possible outcomes, risks, benefits, and possible complications [77]. In addition, the responsible nurse needs to introduce the purpose of TAVR surgery, anesthesia, surgical process, preoperative fasting and drinking time, matters needing attention, etc. to the patient and his family [78]. One of the most difficult challenges to overcome in TAVR is unexpected complications such as arrhythmia, renal failure, vascular complications [79]. Complications after TAVR such as coronary artery obstruction are associated with patient mortality. So follow-up after discharge should be strengthened to detect problems in time [80]. According to expert recommendations, follow-up including blood routine, echocardiography, and electrocardiogram should be conducted at 1, 3, and 6 months after discharge. At the sixth month, adjust the patient’s medication dose [78].

Conclusion

The careful preoperative visit-care is crucial for achieving the success of TAVR and avoiding serious complications. This review summarizes the best evidence on preoperative visit-care for transcatheter aortic valve replacement, and divides it into three evidence themes: multidisciplinary team, Pre-operative evaluation, and preoperative education. This provides a basis for clinical healthcare providers to conduct preoperative evaluation for patients undergoing TAVR, which is conducive to increasing surgical safety and patients satisfaction. However, TAVR still faces many challenges from anatomical and clinical factors. Therefore, multidisciplinary cooperation is extremely crucial. During the process of preoperative visit, it is necessary to improve surgical preparation and formulate a surgical plan suitable for the patient.
