Prevalence of Disorders of Gut-Brain Interaction in Indonesian Adolescents: Has It Increased during the Coronavirus Disease Pandemic?
Authors: Andy Darma, Ni Nyoman Metriani Nesa, Khadijah Rizky Sumitro, Evi Rokhayati, Budiyanto, Karolina Trigemayanti Tallo, Novi Rahayu Ariyanti, Lucia Pudyastuti Retnaningtyas, Dian Dwi Sari Mustika Febryani, Imanuel Yulius Malino, Herwina Brahmantya, Wenas Wenas, Alpha Fardah Athiyyah, Reza Gunadi Ranuh, Subijanto Marto Sudarmo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe prevalence of disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI), previously known as functional gastrointestinal disorders, has increased among children and adolescents, impacting their quality of life. Lifestyle changes during the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, such as decreased physical activity and altered dietary habits, may have contributed to this increase rise. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of DGBIs among Indonesian adolescents during the COVID-19 pandemic using the Rome IV diagnostic criteria.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from March 2022 to April 2023 involving 5,247 adolescents (aged 13–18 years) from nine provinces in Indonesia (Riau, Riau Islands, Central Java, East Java, Bali, South Kalimantan, East Kalimantan, East Nusa Tenggara, and Papua). Data were collected via online and offline versions of the Rome IV questionnaire and analyzed descriptively to determine DGBI prevalence rates by age, sex, and region.ResultsThe prevalence of DGBIs was greater in females than males and among adolescents living in urban areas. We found that 28.4% of adolescents were diagnosed with at least one DGBI, with functional constipation being the most prevalent (21.8%), followed by functional dyspepsia (4.5%). Additionally, 13.5% of respondents experienced two overlapping DGBIs, and 1.3% suffered from three overlapping DGBIs simultaneously. The prevalence rate of DGBI varied significantly across the participating provinces.ConclusionDGBIs are highly prevalent among Indonesian adolescents after the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings can guide public health strategies to address adolescent gastrointestinal health in Indonesia and other regions.

INTRODUCTION

Disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI), previously known as functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs), are a group of disorders classified by gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms related to any combination of motility disturbances, visceral hypersensitivity, altered mucosal and immune function, gut microbiota, and/or central nervous system processing [12]. Over 40% of adults [3] and one in four children and adolescents [4] across the world have some DGBI. Adolescents with DGBIs have a lower quality of life compared to healthy adolescents. In addition, DGBI exacerbates psychological distress in the form of anxiety and depression, which affects both the parents and their child [56].

Before the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, Velasco-Benítez et al reported the global prevalence of DGBI in schoolchildren and adolescents was 23% [4], among which functional constipation (FC; 12%) was the most common condition, followed by functional dyspepsia (FD; 5%) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS; 3%). The COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent disruptions (e.g., social distancing, school closures, isolation, decreased access to support services) has a multifaceted impact on children and adolescents [78]. Concurrently, the prevalence of DGBIs in Italy increased from the pre-pandemic levels [9].

Despite containing over 50% of the world’s pediatric population, there is a paucity of epidemiological data on pediatric DGBI from the Asia-Pacific region [10], including Indonesia, particularly in the post-pandemic era. Only one study from Jakarta reported that 23% of children experienced at least one DGBI during the online learning phase [11]. Therefore, this national-level epidemiological study aimed to assess the prevalence of DGBI among Indonesian adolescents, focusing during the COVID-19 pandemic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethical considerations

We conducted a cross-sectional survey in 9 randomly selected provinces of Indonesia, representing the five major islands and regions: 1) Riau, 2) Riau Island, 3) Central Java, 4) East Java, 5) Bali, 6) South Kalimantan, 7) East Kalimantan, 8) East Nusa Tenggara, and 9) Papua. The geographical distribution of the respondents is shown in Fig. 1. The Health Ethics Committee Dr Soetomo General Academic Hospital provided ethical approval for the study (no.: 0492/KEPK/IX/2022); additionally, the study protocol was approved by the principals of each school. The authors guaranteed the confidentiality of all data obtained during the study.

Distribution pattern of region and different data collection methods used by the respondents (N=5,247). The total number of respondents from each province is shown as frequency (n) and percentage (%). The data collection methods (paper-based, google-form-based, and both) are depicted as different colored-coded regions.

Participants

At least one junior and or senior high school from nine selected provinces were selected from each province based on the network of the local coordinator in each province. A pediatrician served as the local coordinator in each province. From each school, we included all adolescents (both male and female) aged 13–18 years to achieve generalizability of our results. The details of the study were explained to the participant and either written or electronic informed consent to participate was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. Adolescents with a history of abdominal surgery in the past year, those diagnosed with any malignancy, hormonal disorders, rheumatic diseases, or chronic gastrointestinal diseases, and those taking long-term medications, such as antiepileptics, anticholinergics, antihistamines, antispasmodics, corticosteroids, and nonsteroid anti-inflammatory drugs, were excluded from this study.

Data collection

Data were collected from March 2022 to April 2023 via an internet survey using either Google Forms (online), a paper-based questionnaire (offline), or both (Fig. 1). The local coordinator and a research assistant in each province accompanied the respondents when filling out the questionnaires to avoid errors and empty answers, and offered additional explanations when needed. All completed paper-based questionnaires will be collected by the local coordinator and sent to Surabaya, East Java. These data will be combined with the responses from the online questionnaires. Before analysis, the completeness of the data will be evaluated to determine if any entries need to be excluded. Once confirmed complete, further analysis will be conducted.

Questionnaires

Rome IV diagnostic questionnaire for pediatric FGIDs

This self-reported questionnaire for children and adolescents (≥10 years) consists of 58 questions spread across five sections–Section A: pain and uncomfortable feelings above the belly button, Section B: bellyache and abdominal pain (around and below the belly button), Section C: bowel movements (“poop,” “stool,” or “number 2”), Section D: nausea and vomiting, and Section E: other symptoms [12]. Additionally, a 4-item supplemental questionnaire on sociodemographic data (name, age, sex, residence, and province) was included.

Interpreting the Rome IV questionnaire

The responses were scored based on the standard scoring instructions for the child/adolescent self-reported form [12]. Accordingly, the diagnoses were classified into three subgroups with a total of 11 diagnoses. The 3 subgroups were functional nausea and vomiting disorders, functional abdominal pain disorders, and functional defecation disorders. The functional nausea and vomiting disorders sub-group consists of 5 diagnoses: 1) adolescent cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS), 2) functional nausea, 3) functional vomiting, 4) adolescent rumination syndrome (ARS), and 5) aerophagia. The abdominal pain disorders sub-group consists of 4 diagnoses: 1) FD, 2) IBS, 3) abdominal migraine and 4) functional abdominal pain – not otherwise specified (FAP-NOS). In addition, functional dyspepsia has 2 subtypes as well: 1) epigastric pain syndrome (EPS) and 2) postprandial distress syndrome (PDS). The functional defecation disorder sub-group consists of 2 diagnoses: 1) FC and 2) functional non-retentive fecal incontinence.

Translating the ROME IV questionnaire into Bahasa Indonesia

The original Rome IV questionnaire was translated from English to Bahasa Indonesia by an independent certified translator. The translated questionnaire was then reviewed and revalidated by the research team to ensure accuracy. After finalizing the Bahasa Indonesia version, it was translated back to English by another independent qualified translator to ensure the original meaning was retained during the translation process. The outline of the translation process is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Validation of the Bahasa Indonesia version

Before disseminating the translated version of the questionnaire, both paper-based and electronically, the questionnaire was piloted in 15 healthy junior and senior high school students (aged 13–18 years) in Surabaya, East Java to determine the understandability, relevance and suitability of the questions. During the process of answering the questions, the healthy adolescents reported that the questions were easily understood; however, most subjects found the questionnaire to be quite lengthy, taking 15–20 minutes to complete.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into the Microsoft Excel Sheets program (Microsoft Co.) and analyzed using the Open Epi, version 3.01 [13] for data analysis. A descriptive analysis was performed to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, and residence) and DGBI prevalence (with 95% confidence intervals, CI). Additionally, subgroup analyses based on age, sex, and geographical region were performed.

RESULTS

A total of 5,326 adolescents completed the survey, of which 79 were excluded due to incomplete data (98.5% completion rate from total 5,247 respondents). The median age of the participants was 15 years (interquartile range 14–16 years). Amount of 892 of 5,241 (17.0%) participants reported having previously had confirmed COVID-19. In this particular study, the prevalence rate of any DGBI was similar between the two age groups of adolescents that were examined. In respondents aged 13–15 years, functional constipation was more frequently reported, while functional dyspepsia was more frequently observed in 16–18 years. A sum of 56.9% (n=2,983) of the participants were female. Almost all of the diagnoses of DGBI that were reported in this study were more prevalent in females. On the other hand, CVS and IBS were identified more frequently in males. The prevalence rates of DGBI based on sex, age, and residence are presented in detail in Table 1.

Most of the respondents lived in urban areas (n=3,807; 72.6%). The majority of the respondents (n=3,215; 61.3%) lived in the Java and Bali islands. A higher prevalence has also been reported in adolescents living in urban areas. However, for CVS, functional vomiting and ARS were more commonly found in adolescents living in rural areas. Most of the respondents used the online method (n=3,646; 69.5%) to complete the survey. A total of 664 adolescents (12.7%) in two provinces utilized the paper-based method to complete the survey, whereas the remaining used both methods. The region- and data-collection-method-wise distribution of the respondents is presented in Fig. 1.

The overall prevalence of any DGBI (at least one diagnosis of DGBI based on the Rome IV criteria) in the study was 28.4% (n=1,489/5,247; Fig. 2A). All of the respondents qualified for 9 of 11 DGBIs diagnosable that are applicable to this age group, whereas no respondent qualified for FAP-NOS and functional non-retentive fecal incontinence. The most common diagnoses of DGBIs were FC (21.8%; 95% CI: 20.65–22.88%) and FD (4.5%; 95% CI: 3.95–5.07%). Among the functional nausea and vomiting disorders sub-group, ARS had the highest prevalence rate. It was the third most frequent DGBI, with a total prevalence rate of 1.7% (95% CI: 1.40–2.10%). CVS had the lowest prevalence rate (0.5%; 95% CI: 0.37–0.77%). Functional nausea came in second lowest prevalence rate, with (0.7%; 95% CI: 0.51–0.97%) (Table 1).

Approximately 14.8% (n=221/1,489) of adolescents in this study experienced at least two of three DGBI sub-groups. We also noted varying prevalence rates of different DGBIs across the nine Indonesian provinces in this study (Table 2). Among the participating provinces, Riau had the highest prevalence rate of any DGBI according to the ROME IV criteria and the lowest DGBI was reported in Papua. FC was the most frequent DGBI in the entire research area, ranging from 12.8% in Papua to 25.2% in East Nusa Tenggara. It was followed by FD ranging from 1.0% in Papua to 10.1% in Central Java. PDS was the most common sub-type of FD among all adolescents in this study. The highest prevalence rate of PDS was reported in Central Java, with a prevalence rate more than twice the general prevalence rate. No adolescents were qualified for EPS in Riau, Riau Island, East Kalimantan and Papua. The highest prevalence of ARS was reported in Riau province with 5.3%; 95% CI: 2.92–9.51%, whereas, no ARS cases were reported in the Riau Islands.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first multi-center study to determine the prevalence of DGBIs in Indonesian adolescents using the Rome IV diagnostic questionnaire during the COVID-19 pandemic period. Almost 30% of the Indonesian adolescents included in our study had at least one type of DGBI. A previous study on Indonesian adolescents conducted during online learning from a single center in Jakarta, Indonesia, reported a 23.0% (n=325/1,413) prevalence of any DGBI [11], which is lower than that observed in this study. Furthermore, the prevalence of DGBIs noted in our study is also higher than that described in previous studies from the pre-pandemic phase (23.0%) [4].

Other studies have also indicated a relationship between the COVID-19 pandemic and various gastrointestinal disorders [1617]. Some of the adaptations that children had to deal with during the COVID-19 pandemic were remote learning, restrictions on social activities, and quarantine for those infected [18]. Adolescence is a challenging period of physiological transition and maturation into adulthood, making these children a vulnerable group [14]. The ability of children to adapt to surrounding risk factors is modulated by genetic factors, sex, and their social environment [15]. Because adolescents are particularly sensitive to sudden alterations in their physical, emotional, and social environments, difficulty adapting to these changes can disrupt their psychosocial well-being and serve as a risk factor for developing DGBIs [519].

Interestingly, our study also revealed the co-occurrence of multiple DGBIs. Approximately 13.5% (n=201/1,489) of the respondents met the diagnostic criteria for two sub-groups of DGBIs, and 1.3% (n=20/1,489) met the criteria for three sub-groups of DGBIs. Scarpato et al. [16] found similar results in adolescents aged 11–17 years, i.e., 6% of their subjects had two DGBIs and 1% had three DGBIs. Likewise, a study from Greece reported two DGBIs in 3.3% of their subjects (children aged 6–17 years) and three DGBIs in 0.4% of the subjects [17]. More particularly, the overlap between the functional abdominal pain disorders and functional defecation disorders subgroups was the most common in this study, followed by the co-occurrence of functional nausea and vomiting disorders and functional defecation disorders. The Greek study also noted that abdominal migraine and FC overlap was the most frequent among school-aged Greek subjects (1.3%), followed by the overlap between aerophagia/constipation (0.8%) and constipation/rumination (0.1%) (functional nausea and vomiting disorders and functional defecation disorders overlap) and between abdominal migraine/aerophagia (0.3%), IBS/aerophagia (0.%), and abdominal migraine/rumination (0.12%) (functional nausea and vomiting disorders and abdominal pain disorders overlap) [17].

Social and cultural factors play a significant role in influencing the prevalence of DGBIs across different regions. In our study, the prevalence of some DGBIs varied among different provinces. A notable variation in the prevalence of DGBIs was also reported across various countries in the Mediterranean region of Europe [16]. The discrepancy in DGBI prevalence across the Hainan Province regions in China was presumed to be linked to economic development, which includes healthcare system establishment, major airport and harbor access, and exposure to diverse food cultures and lifestyles [18]. The multifactorial nature of DGBIs makes it plausible that these differences result from variations in environmental factors, diet, microbiome, and genetic background among the countries, and even different regions within the same country [16]. A systematic review also discussed how the prevalence of FD differed by country, economic status, geographical region, and sex [19]. Regional dietary patterns and food preferences can vary significantly between cultures and regions; different regions have different food preferences, such as the consumption of spicy, fatty, or high-fiber foods that may trigger DGBI symptoms in some individuals. Regional variations in the types of food consumed, cooking methods, and portion sizes can also pose a risk to the health condition of an area [202122].

This nine-province multicenter study encompassed geographically diverse data to enhance generalizability among Indonesian adolescents. The emphasis on DGBI prevalence during the COVID-19 pandemic highlights a pertinent topic since the pandemic has profoundly affected lifestyle and health. As the most recent guideline for DGBI diagnosis, the application of the Rome IV diagnostic criteria in this study enabled concordance with the latest research and worldwide comparability. On the other hand, the cross-sectional nature of this study restricts its capacity to establish causation between the COVID-19 pandemic and the emergence of DGBIs. Additionally, recollection and sampling bias may have occurred due to self-reporting of symptoms which was used for DGBI diagnosis and both online and offline tools for data collection. Lastly, the study indicated a notable co-occurrence of several DGBIs, but it was unable to examine the underlying processes of these overlaps.

In conclusion, during the COVID-19 pandemic, DGBIs have become highly prevalent among Indonesian adolescents, affecting nearly 30% of this age group, primarily in the form of FC and FD. The prevalence of different DGBIs varied across different provinces, highlighting the role of environmental and cultural factors. Further research is recommended to explore the long-term impact of these conditions and to emphasize public health strategies and interventions for adolescent health.
