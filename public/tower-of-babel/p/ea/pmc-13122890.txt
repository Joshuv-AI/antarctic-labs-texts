Exploring challenges and opportunities in paediatric bereavement care: a qualitative study from a tertiary care hospital in Pakistan
Authors: Hawra Hussain, Sadaf Altaf, Ayesha Khalid, Syed Muhammad Irtiza Hashmi, Naveed Rehman, Vardah Bharuchi, Fatima Rahman, Aamena Shafi, Areeba Syed, Zehra Fadoo, Naureen Mushtaq, Shahzadi Resham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Pakistan, most children with life-threatening illnesses present at advanced stages and succumb to death, leaving families emotionally traumatised. Parental bereavement leads to long-term psychosocial and financial hardships for families, thus highlighting the need for bereavement care as a crucial part of paediatric palliative care. This study explores key challenges around lack of bereavement care in the healthcare infrastructure in Pakistan and identifies opportunities to establish this programme for grieving families.MethodologyThis study presents a secondary qualitative analysis derived from a larger parent study exploring parental experiences following the death of a child. Participants included bereaved parents 2–5 years after their child’s death from oncologic or non-oncologic illnesses at Aga Khan University Hospital, Karachi. A qualitative, phenomenological design was implemented, with semi-structured interviews. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Thematic analysis was conducted using DeDoose software.ResultsNineteen bereaved parents were interviewed, including parents of children with oncological (n = 10) and non-oncological (n = 9) diseases. No clear thematic differences were observed between parents of children with oncologic and non-oncologic conditions. Parents described gaps in communication with healthcare teams, particularly in receiving emotional support, which led to feelings of isolation. Many faced barriers to accessing psychological care, including limited awareness, cultural stigma, and financial constraints. Parents expressed concerns about siblings struggling silently with grief, highlighting the need for bereavement resources and professional psychological support for them as well. Cultural norms around grief expression were also seen as a challenge, hindering emotional processing, and delaying healing within families. Parents also identified a lack of institutional bereavement care practices, such as a condolence message or note and any educational materials.ConclusionBereavement care in Pakistan remains underexplored in the literature. This highlights critical gaps and provides context-specific recommendations for establishing bereavement services based on parental recommendations. Key opportunities for improvement include institutional initiatives for structured bereavement care and addressing societal taboos around grief expression. These changes can foster an environment where bereaved families receive the support they need.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02054-8.

Introduction

The death of a child is one of the most devastating and deeply traumatic experiences a family can face, leaving long-term emotional, psychological, and social effects on parents, siblings, and communities, even impacting parents’ health, morbidity, and mortality [1]. Failure to provide family-centered care after a patient’s death can leave bereaved families feeling abandoned and without access to crucial psychosocial support [2]. Poorer outcomes such as depression and prolonged grief are especially prevalent among parents who face the sudden death of a child, financial difficulties, limited social support and belonging to ethnic minorities [3–5]. Siblings also face adverse psychosocial and socioeconomic impacts without timely support [6]. Providing compassionate, structured bereavement care before and after a child’s death can help reduce these risks and promote healthier coping [7].

In 2023, an estimated 4.8 million children under the age of five died worldwide, with many of these deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) [8]. In Pakistan, the under-5 mortality rate in 2023 was 58.5 deaths per 1,000 live births, reflecting the ongoing challenges in maternal and child health [9]. Globally, leading causes of paediatric deaths include lower respiratory tract infections, infectious diseases like malaria, tuberculosis, and congenital anomalies [8]. While communicable diseases predominate, non-communicable conditions like cancer are contributing to an increasing proportion of paediatric mortality. Globally, childhood cancer accounted for an estimated 11.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2017, with the vast majority (97.3%) attributable to years of life lost [10]. In adolescents, non-communicable diseases are a rising cause of mortality, accounting for up to 1 million deaths a year [11]. Approximately 400,000 children develop childhood cancer each year globally, including more than 7,000 in Pakistan [12, 13]. Five-year survival rates exceed 80% for the 45,000 children with cancer in high-income countries (HICs) but are less than 30% for the 384,000 children in LMICs due to disparities in early detection, access to treatment, and supportive care [14]. As a result, nearly 21 million children require palliative care globally, with 97% belonging to LMICs [15, 16]. Yet, a recent study showed that 65.6% of countries still lack paediatric palliative care services [17]. These disparities highlight the growing need to strengthen paediatric palliative care services in LMIC settings, where children and families often face significant unmet supportive care needs.

Bereavement care is embedded within the World Health Organization’s definition of palliative care, which emphasises support for families during illness and throughout the bereavement period [18]. Each year, the majority of the 20,000 U.S. children who die of disease-related causes do so in the hospital [19]. Although precise data on paediatric in-hospital deaths in Pakistan is unavailable, global trends in hospital-based deaths underscore a clear need for bereavement care services [20]. Several studies from HIC have demonstrated that structured bereavement care programmes, delivered either in person or online, have proven to be beneficial to families after the death of their child [21, 22]. However, grief and bereavement support for parents in LMICs were identified in only nine studies, out of which only one paper described having a fully established grief support programme [23].

In Pakistan, despite high child mortality, with an under-five mortality rate of approximately 58 deaths per 1,000 live births [24], recognition of palliative care including bereavement support remains almost entirely absent. This gap in care highlights how bereavement is overlooked as a public health issue, and emphasises the need for culturally sensitive, evidence-based bereavement care support as an adjunct to palliative care services. This support is critical not only to improve the psychosocial well-being of bereaved families in Pakistan but also to inform the development of contextually appropriate, evidence-based palliative care models in LMICs.

Given the emotional and context-dependent nature of parental grief, a qualitative study allows in-depth understanding of the challenges bereaved parents face and helps identify interventions that can improve outcomes. Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore the key challenges related to lack of bereavement care within the health infrastructure in Pakistan and to identify opportunities to establish such programme for grieving families at the institution and national level.

Methodology

Study design

This qualitative study is reported in accordance with the COREQ guidelines for reporting qualitative research [25]. A phenomenological study design was used to explore the lived experiences of bereaved parents during their child’s treatment, end-of-life, and after their child’s death. In-depth interviews (IDIs) facilitator’s guide was used to conduct semi structured IDIs where bereaved parents shared their experiences of losing a child to cancer. Their insights were used to better understand the challenges families face and to develop recommendations for improving care for children with a poor chance of survival.

Participant selection and data collection

Participants were selected using convenience sampling from hospital records of parents whose children had died 2–5 years prior to the study. This timeframe was selected based on prior literature suggesting that bereavement reflections during this period allow for meaningful narrative recall while reducing acute distress [26].

Eligible parents were contacted via telephone and informed about the study. Of 24 parents approached, 19 consented to participate. Five parents declined participation; no participants withdrew after consent.

Interviews were conducted either in person or telephonically based on participant preference and logistical considerations. In-person interviews were conducted in a separate unit in a non-clinical area at the Aga Khan University Hospital Karachi. No non-participants were present during interviews. Telephonic interviews were conducted in a private setting to ensure confidentiality. Interviews were conducted between August and October 2024 and lasted approximately 45 to 60 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and subsequently transcribed verbatim. Field notes were maintained during and after interviews to capture contextual observations and interviewer reflections. Repeat interviews were not conducted. Transcripts were not returned to participants for review.

Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved within the broader parent study, which included bereavement-related experiences, as determined through concurrent transcription and analysis using DeDoose software. The quality of the transcripts was verified by independently checking a random subsample of audio recordings against their transcripts, while the accuracy of translations was assessed through independent back-translations. All identifiers were redacted from transcripts, which were completed in Urdu, to ensure anonymity and confidentiality.

Parents were provided with complete information about the study and informed of their right to refusal at any stage. The information about the study was communicated both verbally and in writing when informed consent was obtained.

Secondary analysis and interview guide

Data for this study was derived from semi-structured IDIs conducted as part of a broader qualitative research project. A facilitator’s interview guide was used to conduct the interviews; this guide was adapted for the parent study, informed by an interview guide previously developed and used at St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital to explore bereaved parents’ experiences following the death of a child with cancer [27]. The interview guide covered multiple thematic domains relevant to the parent study. The original parent study explored broader parental experiences throughout the trajectory of their child’s illness, including the period around the time of death. Ethical approval for the parent study included permission for secondary analyses of de-identified data for related research questions. Participants provided informed consent for the use of their interview data in future research addressing psychosocial aspects of paediatric palliative care. The parent manuscript is currently under preparation and focused on care priorities in paediatric palliative care, whereas the current analysis specifically examines bereavement care gaps and recommendations. Findings related to bereavement care are not reported in the parent manuscript, which focuses on broader paediatric palliative care priorities. The guide was reviewed by the project team to ensure cultural appropriateness and relevance to the local context. The final guide was developed in English, translated into Urdu, and administered by trained bilingual interviewers. An English-language version of the interview guide used for the parent study is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis and rigour

Descriptive quantitative information (e.g., residence, respondent type, and mode of interview) was extracted from participant demographic forms and interview records. Counts and percentages were calculated to summarise sample characteristics and are presented for descriptive purposes only, without inferential statistical analysis.

For qualitative data, a thematic analysis was performed following the approach described by Braun and Clarke [28], with themes derived directly from the data rather than pre-determined categories. Three researchers (AK, AS, SMIH) independently coded transcripts through an iterative process, to ensure data familiarization. The codebook was reviewed by the team during weekly debriefing meetings to achieve consensus. Patterns identified within the coded data were then synthesised into overarching themes. All data management and analysis were performed using DeDoose (Version 9.0.46). This study was approved by the ethical research committee of Aga Khan University Hospital, ERC#2023-8417-26040. De-identified participant quotations are presented in the Results to illustrate key themes. Care was taken to ensure consistency between participant narratives and thematic interpretations. Major themes are clearly presented; minor themes were not separately categorised within the bereavement analysis, as patterns converged around primary thematic domains.

Interviews were conducted by authors FR, AS and AS, who were working as research assistants within the institution. All interviewers were female and bilingual, trained in conducting interviews in both English and Urdu. The interviewers received formal training in sensitive communication and qualitative interviewing techniques under the supervision of a formally trained Paediatric Palliative Care (PPC) Physician. Emphasis was placed on trauma-informed interviewing and the use of culturally sensitive language.

No prior relationship was established between interviewers and participants before study commencement. Participants were introduced to the interviewers as research assistants affiliated with the study. Given the emotionally sensitive nature of bereavement research, reflexive discussions were conducted within the research team throughout data collection and analysis to minimise potential bias. The team remained aware that their institutional affiliation could influence participant responses and maintained a neutral, empathetic approach, allowing participants to guide discussions freely.

Trustworthiness of the study was established in accordance with Lincoln and Guba’s framework. Credibility was strengthened through iterative coding and prolonged engagement with the data; dependability through regular team discussions to refine emerging interpretations; confirmability through reflexive dialogue and verbatim quotations; and transferability through detailed contextual descriptions, enabling readers to assess the relevance of the findings to similar clinical and cultural contexts.

Theme 1: the need for psychosocial and emotional support

Parents emphasised that while physicians are skilled in managing symptoms and guiding treatment, they often lack the time to address parents’ emotional needs in depth.

“If a psychiatrist or psychologist could hold sessions for parents to talk, it would help. The primary team simply doesn’t have time to sit with each parent.” (Bereaved Parent Non-Oncology).

“If a psychiatrist or psychologist could hold sessions for parents to talk, it would help. The primary team simply doesn’t have time to sit with each parent.” (Bereaved Parent Non-Oncology).

Parents also commented on the need for clear counselling regarding their child’s illness and prognosis to help streamline treatment approach and to mentally prepare the parents for a poor outcome.

“There should be a counselling team that talks to parents daily, asks how they’re feeling, and gives them someone to open up to. Such support would ease their hearts and make a big difference.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“There should be a counselling team that talks to parents daily, asks how they’re feeling, and gives them someone to open up to. Such support would ease their hearts and make a big difference.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

Memory-making emerged as a critical component of the bereavement process for parents, who expressed a strong desire to have tangible moments to hold on to after their child’s passing. If hospitals were to formally recognise the importance of this process and develop structured frameworks for memory making, it may help foster emotional resilience and acceptance in parents as they navigate their grief after the child’s passing.

“Outside of Pakistan, families are encouraged to focus on making memories, not just treatment. Psychosocial teams ask children about their wishes…I have no memories with my child and felt alienated seeing others share theirs. Even small moments matter later….it would mean a lot.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“Outside of Pakistan, families are encouraged to focus on making memories, not just treatment. Psychosocial teams ask children about their wishes…I have no memories with my child and felt alienated seeing others share theirs. Even small moments matter later….it would mean a lot.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

Theme 2: parents supporting parents

Parents shared how deeply healing it can be to connect with other bereaved families, the only ones who can relate with the weight of such loss.

“You can’t connect with regular people in the same way. You can’t share your grief with them, because normal people get bored of hearing about it…people tell you to forget about it, to move on. But no one understands that you can never really move on from this” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“You can’t connect with regular people in the same way. You can’t share your grief with them, because normal people get bored of hearing about it…people tell you to forget about it, to move on. But no one understands that you can never really move on from this” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

Parents shared that connecting with the parents who have been through the same journey offers not only comfort and understanding but also practical guidance on managing grief and an improved quality of life.

“But now we have activities, we go for outings, and friends were a big support. Then I started going for therapy, and slowly things started to happen, and we also brought friends (bereaved families) into a team, like our social group with all our friends.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“But now we have activities, we go for outings, and friends were a big support. Then I started going for therapy, and slowly things started to happen, and we also brought friends (bereaved families) into a team, like our social group with all our friends.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

Many parents spoke with hope and determination about giving back, wanting to support such groups so their own grief could be transformed into a source of strength and care for others walking the same path, especially those lacking support from their own families.

“I would love to do it! I would be ready to dedicate my whole life to it. I feel like, if I’ve done something for someone else’s child, it would truly feel meaningful to me.” (Bereaved Parent, Non-Oncology)

Theme 3: supporting siblings through their grief

Siblings suffer majorly as a result of the broader family impact of a child’s serious illness. They often get emotionally neglected while parents focus on the sick child.

“Then I also thought, what about my other child? If one is gone, they also need time, right? They’re still here. They used to say, ‘Mama, even if they aren’t here, you still have me.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“Then I also thought, what about my other child? If one is gone, they also need time, right? They’re still here. They used to say, ‘Mama, even if they aren’t here, you still have me.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“After my child passed away, the first six to eight months were terrible—we couldn’t process anything or function properly, even when it came to our other child’s schooling.” (Bereaved Parent, Non-Oncology).

“After my child passed away, the first six to eight months were terrible—we couldn’t process anything or function properly, even when it came to our other child’s schooling.” (Bereaved Parent, Non-Oncology).

While having an alternative caregiver was invaluable, siblings craved the presence of a parent.

“Because of our joint family, she had her grandparents and father while I stayed at the hospital. They cared for her and met all her needs, but she still longed for her mother.” (Bereaved Parent, Non-Oncology).

“Because of our joint family, she had her grandparents and father while I stayed at the hospital. They cared for her and met all her needs, but she still longed for her mother.” (Bereaved Parent, Non-Oncology).

Some recommendations for improved sibling support include activities in wards that siblings can also participate in and age-appropriate grief counselling.

“During treatment outside of Pakistan, there was sibling support. They explained how to care for the patient and maintain distance. Such guidance is important, especially for younger siblings, who also need counselling and clarity.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“During treatment outside of Pakistan, there was sibling support. They explained how to care for the patient and maintain distance. Such guidance is important, especially for younger siblings, who also need counselling and clarity.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“Simple gestures would have meant a lot, like inviting the siblings to hospital events or giving small tokens, this could have made them feel included and remembered.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

Theme 4: institutional bereavement support

Institutional support in the bereavement phase was a missing part of holistic care. Parents believed that even simple gestures such as providing reading material, offering a condolence call or message, or arranging counselling could have helped them cope and move forward. Many shared that small acts of remembrance would have offered comfort and a sense of closure.

“Such losses can never be completely compensated, but if you make a courtesy call or share someone’s grief, it gives them a little bit of relief.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

“Such losses can never be completely compensated, but if you make a courtesy call or share someone’s grief, it gives them a little bit of relief.” (Bereaved Parent, Oncology).

Discussion

Our study explores the opportunities and challenges within paediatric bereavement care at a tertiary care hospital in Pakistan, through the lived experiences of bereaved parents. To our knowledge, there is limited qualitative research from Pakistan exploring parental bereavement in tertiary care settings. This study contributes narrative-driven insights into this delicate topic, highlighting profound gaps in emotional, social and institutional care, contributing to a growing understanding of bereavement care needs in LMIC settings. These findings show how bereavement distress may be shaped not only by emotional loss but also by structural limitations and sociocultural barriers.

The lack of dedicated psychological support emerged as a significant gap in paediatric bereavement care. Our findings highlighted major gaps within the healthcare system in Pakistan, including the absence of a formal bereavement programme. This lack of support led to parental difficulty in understanding their child’s disease trajectory, navigating difficult decisions, and preparing for possible loss. Similar to other LMIC studies, clinicians were perceived as compassionate and clinically competent, but limited time and the lack of dedicated psychosocial staff left families feeling unsupported at critical moments [29]. The presence of institutional support, in the form of providing anticipatory guidance, condolence calls, bereavement resources would have addressed parental grief both during and after the passing of a child. Additionally, unlike high-income countries [30], absence of such services along with societal taboos, financial stressors, and lack of awareness, further increases parents’ struggle with grief. Spousal resistance to therapy mirrored findings from prior studies reflecting patriarchal influences and culturally linked stigma that discourage South Asian women from seeking help, particularly when mental illness is perceived as a source of familial shame [31]. Amid these cultural and systemic barriers, parents also expressed a longing for supportive, non-clinical practices such as memory-making, which align with evidence that such rituals ease grief and promote long-term healing [32]. Without them, families mourned not only their children, but also the memories they never had. Many of these supportive practices, particularly anticipatory guidance on grief and bereavement, condolence calls and memory-making activities, may be integrated into routine care without requiring extensive additional resources, making them apt for Pakistan’s resource-limited context.

To cope with isolation, interacting with other bereaved families emerged as a vital source of healing, providing comfort and validation through connection with others who shared similar experiences, an observation consistent with qualitative research demonstrating that peer relationships offer meaningful emotional support to bereaved parents navigating grief [33]. Parents expressing a desire to give back by helping others navigate this difficult time could contribute to the development of parent-to-parent bereavement support groups, similar to those established in high-income countries [34]. In LMICs, where limited attention is given to mental health, there is significant potential for such parent-run bereavement support groups. They represent an accessible, affordable and culturally meaningful strategy to complement professional services, given their ability to offer empathy and shared understanding [35].

Siblings emerged as the “invisible grievers,” a finding consistent with existing literature [36]. Often perceived as too young to understand illness or death, siblings may feel confused and unsupported in processing loss, despite experiencing profound emotional impact [37]. Parents in our study also struggled to remain emotionally available for their surviving children, further heightening this vulnerability. These findings highlight the importance of integrating age-appropriate counselling, activities in the hospital that include siblings, and emotional support into bereavement care. In settings where paediatric palliative resources are limited, even simple guidance to parents, such as involving siblings in care, small celebrations or memory-making rituals, may foster meaningful connection and mitigate long-term psychosocial consequences [38]. These findings emphasise that bereavement support should extend beyond parents to the broader family unit, recognizing siblings as integral participants in the grief process rather than peripheral observers.

In the absence of locally established bereavement care models, evidence from HICs provides valuable insight into how structured psychosocial support can shape families’ experiences after a child’s death. Studies from Australia and other HICs highlight themes similar to ours, such as challenges in grief processing and feelings of isolation [39, 40]. While the emotional dimensions of loss appear universal, the extent of formalised support differs across settings. In HICs such as the USA, UK, and Australia, bereavement services are often standardised and embedded within paediatric care [30]. LMICs remain in the early stages of developing such structures, with only small-scale or pilot initiatives emerging [23]. Our study found a lack of such institutional bereavement frameworks, including limited access to psychological counselling, absence of structured memory-making or memorial activities, and minimal focus on siblings. These findings reflect broader trends across other LMICs, where limited funding, workforce shortages, and inadequate training in paediatric palliative care further constrain these efforts [16, 23]. These contrasts underscore the need for contextually grounded, culturally sensitive and cost-effective bereavement frameworks that can feasibly integrate psychosocial support into existing LMIC healthcare systems, rather than replicating resource-intensive models from high-income settings.

Building on these findings, several contextually grounded implications for bereavement care emerged from parents’ narratives within this tertiary care setting. Parents consistently emphasised the importance of clear, compassionate communication during these challenging times. In resource-limited environments, rather than relying solely on specialist staff, strengthening the skills of existing multidisciplinary teams through simple, structured guidance tools, such as an end-of-life care checklist that includes prompts for effective communication with parents and age-appropriate engagement with siblings, may offer a practical and sustainable approach. Memory making initiatives, including providing hand or fingerprint keepsakes, facilitating photographs using family-owned devices, and sending written messages of condolence through hospital hotlines, may further support emotional closure. Regarding institutional practices, simple yet thoughtful gestures such as culturally appropriate informational brochures, standardised condolence calls, or mailing remembrance cards may be perceived by parents as meaningful expressions of continued support.

Establishing bereaved parent-run support groups in safe and confidential spaces also emerged as a potentially sustainable strategy, particularly in environments where professional psychological services remain limited due to awareness, availability and financial accessibility. This suggestion reflects the expressed willingness of several parents in our study to engage with peer-based support; however, we acknowledge that cultural norms surrounding grief expression may influence its broader acceptability of bereavement services. Therefore, further research across multiple institutions nationwide is warranted to assess their acceptability and patterns of utilization in different sociocultural settings.

Many of these recommendations leverage existing clinical relationships and low-cost interventions, offering realistic entry points for service development. While comprehensive bereavement programmes may represent a long-term goal, these incremental strategies offer immediately implementable opportunities for improvement. Formal recognition of these practices within institutional policy could standardise care without requiring substantial financial investment.

Along with its strengths, this study has several limitations as well, that should be considered when interpreting the findings. It was conducted at a single tertiary care hospital, where the perspectives captured may not fully reflect bereavement experiences in rural or resource-limited regions of Pakistan, thereby limiting transferability. Additionally, recall bias may have influenced parents’ reflections; however, careful consideration of the bereavement timeframe (2–5 years) was intended to balance emotional sensitivity with meaningful narrative recall. Future research across multiple healthcare settings, including rural and public-sector institutions, is needed to further explore the diversity of bereavement experiences. Investigating the feasibility and acceptability of low-cost bereavement interventions, such as peer-support models, structured follow-up practices, and health care provider communication training may also help inform context-specific service development within LMIC settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights critical gaps in bereavement support in Pakistan by bringing forward the voices of parents who have lost a child. Their recommendations highlight the need for emotional and practical support not only during illness, but long after their child has died. As paediatric palliative care continues to grow in LMICs, bereavement support must be recognised not only as a core element of holistic care but also as a public health necessity. Establishing a structured bereavement programme with scalable, culturally sensitive psychosocial interventions can be a meaningful first step toward improving the well-being of families.
