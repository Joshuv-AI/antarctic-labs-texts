Multi-hazard performance assessment of reinforced concrete frame structures subjected to earthquakes and wind loads
Authors: Tong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reinforced concrete (RC) frame structures are subjected to both earthquakes and wind during their service life. However, existing studies rarely address low‑rise RC frames under combined wind–seismic excitations. To fill this gap, we analyze a six‑story RC frame under 100 randomly combined scenarios (10 ground motions × 10 wind speeds) using nonlinear time‑history analysis, comparing uncontrolled and viscous‑damper‑retrofitted configurations. Results show that combined excitation substantially amplifies displacement compared to isolated loads. Under a PGA of 0.159 g and a 16 m/s wind, the top‑story displacement increases by 9.8% relative to the earthquake alone and by 364% relative to wind alone. The proposed damper scheme reduces structural response across all scenarios (9.5–26.9% reduction) and lowers the exceedance probability at the severe damage limit state by up to 42.4% points. After retrofitting, the structure’s sensitivity to seismic intensity decreases by 27.4%. These findings demonstrate that single‑hazard assessments underestimate actual risks for low‑rise RC frames in windy regions. The proposed damping strategy offers a practical pathway to enhance multi‑hazard resilience.

Introduction

Due to the wide applicability of reinforced concrete (RC) frame structures1, they inevitably face the individual or combined actions of earthquakes and strong winds during their service life. Particularly when wind and seismic loads are simultaneously applied to an RC frame structure, it is highly likely to experience more severe damage accumulation or even collapse compared to a single hazard2. This necessitates quantitative assessment of such structures.

Regarding the performance of RC frames under seismic actions, existing research has formed a progressively deepening research lineage, from local components to the overall structure, and from numerical simulation to experimental validation. Scott et al.3 addressed the issue of non‑objective response of force‑based beam‑column elements under strain‑softening conditions by proposing a plastic hinge integration method, which enables the definition of plastic rotations and their relationship with deformations, thereby supporting the refinement of element models for subsequent global structural analysis. Nie et al.4 analyzed various factors affecting the accurate calculation of flexural capacity of beams and the beam‑to‑column flexural strength ratio, identified problems in design practice and corresponding retrofitting techniques, thus providing guidance for the rational utilization of plastic hinge mechanisms. Furthermore, to quantitatively evaluate the safety margin of RC frames under different ground motions, Merter et al.5 proposed an assessment method based on plastic energy comparison from the perspective of energy balance. This method showed that the structural displacement derived from the mean plastic energy demand is reasonably close to the results obtained from various seismic design codes, offering a complementary energy‑based criterion for seismic safety assessment. Ghezeljeh et al.6 employed incremental dynamic analysis to compare the seismic performance and collapse resistance of steel‑beam‑RC‑column composite frames, all‑steel frames, and all‑RC frames at different heights, finding that the composite frame exhibited more stable inter‑story drift distribution under high displacement demands. In addition to numerical simulations, physical experimental studies on RC frame structures have provided valuable theoretical support. Arastu7 conducted a shake‑table test on a 1/4‑scale RC frame, obtaining key engineering parameters such as natural period, damping ratio, floor accelerations, and failure modes. The test revealed that beam‑column joints are the primary failure locations, providing experimental evidence for the calibration of numerical models. Existing research has uncovered the nonlinear response characteristics, plastic energy dissipation mechanisms, and the validity of seismic design criteria for RC frames under earthquake loading8. However, during their service life, structures are not only subjected to earthquakes but also long‑term wind loads. RC frames mainly rely on the bending stiffness and flexural capacity of beams and columns to provide lateral stiffness, and their lateral load‑resisting capacity is relatively weak, which limits their application. Jiang et al.9 focused on the reliability of RC columns under wind‑dominated combined loads, investigating the bearing capacity reliability of biaxially eccentrically compressed columns. The results indicated that the traditional design criterion based on a fixed eccentricity fails to capture the effects of random biaxial eccentricity, providing a more refined reliability basis for component design under wind‑dominated loads. Kandaala10 analyzed the capacity of low‑rise RC frames to resist lateral forces such as wind loads, and proposed methods to improve wind‑induced lateral resistance through indicators such as elastic buckling and column stiffness. On this basis, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the overall stress state of RC frames under wind loads, Jia et al.11 established a finite element model of an RC frame and performed numerical simulations under combined dead, live, and wind loads, finding that stresses and deformations at the bottom of the structure are significantly larger than those in other parts. There are relatively abundant studies on RC frames under wind or seismic actions individually; however, regrettably, these studies have overlooked the coupling amplification effect on structural damage evolution when multiple loads coexist during the service life12.

With the development of research on multi‑hazard resistance of engineering structures13, scholars have gradually recognized that strong earthquakes often occur simultaneously with non‑negligible wind loads. Numerous studies have been conducted on the performance of RC structures under combined wind‑seismic actions from various perspectives. In the field of tall buildings, Huang et al.14 proposed a multi‑objective serviceability design optimization method combining optimality criteria with the non‑dominated sorting genetic algorithm (NSGA‑II), and introduced a neural‑network‑based self‑updating surrogate model to predict structural natural frequencies, successfully applying it to the optimal design of a 50‑story frame‑core tube building. Zheng et al.15 developed a reliability‑based method for calculating the load modification factor for tall buildings under combined wind‑seismic actions. Using a 42‑story steel frame‑RC core tube building as an example, they considered both individual and combined effects of earthquakes and strong winds, finding that current codes that treat the two hazards separately may underestimate failure probabilities. Venanzi et al.16 established a life‑cycle cost analysis framework for tall buildings under wind and seismic loads, pointing out that although one hazard is often considered to govern design, both hazards can be equally important when decisions are based on life‑cycle cost. To further quantify uncertainties, Zheng et al.17 proposed a hybrid AI‑Bayesian methodology for fragility estimation of tall buildings. They used a back‑propagation neural network to replace finite element analysis to reduce computational time, and employed Bayesian statistics to quantify epistemic uncertainty, highlighting the necessity of considering both types of uncertainty in multi‑hazard fragility assessment. Addressing the incoherence between seismic and wind design provisions in codes, Athanasiou et al.18 proposed a multi‑hazard design method introducing a wind‑related reduction factor that allows limited controlled inelasticity under high‑intensity winds, and validated the method using two 15‑story steel braced frame buildings. It is worth noting that the vast majority of the above‑mentioned studies on combined actions focus on tall or super‑tall buildings, because wind loads have a more pronounced effect on tall buildings. For low‑rise RC frames, traditional thinking holds that the magnitudes of wind and seismic loads are quite different, and the coupling effect can be ignored. However, a few recent studies have begun to investigate the performance of low‑rise RC frames under combined wind‑seismic environments. Joyner et al.19 assessed the multi‑hazard resilience of a 7‑story RC frame designed according to current codes. Using nonlinear time‑history analysis and a novel modal‑analysis‑based method, they evaluated structural deformation, non‑structural component damage, and repair costs, identifying irreparable permanent drift as an important factor affecting resilience, and also discussed the necessity of impact‑resistant design for glazing. Miao et al.20 investigated the influence of dominant wind direction on the seismic performance of RC frames, finding that as service time increases, the collapse mode of the structure may shift from column failure to beam failure. Dhawan et al.21 proposed a method for estimating the remaining service life of RC structures considering wind and seismic loads, and carried out a parametric study using a 10‑story RC frame, showing that concrete strength, residual reinforcement area, etc., significantly affect the expected remaining life. These studies indicate that low‑rise RC frames also face non‑negligible risks under combined wind‑seismic actions. Low‑rise frames inherently possess weak lateral stiffness and low redundancy, and the persistent action of wind loads may aggravate seismic damage and even alter failure modes. Nevertheless, systematic performance assessment of low‑rise RC frames under combined wind‑seismic actions is still lacking, and there is an even greater scarcity of targeted performance enhancement studies.

To enhance the multi‑hazard resilience of RC frames, researchers have explored various damping/vibration reduction techniques. Özkaynak22 arranged steel cushion energy dissipation elements in an RC frame, demonstrating through quasi‑static tests and nonlinear time‑history analyses that the steel cushions can dissipate most of the seismic input energy. Li et al.23 investigated the response and fragility of a steel‑concrete composite frame equipped with buckling‑restrained braces under combined wind‑seismic actions, finding that both the response and failure probability under combined actions were reduced due to the effect of buckling restraint. Barrios et al.24 studied the control effectiveness of tuned mass dampers (TMDs) in RC chimneys considering material nonlinearity and soil‑structure interaction, proving that TMDs can effectively reduce structural responses under various loading scenarios. Furthermore, viscous dampers, as effective passive control devices, have been widely used in seismic applications of RC structures. Experimental studies have shown that adding viscous dampers to RC frames can significantly enhance lateral resistance, initial stiffness, and energy dissipation capacity25. Some studies have also proposed design methods for viscous dampers that are convenient for practicing engineers, estimating response indicators of unretrofitted buildings through nonlinear response history analysis and then calculating damper characteristics, thus providing a practical approach for seismic retrofitting of existing RC frames26. For multi‑hazard scenarios, Chen et al.27 conducted multi‑objective optimization of viscous damper parameters for high‑rise conjoined structures using response surface methodology combined with the NSGA‑II algorithm, achieving reductions in structural response under rare earthquakes and multi‑hazard damage risk. However, while the above studies are valuable, the seismic mitigation measures for RC structures are either targeted at tall buildings or focused on the seismic performance assessment of low‑rise structures in isolation. Quantitative evaluation of the damping effectiveness of viscous dampers for low‑rise RC frames under combined wind‑seismic actions is still lacking.

To fill these gaps, this study conducts a comprehensive multi‑hazard performance assessment of a six‑story reinforced concrete frame structure subjected to combined earthquake and wind loads. Unlike previous studies that focus primarily on tall buildings or single‑hazard scenarios, this work specifically targets low‑rise RC frames—a structural type with inherently weak lateral stiffness and high vulnerability to damage accumulation under combined loads. To this end, we systematically evaluate both uncontrolled and viscous‑damper‑retrofitted configurations under 100 randomly combined wind‑earthquake scenarios, quantitatively demonstrate that single‑hazard assessments significantly underestimate failure risks for low‑rise frames, and finally provide design‑oriented benchmarks along with a cost‑effective damping strategy for multi‑hazard resilience enhancement in windy regions.

Methodologically, this study proposes a fragility‑based28 assessment framework that integrates time‑history analysis, log‑linear regression demand modeling and fragility surfaces, while explicitly accounting for uncertainties from loads, materials and models. The sequential loading strategy is adopted to simulate realistic concurrent hazard conditions. With these elements established, the remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the multi‑hazard performance assessment methodology, including the fragility analysis framework. Section 3 presents the finite element model of the prototype RC frame, material properties, and the viscous damper configuration. Section 4 discusses the dynamic response results under combined wind‑seismic loads. Section 5 conducts the multi‑hazard performance assessment through fragility surfaces. Section 6 summarizes the main conclusions.

Methodology

Method overview

This study adopts a fragility-based multi-hazard performance assessment framework for reinforced concrete frame structures subjected to combined earthquake and wind loads. The overall procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, a detailed finite element model of the prototype RC frame is developed, incorporating material and geometric uncertainties. Viscous dampers are then installed in a staggered configuration, with their parameters determined based on structural dynamic characteristics. Second, to address load uncertainties, natural ground motion records are selected from a strong-motion database according to site conditions, and wind speed time histories are generated considering mean wind speeds and fluctuating wind spectra. The two load types are randomly paired to form multiple combined loading scenarios, which are applied to both the uncontrolled and damper-controlled structures using a sequential loading strategy. Third, the maximum inter-story drift ratio (MIDR) is extracted from the nonlinear time-history analyses as the engineering demand parameter (EDP). Finally, a log-linear regression demand model is established to relate the EDP to the hazard intensities, and fragility surfaces are constructed for four limit states, quantifying the exceedance probabilities29 under different wind–earthquake combinations. The framework explicitly accounts for uncertainties from loads, materials, geometry, and the demand model.

Multi-hazard performance assessment process.

Development of finite element analysis model

The prototype structure is a six-story RC frame. Floor heights, bay spans, slab thickness, and beam/column cross-sections are defined according to typical engineering practice. The reinforcement design follows the “strong column, weak beam” principle to ensure plastic hinge formation at beam ends before column ends. A three-dimensional finite element model is established to represent the structural behavior. Concrete and reinforcement are modeled using appropriate element types, with the reinforcement embedded into the concrete assuming perfect bond. The constitutive models for concrete account for compressive and tensile behavior, including tension stiffening and post-peak softening. Steel reinforcement is modeled using an elastic‑plastic constitutive law. Rayleigh damping is defined based on the first two natural frequencies with an assumed damping ratio. To quantify uncertainties, key parameters—such as concrete strength, steel yield strength, and cross-sectional dimensions—are treated as random variables. Their probability distributions are defined based on design code recommendations and typical coefficients of variation from the literature. Sampling methods are used to generate parameter combinations, ensuring that material and geometric uncertainties are properly propagated into the analysis. Viscous dampers are installed in the structure. Their locations are selected to provide effective energy dissipation while maintaining constructability. The damping force–velocity relationship follows a power law, with the velocity exponent and damping coefficient determined based on structural dynamic properties. The P-Delta effect is considered for all columns.

Loading and dynamic response analysis

Ground motion records are selected according to the target seismic design intensity and site classification. A design response spectrum from the relevant code is used as the target. Selection criteria include magnitude, epicentral distance, shear wave velocity, and spectral matching within a period range of interest. The raw peak ground acceleration (PGA) values of selected records are used without scaling to preserve the non‑stationary characteristics of real seismic waves.

Pulsating wind speed time histories are generated using the harmonic superposition method. The base wind speed is defined as the 10‑minute average wind speed at a standard reference height. A surface roughness category is assumed, with an average wind speed profile exponent and turbulence intensity derived from the relevant wind load code. Multiple wind speed levels are generated, ranging from service-level winds to design-level winds, with appropriate increments to enable continuous fragility surface construction. Each wind speed time history has a sufficiently long duration, with a smooth ramp-up at the beginning to mitigate initial transients. The generated wind series are validated by comparing their power spectral density with the target spectrum.

Combined scenarios and sequential analysis: A total of combined loading scenarios are generated by randomly pairing the selected earthquake motions with the wind speed levels. The sequential loading protocol is as follows: gravitational loads are first applied statically and maintained throughout; then wind loads are applied over an initial duration; finally, seismic acceleration time histories are superimposed from a certain time point to the end of each earthquake record. The entire duration of each earthquake record is embedded within the wind loading period, so that the structure experiences wind before, during, and after the earthquake. This design captures both pre‑earthquake and post‑earthquake wind effects, leading to a more realistic assessment of combined hazards. Sensitivity to loading order can be checked through additional analyses.

Nonlinear dynamic time‑history analyses are performed using an explicit solution scheme with a fixed time increment small enough to ensure computational stability. Global energy balance is monitored to verify solution reliability. The maximum inter‑story drift ratio (MIDR) is extracted as the engineering demand parameter (EDP) for each analysis, as it is a well‑established indicator of structural damage under lateral loads.

Development of fragility surfaces

The maximum inter‑story drift ratio is adopted as the EDP. Four displacement‑sensitive limit states are defined based on MIDR, corresponding to increasing damage levels. The threshold values for these limit states are taken from relevant design codes. To account for uncertainty in the structural capacity model, a logarithmic standard deviation is adopted, with its value typically assumed based on literature recommendations.

Based on the results of the nonlinear time‑history analyses under multiple combined scenarios, a binary log‑linear regression model30 is established with PGA and wind speed as the independent hazard intensity measures and ln(MIDR) as the dependent variable:

Finite element analysis model

Prototype structure

A six-story RC frame structure serves as the subject of investigation. The ground floor exhibits a floor-to-floor height of 4.5 m, while subsequent stories are 3.6 m. The structural plan comprises four bays, each 6 m in length, along the X-direction, and three bays along the Y-direction, with the central bay spanning 3 m and the two side bays each spanning 6 m. The floor slab thickness is established at 120 mm, and the concrete strength class (C30) is uniform across beams and columns. The structural foundation is modeled as rigidly fixed, with fixed constraints imposed at all column base joints. A typical story floor plan is depicted in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Building floor plan.

Building floor plan.

Within this frame model, structural columns, transverse beams, and longitudinal beams are all characterized by a rectangular cross-section of 400 mm × 400 mm. Stirrups are fabricated from HRB335 steel bars with a 8 mm diameter, while longitudinal reinforcement utilizes HRB400 steel bars with a 25 mm diameter. To preclude column hinge failure, the reinforcement design adheres to the ‘strong column, weak beam’ principle, achieved by augmenting the design moment capacity at column ends to ensure plastic hinge formation at beam ends prior to column ends. Detailed reinforcement configurations for beam and column cross-sections are illustrated in Fig. 3. The concrete strength class is uniformly set at C30, with its constitutive behavior characterized by the Concrete Damage Plasticity (CDP) model within ABAQUS32. Material parameters are derived from GB 50,010 − 201033: elastic modulus of 30GPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.2, an angle of expansion of 30°, eccentricity of 0.1, a biaxial to uniaxial compressive strength ratio of 1.16, and a tensile yield stress-fracture energy relationship defined in accordance with the standard. Reinforcing steel is modeled using an idealized elastoplastic bilinear constitutive model (HRB335 with a yield strength of 335 MPa, HRB400 with a yield strength of 400 MPa), featuring an elastic modulus of 200GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3.

Fig. 3Cross-sections of beams and columns.

Cross-sections of beams and columns.

The finite element model was developed utilizing the ABAQUS platform. Reinforcement cages for each structural member (beams, slabs, columns) were integrated to form a unified reinforcement framework and meshed independently (Fig. 4a). Concrete is discretized using eight‑node hexahedral linear reduced‑integration solid elements (C3D8R), each node possessing three translational degrees of freedom, resulting in a total of 24 degrees of freedom per element. The reinforcement cage is modeled using two‑node linear three‑dimensional truss elements (T3D2), each node also having three translational degrees of freedom (Fig. 4b). Through the “Embedded region” constraint, the reinforcement elements are embedded into the concrete solid elements, assuming perfect bond without slip. For the material constitutive models, the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) model is adopted for concrete. The compressive behavior follows the stress-strain curve recommended by standard33, while the tensile behavior is governed by the fracture energy criterion. Steel reinforcement is modeled using an elastic‑perfectly plastic bilinear model with an elastic modulus of 200 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3. During the static application of gravity loads, rate effects are not considered in the material models. In the subsequent dynamic time‑history analyses under wind and earthquake loading, the CDP model accounts for strain rate effects by default. Because the ABAQUS/Explicit solver is employed, no implicit iterative convergence criteria are involved in the analysis. Instead, global energy balance serves as the primary stability criterion: the ratio of artificial strain energy to total internal energy is required to remain below 5% throughout the analysis. A fixed time increment of 0.005 s is adopted to ensure computational stability.Fig. 4Meshing diagram.

Load conditions

Selection of earthquake motions

In accordance with the Code for Seismic Design of Buildings (GB 50011 − 2010)33, a seismic intensity of 8 degrees (design basic seismic acceleration 0.2 g) and a site category of Class II (characteristic period Tg = 0.35 s) were adopted. Employing the code-specified design response spectrum as the target, an initial set of 30 seismic records was selected from the PEER strong-motion database35 and FEMA P69536 recommended records using a ‘dual-criterion screening method’. An additional criterion stipulated that the spectral acceleration deviation between the average of selected records and the target spectrum within the period range of 0.2T₁ to 1.5T₁ (T₁ = 0.902 s, i.e., 0.18 to 1.35 s) must be less than 15%. Ultimately, ten natural ground motion records were chosen, characterized by magnitudes ranging from M6.0 to 7.0 and epicentral distances between 15 and 50 km. To investigate the synergistic effects of varying earthquake intensities and wind loads, the selected ground motion records were utilized with their raw peak ground acceleration (PGA) values to preserve the non-stationary characteristics of authentic seismic waves. The selection of ten ground motion records, while modest in number, is justified by the following considerations. First, the primary objective of this study is to demonstrate the multi‑hazard assessment framework and to evaluate the relative effectiveness of viscous dampers under combined wind‑seismic loads, rather than to derive precise fragility parameters for a specific structure. Ten records, covering a PGA range from 0.159 g to 0.753 g and carefully matched to the target spectrum (spectral deviation < 15% over 0.2T₁ to 1.5T₁), are considered sufficient to capture the trend of seismic demand and its interaction with wind loads. Second, each record is paired with ten wind speed levels, resulting in 100 combined nonlinear time‑history analyses. Increasing the number of records to, say, 20 would double the computational effort to 200 analyses. Given that the qualitative conclusions—wind amplification of seismic response and damper effectiveness—remain stable with the current set, the computational cost is deemed acceptable without sacrificing technical rigor. Third, similar or even smaller record sets have been used in peer‑reviewed multi‑hazard studies focusing on low‑rise RC frames19,37. Nevertheless, the authors acknowledge this limitation in the concluding section and plan to validate the results with a larger suite of records in future work.

Wind load simulation

Pulsating wind speed time series were generated via the harmonic superposition method. The base wind speed was defined as the 10-minute average wind speed at a 10 m height. Considering Class B surface roughness (urban fringe), an average wind speed profile exponent α = 0.15 was applied, and turbulence intensity was derived from the relevant code. A total of 10 wind speed levels were generated, ranging from 12 m/s to 30 m/s in increments of 2 m/s. The wind speed of 12 m/s corresponds approximately to a return period of one year, while the wind speed of 30 m/s corresponds approximately to a return period of 50 years. This range covers from service‑level winds to design‑level winds, enabling investigation of structural performance under both ordinary and extreme wind conditions. The intermediate wind speeds capture the transition between these two extremes, allowing systematic analysis of the combined effects of wind and seismic loads. Moreover, an examination of the influence of varying seismic intensities reveals a substantial increase in structural displacement with escalating PGA (E1, E4, E6). For instance, at a wind speed of 16 m/s, uncontrolled displacement increased from 31.89 mm (E1) to 60.59 mm (E6), representing an approximate 90% rise. Conversely, the increment in displacement due to changes in wind speed under the same earthquake intensity was less than 15%. This observation underscores that the dynamic response of reinforced concrete frame structures is primarily governed by seismic loads and exhibits greater sensitivity to seismic intensity. Several lines of evidence support this conclusion. First, as shown in Table 3 for the uncontrolled structure, increasing the earthquake intensity from PGA = 0.159 g to 0.28 g under a constant wind speed of 16 m/s causes the maximum top-story displacement to rise from 31.89 mm to 60.59 mm—an increase of approximately 90%. In contrast, increasing the wind speed from 16 m/s to 24 m/s under the same earthquake (E1) raises the displacement from 31.89 mm to only 36.15 mm, a modest increase of about 13%. Second, the ratio of displacement increments due to seismic intensity variation to that due to wind speed variation exceeds 6:1 across all examined load combinations. To rule out the influence of other factors (e.g., loading sequence, P-Delta effects, damping), we performed a sensitivity check: under pure seismic loading (without wind), the displacement response follows a similar trend with respect to PGA; the addition of wind only modestly amplifies the response without altering the dominance of seismic effects. Furthermore, the fundamental mechanics of low‑rise RC frames—in which the lateral inertial force from earthquake scales with mass and PGA, while wind pressure scales with the square of wind speed—explain why even moderate PGA values (0.15–0.30 g) produce lateral forces far exceeding those from design‑level winds (up to 30 m/s). Therefore, the higher sensitivity to seismic intensity is physically inherent, not an artifact of the analysis setup.

Therefore, a single‑hazard assessment underestimates the actual structural risk. Viscous dampers demonstrate efficacy in vibration reduction within multi-hazard environments, though their damping ratio is contingent upon the load combination. Consequently, further performance-based multi-hazard assessments are requisite to quantitatively delineate the damage states and resilience levels of the structure under diverse load combinations.

Multi-hazard performance assessment

This section quantifies the performance of RC frame structures subjected to concurrent seismic and wind loading utilizing a fragility analysis framework. Initially, an engineering demand model is formulated with the maximum inter-story displacement angle (MIDR) serving as the engineering demand parameter (EDP). A logarithmic linear regression model is employed to establish the relationship between MIDR and hazard intensity, specifically PGA and wind speed. Subsequently, four displacement-sensitive limit states (LS) are delineated. Accounting for uncertainties in structural capacity, a multi-hazard fragility surface is developed to characterize the probability of the structure exceeding each LS under various load combinations.

Engineering demand analysis

The selection of optimal intensity measures (IMs) is a critical step in developing reliable fragility curves, as different IMs exhibit varying efficiency, sufficiency, and practicality in predicting seismic demand. As the study38, five IMs—including PGA, PGV, Sa(T₁), Sa(0.20), and Sa(1.0)—were systematically compared to identify the best IM for fragility analysis of bridges. While we fully acknowledge the value of such an optimal IM selection process, the present study adopts PGA as the seismic intensity measure for the following considerations. First, PGA is the most fundamental and widely used intensity measure in the code33, and maintaining consistency with code-based design facilitates engineering interpretation and practical application of the results. Second, this study focuses on a multi‑hazard framework combining earthquake and wind loads, requiring a unified two‑dimensional intensity space (PGA and wind speed). PGA offers a simple, physically intuitive metric that pairs naturally with mean wind speed, whereas other IMs (e.g., Sa(T₁)) depend on structural period and damping, making their coupling with wind speed less straightforward.

The maximum inter-story displacement angle (MIDR) of the structure is adopted as the EDP. MIDR serves as a critical metric for assessing the non-linear response and damage severity of a structure and is widely incorporated in national and international seismic design codes (e.g., GB 50011 − 201033, FEMA 35636. Employing the results from 100 non-linear time-history analyses of combined wind-earthquake scenarios (10 seismic motions × 10 wind speed levels) detailed in Sect. 3, a binary log-linear regression was conducted with PGA and wind speed as independent variables and MIDR as the dependent variable. This procedure yielded the fitting parameters for the engineering demand models of both the controlled and uncontrolled structures, as presented in Table 4.

Analysis of the fitting parameters in Table 4 reveals that for both the uncontrolled and controlled structures, the fitting coefficient for PGA (b) substantially exceeds that for wind speed (c). This observation indicates that MIDR exhibits significantly higher sensitivity to seismic intensity compared to wind speed, aligning with the conclusion in Sect. 3 that ‘structural response is primarily controlled by seismic loads. Specifically, for the uncontrolled structure, b is 2.3162, implying that a twofold increase in PGA leads to an approximate 2.3162-fold increase in MIDR (based on the logarithmic-linear relationship), whereas the wind speed coefficient c is only 0.0692, suggesting a relatively limited influence of wind speed on MIDR. Post-installation of viscous dampers, b decreased from 2.3162 to 1.682, a reduction of approximately 27.4%, demonstrating the dampers’ efficacy in mitigating the structure’s sensitivity to seismic intensity. Concurrently, c increased from 0.0692 to 0.297. This shift does not indicate an amplification of the wind-induced response by the dampers; rather, it reflects the diminished dominance of seismic forces within the logarithmic regression model, leading to a statistically more pronounced relative contribution of wind loads, even though the absolute values of actual displacements remained lower than those of the undamped structure. Furthermore, the logarithmic standard deviation ε for the controlled structure is lower than that for the uncontrolled structure, signifying that the damper not only reduces response amplitude but also decreases response variability, thereby enhancing structural performance stability under multi-hazard uncertainty. The R² values for both models surpass 0.90, confirming the selected logarithmic linear regression model’s efficacy in explaining the correlation between MIDR, PGA, and wind speed. Fragility analysis

Four displacement-sensitive limit states (LS) are defined based on MIDR, corresponding to distinct damage levels33: (1) Minor Damage (DS1): MIDR > 0.4%; (2) Moderate Damage (DS2): MIDR > 0.8%; (3) Severe Damage (DS3): MIDR > 2.5%; (4) Collapse (DS4): MIDR > 5.0%. These thresholds are informed by relevant domestic and international research and codes, applicable to RC frame structures where lateral deformation is the primary control criterion. To account for uncertainty in the structural capacity model, a coefficient of variation of 0.3 is adopted. By integrating the engineering demand model (Table 4) and the limit state thresholds into the fragility function, multi-hazard fragility surfaces for different damage states are generated, as illustrated in Figs. 10 and 11.

The fragility surfaces presented in Figs. 10 and 11 enable a quantitative assessment of the probability of a structure exceeding various limit states under differing hazard intensity combinations. The analysis results indicate, firstly, a monotonic increase in the probability of structural failure with augmenting seismic ground motion intensity or wind speed. This implies that even a small increase in PGA or wind speed can disproportionately elevate the failure risk—a critical insight for engineering decision-making near design thresholds. Secondly, the implemented viscous damper arrangement scheme significantly curtails the probability of structural failure under multi-hazard conditions, thereby validating its seismic mitigation effectiveness and providing a valuable reference for subsequent engineering applications.

Considering the severe damage limit state as an example, for an uncontrolled structure subjected to a fixed wind speed of 6 m/s, PGAs of 0.2 g, 0.3 g, and 0.4 g result in exceedance probabilities of severe damage of 55.41%, 98.73%, and 99.99%, respectively. This highlights that even a marginal increase in seismic intensity causes a precipitous rise in failure probability. For practitioners, this suggests that designing a low-rise RC frame in a windy region with a PGA value lying on the steep portion of the fragility curve involves high sensitivity; a small earthquake exceedance may lead to a much higher damage probability than anticipated. Therefore, either increasing the design PGA by a modest margin or adding dampers could flatten this curve and provide a robust safety buffer. Following the implementation of viscous dampers, these exceedance probabilities decreased to 13.02%, 76.89%, and 98.48% under identical conditions, corresponding to reductions of 42.39, 21.84, and 1.51% points. This indicates that the dampers are particularly efficacious in mitigating seismic response during low- and moderate-intensity earthquakes. From an economic perspective, this implies that retrofitting with viscous dampers is most cost‑effective for buildings in regions where moderate earthquakes (e.g., PGA 0.2–0.3 g) are expected, as the risk reduction is substantial. Under extreme seismic events, the dampers still provide a noticeable safety margin, though the marginal benefit diminishes because the structure is already near collapse. However, during extreme seismic events, as the structural response approaches saturation, the damping effect becomes comparatively limited, albeit still providing a discernible safety margin.

Utilizing the moderate damage limit state and a fixed PGA of 0.2 g, wind speeds of 12 m/s, 18 m/s, and 24 m/s yield exceedance probabilities of 99.79%, 99.83%, and 99.85% for the uncontrolled structure, approaching complete failure with negligible influence from wind speed variations. This near‑certain failure under any wind speed reveals that once the structure is seismically damaged to a moderate level, wind loads—even low ones—cannot be ignored as they may act as an aggravating factor, but the failure probability is already dominated by seismic damage. In contrast, the controlled structure exhibits corresponding exceedance probabilities of 31.09%, 43.03%, and 52.17%, with the dampers reducing the failure probability by 68.70, 56.80, and 47.68% points, respectively. This demonstrates that under wind-dominant scenarios, the dampers also play a pivotal role in vibration mitigation, effectively preventing the structure from reaching the moderate damage state. For structural engineers, this suggests that even in regions where earthquake hazard is relatively low but wind loads are significant (e.g., typhoon‑prone zones), installing viscous dampers can prevent the accumulation of damage that would otherwise leave the structure vulnerable to subsequent moderate earthquakes. In other words, the dampers act as a dual‑hazard protection device.

In summation, the synergistic action of seismic and wind loads significantly amplifies the risk of structural damage. An escalation in the intensity of either hazard escalates the probability of failure, thereby necessitating control measures. The proposed staggered arrangement of viscous dampers exhibits superior vibration mitigation performance across diverse load combinations, effectively diminishing the probability of structural failure in multi-hazard environments. For code developers, these results imply that current separate checks for wind and earthquake may underestimate the actual failure risk; a multi‑hazard combination factor could be introduced for low‑rise RC frames in windy regions. For practicing engineers, the staggered damper configuration—placed only in the outermost central span—minimizes architectural interference while delivering substantial risk reduction, offering a practical and cost‑effective retrofit solution. This methodology offers a valuable reference for the multi-hazard-resistant design and retrofitting of comparable RC frame structures.

Conclusions

This study investigated a six-story RC frame structure. Nonlinear time-history analyses were performed on uncontrolled and controlled (viscous damper) structures within a multi-hazard environment, considering 10 natural ground motions and 10 wind speed levels in random combinations. Engineering demand models and vulnerability surfaces were established based on maximum inter-story displacement angle, and the failure probability across four limit states was systematically assessed. The primary conclusions are delineated as follows:

An increase in seismic motion intensity (PGA) or wind speed leads to a monotonic escalation in both structural response and failure probability. Despite these findings, several limitations remain. First, this study relies entirely on finite element numerical simulations without empirical validation from experiments or field measurements. Second, the fragility analysis only accounts for the coefficient of variation of structural capacity, while neglecting the spatial variability of material properties and the randomness of loads. Third, the conclusions are specific to a six‑story reinforced concrete frame and a single damper configuration. Fourth, while the use of ten natural ground motion records is adequate for demonstrating the proposed framework and the relative trends, the statistical robustness of the fragility surfaces could be further improved by employing a larger set of records. Therefore, parametric investigations are needed across different numbers of stories, spans, seismic design intensities, and site conditions, along with broader suites of ground motions and wind speeds, to establish the generalizability of the assessment results and their applicability in engineering practice. Future work should also expand the ground motion suite to quantify the associated dispersion more precisely.
