Global, regional, and national burden and trends of headache disorders among children and adolescent (5–24 years old) from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Hua Pei, Jiao Su, Jingjing Li, Yuanhao Liang, Xiaofeng He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeadache disorders are a significant public health concern among children and adolescents, contributing to substantial morbidity. This study assesses the global, regional, and national burden of headache disorders, including migraine and tension-type headache (TTH), from 1990 to 2021 using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021.MethodsWe analyzed the incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLDs) of headache disorders in individuals aged 5–24 years across 204 countries and territories. Age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized YLD rate (ASYR) were evaluated. Trends over time were quantified using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). Additionally, the relationship between the burden of headache disorders and the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) was assessed.ResultsGlobally, the number of incident cases of headache disorders increased from 235.9 million in 1990 to 290.0 million in 2021. The ASIR increased marginally, with an EAPC of 0.04% (95% CI: 0.04–0.05). In 2021, migraine accounted for 45.5 million cases (ASIR: 1,767.15 per 100,000), while TTH contributed 244.5 million cases (ASIR: 9,495.77 per 100,000). The highest ASIR of migraine was observed in Tropical Latin America, whereas High-income North America had the highest ASIR of TTH. The global prevalence of headache disorders reached 811.7 million in 2021, with a 23.72% increase since 1990. The ASPR of migraine and TTH increased at an EAPC of 0.09% and 0.05%, respectively. The global YLDs due to headache disorders increased from 10.3 million in 1990 to 12.8 million in 2021, with an overall ASYR increase (EAPC = 0.09%). High-SDI regions bore the highest burden, while middle-SDI regions exhibited the most significant increase over time. A nonlinear relationship between migraine burden and SDI was observed.ConclusionsHeadache disorders remain a substantial burden among children and adolescents, with a persistent increase in their global incidence, prevalence, and YLDs. Regional and national disparities highlight the need for targeted prevention and management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04367-2.Keywords: Global burden of disease, Headache disorders, Migraine, Tension-type headache, Epidemiology, Children and adolescents

Introduction

Headache disorders are among the most prevalent neurological conditions worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on individuals and healthcare systems [1]. Children and adolescents (aged 5–24 years) are particularly vulnerable to the disabling effects of headaches, which can significantly impact cognitive development, academic performance, and social well-being [2–4]. Despite their high prevalence, headaches in younger populations are frequently underrecognized, misdiagnosed, or undertreated, leading to persistent health consequences that extend into adulthood [5]. Given the critical developmental stage of childhood and adolescence, understanding the burden of headache disorders across different regions is essential for developing targeted public health interventions [6, 7].

Numerous epidemiological studies have examined the prevalence and burden of headache disorders, particularly migraines and tension-type headaches [8–10]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study consistently ranks headache disorders as leading causes of years lived with disability (YLDs) [11]. However, most studies focus on adult populations, with limited comprehensive assessments of headaches among children and adolescents [10]. Although some research highlights potential risk factors (such as genetic predisposition, lifestyle influences, and environmental exposures), few studies have systematically analyzed regional variations in headache burden over time [10, 12]. Understanding these disparities is crucial, as socioeconomic development, healthcare accessibility, and cultural perceptions of headaches vary widely across geographic regions [13].

Significant disparities exist in the burden of headache disorders across different socio-demographic regions [10, 14]. High-income regions, such as North America and Western Europe, often report a higher prevalence of migraines, likely due to better diagnostic awareness and healthcare access. However, these regions also benefit from greater availability of specialized headache care and treatment options. In contrast, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face a disproportionately high burden of headache-related disability due to limited healthcare resources, lack of headache specialists, and inadequate access to effective medications. In many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, headaches remain underdiagnosed and poorly managed, exacerbating their long-term impact on education and workforce productivity [15, 16]. Furthermore, regional variations in healthcare infrastructure, diet, stress levels, and environmental exposures contribute to differences in headache burden, underscoring the need for context-specific interventions [17].

Despite global efforts to reduce the burden of neurological disorders, progress toward mitigating headache-related disability among children and adolescents remains insufficient [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasized the need for improved headache care, but gaps persist in diagnostic capacity, treatment accessibility, and awareness campaigns, particularly in underserved regions [18]. The disparity between current epidemiological trends and the global goal of reducing headache-related disability highlights the urgent need for high-quality, region-specific data to inform health policies and intervention strategies.

To address these gaps, our study provides a comprehensive assessment of the global, regional, and national burden of headache disorders among children and adolescents (aged 5–24 years) from 1990 to 2021, utilizing data from the GBD 2021 [19]. The GBD framework enables standardized comparisons across regions and socio-demographic indices, allowing us to identify trends, disparities, and key determinants of headache burden over time. By leveraging this extensive dataset, our study offers critical insights into the evolving epidemiology of headache disorders among children and adolescents. These findings can inform evidence-based resource allocation and policy development, ultimately contributing to more effective strategies for reducing the global burden of headaches in young populations.

Methods

Overview of the GBD Study

The GBD Study, led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), is a comprehensive research initiative that provides updated estimates of morbidity, mortality, and disability for hundreds of diseases and injuries across countries and regions [20]. By integrating data from multiple sources—including national health surveys, hospital records, vital statistics, and scientific literature—the GBD ensures globally comparable health estimates [20]. Since its inception in 1991, the study has continuously evolved over the past three decades, refining its methodologies and expanding its data coverage to offer a more precise and comprehensive assessment of global health trends [21]. Each new GBD iteration incorporates improvements in data sources, estimation techniques, and computational frameworks, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of the entire time series. The estimates presented in our study are derived from GBD 2021, the most recent and advanced version of the GBD Study [19, 21].

Data source

The GBD 2021 estimates the burden of headache disorders, including migraine and tension-type headache (TTH), using DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool that synthesizes data from multiple sources while adjusting for inconsistencies and biases [19]. Correction factors are applied to address underreporting and variations in diagnostic criteria across different studies. Detailed information on the disease model for headache disorders is available in the GBD 2021 methods appendices (https://www.healthdata.org/gbd/methods-appendices-2021/headaches).

The GBD 2021 does not distinguish between migraine with and without aura, as most epidemiological studies report only on overall migraine prevalence [19]. The reference diagnostic criteria for migraine are derived from the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [22]. According to ICHD-3, migraine is a primary headache disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of moderate to severe headache, typically unilateral, pulsating in nature, and aggravated by routine physical activity. It is often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and/or sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia).

Similarly, the diagnostic criteria for TTH are also based on the ICHD-3 [22]. TTH is the most common primary headache disorder, characterized by bilateral, pressing, or tightening pain of mild to moderate intensity, without the pulsating quality typical of migraine. Unlike migraine, TTH is not aggravated by routine physical activity and is usually not associated with nausea, although mild photophobia or phonophobia may be present.

In terms of classification, migraine is coded under G43-G43.919 in the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) and under 346–346.93 in the 9th revision (ICD-9) [19]. TTH is classified under G44.2-G44.229, G44.4-G44.41 in ICD-10 and under 307.81, 339.1–339.12, 339.3 in ICD-9 [19].

Data collection

In this study, we extracted the annual incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLDs)—both numbers and rates—for headache disorders among children and adolescents (5–24 years old) across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 using the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) results tool [19]. Adolescence, the transitional phase between childhood and adulthood, has long been difficult to define. While traditionally defined as ages 10–19, Sawyer et al. have proposed extending this range to 10–24 years to better reflect contemporary patterns of adolescent development and social role transitions [23]. To facilitate age-specific analyses, individuals were categorized into four age groups at 5-year intervals: 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, and 20–24 years. The GBD 2021 classifies 204 countries and territories into 21 regions based on epidemiological similarities and geographic proximity (Supplementary Table S1) [19].

To evaluate the impact of socioeconomic factors on the headache burden, we utilized the Socio-demographic Index (SDI), a composite measure of national social development. The SDI is calculated as the geometric mean of three standardized indicators [19]: (1) Total fertility rate among women under 25 years; (2) Mean years of schooling for individuals aged 15 and older; and (3) Lag-distributed income per capita. Based on SDI, the 204 countries and territories were grouped into five quintiles: Low SDI, Low-middle SDI, Middle SDI, High-middle SDI, and High SDI (Supplementary Table S2) [19].

To further assess the relationship between the burden of headache disorders and SDI levels, we employed frontier analysis to construct an ASR-based frontier model using the SDI. Unlike traditional regression models that describe relationships or predict outcomes, frontier analysis accounts for the non-linear relationship between SDI and headache burden, capturing the multidimensional drivers of disease burden [25]. This method determines the theoretically lowest ASR that each country or territory could achieve based on its current level of development, serving as a benchmark for optimal performance. The gap between a country’s observed ASR and its frontier ASR quantifies the improvement potential. To model this, we applied LOESS combined with local polynomial regression, using smoothing spans to generate smooth frontier lines and capture the nonlinear relationship between SDI and ASR. To ensure robustness, we performed 1000 bootstrap samples and calculated the average ASR for each SDI value. The absolute difference between each country’s 2021 ASR and the frontier line (i.e., effective difference) was used to assess improvement potential.

This model integrates age, period, and cohort effects to estimate posterior probability distributions, combining prior knowledge with observed data for more reliable projections [27]. Results

Incidence estimates of headache disorders

Globally, the number of incident cases of headache disorders among children and adolescents increased from 235.9 million in 1990 to 290.0 million in 2021. Over this period, the global age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) rose from 11,105.32 (95% UI: 11,103.9–11,106.73) per 100,000 population in 1990 to 11,262.92 (95% UI: 11,261.63–11,264.22) in 2021, reflecting an EAPC of 0.04% (95% CI: 0.04–0.05) (Table 1). In 2021, TTH accounted for 244.5 million cases, with an ASIR of 9,495.77 (95% UI: 9,494.58–9,496.96), while migraine contributed 45.5 million cases, corresponding to an ASIR of 1,767.15 (95% UI: 1,766.64–1,767.67). From 1990 to 2021, the ASIR of migraine and TTH increased globally at an EAPC of 0.05% (95% CI: 0.04–0.06) and 0.04% (95% CI: 0.04–0.05), respectively (Table 1). From 2022 to 2040, the ASIR of both migraine and TTH shows a slight decline over the projection period for both sexes, with a marginally faster decrease observed in females compared to males (Supplementary Figure S1).

Prevalence estimates of headache disorders

In 2021, an estimated 811.7 million children and adolescents worldwide were living with headache disorders, reflecting a 23.72% increase since 1990 (Table 1). The global age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of headache disorders reached 31,525.26 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 31,523.1–31,527.43) in 2021, demonstrating a steady upward trend over the past three decades (EAPC = 0.06% [95% CI: 0.05–0.06]) (Table 1). Among headache subtypes, migraine accounted for 321.5 million cases in 2021, with an ASPR of 12,487.79 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 12,486.42–12,489.15), while TTH contributed 582.3 million cases, corresponding to an ASPR of 22,615.52 (95% UI: 22,613.68–22,617.35). Between 1990 and 2021, the global ASPR of migraine and TTH increased at an EAPC of 0.09% (95% CI: 0.08–0.1) and 0.05% (95% CI: 0.04–0.05), respectively (Table 1).

At the regional level, the highest ASPRs of migraine and TTH in 2021, as well as the greatest increases in ASPR over the study period, were observed in the same regions as those with the highest ASPRs reported earlier (Supplementary Table S3 and S4). At the national level, Brazil (21,979.85 [95% UI: 21,968.48–21,991.22]) and Paraguay (20,992.54 [95% UI: 20,937.9–21,047.28]) exhibited the highest ASPRs of migraine in 2021 (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table S5). In contrast, the highest ASPR of TTH was recorded in Norway (31,931.86 [95% UI: 31,836.09–32,027.85]) and the USA (30,832.38 [95% UI: 30,820.75–30,844.02]) (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S6). Regarding temporal trends, Norway showed the fastest increase in the ASPR of migraine (EAPC = 1.00% [95% CI: 0.8–1.2]), while China experienced the most significant rise in the ASPR of TTH (EAPC = 0.31% [95% CI: 0.25–0.38]) (Fig. 1D and 2D).

The YLDs burden of headache disorders

Globally, the YLDs due to headache disorders increased from 10.3 million in 1990 to 12.8 million in 2021. Over the same period, the global age-standardized YLD rate (ASYR) also exhibited a steady rise, increasing from 483.67 (95% UI: 483.37–483.96) per 100,000 population in 1990 to 495.68 (95% UI: 495.41–495.96) per 100,000 population in 2021, with an average annual percentage change (EAPC) of 0.09% (95% CI: 0.09–0.1) (Table 1). Among headache disorders, migraine contributed the highest YLD burden in 2021, accounting for 11.8 million YLDs, with an ASYR of 458.47 (95% UI: 458.21–458.73) per 100,000 population. In contrast, TTH contributed 0.96 million YLDs, corresponding to an ASYR of 37.21 (95% UI: 37.14–37.29) (Table 1). Between 1990 and 2021, the ASYR of migraine and TTH increased globally at an EAPC of 0.1% (95% CI: 0.09–0.11) and 0.04% (95% CI: 0.04–0.05), respectively (Table 1).

At the regional level, in 2021, Tropical Latin America (802.93 [95% UI: 800.8–805.06]) and Western Europe (592.93 [95% UI: 591.41–594.46]) reported the highest ASYR of migraine per 100,000 population (Supplementary Table S3). Meanwhile, the highest ASYR of TTH was observed in Eastern Europe (56.68 [95% UI: 55.98–57.39]) and Central Europe (49.1 [95% UI: 48.23 to 49.98]) (Supplementary Table S4). Over the study period, Tropical Latin America exhibited the most significant increase in the ASYR of migraine (EAPC = 0.33% [95% CI: 0.20–0.46]), while East Asia experienced the greatest rise in the ASYR of TTH (EAPC = 0.18% [95% CI: 0.14–0.22]).

At the national level, in 2021, Brazil (804.16 [95% UI: 801.99–806.34]) and Paraguay (772.29 [95% UI: 761.84–782.85]) recorded the highest ASYR of migraine, while Russia (57.62 [95% UI: 56.79–58.47]) and Ukraine (55.06 [95% UI: 53.52–56.63]) reported the highest ASYR of TTH (Figs. 1E and 2E, Supplementary Table S5 and S6). Between 1990 and 2021, Norway exhibited the fastest increase in the ASYR of migraine (EAPC = 0.99% [95% CI: 0.79–1.18]), whereas Iran showed the most significant rise in the ASYR of TTH (EAPC = 0.21% [95% CI: 0.16–0.26]) (Figs. 1F and 2F).

Age- and sex-related burden of headache disorders

In 2021, the global and regional ASIR, ASPR, and ASYR of headache disorders were consistently higher in males than in females, particularly for migraine (Supplementary Figure S2A–S2C and S3A–S3C). Notably, from 1990 to 2021, the ASIR, ASPR, and ASYR of migraine increased significantly faster in males than in females (Supplementary Figure S2D–S2F). Similarly, the ASIR and ASPR of TTH showed a significantly faster increase in males than in females during the same period (Supplementary Figure S3D–S3E). However, the ASYR of TTH increased at a significantly faster rate in females than in males (Supplementary Figure S3F).

Among children and adolescents, the incidence rate of migraine peaked at ages 10–14 years in 2021 across the globe and across all five SDI quintiles. In contrast, the incidence rate of TTH increased gradually with age (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the prevalence and YLD rates of both migraine and TTH showed a steady increase with age (Fig. 5B–C). While the incidence and prevalence rates of TTH were consistently higher than those of migraine across all age groups, the YLD rate of migraine remained higher than that of TTH (Fig. 5). From 1990 to 2021, the most rapid increase in the burden of TTH was observed among individuals aged 20–24 years, particularly in middle-SDI regions. In contrast, the burden of migraine showed the fastest increase among children aged 5–9 years, particularly in high-middle-SDI regions (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global, regional, and national burden of headache disorders among children and adolescents (5–24 years old) from 1990 to 2021. Our findings reveal a persistent increase in the incidence, prevalence, and YLDs due to headache disorders worldwide. The global number of incident cases rose from 235.9 million in 1990 to 290.0 million in 2021, while the prevalence reached 811.7 million cases in 2021. Despite only modest EAPCs in the ASIR, ASPR, and ASYR, the absolute burden has grown substantially over the past three decades, underscoring a sustained global health challenge. TTH remained the most common form, while migraine contributed disproportionately to disability. Significant regional disparities emerged, with high-income regions such as North America and Western Europe showing higher ASIRs and ASPRs—likely reflecting better diagnostic awareness and healthcare access. Meanwhile, the fastest-growing headache burdens were observed in Tropical Latin America and East Asia, indicating shifting epidemiological trends. At the national level, Brazil and Norway recorded the highest burdens of migraine and TTH, respectively, while China exhibited the greatest increase in TTH burden over time.

Our findings align with previous studies documenting a rising headache burden among adolescents, potentially driven by lifestyle changes, increased screen time, academic stress, and sleep disturbances [8, 30]. Notably, we extend prior work by including younger children (5–9 years), revealing an emerging acceleration in migraine cases in high-middle SDI regions—a trend not previously emphasized in adult populations. This may reflect earlier exposure to risk factors such as screen overuse and disrupted sleep in urban settings [9]. Furthermore, the frequent underdiagnosis and undertreatment of childhood headaches likely contribute to the sustained burden into adulthood [5, 10, 31], as longitudinal studies have shown an increased risk of chronic headaches among those with childhood-onset migraines [32]. Although our data demonstrate significant regional disparities in headache burden, the underlying mechanisms (such as genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and lifestyle factors) remain speculative and correlational rather than definitively causal. These associations are supported by prior literature but lack confirmation through longitudinal or mechanistic studies. To overcome this limitation and strengthen the attribution of headache burden to specific risk factors, we advocate for the development and application of diverse analytical frameworks to expand and validate our findings. Despite these constraints, the identified regional patterns underscore key priorities for targeted public health interventions.

Regionally, our study confirmed significant disparities in headache burden, consistent with existing literature on the socio-demographic and healthcare influences on headache epidemiology [14, 33]. High-income regions demonstrated higher prevalence rates, likely due to greater diagnostic capacity and healthcare access [34, 35]. However, these regions have simultaneously achieved more effective management strategies, reducing disability despite high prevalence. In contrast, low- and middle-SDI regions faced disproportionately high headache-related disability, exacerbated by limited healthcare resources, a shortage of headache specialists, and restricted access to treatments [36]. Particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, underdiagnosis has amplified the long-term educational and economic consequences of headaches [37]. Moreover, middle-SDI regions experienced the largest rise in headache burden between 1990 and 2021, likely reflecting the impacts of rapid urbanization, economic transition, and changing lifestyles [8].

We also identified a nonlinear relationship between SDI and migraine burden: it initially increased with SDI, declined around an SDI of 0.55, then rose again at SDI levels above 0.7. This pattern echoes previous findings that early-stage economic development increases headache prevalence—due to occupational stress, environmental changes, and diet—while later-stage development improves healthcare access, temporarily reducing burden [38]. However, in high-SDI countries, a resurgence in migraine burden may be linked to sedentary lifestyles, work stress, and analgesic overuse [38, 39]. Similarly, TTH burden showed a positive correlation with SDI, suggesting that occupational stress and screen-related lifestyles increasingly contribute to headache prevalence as development advances [34, 35]. Improved healthcare access and awareness may also account for higher reported prevalence in high-SDI regions compared to underdiagnosed low-SDI areas [36].

At the regional level, the concentration and rapid growth of migraine burden in Tropical Latin America may stem from a combination of genetic predisposition, climate variability, and lifestyle factors. Genetic traits may increase susceptibility [40], while environmental triggers such as high humidity and temperature fluctuations [41], alongside dietary habits rich in processed foods, alcohol, and caffeine [42], exacerbate migraine risk. In High-income North America, the highest TTH burden likely reflects chronic stress [43], high digital device use, and sedentary lifestyles, with psychological factors such as anxiety and depression playing a contributory role [44, 45]. Meanwhile, the sharp rise in East Asia’s TTH incidence—surpassing trends in adults—can be attributed to rapid socioeconomic change, academic pressure, and increasing digital exposure among adolescents [46]. Additionally, Norway's steep increase in migraine burden aligns with previous reports linking climate conditions, genetic factors, and widespread medication use to elevated migraine rates in Scandinavian countries [39, 47].

Sex differences were also evident. While TTH burden was slightly higher in females, migraine burden was significantly higher in females than males, consistent with previous meta-analyses [5]. This sex disparity is primarily attributed to hormonal influences, particularly estrogen fluctuations [48, 49]. Interestingly, from 1990 to 2021, the migraine burden rose more rapidly in males than females, diverging from traditional patterns and potentially reflecting increased recognition of migraine in males, evolving lifestyles, and rising occupational stress [50]. Similarly, although the ASIR and ASPR of TTH grew faster in males, the ASYR of TTH rose more steeply in females, suggesting that while headache frequency is increasing more rapidly among males, the associated disability remains a growing concern for females, possibly due to differences in pain perception, healthcare utilization, and chronicity of headaches [51, 52].

Moreover, our age-specific analysis revealed that global migraine incidence peaked among individuals aged 10–14 years in 2021, consistent with prior studies linking adolescence to migraine onset through hormonal changes, academic stress, and increased screen use [53]. Neurodevelopmental factors, including puberty-related cortical excitability and serotonin dysregulation, are implicated in this trend [54, 55]. In contrast, TTH incidence showed a gradual increase with age rather than a peak in childhood, suggesting cumulative lifestyle influences such as poor posture, prolonged screen exposure, and occupational stress over time.

To translate our findings into practical action, we propose targeted recommendations by SDI level. In high-SDI regions (e.g., High-income North America, Western Europe), where migraine and TTH burdens remain high, we suggest integrating headache-awareness modules into school curricula to promote early recognition, healthy habits, and stress management. Digital tools, such as telehealth apps offering posture correction and guided relaxation, can further support prevention, especially for TTH linked to screen overuse. In middle-SDI settings (e.g., Tropical Latin America, East Asia), public health campaigns should address common triggers (such as diet, climate, and stress) while strengthening primary care through triage training, standardized protocols, and affordable medication access. Our frontier analysis highlights Brazil and Paraguay as priority areas for such interventions. In low-SDI regions (e.g., Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia), training community health workers in headache recognition and basic care, along with school outreach and improved access to essential medicines, could address gaps caused by specialist shortages. Across all contexts, we recommend annual school-based screening for early detection, particularly as migraine peaks at ages 10–14 and TTH increases with age. Finally, sex-specific education is needed: encouraging boys to report symptoms and equipping girls with strategies to manage hormonally influenced disability.

Our study benefits from standardized GBD 2021 data, allowing for robust global, regional, and national trend analysis. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, reliance on modeled estimates may obscure subnational disparities, particularly in data-sparse low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Additionally, the lack of granular data on modifiable risk factors (e.g., screen time, dietary habits) limits causal inference. Future research should integrate mixed-methods approaches, combining GBD data with longitudinal cohort studies to better elucidate sex- and age-specific trends. Identifying modifiable risk factors, such as lifestyle interventions, stress management, and dietary modifications, is crucial for mitigating headache burden in children and adolescents. Furthermore, expanding healthcare professional training, increasing medication accessibility, and implementing school-based headache education programs should be prioritized globally, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the rising burden of headache disorders among children and adolescents worldwide, with significant regional disparities in incidence, prevalence, and disability. The findings call for targeted public health policies, improved headache awareness, and strengthened healthcare systems to mitigate the impact of headaches on young populations.
