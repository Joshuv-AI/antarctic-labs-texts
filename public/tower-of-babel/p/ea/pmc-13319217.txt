A scoping review on the essential role of global neurotrauma in achieving the sustainable development goals
Authors: Laura M. Loaiza-Cardona, Kee B. Park, Franco Servadei, Tariq Khan, Sara Venturini, Niklas Marklund, Peter Hutchinson, Russell Andrews, Andrés M. Rubiano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeurotrauma represents a significant global health burden with profound socioeconomic and public health implications. Beyond its clinical dimension, neurotrauma intersects with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), influencing outcomes related to health, equity, infrastructure, and innovation.ObjectiveThis scoping review aimed to map and categorize existing evidence on the relationship between neurotrauma and the SDGs following PRISMA-ScR guidelines, and to identify areas of mutual reinforcement and unmet needs.MethodsWe conducted structured database searches in five electronic databases, supplemented by manual screening and a review of grey literature. Eighty-nine studies published between 2015 and 2025 were analyzed and grouped into six domains associated with the SDGs: neurotrauma care and outcomes, coverage and infrastructure, training and education, innovation and technology, international collaboration and assistance and neurotrauma prevention.ResultsThe review suggests that neurotrauma care and prevention may contribute to the advancement of multiple SDGs, notably through improved patient outcomes (Goal 3), enhanced access to emergency systems (Goals 9 and 11), technological innovation (Goals 5 and 9), and the strengthening of international partnerships (Goal 17). Persistent disparities in access, research capacity, and data infrastructure were identified, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.ConclusionThis review maps evidence suggesting that strengthening global neurotrauma systems may support equitable progress toward the SDGs. Most identified SDG–neurotrauma linkages represent system-level inferences rather than directly measured causal effects. Future efforts should focus on equity-driven strategies, scaling collaborative models, and integrating neurotrauma indicators into global development frameworks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12982-026-02433-9.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI), spinal cord injury (SCI), and other forms of neurotrauma remain among the leading causes of death and long-term disability globally, with estimates indicating that approximately 50 to 60 million people experience neurotrauma each year, resulting in a profound public health and economic burden that exceeds $400 billion annually [1]. The epidemiology of neurotrauma reveals stark global disparities, further compounded by distinct demographic patterns. In high-income countries (HICs), a rapidly aging population has led to a tremendous rise in the incidence of neurotrauma among the elderly, where low-energy falls are the predominant mechanism [2]. In contrast, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), road traffic incidents disproportionately affect a younger, working-age population, where limited infrastructure and resources magnify the burden, long-term consequences, and societal economic impact of these injuries [1]. Variations in emergency preparedness and rehabilitative services across regions further exacerbate disparities in incidence, outcomes, and access to care.

In 2015, the United Nations General Assembly ratified the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) adopted by all United Nations Member States, a comprehensive agenda of 17 targets to guide international efforts toward equity, health, and socio-economic advancement by 2030 [3]. At least ten SDGs are directly or indirectly linked to neurological health and neurosurgical care. Among these, SDG 3 (“Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages”) stands out as particularly relevant, as it aims to reduce preventable deaths, curb injuries from road traffic accidents, and strengthen universal health coverage [3]. Goal 3.6 specifically calls for halving global deaths and injuries from road traffic accidents by 2020, highlighting the essential role of effective neurotrauma management in global health policy [3].

Despite the critical nature of these interventions, the global capacity to deliver timely, high-quality neurotrauma management is hindered by profound inequities in workforce, infrastructure, and health system integration, particularly in LMICs [3]. Cross-sectional studies, such as the CRASH trial, reveal marked differences in clinical outcomes, observing up to a 6.6-fold difference in the odds of an unfavorable outcome between centers, related to country-specific development indices, the availability of neurosurgical services, and systemic preparedness [1]. The resulting preventable deaths and chronic disabilities transcend a clinical challenge to become a critical development issue, as they reduce economic productivity, reinforce cycles of poverty and social exclusion, and ultimately impede progress toward the Sustainable Development targets [3].

Addressing global neurotrauma management as an integral part of the SDGs requires multidimensional strategies. Strengthening emergency, critical, and operative care systems enhances resilience, promotes equity, and secures health security in fragile settings—all vital components for SDG achievement [4]. Moreover, policy development, workforce training, systems integration, and advocacy for injury prevention measures, such as road safety laws and helmet mandates, are crucial to reducing the global burden of neurotrauma [3]. The United Nations and the World Health Organization highlight that international partnerships and coordinated efforts are crucial to mobilize resources, foster innovation, and ensure that no one is left behind in achieving sustainable development [5].

Following PRISMA-ScR guidelines, this scoping review aims to map and categorize existing evidence on the relationship between neurotrauma and the SDGs and to identify areas of mutual reinforcement and unmet needs. It seeks to synthesize current knowledge, identify gaps and opportunities, and provide strategic directions for aligning neurotrauma system strengthening with sustainable development priorities to ensure equitable global progress.

Methods

We reviewed and analyzed each of the 17 SDGs to identify targets and indicators with direct relevance to the global burden of neurotrauma. This preliminary analysis formed the basis for our analytical framework, from which we derived six key thematic categories: neurotrauma care and outcomes, coverage and infrastructure, training and education, innovation and technology, international collaboration and assistance, and neurotrauma prevention. We mapped these categories with the specific SDG targets they addressed (Table 1; Fig. 1), ensuring a comprehensive mapping of the intersections between neurotrauma management and the 2030 development agenda. This framework subsequently guided our literature review and synthesis.

This scoping review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. No formal protocol was prospectively registered; however, the analytical framework and search strategy were predefined prior to data collection. We conducted structured database searches in MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, SciELO and Scopus in June 2025, restricting the results to articles published in English within the last 10 years (2015–2025) to ensure currency and relevance of the included evidence. Full search strings for each thematic category are provided in the Supplementary Material S1. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they addressed neurotrauma (TBI or SCI) in relation to one or more SDG domains across any study design (including systematic reviews, clinical trials, observational studies, policy documents, and grey literature). Studies were excluded if they were not published in English, predated 2015, did not address neurotrauma (TBI or SCI) as a primary topic, or lacked content relevance to any of the six predefined thematic domains as assessed by the review authors against the predefined analytical framework. Explicit use of SDG terminology was not required for inclusion; studies were eligible if their content was judged to have a plausible connection to one or more SDG targets based on the thematic framework. As initial searches yielded limited direct results linking neurotrauma to the SDGs, we expanded our strategy using supplementary approaches: manual screening of reference lists (snowballing technique, an iterative process of identifying new studies from the references of included articles) and incorporation of grey literature (reports, policy documents, and unpublished materials) from international organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) (Fig. 2). This multi-source approach ensured broad coverage of the available evidence on neurotrauma and sustainable development.

Flow diagram of the study selection process

Title and abstract screening were performed independently by two authors; disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when consensus could not be reached, by a third author. Full-text review of potentially eligible records was likewise conducted independently by the same two authors, with discrepancies resolved by a third author. Data were extracted by one author using a standardized extraction form capturing: author(s), year, country or region, study design, primary thematic category, cross-thematic categories, HIC/LMIC classification, and the SDG targets addressed. Each study was assigned a primary thematic category based on its predominant focus; studies addressing multiple domains were additionally tagged with secondary categories. Category assignment was reviewed and confirmed by at least one co-author. Given the heterogeneous nature of the included evidence and the descriptive goals of the scoping review, formal risk-of-bias assessment (e.g., ROBINS-I, AMSTAR) was not performed, consistent with established scoping review methodology (JBI framework). Instead, evidence quality is acknowledged qualitatively within the Discussion.

Each row represents one of the six thematic domains identified in this scoping review. Colored circles indicate SDG target alignment supported by evidence identified in the included studies. The color of each circle corresponds to the domain from which the alignment was identified. Numbers inside circles indicate the specific SDG. SDGs 3 (Good Health and Well-being), 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure), 10 (Reduced Inequalities), 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions), and 17 (Partnerships for the Goals) align with more than one domain, reflecting their cross-cutting relevance to neurotrauma prevention, care, and research. Most SDG–neurotrauma linkages identified in this review represent system-level inferences and framework-based integrations rather than directly measured causal effects.

From 265 identified records (PubMed = 12, Embase = 48, Web of Science = 14, SciELO = 0, Scopus = 107, supplementary methods = 84), 11 duplicates were removed, leaving 254 records for title and abstract screening. Of these, 151 were excluded as irrelevant. The remaining 103 records underwent full-text review, of which 14 were excluded. A total of 89 articles were included in the final review.

Results

Following structured database and supplementary searches, 265 records were identified. After removing 11 duplicates, 254 records underwent title and abstract screening; 151 were excluded as irrelevant or outside scope, leaving 103 records for full-text review. Of these, 14 were excluded for lacking a discernible link to the SDG framework or to neurotrauma management, yielding a total of 89 articles that met the inclusion criteria. The studies were distributed across the six predefined thematic categories associated to neurotrauma (Table 2): neurotrauma care and outcomes (n = 20), coverage and infrastructure (n = 10), training and education (n = 11), innovation and technology (n = 27), international collaboration and assistance (n = 9) and neurotrauma prevention (n = 12).

Most of the identified publications addressed topics related to innovation and technology (30.3%) and neurotrauma care and outcomes (22.5%), which aligns with the complex, technology-dependent nature. In contrast, limited evidence was found in training and education, international collaboration and assistance, and neurotrauma prevention, suggesting gaps in capacity building, policy implementation, and coordinated global efforts.

Notably, approximately 60% of the studies (n = 59) addressed themes that crossed categorical boundaries, the most frequent crossover occurring between Innovation and Technology and Neurotrauma Care and Outcomes. Category assignment was determined through author assessment of abstracts and relevance to each category’s core themes.

A consistent theme across multiple categories was the explicit focus on resource-limited settings and global disparities in neurotrauma management, aligning with the SDGs’ emphasis on reducing inequality. A complete list of included studies is available in Supplementary Material S2.

In terms of study design, the 89 included studies showed a clear predominance of systematic reviews and meta-analyses (53.9%), particularly marked in the neurotrauma care and outcomes (80%) and innovation and technology (85%) categories. Primary observational and experimental studies (cohorts, cross-sectional studies, randomised trials, and quasi-experimental designs) accounted for 14 studies (15.7%), concentrated mainly in those same two categories. The remaining 27 studies (30.3%) comprised narrative reviews, perspective articles, policy documents, organisational reports, and grey literature, and constituted the dominant evidence type in the training and education (100%) and international collaboration and assistance (89%) categories, where formal empirical evidence is sparse. Regarding geographic distribution, 48 studies (53.9%) were led by HIC institutions, 19 (21.3%) by LMIC institutions, and 22 (24.7%) involved multi-country or international collaborations. The HIC predominance was most pronounced in the international collaboration and assistance category (89%), while LMIC representation was highest in the care and outcomes category (35%). The training and education category showed the highest proportion of multi-country collaborations (73%), consistent with the inherently international nature of capacity-building initiatives. This overall HIC predominance limits the generalizability of findings to the settings where the burden of neurotrauma is greatest.

A key finding from the evidence mapping process was that only 4 of the 89 included studies (4.5%) explicitly referenced SDG terminology or the 2030 Agenda in their text. The remaining 95.5% were classified as presenting an inferred SDG linkage — that is, the alignment between the study content and specific SDG targets was identified and mapped by the review authors based on the predefined analytical framework, rather than being articulated by the study authors themselves. This pattern is not a limitation of the evidence base but rather reflects a well-recognized gap: the neurotrauma research community has largely not yet adopted the language and framework of the SDGs, despite producing evidence that is directly relevant to sustainable development priorities. Bridging this conceptual gap — making explicit what is currently implicit — is a core contribution of this scoping review.

Discussion

Before examining domain-specific findings, a key interpretive consideration warrants emphasis. Of the 89 included studies, only 4.5% explicitly referenced SDG terminology or the 2030 Agenda; the remaining 95.5% present inferred SDG linkages, whereby alignment with specific targets was mapped by the review authors using the predefined analytical framework rather than articulated by the original study authors. This proportion has direct implications for the strength of interpretation: the evidence reviewed supports the conceptual relevance of neurotrauma to sustainable development priorities, but most claims of SDG contribution represent framework-level inferences rather than empirically measured effects on SDG attainment. Readers should apply corresponding caution when interpreting domain-specific findings.

Neurotrauma care and outcomes

The 20 studies identified in this review on neurotrauma care and outcomes suggest that neurotrauma care may function as a lever for advancing multiple SDGs, particularly Goal 3.6 (Road Traffic Accidents), Goal 11.5 (Disaster resilience), and Goal 16.1 (Violence reduction). TBI remains the dominant contributor to neurotrauma-related mortality and long-term disability worldwide, with the greatest burden concentrated in LMICs where assaults and road traffic injuries are the leading mechanisms and where neurosurgical capacity is most constrained. Gaps in care quality across the full continuum — from prehospital response through acute management to rehabilitation — translate directly into preventable deaths and avoidable disability, with clear implications for SDG progress [6–8].

Beyond the thematic mapping presented in Table 2, this review also highlights several evidence-based interventions and emerging technologies that illustrate how neurotrauma care advances SDG targets. The implementation of standardized clinical protocols significantly contributes to reducing premature mortality and aligns with SDG targets. Internationally recognized guidelines, such as those from the Brain Trauma Foundation [9], the American College of Surgeons Trauma Guidelines [10, 11], and the Seattle International Traumatic Brain Injury Consensus Conference [12, 13], provide a critical framework for evidence-based care. Foundational tools such as the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and pupillary reactivity assessment guide triage and resource allocation, particularly in constrained environments [7]. However, a significant gap remains in the development and implementation of resource-stratified guidelines tailored to the specific constraints of LMICs. Initiatives like the BOOTStraP (Beyond One Option for Treatment of Traumatic Brain Injury) model are emerging to directly address this gap. BOOTStraP provides a flexible, consensus-based framework that stratifies clinical algorithms by local resource availability (basic, intermediate, advanced), enabling the application of evidence-based principles across diverse healthcare settings and serving as a methodological reference for context-appropriate care in resource-limited environments [14–16].

Evidence-based interventions, such as liberal transfusion strategies, have been associated with improved six-month neurological outcomes; the role of tranexamic acid (TXA) warrants specific consideration in global health contexts; while its mortality benefit continues to be refined in evidence (CRASH-2, CRASH-3 trials), its excellent safety profile and low cost present a pragmatic intervention option for resource-limited settings where therapeutic alternatives are scarce [17–19]. These advances exemplify how systematic, protocol-driven care reduces preventable deaths and enhances recovery trajectories.

Optimizing functional recovery represents a complementary priority, aligning with Goal 3.6, which targets the reduction of noncommunicable disease-related mortality and the promotion of mental health and well-being. Medical strategies, such as intensive glycemic control and hypertonic saline administration, have shown benefits in improving neurological outcomes [20–23]. Surgically, timely craniotomy for the evacuation of life-threatening mass lesions, such as acute subdural, epidural, and intracerebral hematomas, is widely supported as essential for survival and functional recovery, though evidence on optimal timing and patient selection continues to evolve [24]. For refractory intracranial hypertension, decompressive craniectomy may reduce ICU mortality in selected patients, though evidence from the DECRA and RESCUEicp trials indicates that its effect on functional outcomes is nuanced and highly dependent on patient selection and timing [25–27]. However, it is crucial to consider that craniectomy necessitates a subsequent cranioplasty, imposing a significant burden on surgical resources, a key limiting factor in many settings. In this context, the recent adoption of hinge craniectomy offers a promising alternative, particularly in LMICs, as it can potentially obviate the need for a second major surgery [28]. Advanced monitoring is critical in this context, while intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring is a well-established standard for guiding therapy and improving outcomes, the additive efficacy of brain tissue oxygen (PbtO₂) monitoring to promote recovery is still under investigation and not yet definitively proven [29, 30]. Furthermore, the significant resource demands of PbtO₂ monitoring limit its practicality and equitable application in resource-constrained environments.

In resource-limited contexts, noninvasive neuromonitoring innovations, including transcranial Doppler ultrasonography (TCD), quantitative pupillometry, and optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) ultrasound, represent scalable alternatives to invasive monitoring [31–33]. Their adoption following adequate training promotes equitable, context-appropriate technologies that strengthen health systems and support progress toward universal health coverage.

Advances in precision diagnostics are increasingly informing clinical decision-making in neurotrauma. Blood-based biomarkers offer a promising approach to refine diagnosis, stratify injury severity, and guide management decisions, particularly in settings where CT imaging is unavailable [34], similarly, neurorestorative modalities such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) show promise in enhancing cognitive recovery and mitigating chronic pain [35, 36]. However, their routine clinical implementation faces significant barriers, including high costs and a need for further validation, limiting their global applicability. Widespread clinical adoption, however, remains limited by cost, logistical constraints, and the need for further prospective validation across diverse settings. In parallel, ensuring access to structured post-acute rehabilitation represents a practical and high-impact priority. Evidence supports the role of multidisciplinary rehabilitation teams in reducing long-term disability and improving functional recovery, including in resource-constrained environments [37]. This focus on comprehensive recovery can be framed as relevant to Goal 3.6 by improving mental health, well-being, and quality of life for neurotrauma survivors, bridging the gap between acute intervention and long-term community reintegration.

Finally, strengthening neurotrauma systems enhances resilience during disasters, which is relevant to Goal 11.5. TBI and SCI are frequent in such events, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, with children being notably at risk [38]. These realities demand efficient, well-coordinated responses. Evidence highlights that early surgical decompression for SCI, performed within 24 h, may be associated with improved neurological recovery, reduced complications, and shorter hospital stays [39, 40]. Concurrently, interventions such as early tracheostomy optimize ICU resource use and shorten ventilation duration [41].

In summary, the evidence reviewed suggests that strengthening the neurotrauma care continuum (from prehospital triage and evidence-based acute care to precision diagnostics and rehabilitation) may support the development of more equitable, resilient, and sustainable health systems. The capacity of a health system to manage neurotrauma, from prehospital response to rehabilitation, thus reflects its broader readiness to reduce mortality, prevent disability, and advance equity, all of which are central to the SDG framework.

Neurotrauma coverage and infrastructure

Disparities in neurotrauma coverage and infrastructure between HICs and LMICs represent significant barriers to achieving several SDGs. The 10 studies identified in this review on coverage and infrastructure consistently documented these gaps, particularly in LMICs. These inequities stem from infrastructural deficits, financial constraints, and systemic weaknesses that hinder equitable access to care, thereby directly impacting progress toward Goals 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and 10 (Reduced Inequalities).

Strengthening infrastructure and access

A key obstacle is the lack of resilient infrastructure, which impedes achieving Goal 9.1 and Goal 9.a, which advocate for sustainable and inclusive systems that support human well-being. Globally, 3.9 billion people lack access to neurotrauma services within the critical two-hour treatment window; only 25% of LMIC populations meet this threshold compared to 77.3% in HICs, with sub-Saharan Africa showing the lowest access (~ 20%) [42–44]. Evidence from HICs, particularly the United States, demonstrates that regionalized trauma systems significantly improve survival in TBI, highlighting the life-saving impact of organized networks [45]. However, such systems remain rare in LMICs, where weak prehospital services cause significant delays.

A promising strategy to overcome this fragmentation is the development of integrated Mass Casualty Centers (MCCs), which aim to consolidate civilian, military, and non-governmental resources to provide 24/7/365 surgical and critical care. By optimizing existing but often siloed resources, MCCs enhance both day-to-day emergency care and disaster response, directly supporting the health-related SDGs [46, 47]. Moreover, the development of MCCs not only strengthens neurotrauma response but also enhances overall surgical and obstetric resilience. By providing 24/7/365 access to emergency surgical care, MCCs can address life‑threatening conditions beyond neurotrauma, such as postpartum hemorrhage. Strengthening these systems aligns with Goal 3.d, which emphasizes building capacity for early response and effective health risk management.

Prehospital barriers are particularly severe in LMICs, where the absence of organized emergency services forces reliance on civilian transport and long travel times, especially for SCI [43, 48, 49]. All LMIC studies identified transportation delays as a critical issue in SCI management [43, 48, 49]. In this context, MCC models leverage cost-effective technologies such as smartphone apps for prehospital coordination and telemedicine to support remote regions, helping to bridge the prehospital gap [46, 47]. Strengthening referral networks and prehospital coordination may support progress toward Goal 3.d by improving emergency preparedness and response.

In-hospital deficiencies further compound inequities. Limited access to computed tomography (CT) scanners (available in only 65% of facilities) and inadequate surgical infrastructure, often marked by equipment failures and insufficient operating space, delays timely TBI management [49]. A shortage of trained neurosurgeons aggravates these challenges, impeding progress toward Goal 3.c, which prioritizes workforce strengthening [49, 50]. Positive examples to address these gaps include twinning programs between HIC and LMIC institutions, such as the collaboration between Duke University and Uganda, which has successfully expanded local neurosurgical capacity through long-term partnerships, structured exchange programs, and the development of sustainable training platforms [51, 52].

Addressing financial and equity barriers

Equitable access to neurotrauma services is intrinsically linked to Goal 1.4, which ensures universal access to essential health services and financial protection. In LMICs, 3.4 billion people face the risk of impoverishing health expenditures, and 2.9 billion risk financially devastating health expenditures due to neurotrauma-related costs [43]. Over 60% of individuals report financial barriers to care, with the African region facing a risk of up to 75% impoverishment [43]. The high cost of neurosurgical procedures, estimated between $291 and $1,221 in LMICs, remains unaffordable for individuals often relying on less than $6 a day, and diagnostic tests like CT scans further exacerbate access delays [44, 49]. Integrating neurosurgical care within Universal Health Coverage frameworks and mobilizing international support under Goal 10.b are crucial to promoting equitable financial protection and strengthening national systems [44, 48].

Workforce, technology, and partnerships

Expanding the neurosurgical workforce is crucial to achieving Goal 3.c, which aims to recruit, train, and retain health professionals in developing countries. The global disparity in access is stark, with entire regions, encompassing over 35 million people, lacking any in-country neurosurgical services [49, 50]. Short-term strategies such as task-shifting and task-sharing can mitigate these shortages by enabling trained non-specialists to deliver essential neurotrauma services safely.

Leveraging digital health technologies further supports Goal 9.a and Goal 10.7 by promoting collaboration and responsible mobility across borders. Telemedicine provides a cost-effective and scalable solution to extend neurosurgical expertise, enhance training, and promote regional cooperation [53]. These partnerships not only bridge geographic disparities but also contribute to sustainable capacity-building, advancing the broader SDG agenda through equitable, technology-driven health system strengthening [50].

Neurotrauma training and education

Specialized training in neurotrauma management is foundational to reducing the global disease burden, particularly in LMICs [54, 55]. This review identified 11 studies on training and education, predominantly programs evaluations and curriculum development studies, which collectively illustrate the range of collaborative models being implemented globally. Addressing this need requires sustainable international collaborations that directly support SDG 4: Quality Education. The delivery of quality neurotrauma care is inextricably linked to workforce development, positioning training and education as a fundamental pillar for achieving SDGs related to quality education and decent work (Goal 4 and Goal 8).

Building capacity through twinning and localized training

A core strategy for achieving Goal 4.c is “twinning” programs, where HIC faculty collaborate with their LMIC counterparts to establish robust, locally-led training systems [51, 56, 57]. Through ongoing engagement, local neurosurgeons who complete training transition into faculty and leadership roles, ensuring the self-sustainability of these educational initiatives. The growing reliance on virtual education resources, webinars, and platforms like smart glasses further expands the reach of HIC faculty expertise, enhancing didactic content delivery and remote mentoring [56, 58].

Neurotrauma training initiatives directly contribute to Goal 4.4 by building sustainable neurosurgical capacity. This includes training programs not only for neurosurgeons but also for allied health staff such as nurses and biomedical technicians in LMICs [51, 52, 56, 57, 59, 60]. For instance, establishing neurosurgical residency programs within countries like Uganda has dramatically increased the number of practicing neurosurgeons from 5 in 2007 to over 40 attendings and trainees as of 2024, creating a trained workforce that can sustain local services [51, 56, 59]. Furthermore, educational programs employing a competency-based curriculum, such as the Global Neurotrauma Fellowship (GNTF), allow for the worldwide standardization of knowledge and competencies, ensuring that training is benchmarked irrespective of the country’s income level, developed through North–South collaborations [54].

Furthermore, a critical gap exists in training for the broader multidisciplinary team, including neurotrauma nurses, rehabilitation specialists, and emergency medical personnel, who are essential for managing the full spectrum of neurotrauma morbidity, particularly non-operative mild TBI [1].

The commitment to expanding specialized training aligns with Goal 4.b. Many international collaborations, including the WFNS Foundation, DGNN, and FIENS, offer fellowships, scholarships, or financial support to enable LMIC residents and surgeons to receive specialized training, either locally or abroad, facilitating the acquisition of advanced skills they can apply in their home countries [51, 52, 54, 56, 57, 61, 62]. For example, Ugandan residents may pursue observerships at Duke University, and the WFNS Foundation provides scholarships for comprehensive training at reference centers like Rabat [52, 61, 62].

Towards sustainable and contextualized education

Practical training extends beyond technical mastery to encompass the principles of sustainability and global citizenship, directly advancing Goal 4.7. Curricula now integrate crucial elements such as public policy, global surgery, system science, communication skills, and professionalism, which are vital for leading change in complex environments. These programs emphasize cultural humility and context-appropriate methodologies, ensuring initiatives are driven by local needs [54, 55].

Finally, these specialized educational efforts contribute significantly to Goal 8.6 by providing high-value vocational training and professional career pathways [56]. A critical benefit of in-country training is the retention of professionals, countering the historical problem of “brain drain,” and ensuring that trained neurotrauma experts remain in their home countries where the need is greatest, hereby strengthening local health systems and economies [51, 57, 59, 63].

Ongoing evaluation and adaptation of these training models facilitate the transfer of expertise to settings where neurosurgical capacity is most limited, strengthening local workforces and contributing to sustainable health system development [54, 56].

Neurotrauma innovation and technology

The 27 studies identified in this review on innovation and technology — the most populated category — reflect the growing evidence base in this area, though most studies do not yet demonstrate direct SDG-level impact. The integration of innovative technologies into neurotrauma management is central to advancing the SDGs, particularly Goal 9, which emphasizes resilient infrastructure, inclusive industrialization, and innovation. Strengthening scientific research and technological capacity (Goal 9.5) has accelerated progress in the diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of TBI and SCI [64, 65]. Blood-based biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ubiquitin carboxy-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1) represent a transformative step, offering high accuracy for detecting intracranial injury in mild TBI and potentially reducing unnecessary CT scans [66–72]. However, their routine clinical use remains limited, even in high-resource settings, due to cost and logistical barriers.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) algorithms further enhance diagnostic precision and prognostic modeling, achieving over 80% accuracy in predicting outcomes, morbidity, and mortality following TBI or SCI [73–75]. These models utilize clinical and imaging data, including CT lesion segmentation, to guide therapeutic intensity and personalize interventions [65, 75, 76]. Therapeutically, non-invasive neuromodulation techniques, such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), have demonstrated improvements in motor recovery and consciousness levels [77–80]. Pharmacological interventions are a cornerstone of post-acute neurotrauma rehabilitation, with established evidence base for managing a wide spectrum of cognitive, behavioral, and physiological sequelae [81]. Concurrently, research is actively exploring novel compounds that target specific pathways of secondary injury. This includes repurposed drugs such as minocycline to curb neuroinflammation and statins for their pleiotropic benefits, as well as antioxidants like N-acetylcysteine and natural compounds such as resveratrol to counteract oxidative stress [82]. However, these investigational therapies have yet to yield specific clinical recommendations. For a select subset of patients with severe disorders of consciousness, deep brain stimulation (DBS) has shown promising results in early research, though its application remains highly limited and requires further validation [83].

Achieving equitable access to innovation requires scalable, affordable, and context-adapted solutions, aligning with Goals 9.b and 9.c [31]. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) technologies, including ONSD measurement, provide a non-invasive method for detecting intracranial hypertension in TBI [33, 84, 85]. Additionally, POCUS can accurately identify skull fractures in pediatric patients [86]. Similarly, Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) may provide rapid detection of intracranial hematomas in prehospital or resource-limited environments lacking CT access [87, 88]. Additional emerging technologies, such as non-invasive intracranial pressure waveform monitoring (B4C), use advanced sensors to analyze cranial bone deformation and estimate intracranial compliance via the P2/P1 ratio [89]. These tools have proven reliable even when used by non-physicians, supporting their scalability and alignment with domestic innovation strategies.

Portable and battery‑powered imaging technologies, such as mobile CT scanners and emerging mobile MRI units, represent another leap forward in making diagnostic capabilities accessible in resource‑limited or off‑grid settings [90–92]. These tools not only support neurotrauma diagnosis but also enhance overall hospital resilience during power outages or disasters, aligning with Goal 9.

For advanced digital tools such as AI systems, successful implementation depends on strong digital infrastructure (Goal 9.c). In LMICs, barriers such as data heterogeneity, limited sample sizes, and inadequate standardization hinder the integration of these tools into clinical workflows [93]. The available evidence suggests that building robust digital ecosystems, enhancing data governance, and supporting local research capacity may be important factors for ensuring sustainable and equitable innovation in neurotrauma.

Promoting gender equality through technology

The adoption of decentralized, portable, and user-friendly technologies also contributes to gender equality (Goal 5.b) by improving access to diagnostic and treatment tools for populations traditionally underserved. Portable devices for detecting intracranial hypertension and hemorrhage can reduce care delays and overcome geographical barriers that disproportionately impact women [31, 87, 93]. Simplified and standardized protocols empower a broader workforce—including female healthcare providers in rural and low-resource settings—to deliver timely neurotrauma interventions. Ensuring the equitable deployment and standardization of these innovations is vital to achieving their full potential for empowerment and inclusion, as outlined in Goal 5.b.

Taken together, technological innovation in neurotrauma not only improves patient outcomes but also underscores the importance of coordinated international collaboration. Initiatives like the NIHR HealthTech Research Centre in Brain Injury exemplify this approach through its integrated focus across the entire care continuum, from prevention and acute monitoring to rehabilitation and diagnostics [94]. Equitable dissemination and sustainable adoption rely on global partnerships that bridge resource gaps, harmonize data-sharing standards, and strengthen research capacity.

Neurotrauma international collaborations and assistance

Effective international collaboration is fundamental to advancing global neurotrauma management and achieving key SDGs. The nine studies in this category, largely comprising narrative reviews, registry analyses, and feasibility studies, highlight both progress and persistent gaps in global neurotrauma collaboration. Nearly 90% of TBI-related deaths occur in LMICs, underscoring the urgency of addressing inequities linked to Goal 10 (Reduced Inequalities) [1, 95, 96]. Coordinated global action under Goal 10.b, promoting financial flows and development assistance to countries most in need, and Goal 17.16, strengthening partnerships to mobilize expertise, technology, and resources, is essential to reducing disparities in access and outcomes. In alignment with Goal 10.7, ethically structured migration and exchange programs must facilitate knowledge transfer and capacity-building in LMICs while preventing brain drain.

Embedding equity and social inclusion in neurotrauma research

The current neurotrauma research landscape is disproportionately concentrated in HICs; between 2008 and 2018, over 75% of TBI publications originated from North America and Europe [1, 97]. Consequently, most clinical guidelines are derived from HIC contexts, limiting their relevance in low-resource environments [1, 97]. This imbalance underscores the absence of explicit frameworks that integrate equity and social inclusion into global neurotrauma research and practice [98].

Recent research initiatives propose a PROGRESS-driven approach to examine how social and structural determinants shape cognitive outcomes after TBI. Incorporating frameworks such as PROGRESS-Plus, which integrates social determinants like place of residence, race, occupation, gender, and socioeconomic position, offers a structured mechanism for achieving Goal 16.a by strengthening national and institutional capacities to address inequities through evidence-based policy and data-informed decision-making [98]. Applying principles of equity and social inclusion enables a shift from equality‑neutral toward equity‑transformative strategies, ensuring that outcomes, not just opportunities, are distributed fairly and equitably. This alignment with the SDG vision fosters knowledge generation and clinical practice that is grounded in fairness, contextual adaptation, and shared accountability.

Enhancing knowledge, technology and data systems

Integrating equity and social inclusion within collaborative research accelerates progress toward achieving Goals 17.6 (knowledge sharing) and 17.7 (technology transfer). One of the most widely proposed initiatives—the establishment of a unified global neurotrauma registry—could centralize data to inform global health policy and improve surveillance systems [95–97]. This effort aligns with recent global advocacy initiatives, such as the G4 Alliance TBI Working Group’s position to recognize TBI as a chronic and notifiable condition, which aims to strengthen systematic data collection, enhance public health monitoring, and facilitate targeted resource allocation through mechanisms like the GEO-TBI Registry [99, 100]. However, persistent barriers in LMICs, including insufficient infrastructure, limited standardization, and workforce shortages, hinder implementation [96, 98, 101, 102]. Integrating equity-focused frameworks such as PROGRESS-Plus ensures that global registries and data systems remain inclusive, contextually appropriate, and ethically accessible, directly contributing to Goal 17.18, which calls for enhanced data availability, reliability, and disaggregation by equity-related variables.

Partnerships and capacity-building initiatives aligned with Goals 17.9 and 17.17 are equally essential for sustainable progress. Equity-driven international consortia have proposed targeted fellowships and training programs designed to strengthen local research capacity, promote registry development, and foster collaborations with governments and health systems [101, 103]. The World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) and related organizations can play a pivotal role in integrating TBI indicators into WHO-supported national injury surveillance systems, amplifying the visibility of neurotrauma as a global public health priority [97, 101].

Toward equity-driven global collaboration

Advancing global neurotrauma care may be advanced through international partnerships grounded in equity and shared accountability, where HIC and LMIC institutions jointly develop and implement context-appropriate solutions. Such collaborations, when structured to build local leadership and ensure long-term sustainability, represent a practical pathway to reducing global disparities in neurotrauma outcomes and strengthening health systems in line with SDG priorities.

Neurotrauma prevention

The global burden of neurotrauma, predominantly caused by road traffic collisions, necessitates robust prevention strategies aligned with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development [104]. The 12 prevention studies identified in this review, primarily systematic reviews and policy analyses, provide evidence that legislative and engineering interventions are the most effective strategies, though implementation gaps remain significant in LMICs. The Global Plan for the Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030 directly reinforces Goal 3.6 by setting the target of reducing road deaths and injuries by at least 50% through the implementation of an integrated Safe System approach [105]. Given that TBI and SCI constitute the largest volume of neurosurgical cases in LMICs, these findings suggest a potential role for neurosurgeons in informing evidence-based policy discussions [106]. Globally, road traffic accidents are projected to become the seventh leading cause of death by 2030 [107].

Prevention strategies can be broadly categorized into legislative, engineering, and educational/capacity interventions spanning primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention levels [104, 108].

Legal and behavioral frameworks

Legislative measures represent a proven, high-impact strategy, with evidence demonstrating that comprehensive safety regulations can yield substantial public health benefits across all income contexts [109, 110]. However, achieving optimal efficacy in LMICs requires addressing implementation challenges related to enforcement, resource availability, and public awareness [111]. Successful policy implementation depends on complementary elements including effective governance, affordable safety equipment supply, and community engagement [109, 111, 112]. Similarly, state interventions on impaired driving outperform market-based solutions, though they require sustained enforcement [112]. These efforts collectively support Goal 16.1 by reducing violence-related deaths.

Infrastructure and engineering solutions

Addressing infrastructure deficiencies is equally critical for SDG progress, particularly for achieving Goal 11.5, which aims to reduce the adverse impacts of disasters and improve urban resilience. Vehicle safety standards and road design improvements have driven significant traffic death reductions in HICs, yet these remain under-prioritized in many LMICs [104, 113]. Cost-effective infrastructure modifications and speed control measures offer particularly promising returns in resource-constrained settings, though they require careful integration to avoid unintended consequences [107, 110]. The Global Plan emphasizes that 100% of new and used vehicles must meet high-quality safety standards by 2030, which is critical as automobile companies frequently “de-specify” life-saving features when selling vehicles in LMICs that lack stringent regulatory frameworks [105].

Educational and capacity-building interventions

Education and awareness-raising interventions are commonly adopted, often due to their perceived low cost and low complexity, but studies show they have limited effectiveness in reducing injuries or fatalities when implemented alone [104, 110, 114]. The efficacy of behavioral interventions significantly improves when combined with robust enforcement or legislative changes [109, 110]. For example, comprehensive helmet laws require accompanying educational campaigns directed at the community and police, alongside the provision of affordable equipment, to achieve high compliance and impact in all income settings [104, 109]. Similarly, legislation concerning child restraints requires educational programs for drivers and families, often involving health professionals, to ensure proper installation and use [114, 115].

Data, research and capacity gaps

Robust data systems and capacity building are fundamental to informing effective prevention policy, aligning with Goals 3.d and 17.18. Bridging the deficit in context-specific data from LMICs is essential for designing resource-appropriate strategies that address local risk factors [104, 110]. Technological innovations should be viewed as complementary tools rather than comprehensive solutions.

Available evidence suggests that achieving the 2030 target may require integrated state-led approaches combining legislation, engineering, and education, supported by robust data systems and international partnerships; however, the heterogeneity of included studies and the predominance of inferred SDG linkages limit the strength of this conclusion.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. First, the restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language bias, potentially excluding relevant studies published in other languages, particularly from non-Anglophone LMICs. Second, this review included a heterogeneous mix of study designs — spanning randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, observational studies, narrative reviews, and grey literature — which precludes direct comparisons across studies and limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn. The absence of formal risk-of-bias or quality appraisal is consistent with scoping review methodology but means that weaker evidence may be represented alongside stronger evidence without explicit differentiation. Third, while the review aimed to address global neurotrauma broadly, the included literature shows a relative imbalance toward traumatic brain injury (TBI) compared to spinal cord injury (SCI), reflecting the overall distribution of available evidence rather than an intentional scope restriction; this imbalance is acknowledged as a limitation. Fourth, the thematic categorization of studies, although guided by predefined frameworks and reviewed by co-authors, involved a degree of author judgment and may be subject to subjectivity. Fifth, the incorporation of grey literature, while necessary to capture policy-relevant evidence, introduces variability in quality and may not be fully reproducible. Finally, the SDG-neurotrauma linkages identified in this review largely represent system-level inferences and framework-based integrations. Only 4 of the 89 included studies (4.5%) explicitly referenced SDG terminology in their text; the remaining 95.5% presented inferred linkages, meaning that the alignment between study content and SDG targets was mapped by the review authors rather than articulated by the study authors. Rather than constituting a methodological weakness, this disconnect is precisely what motivates the present review and underscores its contribution. Readers should interpret SDG-specific claims cautiously, as most linkages represent conceptual alignment rather than empirically measured causal effects on SDG attainment.

Conclusion

This scoping review maps available evidence suggesting that strengthening global neurotrauma systems may contribute meaningfully to achieving the SDGs. The identified studies support the view that advances in care, prevention, and technology can be framed as relevant to targets for health, equity, and resilient infrastructure, though most SDG-neurotrauma linkages represent system-level inferences rather than directly measured causal effects. Profound disparities in access and capacity, particularly in LMICs, remain a major barrier to equitable progress. Future efforts could focus on implementing equity-driven strategies, scaling successful collaborative models, and integrating neurotrauma indicators into global development frameworks to ensure inclusive and sustainable progress toward the 2030 Agenda.
