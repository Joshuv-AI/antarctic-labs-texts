Harnessing exosomes in dry eye disease: a triple threat approach

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDry eye disease is a chronic ocular surface disorder primarily driven by tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, which propagates inflammatory cascades leading to ocular tissue damage. Exosomes facilitate the transfer of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids between donor and recipient cells, mediating the exchange of bioactive molecules. Background

Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial chronic ocular surface disorder characterized by an unstable tear film and/or imbalance in the ocular surface microenvironment due to abnormalities in tear quality, quantity, and dynamics. This condition is often associated with ocular surface inflammation, tissue damage, and neurosensory abnormalities, resulting in a spectrum of ocular discomfort symptoms and/or visual impairment. Epidemiological studies report a global prevalence of DED ranging from approximately 5% to 50%, with advanced age and female sex identified as significant risk factors [1]. Common etiological factors in recent years include autoimmune disorders (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome (SS)), long-term contact lens wear, and prolonged exposure to digital screens. Patients commonly report symptoms such as ocular dryness, burning, and foreign body sensation; severe cases may lead to visual disturbances. Clinical signs typically include tear film instability, reduced tear break-up time, inflammatory cell infiltration on the ocular surface, and positive corneal staining.

Exosomes, a major subtype of extracellular vesicles (EVs), have become a central focus of research over the past decade. Initially considered cellular “waste products,” they are now recognized as essential mediators of intercellular communication, participating in various physiological and pathological processes—including autoimmune diseases. Exosomes are ubiquitously present in biological fluids such as tears, saliva, urine, and serum. These nanovesicles feature a characteristic lipid bilayer membrane that encapsulates a diverse cargo of bioactive molecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. This structural packaging facilitates the protected transfer of functional biomolecules between cells, modulating recipient cell functions while shielding cargo from enzymatic degradation. These intrinsic properties endow exosomes with significant potential as therapeutic agents, drug delivery vehicles, and disease biomarkers.

This literature review examines the interplay between exosomes and the host immune system, with a focus on the mechanisms through which exosomes may influence the development and progression of DED. We synthesize recent advances in the use of exosomes for DED management, highlighting three key applications: (1) As therapeutics: exosomes serve as active agents, leveraging specific components (e.g., miRNAs and proteins) to mitigate inflammatory cascades and immune dysregulation central to DED pathogenesis. (2) As drug carriers: exosomes deliver therapeutic molecules to target ocular tissues, improving treatment efficacy. (3) As biomarkers: exosome-associated molecular signatures in accessible biofluids (e.g., tears) enable non-invasive diagnosis, severity assessment, and prognosis of DED, supporting personalized management strategies. This review introduces and elaborates a novel “Triple Threat Approach” framework for exosome application. Unlike previous literature that discusses exosome therapy, drug delivery, and diagnostic functions separately, this paper emphasizes their synergistic integration within an interconnected “circular system.” We propose a forward-looking roadmap: rapid identification and typing of biomarkers via exosomes can guide the selection or customization of optimal treatment regimens—whether exosomes are used as therapeutics or carriers. Moreover, real-time monitoring and adaptive adjustment of exosome-based treatments are essential for enhancing precision and improving patient outcomes in DED. Finally, we address current challenges in clinical translation of exosome-based strategies and discuss future directions for advancing cell-free therapies in ophthalmology.

Methods

This review synthesizes current evidence regarding the pleiotropic roles of exosomes as therapeutic agents, drug delivery vehicles, and diagnostic biomarkers in DED. We conducted a literature search using PubMed with the keywords “exosomes” and/or “extracellular vesicles” in combination with “treatment”, “biomarker”, “carriers”, and “dry eye”. The search period was defined from January 2015 to July 2025, encompassing studies from the past decade. Our search strategy and keyword screening identified 258 publications. After removing 177 duplicates, 81 articles were further screened based on titles and abstracts. Exclusion criteria included: (1) non-research articles (e.g., reviews, editorials); and (2) studies not focused on exosomes. Ultimately, 36 research articles were included.

Results

Exosomes: overview, biogenesis, and characteristics

Definition and classification of exosomes

EVs are lipid bilayer-enclosed structures naturally released by virtually all cell types [2]. Based on size and biogenesis, EVs are broadly classified into three main subtypes: exosomes (30–200 nm), microvesicles (100–1000 nm; also referred to as ectosomes or shedding vesicles), and apoptotic bodies (>1000 nm). Exosomes, first identified in 1983 in the supernatant of cultured sheep reticulocytes by Harding and Johnstone, were formally named by Johnstone in 1987. They represent a specific subclass of small (30–150 nm), spherical, single-membrane EVs with an endosomal origin. Owing to their distinctive biogenesis and functional properties, exosomes have become a major focus in research on intercellular communication, as well as their implications in disease pathogenesis and therapy.

Biogenesis、release and uptake of exosomes

The biogenesis of exosomes is a complex, multi-step process (Fig. 1). It originates from the endosomal system and culminates in the release of intraluminal vesicles upon fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) with the plasma membrane. This process involves inward budding of the endosomal membrane, regulated by mechanisms such as the Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT)-dependent pathway and ESCRT-independent pathways [3]. Following release, exosomes serve as key intercellular messengers by transferring bioactive cargo—including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids such as miRNAs—to recipient cells.

Subsequent uptake by recipient cells occurs via mechanisms including internalization, specific molecular interactions, and non-specific endocytosis, facilitating delivery of their cargo. In the context of DED, exosome-mediated communication plays a critical role in modulating ocular surface homeostasis, immune responses, and tissue repair. Various ocular surface cell types, including corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells, secrete exosomes that participate in both physiological maintenance and pathological processes. For example, exosomes derived from human corneal epithelial cells have been shown to fuse with stromal cells, promoting fibroblast activation and influencing processes such as corneal repair and neovascularization, which are relevant to DED-associated tissue injury and healing [4]. Furthermore, exosomes from limbal epithelial cells enhance the proliferation and migration of neighboring epithelial cells via activation of the Akt signaling pathway—a mechanism essential for maintaining epithelial integrity and facilitating wound closure on the ocular surface [5].

The uptake of exosomes by target cells—such as corneal epithelial cells, immune cells, and lacrimal gland cells—is essential for their functional impact in DED. Internalization may occur through receptor-mediated endocytosis, wherein exosomal surface proteins (e.g., tetraspanins) interact with specific receptors on corneal epithelial cells, or through non-specific endocytic pathways such as micropinocytosis [6, 7]. These surface proteins not only guide exosomal homing to specific target cells but also initiate clathrin-dependent or caveolin-dependent endocytosis [6]. Notably, heparan sulfate proteoglycans associated with EVs may facilitate internalization by target receptor cells [8]. Under inflammatory or hyperosmolar conditions characteristic of DED, the uptake dynamics of exosomes may be altered, thereby affecting their capacity to deliver anti-inflammatory miRNAs to stressed ocular surface cells [9] (Fig. 2).

Isolation and purification of exosomes

The isolation and purification of exosomes critically influence their physicochemical properties (e.g., size, charge, cargo composition), yield, and functional integrity, which in turn affect downstream mechanistic studies and therapeutic applications. Current separation techniques include centrifugation-based methods such as ultracentrifugation (UC) and density gradient centrifugation; size-based techniques such as size exclusion chromatography (SEC) and tangential flow filtration; precipitation-based approaches including polymer precipitation and various commercial kits; as well as immunoaffinity capture [10–15] (Fig. 3). Each method presents distinct advantages and limitations in terms of purity, yield, scalability, cost, and preservation of bioactivity (summarized in Table 1). Importantly, no universally standardized protocol exists, necessitating careful selection based on sample type, downstream applications, and available resources.

Specific capture of EVs expressing specific antigens

The isolation of exosomes with high purity and preserved functional integrity is a critical prerequisite for investigating their roles in DED and developing corresponding diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Tear fluid, the primary biological sample of interest, is characterized by low volume (typically in microliter quantities), low total protein concentration, and an abundance of soluble proteins and lipids that tend to co-precipitate with exosomes [16]. Conventional isolation methods, including UC and polymer-based precipitation, are effective for concentrated cell culture media but often yield low recovery and considerable contamination when applied to tear samples [17]. These limitations impede reliable analysis of low-abundance yet pathophysiologically significant exosomal cargo in DED. To overcome these challenges, advanced platforms specifically designed for low-volume biofluids have been developed. Nanomaterial-based platforms and microfluidic technologies demonstrate substantial potential for isolating and analyzing exosomes from minute volumes, thereby facilitating biomarker discovery for disease diagnostics [18, 19]. A notable advancement in DED research is the integrated Tear Exosomes Analysis via Rapid-isolation System (iTEARS) [20]. This platform innovatively combines Schirmer strip-based clinical tear collection with a negative-pressure oscillation device and integrated immunoaffinity modules. Its main advantages include rapid processing time (<5 minutes) and high efficiency, achieving exosome yields from tear samples that are 10- to 100-fold higher compared to conventional UC or size-exclusion chromatography approaches. Importantly, iTEARS enables direct profiling of exosomal surface markers, allowing differentiation among healthy controls, DED patients, and diabetic DED patients through a targeted liquid biopsy approach [20]. This capability underscores the direct clinical relevance of refined exosome isolation techniques in achieving precision subtyping of ocular surface diseases.

Characterization of exosomes

ESCRT proteins, such as TSG101 and Alix, alongside tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81), are widely recognized markers for identifying exosomes. ESCRT-associated proteins, involved in intraluminal vesicle (ILV) biogenesis, serve as indicators of an endosomal origin [21]. However, their expression profiles extend beyond taxonomic classification and play functional roles. For instance, CD63, which is enriched in endosomally derived vesicles, has been implicated in the selective packaging of specific microRNAs [22, 23]. In DED, exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) with elevated CD63 expression may be enriched with anti-inflammatory miRNAs (e.g., miR-204, miR-146a) that modulate corneal epithelial and immune cell responses [24, 25]. Integrins expressed on exosomal surfaces facilitate tissue-specific homing, potentially directing exosomes to inflamed corneal or conjunctival epithelia in DED [26]. Moreover, the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II molecules on exosomes is donor cell-type dependent. MHC class II molecules present on immune cell-derived exosomes may indicate ongoing antigen presentation on the ocular surface, serving as potential biomarkers for autoimmune-mediated DED [27]. Therefore, comprehensive characterization of exosomes is imperative; this approach transcends mere classification and aims to elucidate how specific exosome subpopulations contribute to DED pathogenesis or, conversely, how engineered exosomes can be developed as targeted therapeutic vectors to restore ocular surface homeostasis.

The characterization of exosomal components and molecular markers predominantly relies on protein marker detection. Essential criteria typically include demonstrating the presence of: (1) transmembrane proteins such as tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81; (2) cytosolic proteins involved in biogenesis like TSG101 and Alix; and (3) the absence of contaminants from other organelles, such as Calnexin from the endoplasmic reticulum [28]. Western blotting remains the most prevalent method, offering semi-quantitative validation [28], although it demands substantial protein input, which can be limiting for low-yield tear-derived exosomes. Morphological characterization and particle size analysis focus on physical properties. Single-particle analyses, including Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) and Tunable Resistive Pulse Sensing (TRPS), are commonly utilized to assess vesicle size distribution and concentration [29]. While label-free and providing general vesicle profiles, NTA cannot distinguish exosomes from co-isolated particles of similar size. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), offering high-resolution visualization of the classic cup-shaped morphology and bilayer membrane, remains the gold standard, although it is qualitative and low throughput [30]. Emerging technologies address these limitations: super-resolution microscopy enables detailed imaging of exosome ultrastructure and interactions with cells [31], and label-free techniques such as Raman spectroscopy, particularly when combined with optical trapping, provide transformative potential by offering unique molecular fingerprints of individual exosomes in real-time [32]. The choice of characterization method should align with the specific research objectives and sample availability. In the DED context, characterized by limited tear sample volume, it is essential to move towards sensitive, label-free, information-rich single-vesicle analyses to fully realize the diagnostic and therapeutic applications of exosomes.

Modification of exosomes

Exosomes can be strategically engineered to enhance their therapeutic efficacy in DED via genetic modification of parent cells or direct chemical functionalization of isolated vesicles. Genetic engineering of MSCs allows production of exosomes preloaded with therapeutic proteins. For example, exosomes derived from IL-10-overexpressing human umbilical cord MSCs exhibit potent anti-inflammatory effects, suppressing pathogenic T cell responses and ocular inflammation in experimental autoimmune uveitis at lower doses than naïve exosomes [33]. This strategy offers a promising avenue for developing exosome-based therapies capable of mitigating the chronic inflammatory cycle central to DED. Complementing this approach, chemical modification of exosome surfaces post-isolation enables functionalization with molecules that enhance therapeutic potential. A notable example involves covalent conjugation of ascorbic acid to MSC-derived exosomes, resulting in engineered vesicles that synergistically integrate intrinsic repair signals with antioxidant activity [34]. This modification leads to enhanced corneal epithelial wound healing, reduced oxidative stress, and pronounced anti-inflammatory effects in DED models, while preserving excellent biocompatibility [34].

Loading of exosomes

The ability to load therapeutic cargo into exosomes is essential for their advancement as precise delivery systems for DED. Efficient cargo loading ensures that key therapeutic agents—such as anti-inflammatory miRNAs, neuroprotective factors, or small-molecule drugs—are protected, effectively transported, and functionally released at the ocular surface. Exosomes can significantly improve targeted drug delivery efficiency through various loading techniques, including transfection, electroporation, incubation, and ultrasound. For example, transfecting parent cells with miR-204 produces exosomes that deliver this miRNA to immune cells on the ocular surface [25]. In chronic graft-versus-host disease-associated DED models, miR-204-loaded exosomes effectively mitigate symptoms by reprogramming macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, demonstrating a targeted strategy to correct immune dysregulation [25]. Hydrophobic drug molecules, such as specific anti-inflammatory agents, can be incorporated by simple co-incubation at 37 °C [35]. This approach is particularly relevant for loading lipophilic compounds that are otherwise difficult to deliver to the ocular surface, potentially enhancing the bioavailability and retention time of drugs aimed at suppressing local inflammation in DED [36]. These cargo-loading strategies are fundamental to engineering exosomes as multifunctional therapeutic vectors. By combining targeted delivery with controlled release of diverse cargo, loaded exosomes present a promising platform to overcome key limitations of conventional eye drops, including poor bioavailability and lack of cellular specificity.

Exosome tracking and identification

Accurate tracking of exosomes both in vitro and in vivo is critical for elucidating their biodistribution, cellular uptake, and functional activity. Fluorescent membrane dyes, such as PKH67 and DiO, are widely employed to monitor exosome delivery and internalization by ocular surface cells, including corneal epithelial cells and infiltrating immune cells [37]. Emerging approaches utilize nanoparticles—such as gold nanoparticles for computed tomography (CT) and superparamagnetic iron oxide for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—to facilitate non-invasive, longitudinal tracking in preclinical models [38, 39]. These methods enable investigation of exosome persistence and homing to inflamed ocular surfaces or lacrimal glands. For diagnostic purposes, label-free techniques that preserve exosome integrity are essential. Raman spectroscopy, particularly when combined with optical trapping, represents a significant advancement by allowing real-time, single-vesicle analysis through generating unique molecular fingerprints based on intrinsic biochemical composition, including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [32]. Despite challenges such as inherently weak Raman signals [40] and the complexity of data analysis [32], its label-free nature, high specificity, and non-destructiveness establish Raman spectroscopy as a powerful tool for exosome tracking and characterization.

Exosomes and immune crosstalk

Exosomes and innate immune system

Exosomes act as crucial mediators of immune regulation through bidirectional interactions with innate immunity. Functioning as information carriers, exosomes activate innate immune responses via pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) contained within exosomes derived from infected cells, such as macrophages. These PAMPs engage pattern recognition receptors on innate immune cells, initiating signaling cascades. Specifically, exosome-macrophage interactions activate the mitogen-activated protein kinase and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathways, promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Activation of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and downstream myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88) further augments macrophage pro-inflammatory responses. For instance, exosomes from Mycobacterium tuberculosis-infected THP-1 cells induce IL-12p40 and TNF-α secretion along with neutrophil and macrophage infiltration in murine lungs, thereby enhancing host defense [41]. This response is significantly reduced in TLR2/Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-deficient macrophages and abolished in MyD88-deficient cells [42], underscoring the complexity of exosomal PAMP composition and signaling specificity. Similarly, Candida albicans-derived exosomes require TLR4 for optimal IL-6 production in bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (DCs) and may engage Dectin-1 via β-1,3-glucan [41], highlighting their role as mediators of intercellular pathogen signaling.

Exosomes play a critical role in modulating immune cell phenotypes and functions. Adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC) exosomes induce macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, evidenced by dose-dependent upregulation of Arg-1 and IL-10 mRNA and protein levels, without affecting the expression of M1 markers such as iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-12. Importantly, macrophages primed by these exosomes display attenuated pro-inflammatory responses upon LPS and IFN-γ stimulation [43]. Conversely, factors secreted by Arg-1^high M2 macrophages facilitate ADSC proliferation, demonstrated by increased EdU^+ S-phase cell entry [43]. Mechanistically, exosomal cargo, particularly miRNAs, reprogram macrophage behavior. miR-let7 suppresses HMGA2 and IGF2BP1, promoting M2 polarization through the HMGA2/NF-κB and IGF2BP1/PTEN signaling pathways to alleviate inflammation in atherosclerosis [44]. Overexpression of miR-181c in exosomes inhibits TLR4/NF-κB signaling, reducing TNF-α and IL-1β levels while enhancing IL-10 expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages [45]. Additionally, retinal astrocyte-derived exosomes deliver MIP-1α (CCL3), which competitively inhibits CCL2-mediated macrophage migration and decreases F4/80^+ cell infiltration in choroidal neovascularization models [46].

Exosomes also regulate DC functions, serving as pivotal mediators between innate and adaptive immunity. MSC-derived EVs impair antigen uptake in immature DCs, suppress the expression of maturation markers (CD83, CD38, CD80), and reduce CCR7-dependent migration to lymph nodes. DCs treated with MSC-EVs exhibit a tolerogenic phenotype characterized by decreased secretion of IL-6 and IL-12p70 and increased TGF-β production upon LPS stimulation [47]. In contrast, DC-derived exosomes (DCex) enhance natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity through TNF–TNFR interactions, with mature DCex demonstrating superior activation capability [48]. However, tumor-derived exosomes impair NK cell function by downregulating NKG2D receptors and cooperating with immunosuppressive factors to facilitate immune evasion [49].

Beyond immune modulation, exosomes contribute to tissue repair processes. MSC-derived exosomes attenuate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by reducing infarct size, fibrosis, and hypertrophy, with effects lasting for three weeks or longer [50]. Retinal astrocyte-derived exosomes inhibit vascular leakage in laser-induced choroidal neovascularization by suppressing macrophage infiltration and endothelial cell migration. This therapeutic effect, which is independent of treatment frequency, involves anti-angiogenic factors such as endostatin that target both macrophages and microvascular endothelial cells [46]. These reparative properties further emphasize the therapeutic potential of exosomes for inflammatory ocular surface disorders like DED.

Exosomes and adaptive immune System

Exosomes orchestrate bidirectional immunomodulation within adaptive immunity by delivering antigenic peptides, MHC molecules, microRNAs, and immunoregulatory factors. They facilitate antigen presentation and engage B cells and T cells through two distinct mechanisms: immune activation and tolerance induction.

Antigen presentation mechanisms

Exosomes present antigens either directly as autonomous antigen-presenting entities or indirectly through cooperation with professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Specifically, exosomes derived from Bacillus Calmette–Guérin-infected macrophages stimulate IFN-γ production by splenic CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in vitro. This effect is enhanced when exosomes are first internalized by APCs, confirming their function as “antigen reservoirs” that require APC processing to achieve optimal T-cell activation [51]. In vivo, intranasal administration of these exosomes elicits memory CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses in murine models [51]. Indirect antigen presentation involves exosome-mediated transfer of alloantigens or donor MHC-peptide complexes to recipient DCs. Once internalized, exosomes facilitate the cross-dressing of peptide-MHC complexes across populations of DCs, amplifying the priming of naïve T cells; however, mature DCs are necessary for this process to be effective [52]. In vivo studies have demonstrated antigen specificity: exosomes derived from male bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells activate Marilyn CD4+ T cells in female mice, whereas female-derived exosomes do not [53]. Notably, exosomes alone induce only weak activation of naïve T cells due to insufficient TCR crosslinking and lack of co-stimulatory signals.

T-cell immunomodulation

Exosomes exert context-dependent effects on T-cell function. DC-derived exosomes carrying MHC class I and II molecules, intercellular adhesion molecule-1, and CD86 can directly induce T-cell proliferation and differentiation [54]. Exosomes released from infected cells deliver viral nucleic acids that activate TCR signaling pathways and expand antigen-specific CD8+ T cells [54]. IFN-γ-containing exosomes enhance T-cell cytotoxicity in antiviral immune responses. Conversely, exosomes also exhibit immunosuppressive effects on T cells. Exosomes derived from human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs) suppress CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell proliferation and reduce IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion [55]. Treg -derived exosomes deliver Let-7d miRNA to Th1 cells, silencing cyclooxygenase-2 expression and inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators [56]. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma-derived exosomes contain immunosuppressive miRNAs (such as miR-24-3p and miR-20a-5p) that suppress Th1 and Th17 differentiation while promoting Treg expansion [57]. Moreover, tumor-associated macrophage-derived exosomes express programmed death-ligand 1, engaging programmed death- receptors on CD8+ T cells to inhibit their antitumor activity [58].

B-cell immunomodulation

Exosomes regulate humoral immunity through multiple mechanisms: (1) Direct B-cell activation, where antigen-MHC class II complexes on exosomes bind to B-cell receptors, triggering proliferation and differentiation. Additionally, cytokine and chemokine cargo within exosomes activates intracellular signaling pathways via interaction with surface receptors. (2) Functional reprogramming, as exosomes can enhance B-cell antigen presentation to T cells while concurrently suppressing excessive B-cell activation to maintain immune homeostasis [59]. (3) Facilitating affinity maturation, whereby B-cell-derived exosomes carry α4β1 integrin that binds vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 on follicular dendritic cells providing “MHC-dressing” through the deposition of MHC class II molecules, thus optimizing antibody affinity [60]. (4) Promotion of antibody class switching, with exosomal miRNAs (e.g., mmu-miR-20a-5p, mmu-miR-25-3p) downregulating the pro-apoptotic protein BIM, thereby enhancing B-cell survival. Both in vitro and in vivo evidence indicate IgG1 class switching mediated by miRNA transfer, which is abrogated in Rab27 knockout mice deficient in exosome secretion [61]. (5) Collaboration with T cells, as activated B-cell-derived exosomes expressing MHC class I and II molecules can directly stimulate T lymphocytes, reinforcing adaptive immunity.

Exosomes in dry eye pathogenesis and therapeutic mechanisms

DED comprises a spectrum of ocular surface disorders defined by tear film instability and inflammation driven by hyperosmolarity. Etiologically, DED is categorized into five principal subtypes: aqueous-deficient DED (characterized by impaired tear production), evaporative DED (due to lipid layer dysfunction), mucin-deficient DED (marked by goblet cell loss), tear film instability DED (associated with abnormal blinking dynamics), and autoimmune-mediated DED (such as SS). Tear hyperosmolarity is the central pathogenic driver, initiating inflammatory cascades through epithelial stress signaling. This process promotes the release of proteases and pro-inflammatory mediators—including IL-1β, TNF-α, and matrix metallopeptidase-9—thereby exacerbating ocular surface damage, inducing apoptosis of goblet cells, and contributing to neural dysfunction. Ultimately, these events establish a self-perpetuating inflammatory feedback loop [62]. Environmental factors, such as prolonged screen exposure, aggravate tear film instability by reducing blink rates and inducing blue light-mediated cytotoxicity. Blue light exposure activates caspase-3 and caspase-7 in corneal epithelium, increases reactive oxygen species production, impairs cell migration, and suppresses meibomian gland lipid secretion through activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [63].

Exosomes mitigate DED pathogenesis through multifaceted mechanisms. Firstly, exosomes exhibit immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties. They suppress the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Specifically, ADSC-derived exosomes downregulate NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 expression via inhibition of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/NF-κB pathway, thereby attenuating corneal inflammation [64]. Exosomal miRNAs, such as miR-204 and miR-100-5p, target the 3’ untranslated region of the interleukin-6 receptor, inhibiting IL-6/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling to suppress Th17 polarization and IL-17 production, while enhancing Treg function and expression of FoxP3 and nuclear receptor-related 1 protein [25, 65, 66]. Additionally, exosomes influence macrophage phenotype switching by promoting the transition from pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, characterized by increased CD206+ and decreased CD86+ expression, synergizing with Tregs to establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment [25, 67]. Secondly, exosomes regulate B cells; MSC-derived exosomes deliver miR-125b to suppress PR domain zinc finger protein 1 -driven autoantibody production and CXCL13-mediated B-cell recruitment [65, 66]. Thirdly, exosomes contribute to tissue repair and neuroprotection: MSC-derived exosomes reduce TUNEL-positive corneal epithelial cells and upregulate proliferative markers to prevent apoptosis [25]. They also transport neurotrophic factors—including brain-derived neurotrophic factor, nerve growth factor, and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor—which protect sensory nerves and inhibit Substance P-mediated neuroinflammation [25, 68]. Exosomal miRNAs such as miR-223-3p and miR-146a alleviate hyperosmotic stress by suppressing the F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (Fbxw7)/NF-κB and interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1)/TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) signaling axes [69, 70]. Fourthly, exosomes support lacrimal gland functional restoration. Although direct evidence is limited, exosomes indirectly enhance lacrimal function: Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC)-derived exosomes inhibit TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling, reversing goblet cell loss and increasing tear secretion [71]. MSC-derived EVs restore conjunctival goblet cell density by suppressing DC-mediated Th17 responses [67]. Similar to salivary gland repair, dental pulp stem cell-derived exosomes activate the G protein-coupled estrogen receptor/cyclic AMP (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA)/cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) pathway to upregulate aquaporin 5 expression, suggesting potential for lacrimal gland functional recovery [72].

Therapeutic potential of exosomes in dry eye management

Managing DED remains a significant clinical challenge due to its chronic nature and multifactorial pathogenesis. Conventional treatments—including artificial tears for symptomatic relief, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for inflammation control, and immunosuppressants for severe cases—offer limited modification of disease progression and are associated with potential long-term adverse effects. In this context, MSCs have emerged as promising therapeutic agents. Specifically, ADSCs secrete immunomodulatory factors that mitigate ocular surface inflammation, while lacrimal gland-derived MSCs provide neurotrophic support crucial for corneal nerve regeneration [73]. As a cell-free alternative, MSC-derived exosomes encapsulate the bioactive components of their parental cells and exhibit favorable safety profiles, including reduced immunogenicity, elimination of tumorigenic risks, and improved storage stability. Exosomes effectively mitigate innate immune hyperactivation, with the NLRP3-IL-1β axis recognized as a central driver of DED pathogenesis. Seminal research by Chaoqun Yu demonstrated that topically administered hADSC-derived EVs are internalized by corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells. This uptake restores goblet cell density and mucin expression (MUC-1 and MUC-5AC), thereby directly counteracting tear film instability. Critically, hADSC-EVs inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and IL-1β maturation in human corneal epithelial cells [74]. Complementary findings reveal that murine ADSC-exosomes suppress downstream effectors caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 at both transcriptional and translational levels [64], confirming the cross-species conservation of this anti-inflammatory mechanism. Exosomes also contribute to restoring immune homeostasis, particularly by correcting the Treg/Th17 imbalance that constitutes a central immunopathological feature in DED. BMSC-exosomes deliver miR-21-5p, which silences TLR4, subsequently inhibiting MyD88/NF-κB signaling. This cascade reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine production while promoting Treg cell expansion [71]. Notably, the efficacy of exosomes extends across DED subtypes: umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell (UCMSC)-derived EVs target multiple NF-κB activators (IRAK1, TRAF6, TAB2), suppressing IL-1, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in evaporative DED [75]. Given the intestine’s role as the largest immune organ, the gut–eye axis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of DED. Intravenous administration of human UCMSC-exosomes decreases serum autoantibodies (Ro/SSA, α-Fodrin), reshapes gut microbiota (increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio; enrichment of SCFA-producing Eubacterium; reduction of pro-inflammatory Escherichia-Shigella), and inhibits TGF-β1/Smad-mediated corneal fibrosis [76, 77]. Probiotic-derived exosomes from L. fermentum HY7302 enhance intestinal barrier function by upregulating tight junction proteins (TJP1, occludin-1), thereby attenuating ocular NFAT5/NFKB1 expression via gut–eye crosstalk [78]. Furthermore, exosomes regulate autophagy and epithelial repair, processes integral to DED progression. UCMSC-exosomes modulate CD4+ T-cell autophagy, inducing cell cycle arrest at G0/G1, reducing Th17 populations, and elevating Treg frequencies without altering Th1/Th2 ratios. This immunomodulation correlates with suppressed autophagic flux, as indicated by increased BECLIN1 and LC3-II expression [79]. Concurrently, exosomes promote structural repair; mouse ADSC-exosomes enriched in miR-223-3p inhibit Fbxw7, attenuating B-cell hyperactivity, which benefits both aqueous-deficient and evaporative DED [69]. Human amniotic epithelial cell-derived EVs enhance corneal re-epithelialization by stimulating proliferative and migratory pathways [80]. Additionally, M2 macrophage-derived EVs inherit potent anti-inflammatory properties, suppressing hyperosmolarity-induced NLRP3/IL-1β activation and restoring tear secretion in benzalkonium chloride -induced models [81].

The salivary gland and lacrimal gland, both secretory organs, share considerable similarities in their repair mechanisms. Investigating therapeutic mechanisms within the salivary gland may thus inform lacrimal gland research. Olfactory ecto-MSC-derived exosomes activate the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in myeloid-derived suppressor cells, enhancing immunosuppressive function and improving salivary secretion [82]. EVs also influence the progression of SS by modulating interactions between salivary epithelial cells and immune components. Induced pluripotent stem cell-MSC-derived EVs reduce lymphocytic infiltration by inhibiting follicular helper T/Th17 differentiation and APC activation [83]. MDSC-derived EVs deliver miR-10a-5p to suppress B cell lymphoma 6, thereby impeding germinal center B-cell maturation [86]. Additionally, T-cell-derived exosomal miR-142-3p disrupts calcium/cAMP signaling via inhibition of SERCA2B, RyR2, and AC9 in glandular epithelia [87], a pathway potentially conserved in lacrimal gland dysfunction. Human deciduous tooth stem cell-derived exosomes activate the Akt/GSK-3β/Slug signaling cascade, enhancing tight junction protein ZO-1 expression. This action reduces apoptosis and suppresses Th1 responses through miR-29a-3p-mediated inhibition of T-bet [88–90].

Recent years have witnessed rapid progression of exosome therapy for DED from preclinical research to clinical investigation. A clinical trial conducted at the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University, China, evaluated the efficacy of umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes in treating DED-related conditions (NCT04213248) [91]. Furthermore, a Phase II clinical trial assessed the safety and effectiveness of exosome eye drops for dry eye management following refractive surgery and blepharospasm (NCT05738629) [92]. The Chinese Clinical Trial Registry also lists exploratory studies investigating the safety and efficacy of exosome therapy in alleviating dry eye symptoms. Notably, a clinical study on human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosome eye drops for treating SS-related dry eye syndrome commenced in 2025 and is currently ongoing (ChiCTR2500099859) [93]. Collectively, these clinical trials underscore the translational potential of exosome therapy from bench to bedside. Despite this progress, several challenges remain. While saliva serves as a valuable surrogate for research, direct targeting data for lacrimal glands remain scarce. Moreover, heterogeneity among exosomes derived from various sources poses additional obstacles [84, 85]. Ongoing clinical evaluations promise to inaugurate a new era of precise, effective treatment for this debilitating disorder.

Exosomes as drug delivery systems for dry eye

The management of DED incorporates a range of therapeutic strategies, including pharmacological treatments (such as lubricants and anti-inflammatory agents), physical interventions, and surgical procedures. However, conventional drug therapies exhibit notable limitations. For example, artificial tears, which are employed as a first-line treatment, are suitable for mild cases of dry eye due to their affordability and ease of administration. Nevertheless, they only provide transient symptomatic relief and do not address the underlying inflammatory mechanisms. Prolonged application of artificial tears may disrupt the ocular microbiota, and the preservatives contained in these formulations can induce ocular irritation [94]. Cyclosporine effectively inhibits T cell activation and cytokine release in moderate to severe DED cases; however, its therapeutic onset is gradual, typically requiring 4 to 8 weeks, and it may cause initial adverse sensations such as pain and burning [95]. Thus, cyclosporine is frequently administered concomitantly with artificial tears to attenuate side effects and improve patient adherence. Lifitegrast, conversely, exhibits a rapid onset of action within a few days and significantly alleviates symptoms, although its use may be associated with side effects including dysgeusia [96]. Autologous serum eye drops are particularly advantageous for patients with severe corneal injuries or those refractory to conventional therapies [97]. However, their preparation involves blood extraction, which poses a risk of infection and limits storage duration. Consequently, autologous serum eye drops must be prepared under clinical conditions, resulting in elevated costs. Emerging therapeutic options such as biologics (IL-17/TNF-α inhibitors), stem cell transplantation, and gene editing (CRISPR-Cas9) introduce novel mechanistic approaches but carry inherent safety concerns, including immunogenicity and increased susceptibility to infections [98–100]. While these modalities demonstrate efficacy in many patients, they are constrained by incomplete therapeutic effects, adverse reactions, and a continued reliance on palliative care. In contrast, strategies employing exosomes represent a paradigm shift toward more comprehensive treatment modalities. Engineered exosomes, functioning as drug carriers, combine their intrinsic biological activity with targeted delivery capabilities, thereby potentially overcoming challenges related to local drug bioavailability and specificity. Exosomes are distinguished as superior carriers by five key properties. First, immunomodulatory capacity: surface proteins (e.g., CD47) facilitate evasion of immune surveillance, thereby attenuating inflammatory responses [101], which constitutes a significant advantage over cellular therapies. Second, inherent targeting specificity: parent cell-derived membrane proteins (including tetraspanins and integrins) mediate receptor-specific uptake via membrane fusion or endocytosis, enabling precise cargo delivery to ocular cells [7]; this contrasts with synthetic nanocarriers (such as liposomes) which lack intrinsic targeting motifs. Third, enhanced biostability: exosomes resist enzymatic degradation during circulation, preserving structural integrity and functionality for longer than one year at −80 °C [102]. Fourth, barrier penetration capability: their nanoscale dimensions (30–150 nm) and lipid bilayer architecture allow efficient traversal of biological barriers (e.g., blood-ocular barriers), thus facilitating localized drug delivery while minimizing systemic exposure. Lastly, engineerability: surface modification via ligand conjugation or genetic engineering further augments drug-loading capacity and tissue specificity. These properties underpin exosomes’ successful application across multiple disciplines, ranging from lncRNA H19-loaded exosomes promoting bone regeneration to antibiotic-encapsulated bacterial vesicles enhancing antimicrobial efficacy [103, 104].

The intrinsic advantages of naive exosomes as drug delivery vehicles are often insufficient to fully overcome the unique challenges associated with ocular surface drug delivery, such as rapid tear clearance, limited corneal permeability, and the requirement for sustained drug presence. Thus, targeted engineering of exosomes or the development of hybrid nanocarrier systems is imperative. Engineering approaches primarily focus on enhancing therapeutic efficacy in dry eye through surface functionalization and optimization of cargo loading. Surface modification of exosomes significantly enhances their targeting precision by conjugating specific ligands or antibodies to achieve selective recognition of corneal epithelial or conjunctival cells. In ocular gene therapy, exosome-mediated delivery of SUMOylated adeno-associated virus type 2 with the K105Q mutant demonstrated efficient retinal transduction at reduced doses, reflecting improved ocular targeting [105]. Load optimization further contributes to enhanced bioavailability. MicroRNA or siRNA naturally packaged within exosomes directly regulate corneal epithelial cell migration and repair. For instance, BMSC-derived exosomes loaded with miR-29b activate corneal autophagy, thereby accelerating epithelial repair while suppressing inflammation [106]. Furthermore, exosomes can be combined with bioactive agents to form multifunctional nanocomposites, amplifying their therapeutic potential. Hybrid nanocarrier systems commonly involve integration of exosomes with other materials to surmount the limitations inherent to single carriers. Vitamin C-conjugated MSC exosomes (mExo@AA) synergistically promote corneal repair and reactive oxygen species scavenging, facilitating re-epithelialization and polarizing macrophages toward the M2 phenotype [34]. This strategy effectively attenuates oxidative stress-induced ocular surface damage and shows promising efficacy in ameliorating DED symptoms. Cerium oxide-decorated exosomes (MSCExo-Ce) markedly increase tear secretion, reduce corneal injury, and mitigate oxidative stress in murine DED models [107]. Liposome-exosome hybrids (HEVs) transporting anti-NFKBIZ siRNA efficiently silence this pro-inflammatory regulator within the corneal epithelium, resulting in increased tear production, reduced corneal fluorescein staining, decreased secretion of TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-6, as well as promotion of M2 macrophage polarization [108]. Construction of HEVs by employing DNA zipper-mediated membrane fusion has demonstrated enhanced drug delivery capabilities. DNA zipper-fused HEVs extend corneal residence time and promote restoration of goblet cells through optimized siRNA delivery [108]. Additionally, encapsulation of exosomes within biodegradable gel matrices facilitates sustained release synchronized with matrix degradation on the ocular surface.

The therapeutic efficacy of exosome-based interventions is substantially influenced by the chosen route of administration, which governs initial biodistribution, tissue penetration depth, retention duration, and ultimately, the bioavailability within target ocular tissues [109]. Selection of administration routes should correspond to treatment objectives and the pathophysiological characteristics of various DED subtypes. Local administration is preferred for pathologies involving the corneal surface, anterior chamber inflammation, ocular surface lubrication, and epithelial repair. However, multiple barriers challenge drug delivery to the corneal surface, including tear film flushing, rapid tear dilution, and tight intercellular junctions of the epithelium. Owing to their nanoscale dimensions, exosomes can penetrate these barriers more effectively, thereby enhancing drug delivery efficacy. Nonetheless, tear film clearance remains a significant limitation. For targeting inflammation within the deeper stromal layers, subconjunctival or intrastromal injections provide higher delivery efficiency [110]. Exosomes can sustain localized release over an extended period following subconjunctival injection, remaining bioactive within adjacent tissues. Encapsulating exosomes with anti-inflammatory drugs for subconjunctival injection maintains stable intraocular drug concentrations while minimizing systemic absorption via the lacrimal duct. Intrastromal injection enables direct bypassing of the corneal epithelial barrier, ensuring a high concentration of therapeutic agents within the stromal layer. This approach significantly enhances bioavailability by delivering drugs precisely to the lesion site. Compared to subconjunctival injection, intrastromal injection offers more accurate targeting of the central cornea, thereby reducing nonspecific drug diffusion into the sclera or choroid [111]. These delivery modalities are particularly suited for treating severe, refractory DED or for administering engineered exosomes carrying neurotrophic factors. For DED resulting from lacrimal gland dysfunction, intralacrimal injection provides a direct but more invasive approach. Combining these delivery methods with established or emerging adjuvant therapies may produce synergistic therapeutic effects. Loading exosomes with anti-inflammatory microRNAs and corneal epithelial growth factors can concurrently suppress inflammation and promote tissue regeneration [112]. Incorporating exosomes into thermosensitive or ion-sensitive in-situ hydrogels enables precise local application while prolonging release durations [113]. Co-administration of cyclosporine A and MSC exosomes enhances tissue repair, restores immune homeostasis, and may alleviate ocular discomfort associated with cyclosporine A treatment [114]. Looking forward, combination therapeutic strategies may be incorporated; however, this requires comprehensive pharmacokinetic investigations to determine ocular clearance rates and tissue half-lives of exosomes via each delivery route, ultimately guiding rationalized dosing regimens.

Exosomes as biomarkers in dry eye

DED can be profoundly disabling, emphasizing the critical importance of early and accurate diagnosis to prevent vision-threatening complications. Exosomes have emerged as promising liquid biopsy biomarkers due to their stable molecular cargo—comprising proteins and nucleic acids—and resistance to environmental degradation. By encapsulating disease-specific signatures, reflected in membrane ligands, adhesion molecules, and nucleic acid profiles, exosomes facilitate quantitative assessment of pathological processes, offering unprecedented opportunities for diagnosis, prognostic stratification, and therapeutic monitoring [115]. The biomarker potential of exosomes was first recognized in 2005 when autoantibodies were identified in salivary exosomes of patients with SS [123]. Importantly, exosomes overcome limitations of traditional serological assays. In pre-symptomatic or antibody-negative SS (often termed “non-SS sicca”), patients frequently exhibit dry mouth and eye symptoms alongside mild chronic inflammation in salivary gland tissues. Salivary gland biopsies typically yield a focus score of 0 or less than 1. Tear and salivary exosomes reveal dysregulated proteins involved in major histocompatibility complex class I presentation, T-cell activation, and retinal homeostasis, distinguishing these from true SS cases [117]. Proteomic analysis of whole saliva-derived exosomes from Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) patients identified enzymes regulating adipogenesis and neutrophil chemotaxis as markers of disease progression [116]. For the first time, plasma exosomes have been shown to differentiate pSS from systemic lupus erythematosus, as demonstrated by the team led by Juliette Ferrant. Plasma exosome subpopulations enriched in pSS—including CD31+/CD146+ endothelial and CD15+ neutrophil-derived vesicles—highlight OLFM4 and RAB10 as novel diagnostic candidates [119]. The iTEARS platform achieves over 98% purity in exosome isolation from 10 µL of tears within 5 minutes, facilitating DED subtyping through analysis of exosomal protein-to-particle ratios [20]. Additionally, microarray analysis of exosomal RNA in tears distinguishes different tear breakup times. Notably, the gene encoding Sodium Channel Modifier 1, which regulates tear film stability, is significantly reduced in individuals with extremely low TBUT, whereas immature miR-130b is significantly elevated [118].

Nucleic acids further expand the diagnostic utility of exosomes. MicroRNAs are a class of evolutionarily conserved short non-coding RNAs and constitute one of the most abundant RNA species in exosomes. They mediate intercellular communication via exosomes and serve as non-invasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Salivary exosomal hsa-let-7b, hsa-miR-150, and hsa-let-7c effectively discriminate SS patients from healthy controls [124]. Serum exosomal miR-127-3p and miR-541-5p, significantly upregulated (p < 0.01) in non-obese diabetic mice, promote lymphocytic activation through BCL6/IL-10 and STAT3 signaling pathways, serving as early SS biomarkers [120]. Combined miRNA signatures derived from saliva and mouth-rinsed cell exosomes achieve 91.7% sensitivity and 83.3% specificity for SS diagnosis, outperforming conventional autoantibody assays [122]. Emerging RNA types also hold biomarker potential. Transfer RNA (tRNA) fragments, a subset of short non-coding RNAs involved in protein synthesis and cell proliferation, show altered expression; RNA analysis of stimulated saliva exosomes in pSS patients reveals a twofold downregulation of tRNA-Ile-AAT-2–1 [121]. Circular RNAs (circRNAs), a class of structurally stable, covalently closed non-coding RNAs resistant to degradation, are implicated in disease. Plasma exosomal circ-IQGAP2 and circ-ZC3H6 correlate with clinical severity, IgG levels, and histopathological features in pSS [10, 125]. Circ-IQGAP2 targets miRNA hsa-miR-142-3p, inhibiting calcium (Ca2 +) signaling and cAMP production, leading to dysfunctional salivary gland cells. Circ-ZC3H6 targets miRNA hsa-miR-200b-3p, which is implicated in diabetic retinopathy [10, 125].

The identification of biomarkers not only facilitates disease diagnosis but, more critically, reveals specific protein pathways that may serve as targets for the development of targeted exosome therapeutics or drug-loading strategies. While these findings open new diagnostic avenues, significant gaps persist. Firstly, most investigations focus on salivary or systemic biomarkers; tear-specific exosomal signatures for DED subtyping remain insufficiently explored. Secondly, promising biomarkers such as circRNAs require validation in larger patient cohorts [10, 125]. Furthermore, no studies to date have correlated exosomal biomarker dynamics with disease activity or therapeutic response. Additionally, technical heterogeneity in exosome isolation and analytical methodologies complicates their clinical translation.

Conclusions and discussion

Despite advances in elucidating the multifactorial pathogenesis of DED, current treatment modalities remain largely palliative and fail to address the fundamental immune dysregulation and tissue injury underlying this debilitating condition. Exosomes have emerged as transformative agents in DED management by functioning as innate immunomodulators and mediators of adaptive immune responses, while simultaneously serving as versatile therapeutic vectors, drug delivery vehicles, and precision biomarkers. This triad of functionalities uniquely positions exosomes to overcome existing therapeutic limitations.

In this article, we comprehensively summarize the primary therapeutic effects of exosomes. Exosomes derived from various sources such as adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and bone marrow effectively resolve inflammation by inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and suppressing Th17 polarization, while promoting Treg expansion and M2 macrophage skewing. Additionally, they activate key tissue-repair signaling pathways, including the Akt/GSK-3β and cAMP/PKA/CREB pathways. Furthermore, as superior drug delivery vehicles, engineered exosomes demonstrate enhanced corneal permeability compared to synthetic nanocarriers, and enable sustained release of therapeutic agents such as miRNAs, siRNAs, and antioxidants (e.g., vitamin C). Hybrid systems utilizing exosomes also extend ocular residence time. Beyond their therapeutic utility, exosomes serve as precise diagnostic tools; for example, tear and saliva-derived exosomes provide non-invasive biomarkers, protein signatures useful for SS subtyping, miRNAs with diagnostic accuracy exceeding 90%, and circRNAs that correlate with disease severity.

Translating this promising potential into clinical application requires systematic resolution of key translational priorities and standardization challenges: (1) Standardization of Production and Characterization: The clinical advancement of exosome-based therapies is impeded by the absence of standardized isolation, quantification, and characterization protocols. Variability in these methods drastically affects exosome purity, yield, and functional consistency. Defining clinically relevant potency assays is imperative to enable accurate dosing and quality control. (2) Pharmacological Profiling and Dose Optimization: Comprehensive studies are needed to delineate the therapeutic window of exosome-based treatments, encompassing parameters such as ocular surface residence time, cellular uptake mechanisms, and biodistribution following both topical and systemic delivery. (3) Decoding Heterogeneity and Source Selection: Considering pronounced variability in exosomal cargo and function dependent on parent cell origin and physiological state, multi-omics profiling is essential to identify the most efficacious exosome populations tailored for specific disease endotypes. (4) Advanced Formulation and Stability: Ensuring long-term storage stability of exosome preparations is vital for their practical clinical deployment. (5) Although exosomes generally exhibit lower immunogenicity relative to their parent cells, attributed to the absence of complete cellular machinery, their immunogenic potential should not be underestimated. Allogeneic exosomes may carry surface proteins, including MHC class I and II molecules, which can provoke immune responses upon repeated administration.

Ultimately, the integration of exosome biology with advanced bioengineering marks the advent of a new era in cell-free therapies. While challenges such as manufacturing scalability and regulatory standardization remain to be fully addressed, exosomes unequivocally represent a paradigm shift toward precision medicine for DED. Their unique ability to simultaneously mitigate inflammation, restore immune homeostasis, and promote regeneration of ocular surfaces offers unprecedented therapeutic potential that warrants prioritized and rigorous investigation.
