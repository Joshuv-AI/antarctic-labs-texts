3-axis computer numerical control machine positioning error dataset for thermal error compensation
Authors: Adalto Farias, Vanessa Seriacopi, Marcelo Otávio dos Santos, Ed Claudio Bordinassi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article reports on a comprehensive dataset detailing positioning errors in a 3-axis milling center machine (MCM) with computer numerical control (CNC) specifically curated for thermal error compensation. The data, which includes separate datasets for the X, Y, and Z axes, was collected through systematic measurements using an interferometric laser (IL) system under monitored thermal conditions. Each axis's acquisition was recorded with a resolution to capture dynamic variations influenced by thermal fluctuations. Temperature measurements were obtained using resistance temperature detectors (RTD) installed in the bearing housings of each axis for monitoring of thermal conditions throughout the data collection process in each axis. The dataset comprises raw positional and error data for each axis alongside metadata describing parameters such as bearing temperature, heating cycle, and machine operating conditions. This dataset can potentially be a valuable resource for researchers, enabling them to develop and validate real-time thermal error compensation algorithms, thereby enhancing CNC machining precision for each axis independently and collectively. Furthermore, the dataset's structured format facilitates comparative studies across different machine configurations and operational contexts, contributing to advancements in manufacturing technology and improvements in process parameter design and optimization.Keywords: Calibration, Accuracy, Metrology, Precision, Machine

Background

Several studies identified thermal error as the primary cause of over 50 % of inaccuracy in machined parts, e.g. [[4], [5], [6]]. Some compensation strategies such as structural redesign, advanced measurement systems integration, and cooling mechanisms are often employed to enhance machine accuracy, albeit at significant time and cost [6,7]. Soft strategies involving error prediction algorithms have gained recognition in academia [8]. An example of soft strategies with error prediction algorithms is found in [1], where a radial basis function neural network was trained using experimental data and successfully implemented directly into the CNC machineʼs command system for real-time positioning control.

Recent examples with a similar approach used the same experimental methodology as the current study to obtain positioning error metadata. Shi et al. [2] implemented a multiple linear regression model on a lathe turn machine, utilizing thermal error measurements from a two-axis machine dataset. The model was integrated into the CNC system using interface hardware, enabling real-time error compensation. Yang et al. [3] comprehensively applied several algorithms (subtraction-average-based optimizer, convolutional neural networks, gated recurrent unit, fuzzy clustering and grey correlation analysis) to develop a model with high-precision prediction capabilities for thermal error prediction. A characteristic of all metal cutting machines featuring a motion system supported by linear guides is their susceptibility to thermal expansion. These machines typically utilize recirculating ball screws driven by servomotors, as exemplified in Fig. 1. At the servomotor bearing housing, the ball screw is constrained in 5 degrees of freedom (DOF), allowing axial rotation. On the opposite side, 4 DOF also allows axial linear expansion.

Single-axis movement diagram system in a CNC machine, composed of a recirculating ball screw and nut, linear guide system, machine table coupling, and drive servomotor. As the ball screw rotates, driven by the servomotor, the rotational motion is converted into linear motion, forward and backward, and transmitted through the coupling to the table supported by the linear guides.

The ball screws, due to their long length and small cross-sectional area, are prone to thermal expansion. As the machine operates, friction occurs within the screw, nut, and bearing housings, gradually increasing their temperature over time and, thus, the susceptibility for linear expansion.

Based on this context, the presented data article provides the access to much more data than the original publication, [1], to enable further investigations and modeling of thermal errors in CNC systems.

The structure of all CSV files in the folder “raw_data_CSV–positioning_measurements” is identical, with the first column representing the reference for the nominal position coordinates for each measurement row. The first row indicates the measurement order (from 1 to 5), the measurement direction (forward or backward), and the type of result presented in the column, which can be either position in millimeters (mm) or error in micrometers (µm).

The second important folder is the one that contains the temperature measurements associated with the axis positioning errors measurement, named “raw_data_CSV–temperatures_measurements”, which includes six CSV (Comma-Separated Values) files. Among these six files, two relate to the X axis, two to the Y axis, and two to the Z axis. These files contain the raw data from the initial and final temperature acquisition during positioning error measurement. The file names indicate which axis was measured in the machine condition (warm_cycle or not-warmed). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The machine employed, displayed in Fig. 4(a), was a standard milling center, the Discovery 560, manufactured by Romi Industries. The main features of this machine are as follows: CNC Siemens 810D, the maximum speed of 7500 rpm, and travel strokes including a longitudinal stroke (X-axis) of 560 mm, transverse stroke (Y-axis) of 406 mm, and vertical stroke (Z-axis) of 508 mm. The maximum axes speed is 20 m/min. The machine's table dimensions are 840 × 360 mm. The AC main motor has a power of 15 kW, and the total installed power is 20 kVA. In Fig. 4(a), the orientation of the X, Y, and Z axes is indicated in yellow to allow the understanding of the machineʼs spatial configuration.

CNC machine used for axis positioning error measurements, (a) Discovery 560 milling center, (b) measurement with a thermal imaging camera of the hot spot on the X axis, (c) detail of the RTD T6 fixing on the X axis and (d) detail of the RTD T3 fixing on the Z axis.

To properly install the RTD sensor, the machineʼs hotspots were identified using a FLUKE VT02 thermographic camera. The machine tool was programmed to cycle-run for two hours, as this was the time required for the machine to reach a higher and constant temperature. The cycle to warm the machine consisted of setting all three axes to move simultaneously and continuously at a steady speed of 12,000 mm/min for the entire length of their strokes for two hours. Fig. 4(b) presents an image of the thermal measurements of one hot spot of the X-axis bearings.

The RTD sensors were inserted at the hot spots, as shown in Fig. 4(c) T6 sensor and Fig. 4(d) T3 sensor. A total of six sensors were used, with two for each axis (X, Y, and Z) at the bearing housing, as illustrated in the diagram in Fig. 5. A thin-film PT100 RTD and the SIMATIC S7-300 Siemens PLC were utilized to acquire the measured temperatures. The PT100 RTD was secured with aluminized adhesive, as depicted in Fig. 4(c) and (d), to protect and ensure its position. All installation regions were protected from machining fluid and chips.

Fig. 5 shows the complete diagram of installing all RTD temperature sensors inside the machine without protective coverings. In addition to the indications of the RTD sensors, the servomotors for each axis movement set, the table, and the machineʼs main spindle are also presented. The orientation of the X, Y, and Z axes is indicated in black in Fig. 5 to allow the understanding of the machine's spatial configuration.

Fig. 6 illustrates the IL measurement components system setup used to evaluate the machine axes’ positioning errors. The IL apparatus setup, Fig. 6(a), consisted of a Renishaw XL-80 laser mounted on a tripod stage kit positioned in front of the machine, connected to its power supply. The Renishaw XL-80 laser was equipped with an external temperature sensor (Troom) placed outside the machine to monitor the ambient temperature during the measuring process and an XC-80 compensator kit placed inside the machine to perform correct temperature compensation for the laser system, ensuring the accuracy and repeatability of the measurements.

Interferometric laser setup for the X-axis positioning measurements.

In Fig. 6(a), the setup was arranged to measure the positioning error along the X-axis in both forward and backward directions. It generates and directs a laser beam along the X-axis of the machine tool, allowing for highly accurate measurement of displacement and positioning. The laser's interference pattern detects even the slightest deviations in the machine's movements.

A close-up view of the machine's interior highlighting the spindle axis of the machine tool is presented in Fig. 6(b). Using magnetic bases, the IL linear optics mirrors kit is attached to the machine spindle and table. This setup reflects the laser beam back to the IL apparatus. The spindle mirror remains stationary, while the table mirror is movable, as the X-axis ball screw is actuated in forward and/or backward directions by the servomotor. This laser reflection creates an interference pattern, which is analyzed to determine the precise position of the machine's table along the X-axis. To collect the laser measurement data, the Renishaw QuickViewXL™ software and the Renishaw CARTO™ software package were used to capture, review, and save dynamic data at speeds of up to 4 m/s and a frequency of 50 kHz from the Renishaw XL-80 laser system.

The measurements were taken under various temperature conditions as documented in the repository folder “raw_data_CSV–temperature_measurements.” Examples of these temperatures are shown in Table 2 for not warmed acquisition on the Z-axis and Table 4 for warmed machine acquisition on the Z-axis.

Before each acquisition, the machine was warmed by cycling all three axes simultaneously and continuously at a constant speed of 12,000 mm/min for the entire length of their strokes over two hours. The exception was the not-warmed acquisition, where the machine was at or near room temperature during the measurements. After completing the warm-up cycle for the machine, axis scan measurements were taken every 25 mm along the entire stroke of each axis. During each axis measurement, the other two axes were held stationary.

Data were acquired at ten points for each nominal position interval: five during the axis's forward movement and five during its backward movement, corresponding to the machine axis's forward and backward motions.

Along the total X-axis travel stroke, measurements were taken at 23 coordinates spaced 25 mm apart, starting at the 0 mm coordinate and ending at the 550 mm coordinate. Along the total Y-axis travel stroke, measurements were taken at 17 coordinates spaced 25 mm apart, starting at the 0 mm coordinate and ending at the 400 mm coordinate. Along the total Z-axis stroke, measurements were taken at 21 coordinates spaced 25 mm apart, starting at the 0 mm coordinate and ending at the 500 mm coordinate. The time interval between the measurements of each linear positioning coordinate was 0.4625 s. During the measurement of the positioning coordinates, the travel speed between points was consistently maintained at 400 mm/min. The time interval for moving from one coordinate to the next, spaced 25 mm apart, was 0.0625 s. Before capturing the coordinate, the IL system waits for 0.4 s to ensure the complete stabilization of the mechanical assembly.

Table 3 presents the measurements of acquisition #6 under warmed machine conditions executed for the Z-axis. For each true position, the IL took 10 positioning measurements, five in each direction. A total of eight acquisitions were performed for the X-axis, eleven acquisitions were performed for the Y-axis, and seven acquisitions were performed for the Z-axis. All data are in the repository's "raw_data_CSV–positioning_measurements" folder.

Limitations

•Each linear ball screw axis position was measured individually, with the other two remaining stationary. There was no simultaneous measurement of more than one axis, therefore, the potential combined error resulting from the joint/simultaneous movement of the three axes was not experimentally evaluated. This implies that a model generated with this data may not yield accurate results when all three axes are activated simultaneously to reach a coordinate in a three-dimensional system. To address this condition in future research, a laser tracking system could be employed.

•The position of the non-operating axes during the measurement of one axis was kept constant at the midpoint coordinate of their total stroke. Measurements were not conducted with the stationary axes positioned differently, such as at the initial or final coordinates of their total travel stroke. It is possible that in different positions there may be varying error results eventually associated with different geometric conditions imposed by the machine's structure and/or the wear condition of the drive system. To address this condition in future research, an additional set of measurements could be conducted at different positions for each stationary axis, thereby further increasing the resulting dataset and potentially generating a more comprehensive model in terms of industrial use.
