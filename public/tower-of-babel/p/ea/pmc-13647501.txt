Latent profiles of kinesiophobia and their associations with exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, and fall risk in older adults with type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wanzhen Ding, Changming Li, Kun Liu, Jiaxue He, Chengxu Liu, Bo Wang, Zengning Li, Kejuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKinesiophobia may hinder exercise participation in older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), but its heterogeneity and related associations remain unclear. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of kinesiophobia and examine the associations of exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, and fall risk with profile membership.MethodsA cross-sectional design was utilized, enrolling 220 older inpatients with T2DM from a tertiary hospital. Data were collected using the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK), the Self-Efficacy for Exercise (SEE) Scale, the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS), and the Morse Fall Scale (MFS). Latent profile analysis (LPA) was applied to identify kinesiophobia subtypes. Multiple logistic regression, correlation analysis, and an indirect-association analysis based on cross-sectional data were used to examine the relationships among variables.ResultsKinesiophobia was classified into three latent profiles: low kinesiophobia-low perceived threat (37.27%), moderate kinesiophobia-moderate perceived threat (35.91%), and high kinesiophobia-high perceived threat (26.82%). Compared with the low kinesiophobia-low perceived threat profile, higher exercise self-efficacy was associated with lower odds of belonging to the moderate profile (OR=0.856) and the high profile (OR=0.667). Higher fall risk was associated with higher odds of belonging to the moderate profile (OR=1.035) and the high profile (OR=1.083). Higher diabetes distress was associated only with membership in the high kinesiophobia-high perceived threat profile (OR=1.058). Kinesiophobia profile membership was negatively associated with exercise self-efficacy and positively associated with diabetes distress and fall risk. The hypothesized indirect-association analysis suggested that diabetes distress and fall risk were involved in the association between exercise self-efficacy and kinesiophobia profile membership.ConclusionsKinesiophobia among older inpatients with T2DM showed three distinct profiles: low, moderate, and high. More severe profiles were associated with lower exercise self-efficacy, higher diabetes distress, and greater fall risk. These findings support profile-oriented nursing assessment that integrates movement-related fear, exercise confidence, diabetes-related emotional burden, and fall risk.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Type 2, Kinesiophobia, Self efficacy, Stress, Psychological, Accidental falls, Latent profile analysis, Cross-sectional studies

Introduction

With the accelerating aging of the population, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has become an important public health issue affecting the health and functional independence of older adults [1]. Exercise therapy is an essential component of comprehensive T2DM management and contributes to glycemic control, complication risk management, and improved quality of life [2]. However, older adults with T2DM often show poor adherence to and persistence with exercise in clinical practice, even when they are aware of its benefits [3, 4]. In addition to physical capacity, disease status, and external barriers, concerns about exercise safety may also hinder exercise participation.

Kinesiophobia refers to an excessive tendency to avoid movement or physical activity due to fear of injury or adverse consequences [5]. Among older adults with T2DM, kinesiophobia may be shaped by multiple disease-related risk perceptions. On the one hand, patients may worry about exercise-induced hypoglycemia [6]. On the other hand, diabetes-related complications may lead to sensory abnormalities, impaired balance, and increased physical vulnerability, thereby strengthening perceived fall risk [7, 8]. These somatic safety concerns may lead patients to view exercise as a potential threat and reduce their participation in physical activity. Previous research reported that the prevalence of kinesiophobia reached 38.5% among patients with painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy [9], suggesting that this issue is clinically important in diabetes nursing and exercise management for older adults.

Beyond somatic risk, the long-term self-management demands of T2DM may also create persistent psychological burden. Patients need to continuously manage glucose monitoring, medication use, dietary control, exercise planning, and complication prevention, all of which may contribute to diabetes distress [10, 11]. Diabetes distress reflects the diabetes-specific emotional burden experienced in daily disease management and has been associated with lower self-efficacy, weaker perceived control, and poorer self-management status [12, 13]. In addition, fear of falling and related avoidance behavior may reduce activity participation among older adults, indicating that concerns about physical safety may further reinforce exercise avoidance [14]. Therefore, exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, and fall risk may not be isolated factors associated with kinesiophobia; rather, they may be interrelated in an association pattern involving insufficient psychological resources, increased disease-management-related emotional burden, and heightened concerns about physical safety.

Existing studies have often treated kinesiophobia as a unidimensional psychological construct, with limited attention to heterogeneity in the severity of fear and patterns of risk perception among older adults with T2DM. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered, data-driven method that identifies latent subgroups with similar response patterns across multiple observed indicators [15]. Compared with describing kinesiophobia using only a total score, LPA can further reveal differences in fear severity and perceived threat patterns across patient subgroups, thereby providing evidence for stratified identification and targeted intervention in clinical practice.

Based on the above theoretical and clinical rationale, this study first used LPA to identify latent profiles of kinesiophobia among older adults with T2DM. It then specified an indirect-association model based on cross-sectional data to examine the indirect associations of exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, and fall risk with kinesiophobia profile membership. In this model, exercise self-efficacy was specified as the primary explanatory variable, diabetes distress and fall risk were specified as model-specified intermediate variables, and kinesiophobia profile membership was specified as the outcome. The possible indirect associations of exercise self-efficacy with kinesiophobia profile membership through diabetes distress alone and fall risk alone were also examined. Given the cross-sectional design, this model was used to test theory-driven statistical associations, rather than to establish temporal ordering or causal relationships among variables.

Methods

Design and participant

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit older inpatients with T2DM from the Endocrinology Department of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University between February 2025 and October 2025. Older adults were defined as individuals aged 60 years or above, consistent with the age threshold commonly used in Chinese ageing-related policy and geriatric research. Inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the T2DM diagnostic and classification criteria specified in the “Guideline for the Management of Diabetes Mellitus in Elderly in China (2024 edition) [16]; (2) age ≥ 60 years; (3) stable medical condition, clear consciousness, and the ability to complete the questionnaire independently or with assistance; and (4) voluntary participation with informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) comorbid psychiatric disorders, intellectual disabilities, or severe visual/auditory impairments; and (2) severe concomitant diseases such as hepatic or renal dysfunction, cardiopulmonary failure, malignant tumors, or active infections.

The sample size was estimated based on the formula for population proportion in cross-sectional studies [17]. Given a reported kinesiophobia incidence of 38.5% in patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain [9], the expected prevalence was set at p = 0.385. Using the formula with and an allowance error d = 0.075, the calculated required sample size was 186. Accounting for a 10% dropout or invalid response rate, a minimum sample size of 207 was determined. We ultimately collected 220 valid questionnaires, fulfilling this requirement. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. [2025]047).

Measurement

General information questionnaire

Designed by the research team. This questionnaire included 9 items: age, education level, exercise habits, exercise regularity, diabetic duration, and the presence of diabetic peripheral vascular disease, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic foot. Complications were diagnosed based on the Guideline for the Management of Diabetes Mellitus in the Elderly in China (2024 edition) [16]. Exercise habits were defined as exercising ≥1 day/week for ≥ 20 min/day; those not meeting this criterion were classified as having no exercise habits [18]. Regular exercise was defined as exercising ≥ 5 days/week for ≥ 30 min/day. Patients with exercise habits who did not meet the criteria for regular exercise were categorized as irregular exercise [19].

Tampa scale for kinesiophobia

The TSK-17 was used to assess the severity of kinesiophobia. Translated and revised into Chinese by Hu Wen in 2012 [20], the scale evaluates patients’ fear of movement. It comprises 17 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with four items (4, 8, 12, 16) reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 17 to 68, where a score > 37 indicates the presence of kinesiophobia, and higher scores denote greater fear levels. In this study, the Cronbach’s αcoefficient was 0.950.

Self-efficacy for exercise

The Self-Efficacy for Exercise (SEE) scale, translated by Lee et al. [21], was utilized to measure patients’ confidence in exercising. It includes 9 items scored from 0 (not confident at all) to 10 (very confident). The final score is the average of the item scores, with higher scores representing greater exercise self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s αcoefficient was 0.961 in this study.

Diabetes distress scale

The DDS, translated into Chinese by Yang Qing in 2010 [22], was used to assess patients’ psychological distress over the past month. It contains 17 items across four dimensions: emotional burden (5 items), regimen-related distress (5 items), interpersonal distress (3 items), and physician-related distress (4 items). Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale from 1 (not a problem) to 6 (a very serious problem). Total scores range from 17 to 102, with higher scores indicating higher psychological distress. The Cronbach’s αcoefficient was 0.849 in this study.

Morse Fall Scale

The Morse Fall Scale (MFS) was used to assess fall risk among hospitalized patients. The Chinese version of the MFS was introduced and applied by Zhou et al. for fall-risk assessment in older inpatients in China [23]. The MFS consists of six clinical risk indicators: history of falling, secondary diagnosis, ambulatory aid, intravenous therapy or heparin lock, gait/transferring, and mental status. The total score ranges from 0 to 125, with higher scores indicating greater fall risk; a score > 45 indicates a high risk of falls. As the MFS is a clinical fall-risk assessment tool composed of heterogeneous risk indicators rather than a unidimensional reflective scale, internal consistency was not used as the primary reliability criterion. Previous validation studies of the Chinese MFS have demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.97) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.98) in hospitalized populations [24]. In the present study, the MFS was scored according to standardized criteria after investigator training.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected via questionnaire surveys, with a total of 225 distributed. Prior to collection, investigators received standardized training. Questionnaires were retrieved and preliminarily reviewed on-site; and missing or illogical data were immediately verified with the participants. Five incomplete or invalid responses were excluded, resulting in 220 valid questionnaires and an effective recovery rate of 97.78%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0, Origin 26.0, GraphPad Prism 10, and Mplus 8.3. Before statistical analysis, the normality of continuous variables was assessed. The results showed that all continuous variables were not normally distributed; therefore, they were described as median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)]. Differences in continuous variables among the three kinesiophobia latent profiles were examined using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, while categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. Raincloud plots were generated using Origin 2026. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to examine correlations among variables, and correlation heatmaps were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10. Multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with kinesiophobia profile membership, with the low kinesiophobia–low perceived threat profile selected as the reference group. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was utilized to identify latent classes of kinesiophobia. Model fit was evaluated comprehensively using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC (aBIC), with smaller values indicating better fit. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR), and the Bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to compare the k-class model with the k-1 class model; p < 0.05 indicated that the k-class model fit better. Entropy ≥ 0.80 was considered indicative of high classification accuracy [25].

An indirect-association analysis based on cross-sectional data was constructed in Mplus 8.3. Exercise self-efficacy was specified as the primary explanatory variable, diabetes distress and fall risk as model-specified intermediate variables, and kinesiophobia profile membership as the outcome. The associations were specified according to theoretical and clinical rationale and were interpreted as statistical indirect associations rather than evidence of temporal ordering or causal effects. Because the outcome variable had three nominal categories, the outcome portion of the model was parameterized using multinomial logistic regression. As complete indirect-association estimates for nominal multicategory outcomes are difficult to obtain directly using MODEL INDIRECT, parameter labels were assigned to the relevant path coefficients. The product-of-coefficients method was then used in MODEL CONSTRAINT to calculate the estimated specific indirect effects through diabetes distress alone and through fall risk alone, and jointly involving diabetes distress and fall risk, as well as the total indirect effect, and the total effect. Parameters were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation. Regression coefficients, standard errors, P values, and 95% confidence intervals were reported. For the categorical outcome, odds ratio (OR = exp(B)) were additionally reported, with 95% confidence intervals derived using the Delta method via CINTERVAL.

Latent profile analysis of kinesiophobia

Latent profile analysis was conducted using the scores of the 17 TSK items as observed indicators to identify latent classes of kinesiophobia among older adults with T2DM. Models with 1 to 4 classes were sequentially estimated. After systematically comparing model fit indices, the three-class model was identified as the optimal solution, as shown in Table 2. As the number of classes increased, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased up to the three-class model. Furthermore, the three-class model showed a significant LMR test (P = 0.0008) and the highest entropy (0.961), indicating excellent fit and classification precision while maintaining model parsimony.

Based on the LPA results, profile plots of the TSK-17 item scores were generated to illustrate the scoring patterns across latent classes, as shown in Fig. 1. Three latent profiles were identified: (1) Low kinesiophobia-low perceived threat profile. This group included 82 participants (37.27%), with a mean TSK-17 total score of 27.94 ± 5.45 and a low mean item score (1.64 ± 0.32). Scores were consistently low across most items, reflecting relatively weak concerns about exercise-related harm and limited avoidance tendency. (2) Moderate kinesiophobia-moderate perceived threat profile. This group included 79 participants (35.91%) with a mean TSK-17 total score of 43.41 ± 4.06 and a moderate mean item score (2.55 ± 0.24). Item scores were generally moderate, indicating more apparent concerns about exercise-related risks than the low-kinesiophobia group, but not a uniformly high level of avoidance. (3) High kinesiophobia-high perceived threat profile. This group included 59 participants (26.82%), with a mean TSK-17 total score of 56.83 ± 4.90 and a relatively high mean item score (3.34 ± 0.29). Scores were highest across most items, suggesting stronger threat perceptions regarding exercise-related harm and more pronounced avoidance or conservative behavioral tendencies.

Latent profile characteristics of kinesiophobia in older adults with type 2 diabetes

Characteristics of participants across kinesiophobia classes

Univariate analysis showed that age, educational level, exercise habit, regular exercise status, duration of diabetes, and diabetic retinopathy differed significantly across the latent classes of kinesiophobia (P < 0.05). Specifically, the proportion of participants classified into the high kinesiophobia-high perceived threat group was substantially higher among those without an exercise habit (40.4%) than among those with an exercise habit (14.7%). Similarly, participants without regular exercise were more likely to belong to the high-kinesiophobia group than those engaging in regular exercise (37.6% vs. 14.5%), as shown in Table 4.

Factors associated with kinesiophobia class membership

Using the low kinesiophobia-low perceived threat profile as the reference group, multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with latent class membership. For the moderate kinesiophobia-moderate perceived threat profile, higher exercise self-efficacy was associated with lower odds of membership in this profile rather than the low kinesiophobia-low perceived threat profile (OR = 0.856, 95% CI: 0.740–0.991, P = 0.038). In contrast, higher fall risk was associated with higher odds of membership in the moderate kinesiophobia-moderate perceived threat profile (OR = 1.035, 95% CI: 1.007–1.063, P = 0.013). Diabetes distress was not significantly associated with membership in this profile (P > 0.05).

For the high kinesiophobia-high perceived threat profile, higher exercise self-efficacy was associated with lower odds of membership in this profile (OR = 0.667, 95% CI: 0.528–0.842, P < 0.001). Higher fall risk (OR = 1.083, 95% CI: 1.048–1.120, P < 0.001) and higher diabetes distress (OR = 1.058, 95% CI: 1.013–1.105, P = 0.011) were associated with higher odds of membership in the high kinesiophobia-high perceived threat profile. Neither age nor duration of diabetes remained significant in the adjusted model. These results are shown in Table 6.

Correlations among study variables

Spearman correlation analysis showed that kinesiophobia class membership was significantly negatively correlated with exercise self-efficacy (r= -0.533, P < 0.001) and significantly positively correlated with diabetes distress (r = 0.429, P < 0.001) and fall risk (r = 0.567, P < 0.001). Exercise self-efficacy was significantly negatively correlated with both diabetes distress (r = -0.460, P < 0.001) and fall risk (r = -0.477, P < 0.001). In addition, diabetes distress was significantly positively correlated with fall risk (r = 0.420, p < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 3.

Indirect associations involving diabetes distress and fall risk between self-efficacy for exercise and kinesiophobia

To examine the hypothesized indirect associations linking exercise self-efficacy to kinesiophobia, an indirect-association model based on cross-sectional data was constructed with diabetes distress and fall risk specified as intermediate variables. The results demonstrated that exercise self-efficacy was negatively associated with both diabetes distress and fall risk, while diabetes distress was positively associated with fall risk. After controlling for the mediators, the direct effect of exercise self-efficacy on kinesiophobia remained significant in both the moderate-kinesiophobia group (β=-0.428, P = 0.001) and the high-kinesiophobia group (β=-0.183, P = 0.010). Diabetes distress and fall risk were positively associated with kinesiophobia profile membership in both comparisons. Discussion

This study identified three latent profiles of kinesiophobia among older adults with T2DM, confirming substantial heterogeneity in fear of movement within this population. The indirect-association analysis further indicated that the patterns of association between exercise self-efficacy and kinesiophobia differ across subgroups. The indirect association involving diabetes distress and fall risk was statistically significant only in the moderate kinesiophobia-moderate perceived threat group. These findings suggest that the association between psychological burden and somatic risk perception may vary across kinesiophobia profiles. By integrating psychological burden and physical vulnerability into the same analytical framework, this study extends previous total-score approaches and provides clinically interpretable subgroup information for nursing assessment and exercise-safety management in older adults with diabetes.

Latent profiles of kinesiophobia

The present study identified three latent profiles of kinesiophobia in older adults with T2DM, which are low kinesiophobia-low perceived threat, moderate kinesiophobia-moderate perceived threat, and high kinesiophobia-high perceived threat. These findings indicate that kinesiophobia is not a uniform phenomenon in this population but rather shows clear heterogeneity. This pattern is consistent with recent LPA-based studies conducted in other clinical contexts, such as hip fracture rehabilitation and postoperative lumbar spine populations, where distinct kinesiophobia subtypes with different psychological and behavioral characteristics have also been reported [26, 27].

Patients in the low-kinesiophobia group tended to show weaker concerns about exercise-related harm and greater acceptance of exercise benefits, suggesting more favorable cognitive appraisals and possibly stronger self-management resources [27]. In contrast, the moderate-kinesiophobia group appeared to hold a more cautious but not fully avoidant attitude toward exercise, which may represent a transitional stage in fear development. The high-kinesiophobia group showed consistently elevated scores across TSK items, especially on items reflecting fear of being injured during movement, indicating a stronger threat appraisal and a more pronounced avoidance tendency. These findings support the clinical importance of moving beyond a simple binary classification of “with” or “without” kinesiophobia and instead recognizing graded and qualitatively distinct fear profiles. Similarly, among individuals with diabetic neuropathy, higher levels of kinesiophobia are often accompanied by greater concerns about falling and poorer balance performance [28]. Furthermore, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that older adults with diabetes are at an increased risk of falls, with multiple clinical factors being associated with fall risk assessment [7].

From a clinical perspective, assessment should not be limited to whether kinesiophobia is present, but should also consider its severity and pattern of expression. In outpatient or community follow-up settings, kinesiophobia screening could be integrated with functional assessment and fall risk screening to improve the interpretability and practical value of patient evaluation [29, 30].

Factors associated with kinesiophobia

The study found that exercise self-efficacy, fall risk, and diabetes distress were independently associated with latent class membership of kinesiophobia among older adults with T2DM. Also, the strength of these associations varied across classes. These findings suggest that different kinesiophobia profiles may reflect different combinations of psychological resources and perceived vulnerability.

Exercise self-efficacy was negatively associated with both moderate- and high- kinesiophobia groups, and the magnitude of this protective association became stronger as kinesiophobia severity increased. This gradient pattern is consistent with previous evidence showing that self-efficacy is closely linked to exercise participation and physical activity behaviors [31, 32]. In the context of diabetes management, self-efficacy is widely recognized as a key psychological factor related to self-management behavior [33]. In the present study, the stronger association observed in the high-kinesiophobia group (OR = 0.667) compared with the moderate-kinesiophobia group (OR = 0.856) suggests that low self-efficacy may be particularly relevant to the transition from moderate concern to entrenched fear and avoidance. Clinically, interventions designed to enhance exercise self-efficacy such as mastery experiences, behavioral feedback, and peer modeling, which may help reduce the likelihood of patients belonging to more severe kinesiophobia profiles.

Fall risk was positively associated with both the moderate- and high-kinesiophobia groups, with a larger association size observed in the high kinesiophobia-high threat perception group. This finding aligns with evidence indicating that fear of falling is closely linked to actual falls and functional limitations in older adults [34]. In older adults with diabetes, multiple diabetes related complications such as visual impairment, hypoglycemia, peripheral neuropathy, and balance dysfunction, which may be related to greater perceived bodily vulnerability [35]. Therefore, incorporating fall risk assessment into routine kinesiophobia management may be especially important in this population.

Unlike the above two variables, diabetes distress was significantly associated only with the high-kinesiophobia group in the adjusted model. This suggests that diabetes distress may play a more prominent role in the deepening or consolidation of kinesiophobia rather than in its initial emergence. Previous studies examining the relationship between diabetes distress and self-management behaviors have yielded inconsistent results. Although most evidence indicates a negative association between diabetes distress, self-management behaviors, and glycemic control, the strength of this relationship varies when considering the specific domain of physical activity [36]. Moreover, a multicenter cohort study showed that individuals with high levels of diabetes distress had heterogeneous clinical characteristics and complication-related risk indicators, including poorer glycemic control and higher odds of diabetic neuropathy [37]. From a clinical standpoint, for patients with both high diabetes distress and severe kinesiophobia, healthcare professionals should pay greater attention to emotional burden, identify major stressors related to diabetes self-management, and provide tailored psychological support and disease-management education. Referral for professional psychological intervention may also be warranted when necessary.

Although age and duration of diabetes were associated with class membership in univariate analysis, they did not remain significant in the multivariable model. Studies on physical activity among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus have also identified individual-level barriers, motivation, and psychological factors as key dimensions explaining variability in exercise participation [38]. This suggests that, within a multivariate framework, psychological and behavioral variables may be more proximal correlates of kinesiophobia than demographic or disease-history characteristics.

Indirect associations involving diabetes distress and fall risk

The indirect-association analysis further clarified the multiple indirect associations linking exercise self-efficacy to kinesiophobia. In the comparison of the moderate- versus low-kinesiophobia profiles, exercise self-efficacy showed both a direct association with profile membership and indirect associations through diabetes distress alone, fall risk alone, and the combined involvement of diabetes distress and fall risk. In the comparison of the high- versus low-kinesiophobia profiles, however, the indirect association involving both diabetes distress and fall risk did not reach statistical significance. This pattern is theoretically consistent with the fear-avoidance model and cognitive appraisal theory [39, 40], which suggest that fear-related beliefs, emotional burden, and perceived bodily threat may be interconnected in shaping avoidance behavior. A meta-analysis examining the core component of the fear-avoidance model demonstrated that fear-related cognitions and vigilance exhibit moderate to strong associations with negative affect and functional limitations [41]. Thus, it provides a comparable theoretical reference for the parallel “psychological-behavioral risk” association patterns observed in the present study.

Fall risk was the strongest mediator in both groups, which is in line with the findings of Wu et al. [42], who reported that fear of falling significantly mediated the relationship between key predictors and health outcomes in community-dwelling older adults. Other studies have similarly suggested that fall-related concerns may be associated with quality of life through psychological and behavioral pathways and are statistically associated with physical activity and related factors [43]. According to the fear-avoidance model, perceived bodily threat is often accompanied by avoidance behavior and involves anticipatory evaluation of risks such as loss of balance and injury [44, 45]. Older adults with diabetes frequently present with peripheral neuropathy, which can disrupt sensory input and impair gait and balance. Related evidence further indicates that neuropathy burden is consistently associated with balance dysfunction and fear of falling [46], making falls more likely to be experienced as a tangible and realistic threat. These findings suggest that, in clinical nursing practice, fall risk screening may be used as an accessible entry point for identifying patients at elevated risk of kinesiophobia. For those with positive screening results, adding balance training and home environmental safety education to routine health education may help improve the assessment and management of movement-related fear from the perspective of physical risk.

The independent mediating effect of diabetes distress was also consistent with previous findings [47, 48], which indicate that emotional burden is associated with reduced physical activity in older adults with diabetes. Cognitive appraisal theory provides a plausible explanation for this association pattern, as negative emotional states may shape individuals’ expectations and interpretations of exercise-related experiences. Of note, diabetes distress was significantly associated with the high-kinesiophobia group in the multivariable regression analysis, whereas independent mediation effects were observed in both groups in the mediation model. This difference indicates that diabetes distress may be related to kinesiophobia through different association patterns across profiles. In clinical care, for patients with low exercise self-efficacy and prominent emotional burden, evidence from systematic reviews [49, 50] supports incorporating mindfulness-based interventions or cognitive-behavioral strategies into supportive care plans to alleviate diabetes distress and improve self-management-related outcomes.

These findings may be further understood from the biological and psychological connections between diabetes distress and fall-related vulnerability. In older adults with T2DM, fall risk is not merely a physical safety issue, but may be related to diabetes-associated physiological changes, including peripheral neuropathy, visual impairment, hypoglycemia, lower-limb weakness, and impaired gait or postural control, all of which may compromise sensory feedback and balance stability [7, 51]. At the psychological level, diabetes distress may be associated with greater symptom vigilance and threat appraisal. Patients with higher emotional burden may pay greater attention to numbness, pain, dizziness, or unsteadiness, and may interpret these bodily sensations as warning signs of falling or injury. Recent evidence also suggests that diabetes distress is an important factor associated with fear of falling among older adults with diabetes [52]. Therefore, diabetes distress and fall risk may interact as psychological and physical vulnerabilities, jointly associated with movement-related fear and activity avoidance in this population.

Interestingly, the indirect association jointly involving diabetes distress and fall risk was statistically significant for the comparison between the moderate and low kinesiophobia profiles, but not for the comparison between the high and low profiles. A possible explanation is that moderate kinesiophobia may represent a potentially modifiable profile in which movement-related fear is closely associated with psychological burden and perceived physical vulnerability. In this subgroup, lower exercise self-efficacy may be linked to greater diabetes distress, which was associated with greater fall-related concerns and higher odds of belonging to the moderate rather than the low kinesiophobia profile. By contrast, high kinesiophobia may reflect a more consolidated and generalized fear-avoidance pattern. Previous reviews have suggested that kinesiophobia and fear avoidance in older adults are closely related to physical inactivity and disability [44], and that the management of kinesiophobia often requires exercise-based and multidisciplinary strategies rather than a single intervention approach [53]. Thus, in patients with high kinesiophobia, movement-related fear may be maintained by accumulated illness experiences, previous negative exercise experiences, habitual avoidance, and maladaptive fear beliefs, rather than by a single specified indirect-association pattern involving distress and fall risk. In addition, the smaller size of the high-kinesiophobia subgroup and possible restricted variability in fear-related responses may have reduced the ability to detect a significant indirect association involving both diabetes distress and fall risk. Given the cross-sectional design, this subgroup-specific finding should be interpreted as an indirect association within a theory-driven model rather than evidence of a confirmed causal process.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the temporal relationships among exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, fall risk, and kinesiophobia.Therefore, the specified order among variables should be interpreted as a theory-driven analytical specification rather than evidence of an actual chronological process. Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine the dynamic evolution of these variables and the temporal stability of the indirect-association patterns. Second, this single-center convenience sample may limit generalizability, although participants came from different regions of Hebei Province. Multicenter studies with broader populations are needed to validate our findings. Third, all key variables were measured using self-report instruments, which may introduce reporting bias. Future studies could incorporate more objective indicators, such as documented fall events or device-based physical activity measures, to improve data validity. Fourth, insulin use, hypoglycemic episodes, and other factors that may influence kinesiophobia were not included in the analysis. History of falls was included as one component of the MFS but was not examined as a separate independent variable. Although diabetic peripheral neuropathy and diabetic foot were reported and analyzed as baseline characteristics, the severity of neuropathy, ulcer status, pain intensity, and objective functional limitations were not further assessed. These factors may independently restrict exercise participation and shape exercise perceptions, rather than reflecting kinesiophobia alone. Future studies should include, adjust for, or stratify by these variables. Fifth, although the MFS was administered using standardized scoring criteria and previous studies have shown excellent inter-rater reliability, inter-rater reliability was not re-estimated in the present study because each participant was assessed once. Future studies may include multiple raters or repeated assessments to further verify rating consistency.

Future studies should address several specific questions. First, multicenter longitudinal studies with repeated assessments are needed to examine transitions between kinesiophobia profiles and to determine the temporal ordering among exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, fall risk, and profile membership. Such studies should incorporate objective and prospective outcomes, including accelerometer-measured physical activity, documented fall events, glycated hemoglobin, and quality of life. Second, larger samples including hospitalized, outpatient, and community-dwelling older adults with T2DM are needed to externally validate the three-profile solution and improve its generalizability. Future analyses should also account for uncertainty in latent-profile classification. Third, profile-tailored interventions should be evaluated in randomized controlled trials. For patients with moderate kinesiophobia, interventions could combine diabetes-distress management, balance and fall-prevention training, and individualized exercise guidance. For patients with high kinesiophobia, more intensive strategies, such as cognitive restructuring, graded exposure to safe movement, supervised exercise, and mastery-based approaches to rebuilding exercise self-efficacy, may be required. Changes in profile membership, physical activity, falls, glycemic control, and quality of life should be evaluated as intervention outcomes.

Conclusions

This study identified three latent profiles of kinesiophobia among older adults with T2DM, showing a graded pattern of movement-related fear from low to high severity. The indrect-association analysis indicated that diabetes distress and fall risk were involved in the association between exercise self-efficacy and kinesiophobia profile membership, although causal inference cannot be made from the cross-sectional data.

Clinically, these findings suggest that the management of kinesiophobia in older adults with T2DM should move beyond general screening or a single-factor approach and instead adopt a risk-stratified, profile-oriented strategy. Routine nursing assessment should integrate kinesiophobia screening with evaluation of exercise self-efficacy, diabetes distress, and fall risk, so that patients vulnerable to moderate or high kinesiophobia can be identified earlier. For individuals with moderate kinesiophobia, combined psychological support, enhancement of exercise self-efficacy, and balance or physical function training may help address the accumulation of psychological burden and perceived physical vulnerability. For those with high kinesiophobia, interventions may need to place greater emphasis on maladaptive fear beliefs, using cognitive restructuring, graded exposure, individualized exercise prescriptions, and safety monitoring to help patients rebuild confidence in exercise participation. Overall, integrating psychological burden and physical vulnerability into a unified clinical assessment framework may help guide individualized nursing interventions, promote safer exercise participation, and improve exercise adherence among older adults with T2DM.
