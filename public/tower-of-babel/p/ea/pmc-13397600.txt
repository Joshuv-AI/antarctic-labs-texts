Rapid phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing of Gram-negative bloodstream isolates: clinical evaluation of the LifeScale AST system
Authors: Shikha Chaudhary, Noah Henry, Katelyn Teckenbrock, Michelle Hege, Nicholas Quitoriano, Arundhati Rao, Manohar Mutnal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBloodstream infections (BSIs) and sepsis require rapid initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy; however, conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) methods are time-consuming and may delay targeted treatment. Rapid phenotypic AST performed directly from positive blood cultures may reduce time to effective therapy and support antimicrobial stewardship.MethodsThe analytical performance and turnaround time (TAT) and the time-to-result (TTR) of the LifeScale AST system were evaluated using monomicrobial Gram-negative organisms from positive blood cultures. A total of 156 adult blood culture isolates were tested in parallel using the LifeScale system and the standard-of-care (SoC) MicroScan WalkAway system. Performance was assessed across 14 antimicrobial agents using Essential agreement (EA), adjusted Categorical agreement (CA), and categorical error rates in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2025 breakpoints. Discrepant results were resolved by broth microdilution (BMD).ResultsAcross 1,360 organism–antimicrobial combinations, the LifeScale AST system demonstrated an overall EA of 97.72% and an adjusted CA of 95.81% following discrepancy resolution. No very major errors remained after adjudication; the final major and minor error rates were 0.37% and 3.90%, respectively. Median TAT was 8.6 h for the LifeScale system compared with 48.1 h for the SoC method, corresponding to a median reduction of 39.5 h and an approximately 80% decrease in TAT; the mean TTR was 4.2 h. ConclusionsThe LifeScale AST system demonstrated high analytical agreement with the SoC method while substantially reducing TAT for AST testing of Gram-negative bloodstream infection isolates. Rapid availability of reliable phenotypic susceptibility results directly from positive blood cultures may enable earlier optimization of antimicrobial therapy and support antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in the management of sepsis. Background

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) represent a major global health concern, often leading to sepsis, multi-organ failure, and death if not promptly managed. They pose a significant threat to critically ill patients, immunocompromised individuals, and those with underlying health conditions, contributing to prolonged hospital stays and increased healthcare costs. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapies, timely and accurate identification of pathogens and their antimicrobial susceptibility remains critical for effective BSI management [1].

In addition, the rising prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) complicates the empirical treatment of these infections. As microorganisms evolve resistance to even the most potent therapies, the effectiveness of modern medicine is progressively undermined [2]. AMR not only jeopardizes individual health but also imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems, economies, and societies that depend on effective infection control. According to a review and the World Health Organization (WHO), drug-resistant infections could result in 10 million deaths annually by 2050 [3, 4], a figure that would surpass current cancer-related fatalities.

Conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) methods widely used in the clinical microbiology labs, are often time-consuming, typically taking 48 to 96 h to yield results [5, 6]. This delay in diagnosis and targeted therapy can have severe consequences, particularly for patients with life-threatening infections who require immediate and effective treatment. As a result, healthcare professionals frequently rely on empirical antibiotics or broad-spectrum antibiotics as an initial treatment strategy. While this approach is necessary in urgent situations, it can inadvertently contribute to the development of AMR in bacteria. Over time, the non-judicious or overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics fosters resistance, making infections harder to treat and increasing the risk of unfavourable patient outcomes [7].

Furthermore, the prolonged use of empirical or higher-class antibiotics not only accelerates the development of AMR but also raises the risk of adverse drug reactions and secondary healthcare associated infections such as Clostridium difficile, MRSA and resistant urinary tract infections. Approximately half of antibiotic treatments nowadays are initiated without a precise diagnosis of the pathogen or optimal antibiotic selection [8–10].

Conventional microbiological diagnostics remain time-consuming, often resulting in excessive or inappropriate antimicrobial use. This underscores the urgent need for faster AST technologies that can expedite treatment decisions, improve patient outcomes, and strengthen stewardship efforts. By providing accurate susceptibility profiles within a shorter timeframe, rapid AST platforms enable earlier initiation of appropriate therapy, help reduce unnecessary antimicrobial exposure, and mitigate the spread of AMR, particularly in the context of BSIs [5, 11–13].

To address this need, we conducted a study evaluating the LifeScale AST system (Affinity Biosensors, Santa Barbara, California, USA), a semi-automated platform for rapid susceptibility testing of Gram-negative organisms directly from positive blood cultures. The analytical performance, error rates, and turnaround time of the LifeScale system were assessed in comparison with the standard-of-care (SoC) MicroScan WalkAway system (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA).

Methods

Study design and objectives

This study was conducted at Baylor Scott & White Health Medical Center, Temple, TX, a large tertiary care teaching hospital and regional referral center with approximately 640 beds. The study was performed over a prospective collection period from March 2023 through April 2025. During this period, prospective residual samples from positive blood cultures were collected, limited to those confirmed as monomicrobial. Gram-negative status was established using Gram stain and a rapid molecular diagnostic assay, such as the VERIGENE® BC-GN system (Diasorin Molecular LLC, USA).

A total of 156 positive blood culture specimens from adult patients were included in the study. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible specimens available within the defined study period. This cohort was sufficient to assess essential agreement, categorical agreement, and error rates across multiple antimicrobial agents, and is consistent with sample sizes reported in comparable studies evaluating rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing methodologies [12].

The analytical performance of the LifeScale AST system was evaluated in comparison with the standard-of-care (SoC) MicroScan WalkAway system across a panel of 14 clinically relevant antimicrobial agents (Table 1). Performance was assessed based on Essential agreement (EA), adjusted Categorical agreement (CA), discrepancy rates, including Minor (MIN), Major (MAJ) and Very Major (VMJ) errors, in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2025 breakpoints [14]. Agreement between the LifeScale AST system and the SoC method was further assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic based on categorical susceptibility results.

The study also compared the turnaround time (TAT) of the LifeScale and the SoC method. The TAT was defined as the interval from the time a blood culture flagged positive in the BACT/ALERT® system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) to the time antimicrobial susceptibility results were available for clinical reporting. TAT was calculated separately for the LifeScale AST system and the SoC MicroScan WalkAway system to allow direct comparison of time to result between methods. In addition, instrument time, defined as time-to-result (TTR), was measured for the LifeScale AST system as the interval from loading of the prepared sample onto the instrument to generation of final susceptibility results. This study was reviewed and approved by the Baylor Scott & White Health Institutional Review Board -Red (IRB #023 − 015). Clinical trial number: Not applicable.

Sample collection and pathogen identification

Blood culture specimens that flagged positive in the BACT/ALERT® system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) were prospectively enrolled in the study within 12 h of positivity. Monomicrobial Gram-negative pathogens were identified using the Verigene® Gram-Negative Blood Culture (BC-GN) molecular panel (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA), with Gram staining performed to confirm the presence of Gram-negative rods. The presence of antimicrobial resistance genes, including CTX-M, was recorded based on the Verigene® test results. In addition, phenotypic susceptibility results from the MicroScan WalkAway system were reviewed to assess concordance with ESBL-associated resistance patterns.

For positive blood cultures identified as Enterobacter spp. by Verigene®, additional species-level resolution was performed on the next day when colonies were grown on the solid media using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). Only Enterobacter species supported by the LifeScale AST system (i.e., Klebsiella aerogenes) were identified via MALDI-TOF and included in the final dataset analysis; other Enterobacter species were excluded. Additionally, blood cultures demonstrating polymicrobial growth and pediatric blood culture bottles were excluded from the study.

LifeScale AST system workflow

Following organism identification, positive blood culture specimens containing monomicrobial Gram-negative organisms were processed for AST using the LifeScale AST system. The LifeScale AST system is a benchtop analyzer for AST that employs a microfluidic sensor to measure the mass of individual microorganisms. Growth metrics, including microbial concentration and mass distribution, are used to determine Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) values and interpretive categories of Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R).

For LifeScale AST testing, a 1.5 mL aliquot was collected directly from each positive BACT/ALERT blood culture bottle and added into the centrifuge vial. The mixture was centrifuged on LifeScale AST system pre-programmed centrifuge for 5 min, and 1 mL of the supernatant was removed and transferred to an automated pipetting system (Integra Biosciences Assist) for preparation of the required dilutions prior to loading onto the LifeScale AST system. In addition, two separate 1 mL aliquots were taken directly from the same positive blood culture bottle at the time of testing and stored at -80 °C for potential future discrepancy analysis. An inoculum was prepared from the aliquot using automated pipetting system and the 96-well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Sensitire™) was then incubated for 3 h at 37 °C before reading the plates on LifeScale instrument. In parallel, a purity plate was inoculated from the positive growth well on the 96-well plate and incubated for 18–24 h at 37 °C, to verify the QC and clinical sample’s purity ensuring no contamination occurred during the process.

The organism’s identification (genus and species) were then recorded in the LifeScale AST system. The microbial growth was analyzed by the LifeScale system post-incubation, generating MIC values and interpretive categories (S/I/R), for each antibiotic on the plate.

Standard-of-care antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The AST using the SoC method was performed in parallel with LifeScale testing using the MicroScan WalkAway system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following organism identification, isolates were subcultured and inoculated into MicroScan Gram-negative susceptibility panels. Panels were incubated and read automatically by the SoC system. The MIC values and corresponding interpretive categories of S/I/R were assigned. The results were used as the comparator SoC method for performance evaluation of the LifeScale AST system.

Discrepancy resolution

Results generated by the LifeScale AST system were compared directly with those obtained using the SoC MicroScan WalkAway system to assess analytical agreement. Discrepant antimicrobial susceptibility results between the two methods were further evaluated by broth microdilution (BMD) reference testing using Sensititre CLSI BMD panels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in accordance with CLSI guidelines [14]. Discrepancies were defined as either essential disagreement, defined as MIC values differing by more than ± one doubling dilution between methods (accounting for end-of-panel ranges), or categorical disagreement, including MIN, MAJ, and VMJ errors, in accordance with CLSI guidelines. VMJ were defined as false-susceptible results, MAJ as false-resistant results, and MIN as discrepancies involving an intermediate result versus susceptible or resistant. The modal MIC value obtained from BMD testing was used as the adjudicated result.

Quality control

Throughout the study, weekly Quality Control (QC) tests were conducted to verify the LifeScale AST system’s performance. Each week, one of several well-characterized organisms was selected on a rotating basis for the QC control, including Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, E. coli ATCC 35,218, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29,212.

Results

Sample collection and organism distribution

A total of 156 monomicrobial Gram-negative blood culture isolates from adult patients were included in the analysis, representing 1,360 organism-antibiotic combinations evaluated across the antimicrobial panel. The organism distribution comprised predominantly Escherichia coli (114/156, 73.1%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (23/156, 14.7%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (12/156, 7.7%). Less frequently isolated organisms included Klebsiella oxytoca (3/156, 1.9%), Klebsiella aerogenes (2/156, 1.3%), Acinetobacter species (1/156, 0.6%), and Klebsiella variicola (1/156, 0.6%). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing performance

The analytical performance of the LifeScale AST system was evaluated by comparison with the SoC using the EA, CA, and error rates such as VMJ, MJ and MIN. Following discrepancy resolution, the LifeScale system demonstrated an overall EA of 97.72% and an adjusted CA of 95.81% across all organism–antimicrobial combinations. After discrepant analysis, no VMJ remained. The final MAJ rate was 0.37% (4/1,091), and the MIN rate was 3.90% (53/1,360). Among individual antimicrobial agents, categorical agreement was ≥ 90% for all antibiotics except cefazolin, which demonstrated a lower adjusted categorical agreement of 82.48% following discrepancy resolution. This reduced agreement was primarily driven by minor categorical discrepancies near interpretive breakpoint concentrations, with no VMJ or MAJ observed for cefazolin.

A single minor error was observed for meropenem, occurring in a Pseudomonas aeruginosa sample. No very major or major errors were identified for meropenem across all evaluated positive blood culture samples, including Escherichia coli and Klebsiella species. Given the clinical importance of meropenem in the treatment of Gram-negative infections, the absence of very major and major errors for these organisms is notable. Additionally, several other clinically relevant antimicrobial agents demonstrated no very major or major errors following discrepancy analysis, consistent with the overall high level of agreement.

The overall agreement between the LifeScale AST system and the SoC method demonstrated a Cohen’s kappa value of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.85–0.89), indicating a high level of agreement.

Resolution of discrepancies eliminated all VMJ and reduced overall error rates. Detailed VMJ and MAJ cases and their resolution status are summarized in Tables 4 and 5, respectively. Discrepancies were most frequently observed among Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, followed by Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae.

Turnaround time

The TAT for the AST testing was compared between the LifeScale AST system and the SoC method. Median TAT was 8.6 h for the LifeScale AST system, compared with 48.1 h for the SoC method. Mean TAT ± Standard Deviation (SD) were 9.5 ± 3.7 h for LifeScale and 47.2 ± 10.7 h for SoC system. The median difference in time to result between methods was 39.5 h, corresponding to an approximately 80% reduction in TAT with the LifeScale system (Fig. 1). In addition to overall TAT, the instrument time i.e. TTR for the LifeScale AST system was evaluated. The mean TTR was 4.2 h and was consistent across organisms, including Escherichia coli (4.2 h), Klebsiella spp. (4.0 h), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (4.5 h), and Acinetobacter spp. (4.2 h). For organisms with low sample counts, TTR was summarized at the genus level (Table 6).

In addition, CTX-M genes were detected in 26 of the 156 isolates using the Verigene® system, confirming the inclusion of clinically relevant ESBL-associated resistance mechanisms within the evaluated population. The concordance between genotypic (CTX-M) detection and phenotypic ESBL results was 100%. Among CTX-M positive isolates, 21 (80.76%) were identified as Escherichia coli, 3 (11.53%) as Klebsiella pneumoniae, and 2 (7.69%) as Klebsiella oxytoca. Given the limited number of CTX-M positive isolates (n = 26), subgroup performance analysis was not performed.

Discussion

In this study, the LifeScale AST system demonstrated high analytical concordance with the SoC MicroScan WalkAway system when testing monomicrobial Gram-negative organisms directly from positive blood cultures. Overall, EA and adjusted CA were 97.72% and 95.81%, respectively. These findings are comparable to previously reported evaluations of the LifeScale platform and support its reliability in a clinically relevant workflow [12, 15, 16].

While overall agreement between methods was high, a small number of categorical discrepancies were observed. A small number of VMJ were identified for Ceftazidime and Piperacillin/ Tazobactam and MAJ were observed for antibiotics such as Piperacillin/ Tazobactam and Trimethoprim/ Sulfamethoxazole. In the discrepant cases involving Piperacillin/ Tazobactam and Trimethoprim/ Sulfamethoxazole, the LifeScale AST system yielded higher MIC values compared to the reference method, resulting in resistant categorizations. Most discrepancies were resolved following adjudication by BMD, resulting in no VMJ and a final MAJ rate of 0.37%. These findings are consistent with previous work by Snyder et al. [12] who reported similar discrepancy patterns with the LifeScale system. Together, these observations highlight the importance of ongoing proficiency testing and updates to the system’s algorithms to ensure the highest quality results, a necessity for accurate therapy selection and in combating AMR [17].

An important advantage of the LifeScale system is its markedly reduced TAT. This efficiency is particularly critical for managing cases that require urgent antibiotic interventions, such as sepsis, where delays in appropriate therapy can significantly impact patient outcomes. In this study, the LifeScale system demonstrated a median TAT of approximately 8.6 h representing an approximately 80% reduction compared with the SoC method. The mean instrument time (TTR) of approximately 4.2 h indicates that the testing is completed within a relatively short, automated runtime. Rapid availability of susceptibility data is particularly critical for the management of severe infections such as sepsis, where delays in administration of appropriate therapy are associated with adverse clinical outcomes. In the context of the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance, rapid TAT combined with reliable phenotypic detection may help limit unnecessary use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, enabling timely, evidence-based treatment decisions [18–20]. Comparable reductions in TAT for LifeScale testing performed directly from positive blood cultures have been reported previously [12], reinforcing the potential clinical value of this approach. By facilitating earlier optimization of antimicrobial therapy, the LifeScale system has the potential to support antimicrobial stewardship efforts, reduce the risk of treatment failure, and contribute to improved patient outcomes.

The detection of CTX-M genes among a subset of study isolates indicates the presence of clinically relevant ESBL-associated resistance mechanisms within the evaluated bloodstream infection population. CTX-M screening was performed for descriptive and identification purposes using the Verigene® molecular assay and was not intended as a separate performance comparison of phenotypic susceptibility results between the LifeScale and SoC methods based on ESBL status. Although CTX-M-positive isolates were identified, the number was insufficient for formal subgroup performance analysis. Among the 26 CTX-M positive isolates identified in this study, Escherichia coli was the most prevalent (80.76%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (11.53%) and Klebsiella oxytoca (7.69%). This distribution is consistent with the well-documented predominance of ESBL production among E. coli and Klebsiella species, which are major contributors to healthcare-associated and community-onset infections worldwide [21, 22]. The predominance of CTX-M enzymes observed in this study aligns with global epidemiological trends identifying CTX-M as the dominant ESBL variant, particularly in community isolates [23, 24].

Extended-spectrum β-lactamase production is well recognized to confer resistance to multiple β-lactam agents, including commonly used cephalosporins and aztreonam, often necessitating the use of broader-spectrum agents such as carbapenems [25]. In clinical practice, the presence of such resistance mechanisms is relevant to antimicrobial selection in Gram-negative bloodstream infections and provides additional context for the value of timely phenotypic susceptibility results.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, this was a single-center study conducted at a large tertiary care institution, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings. Second, the sample size was based on the number of eligible specimens available during the study period and, while sufficient for analytical performance evaluation, may not capture the full spectrum of variability seen in broader clinical populations. Third, the microbiological distribution was predominantly composed of Escherichia coli. While this reflects the commonly observed epidemiology of Gram-negative bloodstream infections, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to clinical settings with a broader diversity of pathogens or a higher prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms. Finally, polymicrobial blood cultures were excluded from this study, and therefore the performance of the LifeScale AST system in such cases was not assessed. Future studies including multi-center data, a broader range of organisms, and polymicrobial infections would further strengthen the evaluation of this platform across different clinical contexts.

The rapid turnaround time and high level of agreement observed with the LifeScale AST system have important implications for patient safety. Timely and accurate antimicrobial susceptibility results can support earlier optimization of antimicrobial therapy, reducing reliance on broad-spectrum empirical treatment and minimizing the risk of inappropriate antimicrobial use. This is particularly relevant in the management of bloodstream infections, where delays in effective therapy are associated with adverse clinical outcomes. In this context, the ability to provide reliable susceptibility results within a shortened timeframe may contribute to improved antimicrobial stewardship and more informed clinical decision-making and may potentially reduce adverse clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings indicate that the LifeScale AST system provides high analytical agreement with the SoC method while substantially reducing turnaround time for Gram-negative bloodstream infections. The system was evaluated using Gram-negative bloodstream infection isolates that encompassed clinically relevant resistance mechanisms, including ESBL-associated resistance. By delivering susceptibility results within hours rather than days, the LifeScale system has the potential to enable earlier optimization of antimicrobial therapy, reduce unnecessary use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, and support antimicrobial stewardship efforts, particularly in the management of severe infections such as sepsis. Overall, these results support the LifeScale AST system as a promising rapid phenotypic testing platform for clinical microbiology laboratories.
