Plasma hippuric acid as a marker of frailty influenced by dietary fruit and vegetable consumption: longitudinal analysis in Italian cohorts of older adults
Authors: Giulia De Simone, Chiara Stival, Michele Rossi, Annalisa Davin, Alessia Giannetta, Carlotta Micaela Jarach, Mirco Luciano Randazzo, Toshiko Tanaka, Aurelia Morabito, Silvano Gallus, Stefania Bandinelli, Luigi Ferrucci, Antonio Guaita, Alessandra Lugo, Alice Passoni, Laura Brunelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrailty is a geriatric syndrome characterised by loss of function and increased risk of death. Identification of individuals at risk of frailty is crucial for effective preventive interventions. This study aimed to investigate the association between plasma hippuric acid concentrations and frailty across two longitudinal Italian cohorts. Plasma hippuric acid was quantified using European Medicines Agency-validated mass spectrometry methods in plasma samples from InCHIANTI (n = 534; 55.4% females; aged ≥65 years, 1998, 2002, 2004, 2008 follow-ups, FU) and InveCe.Ab (n = 912; 53.8% females; aged ≥70 years, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2018 FU) participants who were non-frail at baseline. Frailty was defined through two different classifications: frailty index (FI) and frailty phenotype (FP). Longitudinal association between hippuric acid and frailty indexes within the two cohorts was assessed by linear mixed models adjusting for sex, age, educational status, renal and liver function, and body mass index. Mediation analyses explored the role of hippuric acid in mediating the fruit and vegetable consumption-frailty relationship. Median values of hippuric acid concentrations were significantly lower in frail compared to non-frail participants (measured by FI) in both (i) InCHIANTI study: non-frail = 1.59 (Q1–Q3, 0.99–2.74) μg/ml vs frail = 1.18 (0.75–1.88) (μg/mL in 2004 year FU; non-frail = 1.44 (0.87–2.50) μg/ml vs frail = 1.12 (0.63–1.99) μg/ml in 2007 year FU; and (ii) InveCe.Ab study: non-frail = 2.19 (1.26–3.92) μg/ml vs frail = 1.81 (0.87–3.65) μg/ml in 2018 year FU. No statistically significant cross-sectional associations were observed between hippuric acid and FP. Longitudinal association showed that higher plasma hippuric acid levels were significantly associated with a reduced risk of frailty (p = 0.026 InCHIANTI and p = 0.044 InveCe.Ab). Longitudinal mediation analysis indicated that hippuric acid act as mediator of the relationship between fruit and vegetable intake and FI (p < 0.05) in both cohorts. Low plasma hippuric acid levels may serve as a marker of frailty, potentially linked to reduced fruit and vegetable intake.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Epidemiological studies show that 11% of the world’s population is over the age of 60, with a projected growth to rise to 22% by 20501. Consequently, the burden of age-related morbidities is increasing, and the importance of health span is being emphasized2,3. Frailty is a geriatric syndrome characterised by a decline in the physiological reserves resulting in increased vulnerability to stressors. Although frailty is closely related to aging, the degree of frailty differs among people of the same age. As a result, identifying people at risk of developing frailty when preventive interventions are much more likely to be effective is an urgent need for health care system4. Despite observational cross-sectional studies have identified biochemical, genetic, and inflammatory markers associated with frailty5, there has been limited longitudinal investigations into the factors casually associated with frailty development with conflicting results6–8.

Frailty status is typically assessed using validated measurement tolls, with the two most widely adopted being the ‘Frailty phenotype’ (FP) and the ‘Frailty index’ (FI). The FP evaluates frailty based on five criteria: weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, walking slowness, and reduced physical activity9. The FI combines a range of progressive impairments into a composite score8. While both methods are effective for determining frailty, they have limited insight into the biological mechanisms underlining frailty development10. In this context, hippuric acid has emerged as a potential marker related to aging and geriatric syndromes, including frailty. Hippuric acid is produced through the hepatic conjugation of glycine and benzoic acid, derived from gut microbial metabolism of polyphenol compounds present in food or from the gut bacterial metabolism of phenylalanine. Older adults with frailty and other geriatric syndromes typically exhibit lower plasma and urine levels of hippuric acid, despite evidence that hippuric acid levels increase during healthy aging11.

This study aimed to investigate the longitudinal association between plasma hippuric acid levels and frailty and its possible predictive value, assessed using both FI and FP in two Italian longitudinal cohorts of older adults. Moreover, we examined the role of hippuric acid as a mediator of the relationship between fruit and vegetable consumption and frailty outcomes in two Italian longitudinal cohorts.

Results

Study design

The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of InCHIANTI and InveCe.Ab cohorts are summarised in Tables 1 and 2 respectively. In the InCHIANTI cohort at baseline (1998), the mean age was 71.7 years (standard deviation, SD = 4.8), 55.4% were female, 47.6% were overweight, 0.4% had a severely decreased renal function (eGFR between >90 mL/min, in line with ages), 3.6% had an altered liver function, and 56.9% reported elementary school as the highest level of education. No statistically significant differences have been observed in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics between subjects with missing and non-missing values, besides in 2007, where subjects with missing values were older and less educated (data not shown). Frail individuals classified by the FI accounted for 4.4%, 15.4%, and 19.6% of participants in 2001, 2004, and 2007 weaves, respectively (Fig. 1A). Using the FP classification, frail individuals accounted for 5.1%, 7.9%, and 14.3% in 2001, 2004, and 2007, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1B). At baseline (2010), participants in the InveCe.Ab cohort had a mean age of 72.8 years (SD = 1.4), were predominantly female (53.8%), overweight (44.5%), 1.5% had severely impaired renal function (CIRS classification >2) and 1.5% had severely impaired liver function. The majority reported elementary school as their highest level of education completed (54.9%). Frail individuals classified by FI accounted for 5.3%, 9.3%, and 24.9% of participants in 2012, 2014, and 2018, respectively (Fig. 1B).

Cross-sectional association between plasma hippuric acid levels and frailty status

At the 2004 and 2007 follow-ups, InCHIANTI cohort frail participants had significantly lower plasma hippuric acid levels in frail compared to non-frail subjects (median values: frail 1.18 (Q1–Q3, 0.75–1.88) and non-frail 1.59 (Q1–Q3, 0.99–2.74, p < 0.001) and 2007 (median values: frail 1.12 (Q1–Q3, 0.63–1.99) and non-frail 1.44 (Q1–Q3, 0.87–2.50, p = 0.018) when classified using the FI (Table 3). Among InveCe.Ab participants, those who were frail had statistically significant lower hippuric acid levels (p = 0.016) (median: 1.81; Q1–Q3, 0.87–3.65) than those who were non-frail (median: 2.19; Q1–Q3, 1.26–3.92) in 2018 while no differences were found in 2014 and 2012 follow-up visits. In the InCHIANTI cohort, no differences were observed based on sex; however, significantly lower plasma hippuric acid levels were found in InveCe.Ab frail females compared to their non-frail counterparts at both the 2014 and 2018 follow-ups. Specifically, in 2014, the hippuric acid median level in frail females was 1.59 (Q1–Q3, 0.67–2.49) versus 1.97 (Q1–Q3, 1.05–3.53) in non-frail females (p = 0.049). In 2018, frail females had a hippuric acid median level of 1.48 (Q1–Q3, 0.68–3.63) compared to 2.19 (Q1–Q3, 1.16–3.76) in non-frail females (p = 0.011). Considering the classifications of Fit, Pre-Frail, and Frail, no significant differences were noted among the three groups in the InCHIANTI cohort, except in 2004, when hippuric acid median levels in frail participants significantly differed from those in fit (p < 0.001) and pre-frail participants (p = 0.006) (Supplementary Table 1). In the InveCe.Ab cohort, while no significant differences were found among the three groups across all follow-ups, differences persisted among females in 2018, where plasma hippuric acid median levels were significantly lower in frail compared to pre-frail females (p = 0.012) (Supplementary Table 1).

Using the frailty phenotype classification in the InCHIANTI cohort, no significant differences in hippuric acid levels were observed between non-frail and frail subjects during any follow-up period (Supplementary Table 2).

Cross-sectional linear regression analyses, adjusted for sex, age, education, BMI, and renal function, indicated that higher log(hippuric acid) levels were associated with lower FI values in the 2004 follow-up in the InCHIANTI cohort and in the 2018 follow-up of the InveCe.Ab cohort (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Longitudinal association between plasma hippuric acid levels and frailty status

Longitudinal analyses of hippuric acid and FI trajectories over eight and nine years showed a significant association between the temporal decrement in log(hippuric acid) levels and increment of the FI scores over time. This association was observed in the InCHIANTI cohort (β = −0.004; p = 0.026) and the InveCe.Ab cohort (β = −0.002; p = 0.044) (Tables 4 and 5). Results found with a sensitivity analysis using the Cox regression models were consistent, although not statistically significant (β = −0.102; p = 0.208 for the InCHIANTI cohort and β = −0.030; p = 0.520 for the InveCe.Ab cohort; results not shown). Moreover, the regression analysis indicated an increased risk of frailty in older adults with impaired renal function in both cohorts. No longitudinal association was observed between log(hippuric acid) levels and the FP classification in the InCHIANTI cohort (Supplementary Table 5).

Cross sectional and longitudinal mediation analysis

We found an inverse association between Fruit–Veg consumption and FI. This association was evident in the 2018 follow-up of the InveCe.Ab cohort and in the 2004 follow-up of the InCHIANTI cohort (Supplementary Table 6). The role of plasma hippuric acid as a mediator of the relationship between Fruit–Veg consumption and frailty was explored by mediation analysis. In cross-sectional analyses at the 2018 follow-up of the InveCe.Ab population, the indirect effect of Fruit–Veg consumption on FI through plasma hippuric acid did not reach statistical significance (mean indirect effect = −0.00099; 95% CI: −0.00271–0.00014; p = 0.092), with an estimated mediated proportion of 6.3% (Supplementary Fig. 2A). No evidence of a cross-sectional mediation effect was observed in the InCHIANTI population at the 2004 follow-up (mean indirect effect = −0.00033; 95% CI: −0.00224–0.00083; p = 0.664), with a non-significant mediated proportion of 8% (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Notably, the longitudinal mediation analysis highlighted the mediating role of plasma hippuric acid in the association between Fruit–Veg consumption and FI trajectories in both cohorts. In the InveCe.Ab study, plasma hippuric acid mediated approximately 10.0% of the longitudinal association between Fruit–Veg consumption and FI scores (mean indirect effect = −0.00040; 95% CI: −0.00093 to −0.00006; p = 0.006) (Fig. 3A). In the InCHIANTI cohort, a statistically significant mediation effect was also observed, with a mediated proportion of 29.4% (mean indirect effect = −0.00060; 95% CI: −0.00140 to −0.00006; p = 0.022) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

In this study, we assess the ability of plasma hippuric acid to discriminate frail individuals and to investigate the relationship between plasma hippuric acid levels, fruit and vegetable consumption and frailty status, which was determined by two widely used clinical scales: the FI and FP. Plasma hippuric acid concentration was assessed in two longitudinal Italian cohorts of older adults monitored over eight-nine-year follow-ups at three different time points. Plasma hippuric acid quantification was performed using a EMA validated an analytical method, ensuring the robustness and reliability of the plasma measurements. Our findings, both cross-sectional and longitudinal, consistently demonstrated a significant association between declining plasma hippuric acid levels and the increased risk of frailty as assessed by the FI scale in adults aged over 65 years old. This association remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, education, BMI, renal function, and liver function. However, a sensitivity analysis performed using a Cox model, rather than mixed models, did not provide statistically significant results, although underling an inverse relationship between plasma hippuric acid levels and the risk of frailty. This results build upon limited scientific evidence from small cross-sectional studies, which linked reduced plasma and urinary hippuric acid levels to frailty and aging-related morbidity such as sarcopenia and impaired mobility in older adults11–14. Importantly, our results emphasises that plasma hippuric acid levels tend to increase with age in non-frail individuals, underscoring the significance of hippuric acid depletion as a marker for vulnerability outcomes in older adults. The observed increase in plasma hippuric acid during aging, in the absence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), aligns with previouse findings, reinforcing the known association between frailty and moderate-to-severe CKD15,16. Furthermore, a sex-stratified analysis within the InveCe.Ab cohort revealed that the association between plasma hippuric acid levels and frailty was more pronounced in females, while frail males exhibited a wide dispersion of hippuric acid levels across groups. In the InCHIANTI cohort sex-stratified analyses were not conducted due to an insufficient number of frail males at each follow-up. This sex-specific difference aligns with findings from the Global Ageing and Adult Health survey, which included 14,585 participants aged ≥65 years across six countries (China: n = 5360; Ghana: n = 1975; India: n = 2441; Mexico: n = 1375; Russia: n = 1950; South Africa: n = 1484). This survey reported a significant association between fruit consumption and reduced odds of sarcopenia, but notably, the effect was observed only in older women17.

Interestingly, no significant associations were observed between hippuric acid levels and frailty when older adults in the InCHIANTI cohort were classified using the FP, either cross-sectionally or longitudinally. This may be because the FP emphasises physical frailty attributes, while FI captures cumulative deficits and broader functional declines that more closely reflect physiological changes affecting hippuric acid precursor availability5 and its biosynthetic pathways.

In line with these findings, we observed that frail older adults consumed fruits and vegetables less frequently, which are primary dietary sources of hippuric acid derived from polyphenol-rich foods. This downward trend was evident in the later follow-ups of the InveCe.Ab cohort, and in the 2004 InCHIANTI wave, which had the largest sample size and the highest median frailty prevalence. Longitudinal mediation analysis in both cohorts further supported the role of hippuric acid as a mediator of the in the relationship between fruit and vegetable consumption and frailty. Although the interplay between fruit and vegetable consumption, hippuric acid levels, and frailty appears significant, these factors alone do not fully explain the observed associations. Other variables, such as gut microbiota, which play a key role in the biosynthetic pathways leading to hippuric acid synthesis. As hippuric acid is co-produced by host metabolism and gut microbiota, dysbiosis could impair its synthesis, thereby attenuating the protective effects of polyphenol-rich diets and contributing to heightened frailty in older adults.

This study also has limitations. Sex-stratified analyses were only conducted in the InveCe.Ab cohort due to the limited number of male frail individuals in the InCHIANTI cohort, which restricted the statistical power of sex-specific evaluations. As a result, findings related to sex differences should be interpreted with caution and require further validation. Additionally, while the two cohorts represent different Italian regions, the dietary homogeneity within these populations may have influenced the observed associations between hippuric acid levels, frailty, and diet. Future research should include more diverse populations with varying dietary patterns to improve the generalisability of these findings.

In conclusion, this study highlights the connection between hippuric acid, frailty, and fruit-vegetable intake in older adults in Italy. Overall, our findings indicate that hippuric acid levels, influenced by dietary fruit and vegetable consumption, may serve as a valuable biomarker for identifying frailty. Additionally, hippuric acid presents a promising target for interventions aimed at mitigating frailty and promoting healthy aging.

Methods

Study population and design

InCHIANTI study population

The Invecchiare in Chianti study (InCHIANTI, ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04899531) is a population-based aging study that investigates risk factors for late-life disability. Conducted in two municipalities near Florence, Italy (Bagno a Ripoli and Greve in Chianti), the study enroled 1270 participants aged 65 years and older at baseline (1998–2000) and followed them every 3 years (2001, 2004, 2007). At follow-up, blood samples were collected for biological analyses18. In this study, plasma samples were collected from 515 non-frail older adults (as classified by the frailty index) aged 65–85 years at baseline, all of whom completed examinations at least twice. Of these, 323 participants completed all follow-ups (Fig. 1A). According to the FP index, there were 534 non-frail older adults (aged 65–85 years) at baseline who participated in at least two examinations, and 477 completed all the follow-ups (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

Additionally, plasma samples and clinical data were obtained from 419 non-frail individuals (based on the FI index) aged 21–85 years at baseline to investigate hippuric acid age-related trends.

InveCe.Ab study population

The Invecchiamento Cerebrale in Abbiategrasso study cohort (InveCe.Ab; ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT01345110), is a longitudinal population-based study involving 1321 participants at baseline, born between 1935 and 1939 (aged between 70 and 75 years when the study started). The participants are of Caucasian ethnicity and reside in Abbiategrasso a town in the province of Milan19. The baseline assessment was conducted in 2010, followed by three subsequent follow-ups at 2, 4, and 8 years. At each follow-up, blood samples were collected from participants for biological analyses.

For this study, plasma samples were retrieved from 912 non-frail older adults (classify by the FI classification) at baseline who completed examinations at least twice. Among them, 608 partecipants completed all the examinations (Fig. 1A).

Our study examined male and female individuals, and sex-dimorphic effects were evaluated and reported.

All participants in both InCHIANTI and InveCe.Ab cohorts provided informed consent at each wave, and the use of plasma samples and clinical data for this study was approved by the ethical committee of the IRCCS Foundation Carlo Besta Neurological Institute on January 25 2023 (Minutes No. 13). The study procedures were in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and amendments.

Frailty scales

The frailty status of the InCHIANTI population was assessed using two different scales: the FP and the FI. The FP was defined according to the criteria proposed by Fried and colleagues9. Participants were categorised as: Fit (no criteria present); Pre-Frail (one or two criteria present); Frail (three–five criteria present). The FI was calculated following the standard procedure, as described by Hoogendijk et al.20, using a list of 42-health deficits covering various functional domains.

For the InveCe.Ab cohort, frailty status was assessed using the FI following the procedure described in Brunelli et al.6, monitoring a list of 32 health variables.

Participants were categorised by the FI into the following groups: Fit (FI ≤ 0.08), Pre-Frail (0.08 < FI < 0.25), and Frail (FI ≥ 0.25). These classifications align with established literature and provide a comprehensive measure of frailty in both cohorts21. Sankey diagrams (SankeyMATIC) are used to visualise frailty trajectories

Dietary habits

In the InCHIANTI study, food consumption was assessed using the Italian version of the Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), developed and validated as part of the European Prospective Study into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC)22. The frequency of food consumption was transformed into daily intake23. A composite score, called the ‘fruit–vegetable (Fruit-Veg) consumption’, was created by summing the daily intake scores for fruits and vegetables. This resulted in a synthetic variable ranging from 0 to 8.6.

In the InveCe.Ab study, the intake of fresh fruit and vegetables was obtained from simple questions on the current frequency of consumption (“Thinking about the last month, how often did you eat fresh fruit? How often did you eat raw vegetables?”)6,19. The variables reflecting fruit and vegetables consumption were rated on a scale from 0 to 3, representing 0, 1–2, or more than 3 times per week. The Fruit-Veg composite score was calculated by summing the fruit and vegetable scores, resulting in a synthetic variable ranging from 0 to 6.

In both cohorts, tertiles were used to categorise participants into low, medium, and high levels of fruit and vegetable consumption based on the distribution of the composite Fruit-Veg variable.

For the InCHIANTI population, data on total energy intake (kcal/day) were also available.

Renal function

In the InCHIANTI study, renal function was assessed using CKD status, estimated by the glomerular filtration rate (GFR). The equations used to estimate GFR have been reported elsewhere24. According to established criteria: GFR ≥ 90 mL/min represents no CKD (normal or high renal function), GFR between 45 and 90 mL/min represents a moderately increased risk of CKD (mildly or moderately decreased renal function), and GFR < 45 mL/min represents a very high risk of CKD leading to kidney failure, as reported in the literature (severely decreased renal function)25.

In the InveCe.Ab study, renal function was evaluated by the geriatrician who conducted anamnesis and physical examination using the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale-Geriatric (CIRS-G) (0: no signs or symptoms are present; 1: Signs or symptoms referable to renal function, which do not require treatment; 2: Renal function disorders are present that require treatment, but without reducing overall physical efficiency; 3: Renal failure requires complex treatment more than one drug or high doses of diuretics, reduces psychophysical efficiency and the prognosis is negative or guarded; 4: Renal failure requires dialysis and the prognosis is serious)26. This evaluation was then reviewed by another physician considering also the renal classification with the anamnesis (renal disease yes/no) and, if reported, the presence of severe chronic renal failure yes/not (dialysis treatment, anamnesis of acute renal failure on chronic failure, history of disease with uraemia, hyperkalemia, fluid overload and indication for dialysis, uraemic pericarditis, hepatorenal syndrome). If a discrepancy is detected, the supervising physician and the compiling physician must reach an agreement and/or proceed with a new verification with the patient or family members. For this study, renal function is scored as follows: score 0 or 1: absence of kidney impairments or asymptomatic kidney conditions (normal, mild renal function); score between 2 and 4: presence of varying levels of kidney pathologies (decreased renal function)27. This approach ensures a cohort-specific evaluation of renal function, considering the differences in methodology and population characteristics.

Liver function

In the InCHIANTI study, liver function was assessed by measuring plasma levels of glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT; also known as aspartate aminotransferase, AST) and glutamic pyruvic transaminase (GPT; also known as alanine aminotransferase, ALT). These enzymes were quantified using a commercial kit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) based on the UV kinetic method on a Roche analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Participants were categorised based on established clinical reference ranges: for baseline, 2001, and 2004 follow-ups, AST < 60 U/L and ALT < 40 U/L; for the 2007 follow-up, AST < 37 U/L (men) or <31 U/L (women) and ALT < 41 U/L (men) or <31 U/L (women). Normal liver function: both AST and ALT within reference ranges. Mild impairment: One enzyme above the reference range. Altered liver function: both enzymes above the reference ranges. Altered liver function: One or both enzymes above the reference ranges.

In the InveCe.Ab study, liver function was evaluated by the geriatrician who conducted anamnesis and physical examination using the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale-Geriatric (CIRS-G) as assessed for renal function. For this study, liver function is scored as follows: score 0 or 1: absence of liver impairments or asymptomatic liver conditions (normal, mild liver function); score between 2 and 4: presence of varying levels of liver pathologies (decreased liver function). This evaluation was then reviewed by another physician considering also the liver function classification with the anamnesis (hepatic disease yes/no) and, if reported by the physician, the presence of severe liver failure yes/no (criteria for severe liver function: liver cirrhosis classifiable as B–C stage of C H I LD- Pugh score28, history of hepatic encephalopathy, bleeding from oesophageal varices, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, ascites, and/or hepatorenal syndrome). If a discrepancy is detected, the supervising physician and the compiling physician must reach an agreement and/or proceed with a new verification with the patient or family members. This approach, similar to that of renal function, ensures a cohort-specific evaluation of liver function, considering the differences in methodology and population characteristics.

Chemicals and reagents

Methanol, acetonitrile, and formic acid of analytical grade (LC-MS grade) were purchased from Carlo Erba (Italy). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was sourced from Fisher Scientific (USA), bovine serum albumin (BSA) from Alfasigma (Italy), and standard plasma from Biopredic International (France). The automated preparative system, Extrahera, and the 96-well plates used for protein precipitation were supplied by Biotage (Sweden). Hippuric acid was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), and hippuric acid -d5 was obtained from ChemCruz (Germany). Standard solutions of hippuric acid and hippuric acid -d5 were prepared in water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and stored at 4 °C. Hippuric acid was subsequently diluted in water to concentrations of 400 µg/mL, 40 µg/mL, and 4 µg/mL. Hippuric acid-d5 was diluted to a concentration of 40 µg/mL.

Selection of the surrogate of the matrix

Four different plasma matrix surrogates were tested: water, PBS, and PBS supplemented with two different concentration of BSA (35 g/L and 50 g/L), reflecting the physiological range of the most abundant plasma protein30. To determine the optimal matrix surrogate, multiple calibration curves (N = 3) were generated and analysed, assessing linearity and slope.

Quality controls (QC)

QC samples were prepared using the selected surrogate matrix (PBS with BSA 35 mg/mL) at three concentrations levels according to the EMA guideline: low QC (LQC = within three times the lowest limit of quantitation; 0.300 µg/mL), medium QC (MQC = 30–50% of the calibration curve range; 3.000 µg/mL), and high QC (HQC = at least 75% of the upper calibration curve range; 30.00 µg/mL).

Native sample controls. also referred as endogenous quality controls (eQCs), were prepared by pooling plasma sample from voluntary donors. These eQC were employed to monitor assay performance over time using control cards31.

Linearity and limit of quantitation

The linearity of the detection method was evaluated by analysing the surrogate plasma matrix spiked with standards across a concentration range of 0.100–40.00 μg/mL comprising nine calibration points. Calibration curves were assessed over multiple analytical sessions (N = 6), with the mean measured concentration, standard deviation (SD), accuracy, and precision (expressed as coefficient of variation, CV%) calculated for the analyte. The lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) was defined as the concentration of the lowest calibration standard that met the criteria for accuracy and precision.

Accuracy and precision

The concentration of the analytes in QC samples was assessed across multiple analytical runs between-run accuracy) and within a single analytical run (within-run accuracy) to evaluate accuracy. Precision was determined as the coefficient of variation (CV%). Intra-day accuracy and precision were calculated by analysing six replicates of QC samples at each concentration level (LQC = 0.300 µg/mL, MQC = 3.000 µg/mL, HQC = 30.00 µg/mL) within a single analytical run. Inter-day accuracy and precision were determined by analysis of six replicates of each QC sample across three separate days.

Dilution integrity

The dilution integrity was assessed by using the surrogate matrix (PBS with BSA 35 mg/mL) and applying a 10-fold dilution factors. Samples were initially prepared at a concentration 2.5 times greater than the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ), at 100 µg/mL, and then diluted with a blank matrix (N = 6). This approach ensured the accuracy and reliability of the method when analysing samples that exceed the ULOQ.

Matrix effect and recovery

The matrix effect (ME) and recovery calculations posed a challenge due to the presence of hippuric acid in the plasma, making it difficult to obtain a completely blank matrix for testing. Therefore, ME and recovery were evaluated only for the deuterated hippuric acid (HA-d5) used as the internal standard, based on the assumption that the analyte and internal standard share identical chemical properties. The assessments were performed using an independent plasma matrix, with triplicate biological samples at two different concentration levels (LQC = 0.300 µg/mL and HQC = 30.00 µg/mL). The ME was calculated as the ratio of the peak area of hippuric acid-d5 added to the biological matrix after extraction to the peak area of the analyte added to a pure solution without the matrix (water). The recovery efficiency (extraction efficiency) was determined by comparing the peak area of hippuric acid-d5 spiked into matrix samples before extraction to the peak area of the same analyte spiked into the matrix after extraction.

Stability in the stock and working solutions

The long-term stability of stock and working solutions was assessed by storing them at 4 °C for a duration of 4 months, which corresponds to the time needed to analyse the samples of one study cohort. Stability testing was conducted by analysing the solutions at monthly intervals over the 4-month period. For each standard, the stock and working solutions were appropriately diluted (1:100) prior to testing to evaluate their stability over time. The results were analysed to ensure the consistency of the standards’ concentration and the reliability of the quantification method throughout the study period.

Analyte stability in plasma

The stability of hippuric acid in plasma was assessed under three distinct conditions, with six replicates for each condition: (i) Long-term storage stability: eQCs were stored at −20 °C for durations of 24 h, 1 week, and 1 month to assess stability over extended periods. (ii) Freeze-thaw stability: Plasma samples were stored at −80 °C and subjected to freeze-thaw cycles after 24 h, 1 week, and 1 month to evaluate any potential degradation upon thawing. (iii) Autosampler stability: eQC samples were kept at 4 °C in the autosampler for 24 h to evaluate the stability of hippuric acid in plasma during analysis. These stability assessments were performed to ensure that the method remained robust and reliable across varying storage and handling conditions, ensuring accurate quantification of hippuric across the study.

Batch effect control and correction (target value normalisation)

For data normalisation of the study samples, medium quality controls (MQCs) were used as reference standards. MQCs were randomly analysed on each analytical plate (three per plate) and served to calculate a correction factor (Fx) for each plate based on the mean concentration of the MQCs. To ensure consistency across batches, the analyte concentrations in the real samples were normalised by dividing each raw concentration by its corresponding plate-specific correction factor. Quality control charts, constructed with MQC and endogenous QC (eQC) data, were employed to monitor potential batch effects.

Plasma Hippuric acid extraction and quantification

A total of 20 μL of each plasma sample was spiked with hippuric acid-d5 (final concentration of 4 μg/mL) and loaded onto an Isolute PPT+ Protein Precipitation 96-well plate cartridge (Biotage, Sweden). Protein precipitation was performed using the Biotage Extrahera Automation System (Biotage, Sweden). This involved adding 100 μL of LC-MS grade Methanol (Carlo Erba, Italy) to the samples, followed by thorough mixing using repeated aspirate/dispense steps. The samples were then incubated at room temperature for 30 min and eluted by applying a pressure of 3 bar for 30 s. A 5 μL aliquot of the extract was injected into the LCMS8060 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer interfaced to an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (UHPLC) (Shimadzu). The mass spectrometric method was adapted from Brunelli et al.6, with slight modifications to improve chromatographic separation. A Gemini C18 (50 × 2 mm; 5 μm particle size; Phenomenex) was used to separate hippuric acid and hippuric acid -d5 employed a 7-min gradient, from 5% to 99% solvent B (acetonitrile), with solvent A (10 mM NH4HCO2 pH 3.5) at a flow rate of 450 μL/min, at 40 °C. The HPLC-MS/MS data were processed using LabSolution Insight LC–MS software (Shimadzu) for calibration curve plotting and quantification of hippuric acid plasma levels. hippuric acid plasma levels were evaluated for potential batch effects and corrected as needed.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for hippuric acid were presented as medians and Quartile 1–Quartile 3 (Q1–Q3). Differences between two groups (Non-Frail and Frail) were assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test, while differences among three groups (Fit, Pre-Frail, and Frail) were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Linear mixed models with random intercept were used to estimate the longitudinal association between hippuric acid level over time and FI in both populations, adjusted for sex, age, education, body mass index (BMI), renal and liver functions and survey year. A sensitivity analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazard models adjusted for sex, age education, body mass index (BMI), renal and liver functions and survey year

Since hippuric acid did not meet the assumption of normality, a logarithmic transformation was applied to the variable. In addition, linear regression models were performed to assess cross-sectionally the association between hippuric acid level and FI (using samples from the 2004 wave for the InCHIANTI population and the 2018 wave for the InveCe.Ab population). All models were adjusted for sex, age, education, BMI and renal function. For the InCHIANTI population, the longitudinal association between hippuric acid and FP was also tested, through multiple mixed logistic regression models after adjustment for sex, age, education, BMI, renal function and year of survey.

A mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the potential role of plasma hippuric acid as mediator in the association between fruit and vegetable consumption and FI.

Mediation analyses were conducted following Baron and Kenny’s methodological framework. Three models were fitted for each population: (1) the association between fruit and vegetable consumption and FI; (2) the association between fruit and vegetable consumption and the mediator (plasma hippuric acid); and (3) the association between fruit and vegetable consumption, the mediator, and FI. Cross-sectional mediation analyses were conducted using linear regression models, based on data from the 2004 InCHIANTI follow-up and the 2018 InveCe.Ab follow-up. Longitudinal mediation analyses were conducted using linear mixed models with random intercepts to account for repeated measurements within individuals. All models were adjusted for sex, age, education, BMI and renal function. For the InCHIANTI population, models were additionally adjusted for total energy intake.

The indirect effect was calculated as the product of the regression coefficients for fruit and vegetable consumption on plasma hippuric acid and plasma hippuric acid on FI. The significance of the mediation effect was assessed using a non-parametric bootstrap procedure with 1000 resamples, resampling at the individual level with replacement. For each resample, the indirect effect and the proportion of mediation were estimated. Confidence intervals were derived using percentile bootstrapping, and a two-tailed bootstrap p-value was computed to test the null hypothesis of no mediation.

Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated used to estimate the correlation between plasma hippuric acid levels and age in a cohort of non-frail individuals from the InCHIANTI cohort.

All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software, version 9.4 (Cary, North Carolina, USA), Prism, version 10.0 (GraphPad, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and R, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
