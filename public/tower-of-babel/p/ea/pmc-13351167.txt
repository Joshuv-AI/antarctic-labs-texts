An artificial intelligence model integrating clinico-laboratory data and single nucleotide polymorphism-based genomic risk for prostate cancer diagnosis in Korean men
Authors: Jae Hung Jung, Gong Ho Han, Beomgi So, Myunghee Hong, Si Hoon Ahn, Sung Hyun Lim, Soo Min Han, Hongzoo Park, Sang Wook Lee, Geehyun Song, Sukjung Choi, Hyun Chul Chung, Hong Chung, Tae Wook Kang, Minseob Eom, Sang Baek Koh, Jeong Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeProstate cancer (PCa) is traditionally diagnosed using prostate-specific antigen (PSA)-based testing together with demographic and clinical factors. Building on this framework, we aimed to develop an AI (artificial intelligence) model for prebiopsy PCa diagnosis by integrating Korean population-relevant risk-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to improve diagnostic accuracy.Materials and MethodsThree models were developed in this study: Korean PCa–specific genomic score (GenPCa-Kor score), electronic medical record (EMR) meta-model, and Geno-EMR meta-model. From genome-wide association study summary statistics, 1,347 PCa-associated SNPs were selected for a deep neural network to derive the GenPCa-Kor score. Thirteen clinico-laboratory EMR parameters were used to build a stacking ensemble (EMR meta-model) with Light Gradient Boosting Machine, and Histogram-based Gradient Boosting Machine, and logistic regression as base learners and logistic regression as the meta-learner, using 10-fold cross-validation and Bayesian hyperparameter optimization. The Geno-EMR meta-model added the GenPCa-Kor score as a 14th feature to the same architecture.ResultsOf 1,590 systematic biopsy-confirmed participants, 1,006 were analyzed; 757 comprised the training cohort and 249 consecutive patients comprised the independent test cohort. In the training cohort, the EMR meta-model and Geno-EMR meta-model achieved area under curves (AUCs) of 0.868 and 0.924, respectively. In the test cohort, their AUCs were 0.859 and 0.892, respectively. For clinically significant PCa (Grade Group ≥2), the Geno-EMR meta-model further improved the AUC from 0.887 to 0.911.ConclusionsThe Geno-EMR meta-model that integrate routine clinico-laboratory parameters with the SNP-based GenPCa-Kor score showed improved discrimination for PCa compared with the EMR meta-model alone.Keywords: Early detection of cancer; Genome-wide association study; Polymorphism, single nucleotide; Prostate cancer

INTRODUCTION

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignancies in men worldwide, and its incidence has been rising, particularly in East Asia including South Korea, alongside Westernized dietary habits and population aging. However, the conventional prostate-specific antigen (PSA)-based approach to PCa detection is limited by the threshold-dependent trade-off between sensitivity and specificity [1,2].

Recent research suggests that PCa is influenced by an interplay of clinical and genetic factors, including demographic characteristics (e.g., age), physiological measures (e.g., body mass index [BMI]), and inherited genetic variation [3,4]. In addition, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified genomic regions associated with PCa susceptibility, most commonly in the form of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). However, the effects and clinical relevance of PCa-associated SNPs can vary across ethnic groups [4,5]. Therefore, incorporating ethnicity-informed SNP markers may improve diagnostic performance.

With the integration of artificial intelligence (AI), researchers have increasingly leveraged clinical, laboratory, and genomic data to develop models that enhance PCa diagnosis. AI approaches span interpretable linear methods (e.g., logistic regression) and more complex non-linear models, such as deep learning and tree-based algorithms, which are well suited to high-dimensional data; together, these data-driven models can effectively manage and interpret such data and are yielding new insights into the diagnosis of PCa [6].

This study aims to develop an AI model that integrates clinico-laboratory variables with genomic features, with a particular focus on SNPs to support PCa diagnosis prior to biopsy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted on the participants who underwent systematic prostate biopsy from July 2013 to October 2023. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating institutions (Kangwon National University Hospital, approval number: KNUH-2021-09-010-005; Yonsei University Wonju Severance Christian Hospital, approval number: CR322048) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Three models (Korean PCa specific genomic score [GenPCa-Kor score] model, electronic medical record [EMR] meta-model, Geno-EMR meta-model) were developed in this study.

1. GenPCa-Kor score model

Exome genotype and quality control: GWAS was conducted using Infinium Global Screening Array-24v1.0 BeadChip (Illumina Inc.). As a part of quality control, SNPs with a missing genotype call rate less than 2%, deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p≤0.0001) were excluded. Further, all samples with more than 98% missing genotypes and increased heterozygosity were excluded. The association analysis was conducted using 732,752 remaining SNPs.

1) SNPs selection and data preprocessing

Genetic risk score (GRS), computed as the cumulative sum of variants across genomes, has emerged as a tool for predicting diseases in the context of intricate genomic variants [7]. However, the most significant challenge associated with GRS analysis is the curse of dimensionality, as the large number of SNPs results in a high-dimensional feature space. Therefore, given that GWASs typically investigate a large number of genetic variants, predominantly SNPs [8], we selected SNPs using the following three steps.

(1) The logistic analysis results from a total of 301,559 people (European 211,227; East Asian 90,332) were obtained from the GWAS catalog [9].

(2) The overlapping SNPs were identified based on the SNPs information provided by the used Illumina chipset and the p-value cut-off for SNPs associated with PCa from the GWAS catalog.

(3) A total of 1,347 SNPs were selected using the p-value cut-off of 10-4. Each SNPs value was calculated by multiplying minor allele numbers from the GWAS catalog with logit value used as an input values.

After preprocessing SNPs data, we employed deep neural networks to develop a novel score, GenPCa-Kor score (Fig. 1).

Structure of the neural networks that generate the Korean prostate cancer specific genomic score (GenPCa-Kor score). The input single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) values were determined based on the statistical data from genome-wide association study (GWAS) catalog. The process involves passing these values through a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5, followed by activation functions (exponential linear unit [ELU]), fully connected layers, and another dropout layer with a rate of 0.5. After this sequence, 1,347 values are produced. The weights of these values, also trained variables, ultimately contribute to the final GenPCa-Kor score. This score is then processed through a sigmoid function output for labeling as prostate cancer or benign, and the parameters are trained accordingly.

The GenPCa-Kor score model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001. A Warmup Cosine Schedule linearly was employed, where the learning rate was linearly increased during an initial warmup phase and then gradually decayed using cosine function. Early stopping was applied to prevent overfitting.

2. EMR meta-model

EMR data preprocessing: Sixteen clinico-laboratory parameters such as age, height, weight, prostate volume (PV), PSA, free PSA (fPSA), testosterone, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), history of prior biopsy, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, PCa family history, use of finasteride, presence of prostatic nodule on digital rectal exam (DRE) were collected from EMR.

1) Clinico-laboratory features selections

(1) Removal of clinical features influenced by subjective factors: IPSS and DRE.

(2) Selection of clinical features with a low null ratio: As the correlation between PSA and fPSA is high, fPSA was removed.

(3) Define new features: BMI was introduced as a replacement for weight and height. In addition, by defining PSA density (PSAD), derived from PV and PSA, was established as an influential clinical feature for PCa [10]. Although PSA and PSAD are inherently correlated, and reducing multicollinearity is beneficial in linear models such as logistic regression, tree-based non-linear models often achieve optimal results through empirical models training [11]. Therefore, despite the multicollinearity, key clinical features such as PV, PSA, and PSAD were retained for PCa screening due to their clinical significance.

2) Eliminating of nosy data

(1) Removal of patients with duplicated data: Duplicate biopsy results indicating multiple entries for the same patient in the biopsy outcomes, or biopsy results associated with duplicated genome files, were removed.

(2) Elimination of patients with null values.

(3) Scaling of continuous features: Six continuous features (PSA, PV, PSAD, testosterone, triglyceride, HDL) that exhibited left-skewed distributions were processed using robust scaling (Xrobust=[X-Q1]/[Q3-Q1], Q1=first quartile, Q3=third quartile), while the remaining two continuous features (age and BMI) were standardized using standard scaling (Xstandard=[X-µ]/σ, µ=mean, σ=standard deviation).

Additionally, since taking finasteride for more than six months can reduce PSA levels by 50%, PSA values were adjusted by doubling them [12]. One-hot encoding was applied for categorical features learning.

Finally, we determined that integrating the 13 common clinico-laboratory parameters available was feasible.

3. Geno-EMR meta-model

In general, tabular data, such as EMR data, tend to perform better in tree-based models than in the deep learning model. We therefore compared 11 machine learning models, including Random Forest (RandomForestClassifier), Extreme Gradient Boosting, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Histogram-based Gradient Boosting Machine (HistGBM), and logistic regression, on the training dataset (80:20 split) across 100 random states, and evaluated area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity [13,14,15].

To mitigate bias and variance and to leverage the strengths of these models, we constructed a stacking ensemble (EMR meta-model) using LightGBM, HistGBM, and logistic regression as base learners and logistic regression as the meta-learner [16]. The base learners received 13 clinicolaboratory variables as input and produced predicted probabilities of PCa, which were then used as input features for the meta-learner. A 10-fold cross-validation scheme and Bayesian greedy search were used to tune hyperparameters and to prevent overfitting.

To assess the synergistic effect of the GenPCa-Kor score, we further developed the Geno-EMR meta-model by adding the GenPCa-Kor score as an additional input feature to the same stacking architecture, resulting in a total of 14 input variables (13 clinico-laboratory parameters with GenPCa-Kor score).

4. Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and number (percentage), respectively. For the model performance evaluation of the model, the bootstrap method was employed to split train set into an 8:2 ratio for 1,000 iterations, assessing the model’s stability [17]. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was employed to determine the biomarkers’ importance in the model. SHAP values were calculated for each biomarker, highlighting their relative contribution to the model’s predictions [18].

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, AUCs, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were evaluated for each model. The machine learning models, including the HistGBM model and Logistic regression were implemented using the Scikit-learn package (version 1.2) in Python 3.9. The LightGBM model was developed using LightGBM (version 3.35), and the neural networks structures were implemented using Pytorch (version 1.12). All statistical values were computed using SciPy.

In each meta-model, predicted probabilities of PCa were compared across three clinical groups (benign, Grade Group 1, and Grade Group ≥2). Group differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p<0.05.

RESULTS

Overall, 1,590 participants with histologically confirmed as either benign or the PCa were included in the study. After applying the predefined data-cleaning criteria, 1,006 unique biopsy-confirmed participants constituted the model-development dataset. Of these, 757 participants who underwent biopsy before April 30, 2023 had complete clinico-laboratory and genotype data and constituted the training cohort, an independent test cohort of 249 consecutive participants recruited between May 2023 and October 2023 was used exclusively for the final performance evaluation of all models (Fig. 2).

Among the three base learners in the training cohort used to develop the EMR meta-model, the tree-based models, such as LightGBM and HistGBM, achieved AUCs of 0.866 and 0.864, respectively, accuracies of 0.782 and 0.781, and sensitivities of 0.733 and 0.700, whereas the EMR logistic regression model showed the highest specificity of 0.885. The predicted probabilities from these three base learners were then used as inputs to a logistic-regression meta-learner, yielding the final EMR meta-model with an AUC of 0.868, an accuracy of 0.783, a sensitivity of 0.711, and a specificity of 0.854 (Table 2).

In the evaluation of the Geno-EMR models using the training cohort, incorporation of the GenPCa-Kor score into the EMR features led to an across-the-board improvement in AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity compared with the EMR models. Among the three base learners, the LightGBM and HistGBM achieved AUCs of 0.917 and 0.927, respectively, accuracies of 0.836 and 0.850, sensitivities of 0.807 and 0.829, and specificities of 0.864 and 0.871, whereas the logistic regression model showed slightly lower yet comparable performance, with an AUC of 0.869, an accuracy of 0.812, a sensitivity of 0.773, and a specificity of 0.850 (Table 2).

The diagnostic performances of the EMR meta-model and Geno-EMR meta-model were evaluated in the test cohort (Table 3). In this cohort, the EMR meta-model achieved an AUC of 0.859, an accuracy of 0.767, a sensitivity of 0.776, and a specificity of 0.750. The Geno-EMR meta-model resulted in higher values across all metrics, with an AUC of 0.892, an accuracy of 0.835, a sensitivity of 0.870, and a specificity of 0.772. For the detection of PCa with Grade Group ≥2, the EMR meta-model yielded an AUC of 0.887, an accuracy of 0.804, a sensitivity of 0.838, and a specificity of 0.750, whereas the Geno-EMR meta-model increased the performance to an AUC of 0.911, an accuracy of 0.857, a sensitivity of 0.912, and a specificity of 0.773, indicating better discrimination for clinically significant disease (Fig. 3). Box plots of predicted probabilities from the EMR and Geno-EMR meta-models (Fig. 4) show a stepwise increase across the three clinical groups (benign, Grade Group 1, and Grade Group ≥2). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc testing demonstrated that predicted probabilities differed significantly between each pair of groups (benign vs. Grade Group 1, benign vs. Grade Group ≥2, and Grade Group 1 vs. Grade Group ≥2) in both meta-models (p<0.05).

DISCUSSION

In our study, we developed a Geno-EMR meta-model that integrates an SNP-based GenPCa-Kor score with clinico-laboratory EMR variables to predict PCa. This genomics-augmented model showed improved overall prediction performance compared with an EMR meta-model. The Geno-EMR meta-model provided clinically significant stratification, with predicted risk increasing from benign disease to Grade Group 1 and to Grade Group ≥2.

Several pre-biopsy risk calculators, such as the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT) risk calculator and the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC) risk calculators, were developed using demographic and routine clinical variables including PSA [19,20]. In 2017, the Korean Prostate Cancer Risk Calculator for High-Grade Prostate Cancer (KPCRC-HG) was developed and externally validated in a Korean cohort [21]. Similar to Western prebiopsy risk calculators, such as the PCPT and ERSPC tools, KPCRC-HG was constructed using a conventional logistic regression model based solely on demographic, clinical, and imaging-derived variables. Logistic regression assumes linear and additive relationships on the log-odds scale and is well known for yielding relatively high specificity; however, this linear perspective may limit its ability to capture complex interactions among clinical features and is reflected in the wider confidence intervals [22]. By contrast, we implemented a stack-based ensemble in which LightGBM, HistGBM, and logistic regression served as base learners. Their predicted PCa risks were then combined using a logistic-regression meta-learner. The tree-based ensemble models provide a flexible, non-linear representation of the data and tended to yield more stable sensitivity estimates than logistic regression [22].

Recently, there have been increasing efforts to use genomic and SNP variations as biomarkers influencing PCa [23,24]. Twin and family studies indicate that up to approximately 60% of the variance in PCa risk is attributable to heritable factors, and more than 100 well-recognized SNPs have been identified as being associated with PCa [25]. Because each individual SNP may exert only a low-to-moderate effect on disease risk, these variants are typically combined into quantitative summary measures, such as genetic or polygenic risk scores, which aggregate the weighted effects of multiple loci into a single index of inherited risk. A prominent example is the development of GRSs based on GWAS [9,26]. Conventional GRSs are typically constructed using statistical logistic regression models, in which log-odds coefficients are estimated from large case-control datasets. However, in diseases such as PCa, where ethnic differences in allele frequencies and effect sizes have been reported, deriving reliable, ethnicity-specific GRSs generally requires very large sample sizes. To address this limitation, our study aimed to propose a framework for developing ethnicity-tailored genomic biomarkers even when only a limited SNP dataset is available [5]. We adopted a strategy that leverages effect-size estimates from large sets in European and Japanese populations to inform SNP weighting in Koreans [8,27]. Based on this approach, we derived the GenPCa-Kor score, a genomic biomarker optimized for the Korean population using SNP information from other ethnic groups.

In exploratory feature-importance analyses of the Geno-EMR modeling framework (data not shown), PSAD emerged as the single most influential predictor across the three Geno-EMR base models (LightGBM, HistGBM, and logistic regression), accounting for roughly one-third of the total importance (about 32%–35%). GenPCa-Kor consistently ranked immediately after PSAD in the tree-based learners and in the final Geno-EMR meta-model, contributing approximately 7%–22% of the total importance. These patterns indicate that GenPCa-Kor functions as a major predictor alongside PSA-derived measures in the Geno-EMR framework. Although the GenPCa-Kor model alone showed only moderate discrimination in both training and test cohort, its integration with clinic-laboratory parameters clearly improved the performance of the EMR meta-model. Moreover, adding the GenPCa-Kor score increased AUC and sensitivity, with a slight gain in specificity for overall PCa or clinically significant PCa (Grade Group ≥2) diagnosis.

Our study has a few limitations. Firstly, the sample size of our model-development dataset (n=1,006), is modest for contemporary machine-learning approaches, although the number of events per predictor was acceptable. Second, both the EMR meta-model and the Geno-EMR meta-model were developed using data from Korean men. Therefore, applying this framework to non-Korean populations would necessitate the development of analogous population-specific genomic risk scores, followed by validation in independent cohorts from those populations. Additionally, we measured 1,347 PCa-related SNPs. Although this represents a relatively small panel compared with contemporary genome-wide arrays, complex machine-learning models were prone to overfitting despite regularization and cross-validation. Finally, we evaluated classification performance using confusion matrices in the independent test cohort, confirming stable discrimination despite differences in prevalence. However, calibration of absolute risk estimates and evaluation of clinical utility (e.g., threshold-based analyses or decision-curve analysis) are warranted in subsequent external validation studies focused on clinical implementation.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we developed machine learning-based PCa risk prediction models tailored to a Korean population by integrating EMR variables with an ethnicity-specific genomic score, yielding the Geno-EMR meta-model. The model consistently improved AUC, accuracy, and especially sensitivity, while maintaining acceptable specificity in PCa detection.
