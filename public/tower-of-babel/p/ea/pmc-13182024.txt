Clinical impact of metagenomic next-generation sequencing on pathogen detection and outcomes in non-immunocompromised patients with severe pneumonia supported by veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
Authors: Xing-Xing Li, Bo-Yan Li, Guo-Wei Fu, Hui Zhao, Jun Li, Yi-Heng Zhou, Xin Zhang, Yang-Chao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely pathogen identification is crucial for guiding antimicrobial therapy in severe pneumonia requiring veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (vv-ECMO). Compared with slow and often insensitive conventional culture, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) enables more rapid and comprehensive pathogen detection and may improve clinical management. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical impact of mNGS compared with conventional culture in non-immunocompromised patients undergoing vv-ECMO for severe pneumonia.MethodsThe retrospective study explored non-immunocompromised patients with severe pneumonia who received vv-ECMO support between January 2017 and June 2023. A total of 151 patients were categorized into the mNGS group and the non-mNGS group, based on whether they underwent mNGS testing. Furthermore, they were stratified into the Death group and Survive group according to their survival status at day 30. Demographics, laboratory test results, pathogens, antibiotic treatment, and clinical outcomes data were recorded and analyzed.ResultsThe positivity rate identified through the mNGS method (73.3%) was notably higher than that obtained through conventional culture (43.1%, P < 0.001). mNGS exhibited superior capabilities in identifying co-infections compared to conventional culture (70.9% vs. 30.1%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the 30-day mortality rate within the mNGS group demonstrated a significant decrease compared to the no-mNGS group (P = 0.045). Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis yielded comparable outcomes in both groups (P = 0.035). Factors protecting against adverse outcomes encompassed prolonged hospital stay time, extended ECMO duration, lower Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, successful ECMO weaning, and the application of mNGS. Antibiotic adjustments were implemented in 29 patients (58%) in the mNGS group and 39 patients (38.6%) in the no-mNGS group, with a markedly higher adjustment ratio in the former, demonstrating statistical significance (P = 0.024). Following adjustment of the treatment plan, the mNGS group demonstrated reduced APACHE II scores compared to the no-mNGS group after 5 days of treatment (P = 0.006).ConclusionsThe mNGS technique, with its superior pathogen detection rate, emerges as a promising tool for microbiological diagnosis and antibiotic management, ultimately contributing to enhanced patient outcomes.Keywords: High-throughput nucleotide sequencing, Non-immunocompromised host, Venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, Severe pneumonia, Short-term outcomes

Background

Severe pneumonia, a life-threatening infectious disease, requires admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) and is linked to elevated rates of morbidity and mortality [1–3]. The need for ICU admission is observed in approximately 10% of hospitalized pneumonia patients, with mortality rates ranging from 11% to 56% [4–6]. Timely and accurate pathogen diagnosis is crucial for tailoring treatment and improving prognosis [7, 8].

The use of veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (vv-ECMO) is indicated for patients with refractory respiratory failure. Timely and precise implementation has the potential to significantly improve both survival rates and prognostic outcomes [9, 10]. Nevertheless, patients with vv-ECMO who developed nosocomial infections were linked to an elevated risk of mortality [11].

Conventional microbiological testing methods possess inherent limitations regarding detection speed, positivity rates, and specificity, especially in severe infection cases [12–14]. Emerging as a diagnostic breakthrough, metagenome next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was initially successful in diagnosing a leptospirosis-induced central nervous system infection in 2014. It boasts benefits such as reduced turnaround time, enhanced accuracy in identifying pathogens, and the capacity to detect rare pathogens and multiple microbial strains [13, 15, 16]. Crucially, prior antibiotic exposure had a relatively lower impact on mNGS results compared to traditional methods [17, 18]. Moreover, the clinical application of mNGS technology has efficiently expedited pathogen identification in severe pneumonia diagnoses as well as aiding drug decision-making processes [19–21].

Presently, studies exploring the clinical use of mNGS in non-immunocompromised patients undergoing vv-ECMO for severe pneumonia are lacking. Hence, our retrospective analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of mNGS technology in identifying causative agents, guiding clinical management strategies, and assessing the prognostic implications of mNGS in enrolled patients.

Methods

Study population and design

A retrospective analysis was conducted on non-immunocompromised patients who received vv-ECMO support for severe pneumonia from January 2017 to June 2023 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The inclusion criteria were: (1) vv-ECMO support for severe pneumonia (duration > 48 h); (2) at least one mNGS or conventional culture test. The exclusion criteria were: (1) immunocompromised patients; (2) aged below < 18 years; (3) pregnant; (4) incomplete data.

In this study, the term “non‑immunocompromised” was used to describe patients without predefined immunocompromised conditions. Patients were considered immunocompromised and excluded if they had acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; had received continuous (> 3 months) or high‑dose (> 0.5 mg/kg/day) corticosteroid or other immunosuppressive therapy; undergone solid‑organ transplantation; had rheumatic autoimmune diseases requiring immunosuppressive treatment; had solid‑organ malignancies requiring chemotherapy within the last 5 years; had hematological malignancies; or had primary immune deficiencies. Thus, “non‑immunocompromised” in this manuscript denotes the absence of these predefined immunosuppressive states, rather than a formal quantitative assessment of immune competence.

During this period, 194 non-immunocompromised patients with severe pneumonia had indications for vv-ECMO assistance and underwent mNGS or traditional culture at least once. Finally, a total of 151 patients were included and classified into the mNGS group (n = 50) and the no-mNGS group (n = 101), based on whether they underwent mNGS testing (Fig. 1). Additionally, these 151 patients were further stratified into the Death group and Survival group, according to their survival status at day 30.

Flowchart of analyzed patients VV-ECMO, veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; mNGS, metagenomic next-generation sequencing

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Title: Application of mNGS in patients with ECMO; Date of approval: May 4, 2022; Ethics Number: 2022-KY-0272) and written informed consent was waived. All procedures were followed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Clinical data collection and samples collection

All enrolled patients received empirical antibiotic therapy prior to sample collection for microbiological testing. At the time of microbiological testing, all patients were receiving ongoing empirical or adjusted antibiotic therapy; thus both mNGS and conventional culture were performed under antibiotic exposure. Details regarding antibiotic treatment before sampling, identified pathogens, and subsequent adjustments based on mNGS results, were retrieved from the electronic medical record system. Evaluation of patients’ conditions was conducted using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores. The primary outcome was assessed through 30-day mortality. Secondary outcomes encompassed ICU stay time, mechanical ventilation time, ECMO duration, hospital stay time, and the successful weaning from ECMO. Within 48 h of ECMO assistance, whole blood samples or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected from the patients. In the mNGS group, each sample underwent both mNGS and conventional culture. In the no‑mNGS group, only conventional culture was performed; samples were not retrospectively tested by mNGS. The decision to perform mNGS testing was made by the treating intensivists or infectious disease specialists based on clinical judgment, including factors such as persistent fever, unclear microbiological etiology, suspicion of atypical or mixed infection, and inadequate response to empirical antibiotic therapy.

The mNGS procedures

Blood samples or BALF samples were collected from infected patients within 48 hours of ECMO support initiation. Subsequent to sample preprocessing, nucleic acid extraction was carried out using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Following this, library construction involved several steps: genomic DNA fragmentation, end-repair, 3’ adenylation, adapter ligation, PCR amplification, and purification of resultant products. The qualified single-stranded circular DNA library underwent sequencing on the NextSeq 550Dx platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) using a single-end 75 bp sequencing strategy. To identify potential environmental contamination and cross-sample/reagent contamination during the experimental process, a negative control devoid of template water was included for each test. Additionally, a historically positive sample was used as a positive control. Finally, bioinformatics analysis was conducted to identify pathogenic microorganisms based on mNGS.

Diagnostic performance evaluation for mNGS

Presently, the exclusive reliance on mNGS detection results is insufficient for establishing the etiological diagnosis of pneumonia. Alongside the information provided by the mNGS report, physicians must conduct a comprehensive assessment of infection-related clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, imaging findings, and other microbiological test results. In intricate cases, a collaborative evaluation involving multiple experienced experts is imperative to reach a definitive diagnosis.

VV-ECMO management

All enrolled patients received vv-ECMO support for reversible and refractory respiratory failure caused by severe pneumonia, in accordance with the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) 2021 guidelines regarding ECMO indications and contraindications [22].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows (version 21.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using an unpaired t-test. Skewed continuous variables were expressed as quartiles and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were represented as frequency (composition ratio) and compared using Pearson’s χ test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to assess the primary endpoint, with differences evaluated through the log-rank test. Additionally, Cox univariate analysis was conducted on all candidate variables, and Cox multivariate analysis was performed using variables that showed a significance level of P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis. To partially address potential indication bias in the non-random selection of patients for mNGS, we additionally included age, sex, baseline APACHE II score, SOFA score, and major comorbidities in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. Statistical significance was established at a two-tailed P-value of < 0.05.

Comparison of mNGS and conventional culture results

Certain pathogens have limited detectability by routine culture methods. In this study, we classified them into three categories: (1) mycobacteria (e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and non-tuberculous mycobacteria), which require prolonged incubation and specialized media; (2) fastidious bacteria such as Nocardia and Legionella, which also require specific culture conditions; and (3) atypical organisms that are not culturable by standard routine methods, such as Mycoplasma and Chlamydia, which are usually diagnosed by serology or PCR. For clarity, we refer to these collectively as ‘special pathogens’. Severe pneumonia is not caused by a single pathogen and is typically accompanied by co-infections.

The mNGS technology detected a total of 75 samples, with a positive rate of 73.3%, while traditional culture identified 239 samples, resulting in a positive rate of 43.1%. There was a statistically significant difference between the two methods (P < 0.01).

In the no-mNGS group, a total of 164 samples were cultured routinely, with a positive rate of 42.1% (Fig. 3). Among these, there were 126 BALF samples and 38 blood samples, with positive rates of 45.2% and 31.6%, respectively (P = 0.297). A total of 75 sample groups were collected in the mNGS group, with an mNGS positivity rate of 73.3%, while the conventional culture had a positivity rate of 45.3% (Fig. 3). For BALF, the positivity rate of mNGS was 78.3% compared with 48.3% for culture; for blood, mNGS positivity was 53.3% compared with 33.3% for culture. When restricting the comparison to BALF samples, mNGS consistently demonstrated higher positivity than conventional culture, highlighting its advantage in respiratory specimens.

Comparison of mNGS results and culture results in the mNGS group

Our investigation delved into the congruence of pathogen identification outcomes yielded by both techniques during ECMO assistance. In this analysis, we categorized test results as consistent when the pathogens identified by mNGS aligned precisely with those isolated through culture. Furthermore, if mNGS unveiled more pathogens than the culture method, we deemed the results as consistently aligned. Partial consistency was considered when pathogens identified by both techniques were partially congruent. Conversely, results were classified as inconsistent when the pathogens identified by both approaches diverged entirely.

Among the 50 patients in the mNGS group, a total of 75 samples underwent both culture and mNGS testing within 48 h after ECMO assistance. The comparison yielded intriguing results: 37 samples (49.3%) exhibited consistent pathogen identification, 4 samples (5.3%) were partially consistent, and 34 samples (45.3%) demonstrated complete inconsistency. In instances of complete inconsistency, 24 (70.6%) were negative when subjected to the culture method, while 3 (8.8%) yielded negativity when examined using mNGS. Additionally, 7 (20.6%) showcased mismatched results between the two techniques (Fig. 5A).

Clinical medication guidance between mNGS and no-mNGS groups

All patients received empirical therapy prior to sampling. Antibiotic adjustments were made in 29 patients (58%) in the mNGS group and 39 patients (38.6%) in the no-mNGS group, with a significantly higher adjustment ratio observed in the former compared to the latter (P = 0.024). The trends of APACHE II scores and SOFA scores were assessed seven days after ECMO assistance. Among the surviving patients, those in the mNGS group exhibited lower APACHE II scores than those in the no-mNGS group (p = 0.006), while SOFA scores showed similar results between both groups (P = 0.324) (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The diagnosis and treatment of severe pneumonia present a critical challenge due to the limitations of current diagnostic techniques in pathogen identification and the escalating issue of antibiotic resistance [23, 24]. Previous studies on mNGS have mainly focused on immunosuppressed patients, such as those with primary immune deficiencies or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, while there is a lack of relevant research involving non-immunosuppressed individuals. This study aims to assess the efficacy of mNGS in comparison to conventional culture methods in non-immunocompromised patients undergoing vv-ECMO support for severe pneumonia.

In our cohort, mNGS showed a substantially shorter turnaround time, with a median of 2 days from sample submission to report, compared with the 3–5 days typically required for conventional culture. The overall positivity rate of mNGS was also significantly higher than that of culture (73.3% vs. 43.1%, (P < 0.01)), and mNGS detected a much broader range of pathogens (58 vs. 23 types), including viruses and other atypical or fastidious organisms. These advantages persisted despite the fact that all patients had received prolonged antibiotic therapy before sampling for mNGS, and are consistent with findings reported by Zhao et al. [25]. Within the mNGS cohort, complete agreement between mNGS and culture was observed for 49.3% of identified pathogens, whereas complete disagreement occurred in 45.3%. Among discordant cases, 70.6% were culture-negative but only 8.8% were mNGS-negative, further supporting the superior sensitivity of mNGS over conventional methods.

Antibiotic exposure is known to substantially decrease culture positivity, whereas its effect on mNGS appears to be relatively limited [17, 18]. This likely explains why, among samples with completely inconsistent results, 70.6% were negative by culture but only 8.8% were negative by mNGS. Thus, part of the apparent discordance between the two methods may reflect antibiotic-induced suppression of viable organisms that are recoverable by culture rather than a true disagreement in pathogen detection. Because positivity rates differ markedly between BALF and blood, pooling different specimen types may bias comparisons between mNGS and culture. In our stratified analysis restricted to BALF samples, mNGS positivity remained substantially higher than that of conventional culture, supporting the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, the lower inherent sensitivity of blood cultures and blood‑based mNGS for pulmonary infections should be considered when interpreting overall positivity rates.

It is also important to emphasize that for certain atypical organisms (e.g., Mycoplasma, Chlamydia), culture negativity is expected with standard microbiological techniques, whereas others (e.g., mycobacteria, Nocardia, Legionella) are fastidious and may be recovered only with specialized methods. The apparent superiority of mNGS for these ‘special pathogens’ likely reflects both the suppressive effects of prior antibiotics on culture and the inherent limitations of routine culture. Crucially, the early and definitive identification of such pathogens by mNGS can profoundly alter the treatment course. For instance, Nocardia infections require prolonged courses of specific antibiotic regimens (e.g., trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) that differ significantly from empiric broad-spectrum coverage for typical bacterial pneumonia. Similarly, mycobacterial infections necessitate multi-drug anti-tuberculosis therapy that would not typically be initiated empirically. Without mNGS, delays in diagnosing these infections using conventional methods can lead to inappropriate or ineffective antibiotic therapy, prolonged critical illness, and potentially worse clinical outcomes. Therefore, mNGS’s ability to rapidly identify these difficult-to-culture pathogens directly facilitates timely initiation of targeted and effective antimicrobial therapy, thus improving treatment efficacy and patient prognosis.

Regarding the pathogen spectrum, our study revealed that bacteria were the most frequently detected pathogens by mNGS, accounting for 56.1% of all identified pathogens. Nonetheless, it is imperative to account for the potential of viral and fungal infections, as they can lead to fatal outcomes. Qian et al. [26] reported relatively lower sensitivity of mNGS for viral detection compared with conventional virological assays, and our study did not systematically incorporate standard tests for viral identification, which may have led to underestimation of viral infections. In our cohort, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii were the predominant bacterial species, Aspergillus was the leading fungal pathogen, and human herpesvirus was the most common viral pathogen. This pattern differs from that reported in immunocompromised adults with severe community-acquired pneumonia, where Pneumocystis jirovecii, Candida albicans, and human betaherpesvirus 5 were among the most frequently detected pathogens [19], highlighting distinct pathogen distributions between immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised hosts.

Consistent with previous studies [27–29], mNGS was markedly superior to conventional culture in identifying mixed infections (70.9% vs. 30.1%, (P < 0.01)). Patients with co-infections may present with more severe clinical manifestations and often require combination antimicrobial therapy, although the associated toxicities and drug–drug interactions may themselves adversely affect outcomes. The prevalence of co-infection in our study was similar to that reported in immunocompromised patients (68.8%) [30], suggesting that even non-immunocompromised individuals with severe pneumonia are susceptible to multiple pathogens following antibiotic exposure and invasive procedures common in critical care. Experimental studies show primary infections can dysregulate immune responses, causing severe lung damage and promoting pathogen persistence [31]. In advanced stages, pathogen-mediated suppression of immune cells or signaling pathways may result in secondary immune dysfunction, impairing pathogen clearance and predisposing patients to co-infections [31, 32].

When comparing clinical outcomes, we observed a significantly lower 30-day mortality in the mNGS group (P = 0.045). Cox regression analyses confirmed that belonging to the mNGS group was associated with improved prognosis, while unsuccessful ECMO weaning, shorter hospital stay and ECMO duration, and higher APACHE II scores were associated with worse outcomes. These findings align with Xie et al., who reported improved 28-day and 90-day survival in patients with severe pneumonia undergoing NGS-based diagnostic workup [33]. Moreover, our results corroborate prior reports of the strong discriminatory ability of APACHE II for predicting 30-day mortality in pneumonia patients [35, 36].

The observed difference in 30-day mortality is unlikely coincidental, but rather reflects the cascade of clinical benefits derived from mNGS-guided antimicrobial stewardship. In the absence of rapid and accurate etiologic diagnostics, initial management of severe pneumonia still relies heavily on empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. While timely empiric treatment is essential for controlling disease progression, it is inherently limited by both inadequate pathogen coverage and excessive use of broad-spectrum agents. These limitations can drive antimicrobial resistance, increase toxicity, raise treatment costs, and potentially worsen outcomes [34–36]. Prior studies have shown that 38.0–60.8% of patients require modification of their initial antibiotic regimen [37, 38]. In our cohort, all patients received empirical therapy before sampling. Antibiotic regimens were subsequently adjusted based on a comprehensive assessment integrating microbiological results, laboratory data, imaging, and clinical manifestations. The rapid and accurate pathogen identification by mNGS enabled more informed and timely adjustments. Specifically, 58% of patients in the mNGS group had initial regimens that did not adequately cover the clinically diagnosed pathogens and therefore required adjustment, compared with 38.6% in the no-mNGS group. This active adjustment, guided by mNGS, likely contributed to the significantly lower APACHE II scores observed in the mNGS group after 5 days of treatment (P = 0.006), indicating a more rapid improvement in disease severity and overall clinical condition early in the treatment course.

From an antimicrobial stewardship perspective, mNGS may offer several additional advantages in vv-ECMO patients [19, 21]. By rapidly identifying causative pathogens (including those difficult to culture or present in mixed infections), mNGS allowed for: (1) the de-escalation or narrowing of broad-spectrum antibiotics, reducing drug toxicity and selection pressure for resistance; (2) the initiation of specific, highly effective therapy against pathogens missed by conventional methods (e.g., atypical bacteria, fungi, viruses, or drug-resistant strains); and (3) the discontinuation of unnecessary antibiotics, preserving the microbiome and preventing adverse drug events. Detection of unexpected viral or fungal pathogens also supported the initiation of appropriate antiviral or antifungal therapy. Accurate characterization of true co-infections helped distinguish patients genuinely requiring combination antimicrobial therapy. Furthermore, resistance gene information (when available) could further guide agent selection. These prompt and precise antimicrobial modifications, informed by mNGS results, likely curtailed pathogen proliferation, mitigated host inflammatory responses, and ultimately translated into better control of the infection, a more rapid resolution of critical illness, and improved patient survival.

Despite its many advantages, mNGS also has important limitations. A major challenge is the lack of standardized interpretive frameworks or authoritative guidelines. The immense diversity of potential pathogens makes it difficult to reliably distinguish true infection from background noise, environmental contamination, or colonization [39]. In our study, mNGS failed to detect pathogens in 12.5% of cases that were positive by conventional culture, underscoring that mNGS is not a complete replacement for traditional methods. Therefore, integrating mNGS with conventional culture and other targeted microbiological tests currently represents the most rational strategy, providing more comprehensive and balanced guidance for clinical decision-making.

Our study is limited by its retrospective design and the use of primarily DNA-based sequencing, with only a subset undergoing RNA sequencing, which leaves RNA viruses and microbial transcriptomes incompletely characterized. Because mNGS testing was ordered at clinicians’ discretion rather than by random allocation, indication bias is likely; although we adjusted for several baseline covariates in multivariable models, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Additionally, only the mNGS group received both mNGS and conventional culture (with no retrospective mNGS testing of culture-positive samples in the no-mNGS group), making concordance one-directional and potentially underestimating the additional diagnostic yield of mNGS. Finally, although no patients had confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, pandemic-related changes in care may have introduced unmeasured bias.

Conclusions

The application of mNGS technology surpasses conventional culture in detecting specific pathogens and co-infections. This study suggests that mNGS might lead to more rapid and accurate diagnosis with better clinical prognosis than conventional culture in non-immunocompromised patients who received vv-ECMO support for severe pneumonia.
