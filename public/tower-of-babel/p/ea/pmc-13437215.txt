Cerebrospinal Fluid Shunts: An Updated Radiologic Review of Devices, Malfunctions, and Complications
Authors: Annie Singh, Ishaan Singh, Nikita Goyal, Pranjal Rai, Parv Mehta, Amit Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shunt devices are essential for managing hydrocephalus by diverting excess CSF from the ventricles and thereby reducing symptoms such as headaches, vision problems, and cognitive deficits. A typical shunt system includes a ventricular catheter, reservoir, valve, and distal catheter. These components are usually made of silastic and radiopaque materials for durability and imaging. The systems also include enhancements like programmable valves, anti-siphon devices, and gravitational units to improve function. Shunts are categorized by outflow destination (ventriculoperitoneal, ventriculoatrial, ventriculopleural, and lumboperitoneal) or by valve type (fixed or adjustable). While fixed valves have been the standard of care for years, adjustable valves allow non-invasive pressure changes, reducing the need for repeated surgeries. Radiographic evaluation of shunt placement and function is crucial in clinical care. Common complications associated with the use of such systems include mechanical failure (e.g., obstruction, fracture, and migration), infections, functional issues, and metastasis, which often require surgical revision. This paper offers an overview of CSF shunts, including their structure, functions, imaging characteristics, and potential complications. Additionally, we have summarized the imaging characteristics of currently available programmable shunt devices and briefly discussed other types of commonly used drainage devices along with their specific indications. A thorough understanding of these aspects is critical for effective diagnosis, treatment, and management of hydrocephalus and its related challenges.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shunts are important devices for managing hydrocephalus, a condition marked by excessive accumulation of CSF in the brain’s ventricles. CSF shunts are not merely therapeutic devices but are crucial for the survival and improved quality of life of individuals with hydrocephalus. These shunts mitigate symptoms associated with elevated intracranial pressure, including severe headaches, visual disturbances, and cognitive impairments. Recent advancements in shunt systems, such as the integration of programmable valves and anti-siphon devices, have improved the management of hydrocephalus. These improvements have decreased the frequency of complications, such as over-drainage or infection, and facilitated the precise adjustment of shunt settings to better match the needs of different patients, especially in the pediatric age group. Imaging techniques, predominantly MRI, are crucial for evaluating shunt placement and function, and play an important role in identifying and addressing potential complications. Complications can range from mechanical issues like obstructions or catheter dislocations to infections and functional problems such as over-drainage, which could necessitate further surgical interventions. In this paper, we provide a comprehensive overview of CSF shunts, detailing the basic and advanced components integral to their operation and their application in clinical settings, their imaging characteristics, and common shunt complications.

COMPONENTS OF CSF SHUNTS

Basic Components

The basic components of CSF shunts are a proximal or ventricular catheter, a reservoir, valve, and a distal catheter (Fig. 1A, B). The catheter part of the shunt is composed of silastic, a rubber tubing material that is resistant to breakage in the body and is infused with radiopaque material for radiographic visualization. The proximal catheter is placed in one of the ventricular spaces in the brain, commonly the frontal horn of the right lateral ventricle, and exits through a burr hole to be connected to a reservoir placed subcutaneously. The flow from the reservoir to the distal catheter is regulated by a one-way valve, and the distal catheter can then be drained into any body cavity capable of fluid resorption. Traditionally, fixed-pressure valves had predetermined pressure settings (low, medium or high) that drain according to the differential pressure gradient between the ventricle and the tip of the distal catheter. This required the surgeon to select an appropriate valve for implantation. Shunt complications such as over drainage or under drainage could occur, often necessitating subsequent surgical intervention. Recent advancements in medical technology have yielded programmable pressure valves, which contain magnets that allow for transcutaneous pressure adjustment and whose setting can be adjusted non-invasively using a magnetic external adjustment tool [1].

CSF shunts also incorporate several ancillary components, including prechambers, connectors, and deflectors (Supplementary Fig. 1). The prechamber is primarily designed to facilitate percutaneous punctures for intraventricular pressure measurement, medication administration, and valve control operations, as necessary. Additionally, its design permits the removal of CSF and the addition of medication through puncture of a silicone membrane using a cannula. To enhance the durability and safety of the mechanism, a solid titanium base is employed to prevent accidental cannula punctures and needle penetration into the underlying tissues. All shunt systems include connectors, except those with one-piece designs, and these connectors link the individual components of the system. They are typically manufactured from either titanium, which offers high mechanical stability but produces increased imaging artifacts, or plastic, which avoids artifacts but provides inferior mechanical stability [2]. Lastly, a deflector allows controlled angulation of the ventricular catheter, typically around 90°, at the burr hole without kinking or lumen obstruction. Its visibility on imaging depends on the material: radiopaque designs are clearly visible and silicone or other radiolucent versions are often poorly visualized [3].

Additional Components

In addition to the fundamental components of CSF shunts, several advanced adjuncts such as programmable valves, anti-siphon devices, or gravitational units are frequently incorporated into shunt systems (Fig. 1C-E). While fixed-pressure valves are predominant in the United States, the introduction of programmable valves has substantially enhanced the management of hydrocephalus, particularly among patients with normal-pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) and pediatric patients with complex hydrocephalus scenarios [4]. Individuals with NPH often require repeated adjustments to the valve’s opening pressure to finely balance cognitive functions and gait stability, which can mitigate the risks associated with over-drainage and the subsequent subdural effusions. Similarly, other patients with hydrocephalus exhibit a critically narrow therapeutic range, where the distinction between the symptoms of over- and under-drainage is minimal. Programmable valves, which permit non-invasive precise adjustments of the pressure settings, are increasingly favored for their capacity to address these challenges in managing hydrocephalus [5].

Siphoning involves continuous fluid flow through a conduit resembling an inverted U-shaped tube that connects two compartments at different heights. This mechanism facilitates the movement of fluid from a region of higher potential energy to one of lower potential energy. The process continues until the hydrostatic pressures between the two compartments equilibrate [6]. This siphoning action is particularly relevant in the context of ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) systems, which connect intracranial to intra-abdominal compartments. When the patient is in a vertical position, the system can function as a siphon, potentially leading to over-drainage, a significant complication post-implantation. To counter this siphoning effect associated with upright posture, many shunt systems also include an anti-siphon device, this reduces over-drainage when the patient is standing and can be used with both fixed and programmable valve systems. The various types of anti-siphoning devices used include 1) diaphragms, 2) gravitational units, and 3) flow-reducing devices [7].

TYPES OF CSF SHUNTS

CSF shunts are critical for managing hydrocephalus and other conditions affecting CSF dynamics. CSF shunts are classified on the basis of the outflow destination and the type of valve used in the shunt. Understanding these classifications and features is vital for selecting the appropriate shunt type for patient-specific needs.

Among these, the VPS is the most common type of CSF shunt used in clinical settings owing to the high absorption capacity of the peritoneal cavity. In this shunt type, the proximal catheter is placed in the ventricular system, while the distal catheter is positioned in the peritoneal cavity. The pathway is routed along the neck and hemithorax, with the tip settling in the abdominal region (Fig. 2A, B). This catheter position is favored due to the straightforward access it offers to the peritoneal area and a relatively lower incidence of complications. The VPS is the preferred shunt type in pediatric patients since it allows for an additional length of distal catheter, thereby compensating for issues of growth. These shunts have a failure rate of 11%–25%, with catheter obstruction and infection being the two common causes of failure [8]. This type of shunt is associated with complications such as peritonitis, adhesions, ascites, and pseudocyst formation.

In a VPLS, the proximal catheter is in the ventricular system, and the distal catheter is placed in the pleural cavity. This shunt consists of a proximal catheter that begins in the brain’s ventricles and a distal catheter that extends to the pleural cavity, traversing the neck and hemithorax, with the tip terminating within the pleural space (Fig. 2C, D). These shunts have a lower absorption capacity than VPSs, and are not preferred among pediatric patients due to their relatively smaller chest capacity. VPLSs are often considered a temporary solution rather than a long-term treatment due to their high failure rate (54%) [9] and the likelihood of developing complications such as hydrothorax, where fluid accumulates in the pleural cavity, and fibrothorax, which involves fibrous thickening of the pleural space. These complications substantially limit the use of this type of shunt for extended periods. These shunts are primarily utilized for temporary CSF drainage when managing infections related to other shunt types, such as VPSs or VASs.

The VAS has the proximal catheter in the ventricular system and the distal catheter in the right atrium. The catheter courses along the neck, with the catheter tip terminating in the right atrium (Fig. 3A, B). Although the VAS is effective for CSF pressure management, its use is associated with the risk of potential vascular complications such as tricuspid valve stenosis, thromboembolism, pulmonary hypertension, coronary sinus thrombosis, and other related issues. Each of these issues underscores the inherent risks associated with the use of VASs in clinical settings. In their study published in 2020, Rymarczuk et al. [10] found that VPSs have a higher overall survival rate than VASs but also show higher infection and proximal failure rates than VASs.

Although VASs remain a viable option for CSF diversion, their use in pediatric patients requires careful selection. Somatic growth in younger children can cause displacement of the distal atrial catheter, necessitating elective lengthening procedures over time. VASs are generally considered more appropriate in adolescents who have nearly completed growth. In addition, VASs may serve as an important alternative in patients with acute abdomen or repeated VPS failures. In such situations, a VAS can be used as a secondary CSF diversion strategy when VPS is contraindicated [11].

LPSs are less invasive than VPSs, and they involve the placement of a proximal catheter within the lumbar spinal canal, which is then routed to its distal position within the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 3C, D). While this procedure is instrumental in managing CSF pressure, it is not free of potential complications. An additional valve/reservoir is important since these shunts may lead to orthostatic over-drainage and cause acquired Arnold–Chiari malformations [12]. In their meta-analysis comparing VPS and LPS, Ho et al. [13] found a significantly lower complication rate of 12.98% in LPS vs. 23.80% in VPS, with no differences in effectiveness between the LPS- and VPS-based Modified Rankin Scale scores. Other notable complications include the onset of radiculopathy, the development of cerebellar tonsillar herniation, and the formation of syrinx, each posing significant challenges to patient outcomes.

Fixed-pressure valves regulate the intracranial pressure through a one-way valve that drains to a predetermined intracranial pressure level. These valves are implanted with a predetermined setting that cannot be altered further without additional surgery.

Adjustable (programmable) valves regulate the intracranial pressure on the basis of a pressure setting, like fixed-pressure valves, but their setting can be altered by using an external adjustment tool (Supplementary Fig. 2), allowing alterations in pressure settings without any further intervention. Despite this innovation in valve design, patient outcomes have shown no remarkable difference between fixed and programmable shunt valves. Multiple studies conducted worldwide have found no significant differences in the 1-year shunt survival rate, overall complication rate, infection rate, or catheter-related complication rate associated with these two types of valves [1415]. However, despite the lower upfront cost of fixed-pressure valves, the long-term expenses associated with both types of valves tend to converge within a similar range [1617].

Most programmable shunt valves incorporate magnetically adjustable components, raising concerns regarding MR safety, particularly during imaging at 3T; more pertinently, programmable valves are known to be susceptible to electromagnetic field-related hazards [18]. Exposure to strong magnetic fields may lead to device displacement, unintended changes in pressure settings, or valve malfunction. However, newer programmable valves that incorporate a locking mechanism, such as the proGAV, are designed to secure the pressure setting via a braking or locking system and have been shown to maintain valve settings despite exposure to a 3T static magnetic field [19]. In contrast, in valves lacking such safeguards, routine post-exposure monitoring of pressure settings is generally advised. Programmable valves like proGAV and Polaris demonstrate minimal magnetic field interactions, no clinically relevant MR-related heating under standard conditions, and preserved valve function, supporting their safe use during routine clinical MR imaging [20]. Accordingly, patients with a proGAV valve may undergo MR imaging at 3T or lower immediately after implantation. Standard safety practices for such examinations include verification of the valve setting by appropriately trained personnel before MR imaging and reassessment of the setting after the examination, with readjustment if required. In addition, exposure to radiofrequency energy should be limited to a whole-body averaged surface absorption rate of 2.1 W/kg for no more than 15 minutes to provide an adequate margin of safety against excessive heating, a condition compatible with most routine MR examinations [19].

RADIOLOGIC FINDINGS OF NORMAL CSF SHUNTS

In addition to knowing about the various components of CSF shunts, radiologists should be aware of the normal lucencies of these components. The shunt catheter is impregnated with radiopaque material such as barium sulfate and tantalum for radiographic visualization. Most shunt valves are typically radiopaque due to their metallic composition, while some modern valves use non-metallic covers or low-attenuation metals like titanium to reduce artifacts on imaging while retaining radiopaque features since they are pertinent to ensure visibility and shunt setting evaluation on imaging (Fig. 4) [21]. Shunt reservoirs are commonly integrated with the valve and can be radiolucent or radiopaque depending on their composition. Similarly, connectors and deflectors can be radiolucent or radiopaque depending on their composition [22].

Universally accepted or society-endorsed guidelines recommending routine fixed-interval CT or MRI surveillance following CSF shunt placement in asymptomatic patients are currently lacking. Contemporary guideline documents, systematic reviews, and multi-center surveys consistently highlight substantial practice variability and emphasize clinical follow-up with selective imaging rather than routine surveillance. Imaging is generally reserved for patients with new or worsening neurological symptoms, suspected shunt malfunction or infection, or when postoperative baseline assessment is clinically warranted [232425]. When imaging is required, rapid-sequence MRI is increasingly preferred to minimize radiation exposure, particularly in pediatric populations.

SHUNT MALFUNCTION AND COMPLICATIONS

CSF shunts are among the most failure-prone life-sustaining medical devices used in modern medical practice. In pediatric patients, these devices exhibit failure rates of 30%–40% within the first year and approximately 50% by the second year, with atrial and pleural shunts exhibiting higher failure rates [26]. Symptoms of shunt failure may be acute or chronic. Acute CSF shunt failure can present as nausea, vomiting, irritability, seizure, headache, lethargy, coma, stupor, or a combination of these presentations. In chronic CSF shunt failure, the presentation may include neuropsychological signs, changes in feeding patterns, developmental delays, headaches, or increased head size [27]. Early or late complications can arise following a CSF shunt placement. Early postoperative complications include improper placement or connection of shunt components, fluid collection, and infection. In contrast, late complications can be categorized into issues related to the inflow compartment, drainage compartment, proximal or distal catheters or their connections, valve malfunctions, and fractures of the tubing [1].

In general, a multimodal approach is often essential for diagnosing shunt malfunctions. These approaches typically involve the use of plain radiography, CT scans, radionuclide studies, MRI, and CSF pressure studies and cultures, all of which provide complementary information. In acute settings, CT is the primary modality of choice, offering critical insights into ventricular size, shunt location, and the type and integrity of shunt components. Prior imaging studies are essential to allow comparisons and thereby determine if an increase in ventricular size over time suggests shunt malfunction. In the absence of such enlargement or initial dilation, alternative diagnoses should be considered, including “slit ventricle syndrome (SVS)” [27]. Ancillary findings often include transependymal edema, subgaleal fluid, and edema along the catheter (Fig. 5).

Mechanical Obstruction

Mechanical obstruction is the most common cause of shunt malfunction, with proximal catheter obstruction accounting for over 50% of the shunt failures in the pediatric population [28]. Obstruction can occur at any time after shunt placement and affect any segment of the system. Clinically, obstruction presents with signs of raised intracranial pressure. In infants, symptoms include nausea, vomiting, irritability, and a bulging fontanel, while older children and adults may experience headache, nausea, vomiting, cranial nerve palsies, and ataxia [1].

Obstruction typically occurs at the proximal catheter tip or the valve. The tip may be blocked by choroid plexus ingrowth, and the valve by blood or debris. Distal catheter obstruction, although less common, can result from kinking or pseudocyst formation (Supplementary Fig. 7). Kinking may occur at the valve connection or at the abdominal entry site. Shunt function can be assessed by manually pumping the reservoir with Indium-111 diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) or technetium Tc99m DTPA. Sequential gamma camera imaging can track the radiotracer flow. Absence of activity in the catheter limbs suggests obstruction (Fig. 6) [29]. On radionuclide shuntography, peritoneal tracer distribution can be classified to assess VPS or LPS patency: diffuse bilateral distribution indicates normal CSF shunt function; loculated, unilateral tracer accumulation confined to the region of the distal catheter indicates partial shunt obstruction; and absence of abdominal tracer activity is consistent with complete CSF shunt occlusion [30].

Fracture

Fracture of the shunt tubing is typically a late complication, occurring almost exclusively along the distal catheter between the valve and peritoneum. It most often occurs in the neck due to mobility, especially in pediatric patients, where scar tethering and growth increase risk. Ideally, the catheter should remain flexible and mobile within the fibrous subcutaneous tract. However, over time, calcification can raise the risk of fracture. Early fractures may result from placement-related lacerations, so surgical care is critical to avoid catheter damage [28].

Patients may present with mildly elevated intracranial pressure, along with pain, swelling, or erythema over the shunt tract. Many fractures are detected incidentally during routine follow-up. While scar tissue may initially allow CSF flow between broken ends, this conduit eventually fails. Shunt series are the most effective imaging modality to identify fractures. Fractures commonly occur in the distal tubing near the clavicle or lower ribs. Large gaps are frequently observed between the fractured segments, since the distal portion often migrates through the subcutaneous tunnel and may reside entirely within the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 7) [31].

Disconnection

Most CSF shunts consist of separate parts, and disconnection between them can lead to shunt failure. This usually occurs soon after placement, commonly at the junction of the proximal catheter and reservoir or between the valve and distal catheter. The causes of disconnection include material failure, such as broken sutures, and surgical errors like poor knot placement or improper snap assembly. Additionally, loosened parts may contribute to this issue [28].

Notably, although shunt disconnection often occurs in the early postoperative period, delayed disconnection may also develop, particularly in pediatric patients. Progressive somatic growth can exert traction on the catheter and its junctions, predisposing them to separation over time. On imaging, calcification surrounding the disconnected segments on a shunt series reflects a chronic process, indicating that the disconnection has likely been present for a prolonged duration.

Disconnection obstructs CSF flow, producing symptoms of hydrocephalus that range from mild to asymptomatic. CSF collections due to disconnection are visible on CT or MRI. Diagnosis is typically made with a shunt series (anteroposterior [AP] and lateral skull radiographs, AP chest radiograph, and AP kidney–ureter–bladder [KUB] radiograph), which is the most sensitive option and often used with CT (Fig. 8). The shunt series can reveal gaps between segments, which appear radiolucent with areas of calcification [31]. Since some components are radiolucent and mimic disconnection, 3D reconstruction can help distinguish these. CT findings often include subgaleal collections and enlarged ventricles [27].

Migration

Shunt migration can occur at both distal and proximal ends. After the catheter becomes adherent to subcutaneous tissues by fibrous tissue, a child’s growth may cause traction and migration. Proximal catheters can move into the brain parenchyma, while distal catheters may erode into hollow or solid abdominal organs like the bladder, colon, or liver [1]. Additionally, migration into the scrotal sac may occur through the patent processus vaginalis. The subcutaneous tunnel created during shunt placement can also allow the catheter to loop back into the abdominal wall [32].

The catheter type influences migration risk. “Snap” connectors form a rigid 90° angle at the skull entry, helping resist migration. Migration may also result from tethering at the chest wall or peritoneal entry, where sutures may contact the tubing. The clinical signs of migration are similar to those of obstruction, such as increased intracranial pressure. Other findings may include a palpable change in the valve position or fluid collection. Most migration failures are not evident on physical examination and require imaging for diagnosis [27]. CT is the most effective modality for assessing catheter position, with signs including enlarged ventricles and catheter regression into the brain parenchyma (Fig. 9) [31].

Infection

Infection is the second-most common cause of shunt malfunction. Most infections occur within six months of implantation, with their incidence decreasing thereafter [3334]. The use of antibiotic-impregnated catheters has further reduced infection rates [35]. Key risk factors include younger age, prior neurosurgery or revisions, and a gastrostomy tube [36]. Infections mainly result from intraoperative skin flora contamination, but may also occur via meningitis, peritonitis, or wound infections.

Symptoms often resemble shunt malfunction, manifesting as nausea, headache, and lethargy, while fever may be absent. Meningeal signs are often lacking due to limited CSF communication. Staphylococcus epidermidis causes approximately 50% of infections, followed by Staphylococcus aureus (20%) and gram-negative rods (15%). The results of contrast-enhanced CT or MRI are not diagnostic, although ventriculitis may show irregular ependymal enhancement (Fig. 10). Notably, meningeal enhancement is common in postoperative patients and does not necessarily indicate an infection [27].

Fluid Collections & Loculations

Extra-axial fluid collections are a common early complication after the shunting procedure in older children with significant preoperative ventriculomegaly, occurring in approximately 3% of new shunt placements [37]. Chronic over-drainage can lead to subdural hygromas (Supplementary Fig. 8) or, less often, subdural hematomas, typically presenting with postural headaches (Fig. 11). Most cases are asymptomatic and resolve without intervention. Fluid can also collect along the catheter tract [1]. In patients with long-term shunts, asymmetric lateral ventricles or isolated CSF pockets are common, and they are usually post-infectious or post-inflammatory. MRI can define complex anatomy. Iohexol dye studies can be used to evaluate suspected fluid collections by assessing contrast distribution following injection into the shunt system. Under normal conditions, iohexol diffuses freely through the ventricular system over approximately 30 minutes to 1 hour. In contrast, trapped or sequestered locules are identified when the contrast agent fails to diffuse beyond a confined compartment or when persistent sequestration of contrast is observed, particularly if the catheter tip lies within a loculated segment, thereby confirming the presence of isolated fluid collections rather than free-flowing CSF [2738].

Over-Drainage and Slit Ventricle Syndrome

Over-drainage refers to several scenarios wherein a shunt functions properly but removes more CSF than necessary for a particular patient. It often results from a low opening pressure valve and the lack of anti-siphoning or gravitational devices. A long-term effect of over-drainage is SVS, which is characterized by small ventricles and shunt patency issues (Fig. 12) [1].

In patients who receive shunts in early childhood, the incidence of SVS is approximately 10% after ten years [39]. Patients undergoing initial shunt placement at a younger age or those with hydrocephalus related to infection, trauma, or aqueduct stenosis are at a greater risk for developing SVS. One study reported a two-thirds reduction in the rate of SVS with the use of a flow-regulated valve in comparison with differential pressure valves, both with and without the inclusion of an anti-siphon device [40]. Over-drainage can be addressed by increasing the opening pressure threshold by 3 cmH2O if an adjustable valve is being used. If a fixed valve is in place, shunt revision or the addition of an anti-siphoning or anti-gravitational device may be necessary.

Metastasis Along the Shunt Pathway

Extra-neural metastasis of neoplastic cells along shunts is a rare, albeit well-documented, phenomenon. Germinomas, medulloblastomas, and glioblastomas are the most common forms of tumors associated with metastatic spread, with VASs showing higher rates of widespread metastasis and shorter average survival time from shunt placement (2 months) than the average of all types of shunts [41]. The incidence of spread in pediatric patients is higher than that in the adult population, highlighting the need for vigilance in shunt placement in children with underlying malignancy [4142]. Retrograde metastasis along the shunt is extremely rare; however, a few studies have reported retrograde seeding of gastrointestinal, ovarian, and cutaneous malignancies along the shunt pathway [434445].

Emerging evidence has highlighted the importance of multidisciplinary awareness regarding the risk of tumor spread associated with shunt placement, especially in high-risk populations such as children and patients with germinomas or medulloblastomas. For patients at increased risk of complications from shunt placement, endoscopic third ventriculostomy can serve as an effective alternative to VPS and external ventricular drainage, avoiding implanted hardware and reducing procedure-related risks in appropriately selected cases [46].

CONCLUSIONS

CSF shunts can help manage hydrocephalus by relieving intracranial pressure and improving patients’ quality of life. Evolving shunt technology, like programmable valves and anti-siphon devices, can enhance precision and reduce complications. Advances in MRI have facilitated assessments of placement and function. Understanding the complexities of CSF shunts, from their basic to advanced components and the nuances of imaging evaluations, can equip clinicians with the tools required to optimize patient care and tackle the challenges associated with shunt malfunctions.
