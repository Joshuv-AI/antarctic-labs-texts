Cross trait analysis reveals shared genetic architecture of eight common female reproductive disorders
Authors: Ziwei Xie, Hao Xiong, Yingying Lin, Dongmei He, Yiqi Li, Xinyu Zhou, Yan Zheng, Yuxin Feng, Yunan Zhang, Xiaoyun Chen, Ming Wei, Xiaohong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Female reproductive disorders widely affect women of reproductive age, yet their shared genetic basis remains incompletely understood, limiting insight into the biological mechanisms linking these conditions. Here, we analyse genetic data from eight common female reproductive disorders to define their shared genetic architecture, primarily through a latent common factor genome wide association study and complemented by a multivariate genome wide association study. We integrate functional annotation to characterise associated loci and use fine mapping to prioritise putative causal variants. We also integrate Multi marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation and transcriptome wide association analysis to identify candidate susceptibility genes. In addition, we systematically evaluate associations between female reproductive disorders and a broad range of phenotypes and examine their relationships with metabolites, inflammatory mediators, and plasma proteins. Finally, by integrating tissue specific expression, cell type enrichment, and functional genomic annotation, we investigate prioritised susceptibility loci and their putative regulatory elements.

Introduction

Female reproductive disorders (FRDs) are characterised by multilevel dysfunction of the reproductive system. They pose a severe challenge to global public health because they cause infertility and damage women’s reproductive health. FRDs include mainly core diseases such as uterine leiomyomas, endometriosis, and ovarian dysfunction. Notably, their incidence rates remain high. Uterine leiomyomas affect ~70–80% of premenopausal women, with the global number of patients reaching as high as 171 million1. Endometriosis affects 6–10% of women of reproductive age and is often accompanied by chronic pelvic pain and infertility2. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder and a leading cause of ovulatory dysfunction, affecting 5–15% of women of reproductive age3. These diseases have become key factors leading to infertility among 17.5% of couples of reproductive age worldwide4, imposing heavy physical, psychological, and social burdens on patients and their families. Although remarkable progress has been made in clinical interventions, the complex genetic and molecular mechanisms of FRDs remain largely elusive. Thus, in-depth research is urgently needed to develop more effective prevention and treatment strategies.

With the rapid advancement of high-throughput genomic technologies, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of common FRDs have identified multiple significant genetic susceptibility loci5–8, providing critical clues to their pathogenic mechanisms. Recent genetic correlation analyses have further revealed substantial genetic overlap: the genetic correlation between overall female infertility and endometriosis reached 0.585, and anovulatory infertility showed a genetic correlation of 0.403 with PCOS. Approximately 50% of endometriosis-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) overlapped with infertility-related loci, and this overlap rate was ~25% for uterine leiomyomas9. Collectively, these findings underscore marked genetic interconnections across reproductive phenotypes and highlight the value of multiphenotype analyses for delineating shared and disorder-specific genetic architectures. Accordingly, comprehensive integrative analyses spanning distinct FRDs are warranted to delineate the genetic basis of multi-FRD susceptibility.

Genomic structural equation modelling (GSEM) has been used to investigate the shared genetic architecture of genetically correlated diseases10. Thus, we applied GSEM to model eight common FRDs and identify shared genetic variants. We integrated tissue and cell-type expression, functional genomic annotation and transcriptome-wide association studies (TWASs) to highlight candidate susceptibility genes and regulatory elements and extended these analyses to cross-phenotype genetic associations and biomolecular traits, including plasma metabolites, inflammatory proteins and circulating proteins. Together, these analyses revealed shared genomic risk loci across FRDs, highlighted candidate susceptibility genes including SYNE1, ESR1 and EEFSEC, and provided insights into the shared genetic architecture of FRDs.

Genomic structural equation modelling

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression revealed that the genetic variance SNP-based heritability (h²) Z scores for all eight FRD GWAS summary statistics exceeded 4 (Supplementary Data 3), indicating significant and robust heritable contributions. The LD score regression genetic correlation matrix further revealed consistently high genetic correlations among FRD phenotypes, suggesting shared susceptibility across these reproductive disorders (Supplementary Data 4; Fig. 2). The fit of the common-factor model was good, as indicated by the comparative fit index (CFI, 0.954) and standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR, 0.083), meeting conventional thresholds (CFI > 0.95, SRMR < 0.10)11, and the estimated factor loadings together with residual covariances provided additional support for a shared genetic factor in the GSEM (Supplementary Data 5). The multifactor model also demonstrated acceptable fit (CFI = 0.969; SRMR = 0.071). In this hierarchical structure, the low-level factor F1 reflects mainly the common susceptibility to female infertility, endometriosis, female reproductive tract polyps, ovarian cysts, and uterine fibroids, whereas F2 reflects mainly the susceptibility to menstrual disorders, female pelvic inflammatory diseases, and ovarian dysfunction. This hierarchical representation suggests that the global factor G captures the shared genetic architecture across the eight FRDs (Supplementary Data 5). Functional annotation and pathway enrichment of the GSEM–GWAS

We functionally annotated the GSEM–GWAS summary statistics using Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA). In the common-factor GWAS, we identified 2281 candidate SNPs, 85 independent genome-wide significant SNPs, 44 lead SNPs and 25 genomic risk loci. In the multivariate GWAS, we identified 3631 candidate SNPs, 140 independent significant SNPs, 67 lead SNPs and 32 genomic risk loci (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Data 7–10). Notably, the results of the common-factor GWAS revealed that rs4668716 is an additional genome-wide significant signal. In the multivariate GWAS, we identified five additional genome-wide significant SNPs: rs4654991, rs4668716, rs2253950, rs479552 and rs77567232. These signals may indicate additional susceptibility loci for FRDs (Supplementary Data 11). We performed Hypothesis Prioritisation in multi-trait Colocalization (HyPrColoc) multi-trait colocalization analysis across eight traits and identified 24 colocalization signals, indicating that distinct traits share the same genetic signal in certain genomic regions. Specifically, rs6916221 showed colocalization across five traits, whereas rs571174 and rs17623478 colocalized across four traits, suggesting that these loci may reflect a shared genetic basis among the related phenotypes (Supplementary Data 12).

In Multimarker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) gene-based analyses, we identified 262 genes significantly associated with latent FRD liability in the common-factor GWAS. The multivariate GWAS yielded 354 significant genes, of which 262 were shared between the two GWASs, suggesting a core set of genes underpinning the shared genetic architecture of FRDs and likely contributing to key pathogenic mechanisms (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Data 13). Tissue-specific expression enrichment analyses based on Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) v8 revealed that, in both GWASs, enrichment was concentrated in reproductive tissues. In the multivariate GWAS, enrichment was strongest in the uterus and fallopian tube, and the same tissues were most significantly enriched in the common-factor GWAS. Evidence of enrichment was also detected for the cervix and blood vessels, whereas these parameters did not significantly differ among the other tissues (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Data 14). These patterns aligned with the tissue biology of FRDs and further supported that GSEM–GWAS captured relevant underlying genetic signals.

GENE2FUNC analysis revealed that the enrichment of genes in the GWAS Catalog gene set was concentrated mainly in traits related to body fat distribution and metabolic phenotypes, particularly waist–hip measures, suggesting that the distribution of adiposity and metabolic regulation may contribute to the susceptibility to shared FRD. Additional enrichment was also observed for bone metabolism/bone density, blood pressure, and brain morphology phenotypes, suggesting that shared genetic signals may influence biological processes across multiple organ systems. Positional gene-set enrichment highlighted several chromosomal regions, including chr20q12, chr10q24, chr6q25 and chr2q21, providing leads for prioritising genes and regulatory elements in downstream analyses (Supplementary Data 15).

Fine-mapping of GSEM–GWAS

Across the GSEM–GWAS association signals, we performed Bayesian statistical fine-mapping using SuSiE and FINEMAP under two LD reference panels. Using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European-ancestry panel as the LD reference, fine-mapping prioritised several high-confidence variants. In the multivariate GWAS, these included rs4918073 at the ITPRIP/OBFC1/WDR96 locus, rs17033114 near LINC00485, rs78081080 near NLGN2, rs72648445 near SULT1B1, and rs36069217 near VIP. In the common-factor GWAS, rs4918073 near WDR96 was similarly prioritised (Supplementary Data 16; Supplementary Figs. 1–8).

We then repeated fine-mapping using the UK Biobank LD reference panel (Supplementary Data 17). Under the same prioritisation criteria, the common-factor GWAS prioritised rs79703198 near ESR1, whereas the multivariate GWAS prioritised rs17033114 near LINC00485, rs78081080 near NLGN2, and rs17115125 near WBP1L (Supplementary Data 17; Supplementary Figs. 9–12).

We quantified whether each prioritised SNP acted as a multi-trait driver by decomposing its association evidence across traits using Z-score contributions. For each SNP–trait pair, we computed Zᵢ = βᵢ/SEᵢ and defined the normalised contribution as wᵢ = |Zᵢ|/Σj|Zj| across traits. Using maxᵢ(wᵢ) ≤ 1/3 as the operational criterion, SNPs were considered to have association evidence distributed across multiple traits rather than being driven by a single trait (Supplementary Data 18). Using this criterion, we prioritised six SNPs (rs17033114, rs17115125, rs4918073, rs72648445, rs36069217 and rs79703198).

Cross-phenotype genetic associations of the GSEM–GWAS

Cross-trait enrichment analysis using interactive Cross-Phenotype Analysis of GWAS Database (iCPAGdb) revealed that loci identified by the common-factor and multivariate GWASs were enriched for several biologically related phenotypes recorded in the GWAS Catalog. The strongest enrichments involved gynaecological traits, including uterine fibroids and endometriosis. Enrichment was also observed for skeletal phenotypes, including heel bone mineral density and ultrasound-derived bone measures, as well as fracture risk, suggesting the involvement of pathways related to bone remodelling and tissue integrity. Additional enriched categories included anthropometric traits, immune and haematological indices, and reproductive/endocrine or cancer-related traits (Supplementary Data 19).

We next applied bivariate LD score regression to estimate genetic correlations (rg) between the GSEM–GWAS and up to 1589 UK Biobank phenotypes that were successfully analysed (Supplementary Data 20). After multiple-testing correction, 316 phenotypes were significantly correlated with the results of the common-factor GWAS, and 314 were significantly correlated with those of the multivariate GWAS. The strongest correlations were observed for histories of hysterectomy and urogenital system diseases. In addition, significant correlations were observed with general and self-rated health measures, multisite chronic pain, mood-related phenotypes, lifestyle factors, and multiple metabolic traits. The observed-scale heritability estimates, LD score regression heritability intercepts close to 1, and genetic covariance intercepts close to 0 suggest that these genetic correlations are unlikely to be driven primarily by residual confounding.

In the UK Biobank phenome-wide two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis, we first performed a screening step using the common-factor and multivariate GWAS results as alternative sources for the outcome association estimates. After correction for multiple testing, only a small number of outcomes remained significant. Among these, menopause status, age at first sexual intercourse (AFS) and the fluid intelligence score retained evidence of an association (Supplementary Data 21; Fig. 4). For AFS, the estimated effect was negative, and the direction was unchanged across robustness checks; although heterogeneity and outlier instruments were observed, the direction of the effect remained stable after outlier correction. For the fluid intelligence score, the estimated effect was also negative, and sensitivity analyses for heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy did not indicate major violations. Integrative prioritisation of FRDs susceptibility variants and genes from GSEM–GWAS

We further integrated the candidate variants prioritised by fine-mapping with GTEx v8 cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTL) and cis-splicing quantitative trait locus (cis-sQTL) signals across 47 tissues. Several SNPs showed multi-tissue colocalisation with GTEx v8 cis-eQTL and/or cis-sQTL signals, including the high-confidence putative causal variants rs4918073 and rs72648445. In the multitissue sparse canonical correlation analysis-based transcriptome-wide association study (sCCA–TWAS), we identified genes whose genetically predicted expression profiles were associated with GSEM–GWAS signals. A core set of genes, including SYNE1, ESR1, ARL14EP, ZBTB40, PDCD11, STN1, TCF19, EEFSEC, RP1-292B18.1, and AKAP12, was consistently prioritised in both the common-factor and multivariate GWASs (Supplementary Data 26; Fig. 6B). By integrating these results with MAGMA-significant genes, we further prioritised SYNE1, ESR1, EEFSEC, ARL14EP, TCF19, and PDCD11 as candidate FRD susceptibility genes. We then performed Bayesian colocalisation by integrating GSEM–GWAS summary statistics with GTEx v8 cis-eQTL and cis-sQTL data across 47 tissues using eCAVIAR and fastENLOC (Supplementary Data 27–28). Restricting to signals supported by both colocalisation methods and observed in both GSEM–GWAS components, we identified convergent evidence for 59 genes. Finally, by intersecting the colocalisation-supported genes with our candidate gene set, we prioritised three high-confidence FRD susceptibility genes: SYNE1, ESR1, and EEFSEC.

Phenome-wide association analysis

Using the AstraZeneca phenome-wide association analysis (PheWAS) portal (UK Biobank 470 K V5), we tested nine FRD susceptibility genes and five MR-supported causal proteins across binary and continuous phenotypes. No significant associations were detected for binary traits. Notably, the results of the continuous phenotype analysis revealed that STN1 is consistent with telomere length-related indicators, including the results of the qPCR/WGS telomere length estimation and T/S ratio characteristics (Supplementary Data 29). Taken together, these results suggest that most candidates do not show broad phenome-wide associations in this dataset, whereas STN1 warrants additional scrutiny for potential on-target consequences beyond FRDs.

Cell-type and functional genomic annotation enrichment of the GSEM–GWAS

The cell-type enrichment analyses from both the common-factor GWAS and the multivariate GWAS showed nominally significant enrichment for bone marrow-related progenitor/precursor cell populations, tracheal endothelial cells, and limb skeletal muscle satellite cells in both analyses (Supplementary Data 30). Stratified LD score regression highlighted a concentration of GSEM–GWAS heritability in functionally active genomic regions. Across both analyses, the most consistent enrichment mapped to active enhancer features, including H3K27ac-marked enhancers and superenhancers, together with prominent enrichment in evolutionarily conserved sequences. Additional signals were observed in promoter-proximal marks, open-chromatin and regulatory annotations, as well as coding regions. In contrast, repressed chromatin states and CTCF-associated annotations tended to be depleted, which is consistent with GSEM–GWAS heritability being preferentially carried by active regulatory and accessible chromatin (Supplementary Data 31).

Spatial localisation of genetic risk signals

In the genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) analysis, enrichment significance was evaluated using the FDR-adjusted Cauchy combination test, and supported signals were defined as those with both an FDR-adjusted Cauchy P value and a median-test P value below 0.05. Under these criteria, seven annotations reached significance in the common-factor GWAS, including those for cartilage primordium, cartilage, connective tissue, adipose tissue, kidney, lung, and jaw and tooth, whereas the same seven annotations were also supported in the multivariate GWAS (Supplementary Data 32; Fig. 6C). Together, these results indicate that signals from the GSEM–GWAS preferentially map to cartilage- and connective-tissue-related programmes, with additional enrichment in kidney, lung, adipose tissue, and jaw-and-tooth annotations. Diagnostic genes from gsMap further highlighted extracellular matrix components and matrix chaperones, including SERPINH1, P4HB, SPARC, IGFBP4, and FLNA, as well as multiple collagen and extracellular matrix-related genes, such as COL5A1/COL5A2, COL1A1/COL1A2, BGN, and FBN2 (Supplementary Data 33).

Discussion

In this study, a cross-trait multivariate GWAS of eight common FRDs was performed using GSEM, yielding two complementary genetic frameworks: a common-factor model and a multifactor model. The common-factor GWAS identified 85 independent genome-wide significant SNPs, 44 lead SNPs, and 25 genomic risk loci. The multivariate GWAS identified 140 independent genome-wide significant SNPs, 67 lead SNPs, and 32 genomic risk loci, including five additional genome-wide significant SNPs: rs4654991, rs4668716, rs2253950, rs479552, and rs77567232. We then fine-mapped association signals with SuSiE and FINEMAP and integrated functional annotation, GTEx tissue enrichment, sCCA–TWAS with FOCUS (Fine-mapping Of CaUsal gene Sets), and eQTL/sQTL colocalisation to prioritise candidate susceptibility genes. Cross-phenotype genetic correlation and MR further linked FRDs to metabolites, inflammatory mediators, and plasma protein drug targets, expanding the genetic atlas of FRD comorbidity and highlighting actionable pathways.

Fine-mapping of GSEM–GWAS signals prioritised high-confidence candidate causal variants. Using the European-ancestry subset of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 panel as the LD reference, the common-factor GWAS identified one variant (rs4918073), whereas the multivariate GWAS identified five variants (rs4918073, rs17033114, rs78081080, rs72648445, and rs36069217). Using the UK Biobank as the reference panel, the common-factor GWAS identified one variant (rs79703198), whereas the multivariate GWAS identified three variants (rs17033114, rs78081080, and rs17115125). In total, six candidate variants were further prioritised through cross-trait Z-score contribution decomposition: rs17033114, rs17115125, rs4918073, rs72648445, rs36069217, and rs79703198. Notably, integration with GTEx v8 cis-eQTL and cis-sQTL signals revealed multi-tissue colocalisation for several SNPs, including the high-confidence putative causal variants rs4918073 and rs72648445. At the gene level, sCCA–TWAS identified a core set of ten genes (SYNE1, ESR1, ARL14EP, ZBTB40, PDCD11, STN1, TCF19, EEFSEC, RP1-292B18.1, and AKAP12), and colocalisation provided convergent support for SYNE1, ESR1, and EEFSEC as the most credible candidates.

rs79703198 maps to chromosome 6q25.2 and lies close to the ESR1/SYNE1 region; notably, ESR1 encodes oestrogen receptor α. In our fine-mapping analyses using the UK Biobank LD reference panel, rs79703198 was prioritised as a candidate variant. In parallel, ESR1 and SYNE1 were designated FRD susceptibility genes, and FUMA-based positional enrichment analysis independently highlighted chr6q25. This convergence of variant- and gene-level evidence suggests that 6q25 is a key locus for FRD susceptibility and is plausibly mediated, at least in part, through ESR1- and SYNE1-related pathways.

ESR1 encodes oestrogen receptor alpha (ERα), a member of the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily and a central transcriptional regulator that mediates the biological effects of oestrogens, including 17β-oestradiol. ERα is highly expressed in the endometrium and is also expressed in other reproductive tissues, such as the ovary, where it regulates uterine cell proliferation and differentiation and plays a key role in hormone-dependent conditions, including uterine fibroids and endometriosis12,13. Genetic studies further implicate ESR1 polymorphisms in reproductive endocrine phenotypes, including premature ovarian failure14 and PCOS15. Additional evidence has shown that loss-of-function mutations in ESR1 can lead to female oestrogen insensitivity and delayed pubertal development, underscoring the central role of ERα in reproductive endocrine regulation and linking its disruption to female infertility9,16–18. At the interface of infection and immunity, mouse models have indicated that sex hormones and their receptors can shape the course of Chlamydia infection and the accompanying immune response19. Given that untreated Chlamydia infection can ascend and result in pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy and irreversible tubal-factor infertility, these observations provide biological plausibility for ESR1 and ERα contributing to infection-related risk through modulation of the local immune microenvironment in the reproductive tract. Consistent with this, oestradiol acting via ERα has been shown to downregulate CD44 and CD47 in the vaginal ectocervical and fornical epithelium, promoting the retention of neutrophils at the epithelial surface or within the mucosa and limiting transepithelial migration into the lumen. This, in turn, reduces neutrophil-mediated clearance within the vaginal lumen during ovulation or under oestradiol treatment and increases susceptibility to Candida albicans infection20. These findings position ESR1 as a regulator of lower genital tract mucosal immunity and support a mechanistic axis linking hormonal signalling, mucosal immune control and infection susceptibility. SYNE1, located within the 6q25 region, encodes the KASH-domain protein Nesprin-1 of the outer nuclear membrane. Previous work has reported altered endometrial expression of SYNE1 in disorders such as endometriosis and PCOS, which is supported by genetic evidence for pleiotropic risk loci21. GWAS of uterine fibroids has also highlighted a set of robust susceptibility loci that include signals at 6q25, implicating the SYNE1/ESR1 region22. Therefore, at the locus level, the 6q25.2 association signal observed in FRDs aligns with existing genetic evidence from female reproductive diseases and suggests that this region may act as a shared genetic hub across multiple female reproductive phenotypes.

We identified five plasma proteins that were significantly associated with FRDs, highlighting potential druggable opportunities: RSPO3, LCT, ARSB, TFPI and WASHC3. TFPI plays important roles in regulating endometrial receptivity and ovarian physiology and has been linked biologically to female infertility23. Current therapies for uterine fibroids and endometriosis focus largely on hormonal modulation. Progesterone resistance is a recognised feature of endometriosis, but the pharmacological management of fibroids is often geared towards short-term control, with symptoms or lesions prone to rebound after treatment cessation. These limitations underscore the value of developing nonhormonal targets. As a key modulator of the Wnt and β-catenin pathways, RSPO3 is upregulated in endometriotic lesions and in serous ovarian cancer, where it may promote proliferation and contribute to chemoresistance. In addition, anti-RSPO3 monoclonal antibodies markedly attenuate fibrosis in animal models24–26, suggesting that RSPO3 is a plausible nonhormonal therapeutic target. LCT encodes the brush-border lactase of the small intestine, which is responsible for lactose hydrolysis in the mature gut. Animal studies suggest that Lct expression is altered in endometriosis-related models27, although the pathways linking LCT to FRD-related phenotypes in humans remain to be clarified. Notably, our analyses also implicated ARSB and WASHC3 in FRD risk. Although their pathogenic mechanisms are not yet well defined, molecules involved in extracellular matrix metabolism, intracellular trafficking and cytoskeletal remodelling may contribute to microenvironmental reprogramming in reproductive tissues, inflammatory processes, or the regulation of cellular behaviour, meriting further functional follow-up and validation.

Traditionally, FRDs have been viewed as gynaecological disorders confined to the reproductive tract and pelvis. However, our phenome-wide analyses revealed a markedly systemic genetic signature, with FRDs showing genetic correlations with skeletal health, metabolic function, general physiological traits, immune-related diseases and mental health. Prior work supports this broader view. Endometriosis, for example, shares a genetic architecture with other pain conditions and shows significant genetic correlations with depression and anxiety28,29, which is consistent with its profile as an immune-mediated chronic systemic inflammatory disease. Similarly, PCOS is genetically linked to body mass index (BMI), fasting insulin levels, type 2 diabetes and dyslipidaemia30, and uterine fibroids, which are influenced by both hormonal and metabolic factors, are associated with overweight status and hypertension31. These observations suggest that earlier recognition of FRDs and more personalised interventions should be embedded within a broader framework of whole-person health management.

In phenome-wide MR analyses based on the GSEM–GWAS, we further highlighted three traits with inverse associations with FRD risk: menopause status, AFS and the fluid intelligence score. AFS has been widely studied, and prior MR analyses suggest that earlier sexual debut is associated with a greater risk of certain reproductive disorders32. Large-scale genetic studies have also reported genetic correlations between AFS and uterine fibroids, as well as female infertility9,33. Consistent with these reports, we observed a concordant direction of effect across FRD phenotypes, whereby genetic liability to later AFS was associated with a lower overall FRD risk. AFS is strongly correlated with multiple complex social and behavioural traits34, and the observed association may therefore reflect shared genetic architecture or upstream exposures rather than a single proximate mechanism. Evidence directly linking fluid intelligence to FRDs has been limited. The negative effect direction observed here suggests a potential association with lower FRD risk. Fluid intelligence captures the capacity for novel problem solving and information processing, and genetic studies have linked higher cognitive function to lower BMI and systolic blood pressure, as well as a reduced risk of coronary artery disease and type 2 diabetes35,36. Higher fluid intelligence may therefore be associated with lower cardiometabolic risk burden and more favourable health-related behaviours, aligning with reduced FRD risk. Cognitive ability is also correlated with educational attainment and socioeconomic position, which can shape living conditions and access to healthcare and may contribute to the observed association. These findings remain preliminary and warrant replication in independent cohorts.

Projecting GSEM–GWAS signals onto the Mouse Organogenesis Spatiotemporal Atlas (MOSTA) embryonic reference revealed predominant enrichment within chondrogenic primordia, cartilage and related mesenchymal, connective-tissue compartments. Under both the common-factor and multivariate models, signals were enriched in cartilage primordium, cartilage, connective tissue, adipose tissue, kidney, lung and jaw-or-tooth annotations. Together with the results of gsMap, which revealed enrichment of diagnostic genes involved in extracellular matrix organisation and chaperone function, including SERPINH1, P4HB, SPARC, IGFBP4, collagens, BGN, FBN2 and FLNA, these data suggest that shared FRD susceptibility converges on a conserved mesenchymal and extracellular matrix developmental programme and matrix remodelling pathways. These results align with the established importance of dysregulated extracellular matrix deposition and fibrotic remodelling in uterine fibroids and endometriosis37,38 and prioritise these pathways for validation in relevant adult tissues and cell types.

This study has several limitations. First, the primary GWAS datasets were drawn largely from European-ancestry cohorts, and the generalizability of our findings to non-European or admixed populations remains to be established. Second, our analyses relied on publicly available, de-identified summary statistics without newly generated genotyping data, limiting direct validation of prioritized loci and causal claims. Third, most of the evidence for shared genetic architecture in this study is derived from GSEM. Although GSEM is a multivariate approach that can dissect the joint genetic architecture of multiple complex traits39,40, these shared signals require further validation. Moreover, GSEM models latent factors underlying FRDs; although this approach captures shared liability, the inferred factor does not map to a single clinical phenotype, complicating interpretation and translation. In addition, because the eight traits were derived from the same cohort with substantial sample overlap, HyPrColoc-based colocalization inferences may be influenced by correlated sampling errors, and we therefore interpret locus-level sharing with appropriate caution. Fourth, gsMap leveraged a mouse embryonic atlas, so enrichments may reflect conserved programs rather than adult disease-relevant sites. Fifth, GWAS summary statistics for several omics exposures (plasma proteins, circulating inflammatory proteins, and plasma metabolites) were derived from sex-combined cohorts that included male participants, and female-only summary statistics were not available. Therefore, the corresponding MR estimates may be attenuated or biased and may not fully capture female-specific genetic effects. Moreover, MR inferences depend on key assumptions and may be influenced by measurement error and residual confounding, warranting additional causal validation. Finally, functional validation of prioritized genes, loci, and putative drug targets remains limited and requires further experimental and clinical follow-up.

In summary, using GSEM, we identified multiple genomic risk loci, including previously unreported loci reaching genome-wide significance. We also identified several candidate susceptibility genes associated with FRD, such as ESR1 and SYNE1, as well as several plasma proteins that may be involved in disease pathogenesis, including RSPO3, LCT, ARSB, and WASHC3. Integrated post-GWAS analyses, including statistical fine-mapping, tissue and cell-type enrichment analyses, functional annotation, cross-phenotype genetic correlation, Mendelian randomization, transcriptome-wide association analysis followed by fine-mapping of causal gene sets, and genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits, further identified regulatory elements associated with FRD genetic susceptibility and provided insights into the shared genetic mechanisms underlying female reproductive disorders.

Proteomics data were sourced from two large cis-protein quantitative trait loci GWASs: deCODE genetics and the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP). deCODE measured plasma protein levels with 4907 aptamers in 35,559 Icelanders42; UKB-PPP covered 2923 plasma proteins in 54,219 subjects, with plasma proteins profiled using the antibody-based Olink Explore 3072 platform43. GWAS summary statistics for plasma metabolites were obtained from Chen et al.’s analysis of 8299 European-descent participants in the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Ageing, which measured 1091 circulating metabolites and 309 biologically inspired metabolite ratios using the Metabolon HD4 UPLC-MS/MS platform to investigate systemic regulation in metabolic pathways and perform genome-wide association analyses44. Additionally, summary statistics for 91 inflammation-related plasma proteins were derived from Zhao et al.’s protein quantitative trait loci meta-analysis of 14,824 European-descent participants (11 cohorts) using the Olink Target-96 Inflammation platform45.

All included GWASs were approved by the institutional ethics review committees of their respective institutions, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. A detailed list of the GWAS datasets used is provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2.

Quality control of GWAS summary statistics

We performed quality control on the GWAS summary statistics for the eight FRDs to improve the robustness of the downstream analyses. Specifically, we excluded SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01, as low-frequency variants are more susceptible to imputation or genotyping uncertainty and typically provide limited power in summary statistic-based analyses. This filtering step reduces the influence of unstable estimates and helps minimise potential bias in subsequent multivariate modelling and post-GWAS analyses.

Genomic structural equation modelling

GSEM is a recently developed multivariate approach that evaluates the joint genetic architecture of complex traits and diseases using GWAS summary statistics40. We applied GSEM, implemented in the GenomicSEM R package, to model eight FRDs, aiming to investigate whether common genetic factors influence the risk of all reproductive disorders40.

This method involves the dissection of complex genetic structures through two sequential steps. First, we used a multivariate extension of cross-trait LD score regression to compute the genetic covariance matrix and corresponding sampling covariance matrix. We then analysed genetic associations across eight related traits and estimated the h² for each trait. We subsequently constructed a GSEM to minimise the discrepancy between the hypothesised covariance matrix and the initial empirical covariance matrix, with the core objective of testing for shared genetic factors among eight FRD-related traits.

To this end, a common-factor GWAS model was employed, and model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the SRMR, chi-square statistic (χ²), Akaike information criterion, CFI. Additionally, to ensure consistent effect directions for SNPs identified by GSEM, we performed heterogeneity tests for each SNP and excluded variants with Cochran’s Q statistic p values < 0.05 after Bonferroni correction. Subsequently, as a sensitivity analysis, we conducted a GSEM-based multivariate GWAS. Ultimately, two sets of GWAS summary statistics were obtained: one from the common-factor GWAS and the other from the multivariate GWAS.

Secondary evaluation of the GSEM–GWAS

We applied LD score regression to compute the mean χ², λGC, maximum χ², h², and the LD score regression intercept, thereby quantifying statistical inflation and potential confounding. The λGC serves as a key metric for overall inflation: values significantly >1 may indicate bias from population stratification, batch effects, or other confounders. However, because λGC also increases with true polygenic signal strength, it cannot alone distinguish “true polygenic effects” from “confounding effects.” Therefore, we used the LD score regression intercept to parse the source of inflation: an intercept near 1 implies inflation driven primarily by polygenic architecture, whereas an intercept significantly >1 suggests additional confounding factors46.

Functional annotation and gene prioritisation

FUMA is an online platform that integrates multisource data for functional annotation and gene prioritisation47. We used FUMA (v1.6.6) to map functional consequences and prioritise candidate genes from the GSEM–GWAS results. Taking the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European-ancestry panel as the LD reference panel, we first identified independent genome-wide significant variants (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸; pairwise r² ≤ 0.60) and then defined lead SNPs at r² ≤ 0.10. All variants in high LD (r² ≥ 0.60) with each lead SNP and P < 0.05 were collected for downstream analyses. In addition, we applied HyPrColoc to perform multi-trait colocalization analysis across the eight traits within a ± 500 kb window flanking each lead SNP identified by the common-factor model, to explore whether subsets of traits may be driven by the same putative causal variant. At the gene and gene-set level, we performed tests using MAGMA, adopting a FDR < 0.05 threshold. Tissue expression enrichment was assessed using GTEx v8 across 54 tissues to identify tissue-specific genetic signals. Finally, the GENE2FUNC module in FUMA was employed to evaluate tissue-specific expression patterns and pathway enrichment for MAGMA-identified genes.

SuSiE-FINEMAP fine-mapping analysis

Fine-mapping aims to pinpoint causal variants underlying GSEM–GWAS signals48. We used echolocatoR (v2.0.3) to perform statistical fine-mapping with SuSiE and FINEMAP (v1.4). For each lead SNP, we analysed all variants within ±500 kb and computed locus-specific LD using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European-ancestry panel and the UK Biobank reference panel. Posterior inclusion probabilities were obtained from both methods and summarised as the mean PIP. Putative causal variants were prioritised if they had P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ in the GSEM–GWAS and a mean PIP ≥ 0.95.

Phenome-wide genetic correlations and causal inference

We first performed cross-phenotype enrichment between GSEM–GWAS signals and publicly reported traits in the iCPAGdb, derived from the National Institutes of Health–National Human Genome Research Institute GWAS Catalog, selecting phenotypes with P < 0.05 as candidates49. Next, we estimated genetic correlations between the GSEM–GWAS results and 2361 female-specific UK Biobank phenotypes using bivariate LD score regression, with LD scores computed from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European-ancestry reference panel; correlations were retained at an FDR < 0.05. Finally, we performed phenome-wide two-sample MR, selecting independent instruments at P ≤ 5 × 10⁻⁸, r² < 0.001 within 10 Mb, and F-statistic > 10; causal effects were estimated using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method and evaluated using Cochran’s Q, the MR‒Egger intercept, and MR-PRESSO, with MR results controlled at FDR < 0.05.

Causal analysis of metabolites, inflammatory cytokines, and protein drug targets by GSEM–GWAS

We applied two-sample MR to evaluate the potential causal effects of plasma metabolites, inflammatory cytokines, and protein drug targets on the results of the GSEM–GWAS. Genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected as instruments. To ensure instrument independence, metabolites and cytokines were LD-pruned at r² ≤ 0.001 within 10 Mb; for proteins from deCODE and UKB-PPP, a more lenient threshold of r² ≤ 0.01 was used due to signal sparsity50. Causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio for single-variant instruments and IVW meta-analysis for multiple instruments, with MR‒Egger as a sensitivity analysis. Multiple testing was controlled using the FDR. Heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were evaluated using Cochran’s Q and the MR‒Egger intercept, and MR-PRESSO was applied to identify and remove outlier instruments.

Cross-tissue TWAS and Bayesian fine-mapping of GSEM–GWAS signals

TWAS links genetically predicted gene expression to complex traits, and sCCA captures shared regulatory components across tissues51. We applied sCCA–TWAS to identify genes whose predicted expression was associated with GSEM–GWAS signals using 37,920 precomputed GTEx v8 expression models. Significance was defined as a TWAS association P value < 1.32 × 10⁻⁶. Genes passing this threshold were further prioritised by Bayesian fine-mapping with FOCUS, which accounts for local LD and shared signals to derive PIP for candidate genes within each locus. Genes whose PIP was >0.8 were considered high-confidence candidates.

Colocalisation with eCAVIAR and fastENLOC

To assess whether GSEM–GWAS associations and molecular quantitative trait locus (QTL) signals are driven by shared causal variants, we performed colocalisation using two complementary methods, eCAVIAR52 and fastENLOC53. We integrated summary statistics from the common-factor and multivariate GWAS with GTEx v8 cis-eQTL and cis-sQTL data across 47 tissues. After allele harmonisation, the LD was estimated using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European-ancestry panel. For each lead variant, the loci were defined as variants with r² > 0.1 plus ±50 kb flanks. eCAVIAR was run, allowing up to two causal signals per locus and reporting the colocalisation posterior probability (CLPP), and fastENLOC reported the regional colocalisation probability (RCP). The significance thresholds were CLPP > 0.01 and RCP > 0.10.

PheWAS analysis

We performed a PheWAS using the AstraZeneca PheWAS portal (https://azphewas.com/) to evaluate the cross-phenotype pleiotropy of FRD susceptibility genes and MR-supported causal proteins. The portal provides results based on the UK Biobank 470 K V5 release, which includes 419,391 participants of European ancestry and 14,706 phenotypes.

Cell-type and functional annotation stratified LD score regression

To quantify the contribution of cell type-specific annotations to the genetic architecture of GSEM–GWAS signals, we performed stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC) to test for cell type-specific SNP-heritability enrichment54. Cell type expression annotations were derived from the Tabula Muris single-cell RNA-sequencing atlas, covering more than 100 mouse cell types55. These annotations, together with baseline genomic annotations from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European-ancestry panel, were incorporated into the S-LDSC model. We then partitioned SNP heritability across functional genomic categories—e.g., enhancers, coding regions, and conserved elements—using the same LD reference. P values in both enrichment and partitioning analyses were adjusted by the FDR method, with an FDR < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

gsMap-based spatial mapping of GSEM–GWAS signals

We integrated GWAS summary statistics with spatial expression maps using gsMap56. The spatial reference data were derived from the mouse embryonic spatial transcriptomic atlas in the MOSTA. We then performed association assessments at both the cell-type and spatial-region levels using gsMap in fast mode with default parameters. Statistical significance was defined using a dual-criterion approach: for a given cell type or spatial region, both the FDR-adjusted Cauchy combination test P value and the median-combination test P value were required to be <0.05 to be considered supported by the data.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was not required for this study, as it used only publicly available, de-identified, summary-level GWAS data. Ethical approval and informed consent for the original studies were obtained by the respective study investigators and data providers, as described in the original publications and data provider documentation. This secondary analysis did not involve new recruitment of human participants, collection of biological samples, access to identifiable individual-level information, or animal experimentation. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. The primary datasets were predominantly derived from individuals of European ancestry; therefore, the generalisability of our findings to non-European or admixed populations may be limited.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1). Multiple-testing correction was conducted using the FDR, and FDR adjustment was performed separately within each analysis type, factor model, and analysis module. For MR, FDR adjustment was also performed independently within each exposure category, including UK Biobank phenotypes, plasma proteins, metabolites, and inflammatory factors. FDR adjustment was implemented using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure in R.
