Reproductive aging drives deterministic microbiota assembly to mitigate uterine oxidative phosphorylation impairment via spermidine production in laying hens
Authors: Dong Dai, Cheng Long, Kaixuan Ma, Wenchang Ma, Pengzu Wang, Guanghai Qi, Zhigang Zhou, Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReproductive aging represents a critical physiological bottleneck characterized by a progressive decline in tissue homeostasis and physiological function. While the gut microbiota is known to shift during host aging, the ecological forces governing the assembly of the reproductive microbiota and its functional feedback on uterine homeostasis remain poorly understood.ResultsWe demonstrated that uterine aging drives a transition from stochastic to deterministic microbial community assembly, selecting for a microbiota enriched in Rhodococcus in aged laying hens. Multi-omics analyses revealed that this deterministic shift acts as a compensatory mechanism to counteract age-related energy metabolism decline in the uterus. Mechanistically, the aged uterus suffered from oxidative phosphorylation impairment due to PARP1-mediated NAD+ depletion in response to accumulated DNA damage. However, the specific colonization of Rhodococcus ruber, or the administration of its metabolite spermidine, rescued this phenotype. Spermidine improved uterine energy metabolism by inducing PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, thereby restoring mitochondrial quality control and ATP production essential for eggshell biomineralization.ConclusionsThis study uncovers a previously unrecognized role of the microbiota in reproductive aging: resident microbes enhance oxidative phosphorylation in the aged uterus through the metabolite spermidine, which induces mitophagy. This process alleviates cellular energy deficiency caused by PARP1-mediated NAD + depletion, elucidating a key mechanism of host–microbe interaction in maintaining uterine energy homeostasis during aging. Background

Reproductive aging manifests as a rapid decline in fertility and tissue integrity before the general organic aging. In females across vertebrate species, this process is characterized by ovarian follicular depletion, oocyte-quality deterioration, and dysfunction [1]. In the poultry industry, the age-related decline in egg production and eggshell quality in laying hens poses a considerable challenge to economic sustainability [2]. The persistent burden of frequent oviposition exerts profound physiological stress on hens, driving reproductive aging and causing eggshell breakage rates to surge to nearly 30% in the aged laying hens [3, 4]. Beyond substantial economic losses, compromised eggshell quality poses a significant public health risk by increasing the likelihood of pathogenic contamination and food-borne illnesses. Traditionally considered a sterile environment, the upper reproductive tract is currently recognized to harbor a low-biomass but metabolically active microbiota that maintains a symbiotic relationship with the host [5, 6]. Emerging evidence suggests that the reproductive microbiota is significantly associated with reproductive health during the host aging process [7, 8]. These findings extend beyond indirect regulation of reproductive aging by the gut microbiota [9, 10], highlighting that the reproductive microbiota has a more direct and substantial influence on host reproductive health [11].

Unlike the gut microbiota, which has been extensively studied in the aging and longevity [12], the ecological principles underlying the formation of the reproductive microbiota throughout the progression from youth to old age remain obscure. In environments that are healthy and abundant in resources, where host barriers remain intact, stochastic processes frequently exert a substantial role in shaping microbial communities [13, 14]. We hypothesize that the aged microenvironment of the reproductive tract, characterized by increased oxidative stress, immune infiltration, and altered nutrient availability, imposes a strong deterministic filter for microbial colonization [15–17]. Theoretically, this filter would preferentially select for microbial taxa capable not only of surviving in the senescent niche but also of engaging in metabolic crosstalk to mitigate host stress [13]. Distinguishing whether the age-associated shift in reproductive microbiota represents a random accumulation of opportunistic pathogens or a directed assembly of adapted symbionts is crucial for understanding the ecology of aging.

Moreover, against the backdrop of global demographic shifts [18], reproductive aging has emerged as a critical factor adversely affecting reproductive outcomes, including fertility, pregnancy maintenance, and offspring health [19–21]. However, the biological mechanisms underlying reproductive aging remain inadequately understood, hindering therapeutic development in females. Nevertheless, current research is largely limited to mammals, with a relative paucity of data regarding other vertebrate species [22, 23]. The laying hen (Gallus gallus domesticus) serves as an exceptionally tractable model for investigating reproductive aging due to its high ovulatory frequency and well-characterized aging patterns [24, 25]. Unlike other animal models, the uterine health of laying hens is directly manifested in the quality of the eggshell, which serves as a measurable physiological indicator. This process fundamentally depends on mitochondrial ATP to facilitate active calcium transport and biomineralization [26]. This intense and persistent metabolic demand, combined with prolonged ovulatory stress, makes the uterus highly susceptible to age-associated mitochondrial dysfunction and tissue integrity deterioration [27]. Therefore, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of energy homeostasis in the aged avian uterus not only addresses critical sustainability challenges in poultry production but also provides insights into therapeutic strategies for age-related reproductive pathologies in women.

The significant shifts in aged uterine microbiota have been characterized, while disturbing them will aggravate uterine damage in aged laying hens [15]. Accordingly, we hypothesize a bidirectional interplay between the aged uterine microenvironment and its resident microbiota, wherein specific microbial alterations act as modulators of uterine aging trajectory. Here, by integrating single-cell-resolution and multi-omics approaches, we characterized the landscape of uterine aging in chickens and identified a pivotal role for the resident microbe Rhodococcus ruber. Rhodococcus ruber-derived spermidine promoted mitophagy to mitigate oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) impairment induced by Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP1)-driven NAD+ depletion in the aged uterus. Our findings challenge the view of the aged microbiome as a purely pathological sign and offer comprehensive insights into uterine healthy aging and its interplay with uterine microbiota.

Methods

Animal experiments

In the experiment on uterine changes in aged laying hens, a total of 180 healthy Hy-Line Brown at 38-week-old (peak egg-laying period) and 78-week-old (late egg-laying period) were selected respectively from a flock of 2000 laying hens. To ensure representation of the trial animals, laying hens were selected based on average egg production and eggshell quality in a flock. The animals were divided into the young group and the aged group with 10 replicates. In the study on microbial function, a total of 40 aging laying hens were randomly allocated into four groups. The uterine microbiota of hens in three treatment groups were depleted by daily intrauterine administration of 2 mL of a combined antibiotic solution (containing 0.4 g ampicillin, 0.2 g vancomycin, 0.4 g metronidazole, and 0.4 g neomycin) for 7 days. Subsequently, hens in the Rhodococcus group received a daily supplementation of 2 mL of Rhodococcus ruber suspension (1 × 109 CFU/mL) for 7 days, while those in the metabolite group were administered 2 mL of Rhodococcus ruber metabolites for 7 days. The metabolite solution was obtained by centrifuging and filtering the supernatant of a Rhodococcus ruber culture. All intrauterine administrations were performed using a sterile insemination catheter. To investigate the role of PARP1 in uterine energy homeostasis in aging hens, a total of 60 aging laying hens were randomly assigned to two groups. The treatment group was subjected to inhibition of DNA damage repair with the PARP1 inhibitor Olaparib, delivered via supplementation at 200 mg/kg feed for 14 days. The appropriate dosage in feed was determined by referencing previous studies [28] and ensuring it does not adversely affect animal production performance.

Animal management and sample collection

The control group was fed a basal diet formulated according to the NRC (Table S1). All animals were allowed to acclimatize for 1 week with ad libitum access to feed and water. The room temperature was maintained at 24 °C, and the lights were switched on at 5 am each day for 16 h of continuous illumination. At the end of the experiment, one bird was randomly selected from each replicate. All samples were collected after euthanasia by cervical dislocation.

Cell culture and treatment

The uterine epithelial cell line of hens was used in this study, which was deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC No. 46710). Cells were maintained in PriMed-iCELL-001-SK medium supplemented with 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The culture medium was replaced every 24 h and cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin (Gibco). Cell density was quantified using an automated cell counter (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and adjusted to 2.5 × 105 cells/mL.

The cells were then treated with 20, 40, or 60 mg/mL D-galactose for 0, 24, and 48 h. Successful establishment of the senescent model in uterine epithelial cells was confirmed via β-galactosidase staining and CCK-8 assay. To investigate the role of PARP1 in energy metabolism of senescent uterine epithelial cells, the PARP1 inhibitor Olaparib (0.15 mM, TargetMol) was applied to suppress PARP1 activity, while 0.5 mM nicotinamide mononucleotide (Aladdin) was supplemented to elevate intracellular NAD+ levels in the aged model. Furthermore, to examine the effect of Rhodococcus-derived spermidine on senescent epithelial cells, the culture medium was supplemented with 5 μM spermidine (Aladdin). This concentration was determined as the optimal spot for efficacy without confounding toxicity in preliminary experiments, which evaluated a spermidine concentration gradient ranging from 0.1 to 50 µM on the cultured cells. Each group consisted of six replicates.

Uterine transmission electron microscopy detection

Small pieces of tissue were isolated from the uterus of laying hens and immediately placed in a Petri dish containing 3% glutaraldehyde for division into samples of about 1 mm3. The samples were then fixed in the same glutaraldehyde solution for 24 h. Following fixation, the samples were rinsed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and subjected to gradient dehydration using increasing concentrations of ethanol. After dehydration, the samples were embedded in epoxy resin and sectioned into ultrathin slices of 50–80 nm thickness. These sections were placed on copper grids and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, the stained ultrathin sections were examined and photographed using a transmission electron microscope to record the ultrastructural characteristics of the tissues. Each treatment group consisted of six replicates.

Sample preparation of single-cell transcriptome

We collected six uterine tissue samples from young and aged laying hens in sterile dishes with 10 mL PBS on ice to remove residual storage solution. Each treatment group consisted of three replicates. The tissues were minced and treated with 0.25% Trypsin and 10 µg/mL DNase I solution in PBS with 5% FBS for digestion at 37 °C with 50 rpm for 40 min. Cells were collected every 20 min to optimize yield and viability. The cell suspensions were filtered through a 40 µm nylon mesh, and red blood cells were lysed using Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution. Cells were washed with PBS plus 2% FBS, and viability was determined by 0.4% Trypan blue staining and Countess® II Automated Cell Counter analysis (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

10 × library preparation and sequencing

We utilized a Gel Beads-in-Emulsion system to load beads with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and cell barcodes, ensuring each cell was paired with a bead. After cell lysis and polyadenylated RNA hybridization to the beads, they were pooled for reverse transcription. During this process, each cDNA molecule was tagged with a UMI and a cell barcode at the 5′ end. The 10 × Genomics protocol guided second-strand cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and amplification. We constructed sequencing libraries by enriching for 3′ transcript ends linked to cell barcodes and UMIs through whole-transcriptome amplification. Library preparation adhered to the Chromium Single Cell (v3.1) standard protocol. Quantification was performed using an Agilent High-Sensitivity DNA Chip and Qubit High-Sensitivity DNA Assay. Sequencing was conducted on a Novaseq Xplus platform in PE150 mode, with both sequencing and bioinformatics analyses carried out by Majorbio Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China.

Single-cell sequencing data processing

We processed sequencing reads with the Cell Ranger (v7.1.0), aligning Illumina FASTQ files to the chicken genome sequence (GRCg6a) using STAR [29]. Gene-Barcode matrices were assembled by quantifying UMIs and excluding non-cell-associated barcodes, yielding a matrix of barcoded cells with gene expression counts. Data were imported into Seurat (v4.1.1) for quality control and single-cell RNA-seq analysis [30], applying default parameters unless specified. Quality control involved filtering low-quality cells based on the number of UMIs, detected genes, and mitochondrial read percentage, using Seurat’s Percentage Feature Set function [30]. Data were normalized and variable genes were identified using Seurat’s Normalize Data function. Integration of multi-sample data was achieved through anchor identification with Seurat’s Find Integration Anchors and Integrate Data functions [30, 31]. Principal components analysis (PCA) reduced the data to the top 30 components post-scaling, and Uniform Manifold Approximation (UMAP) was used for two-dimensional visualization. Clustering was performed using the Louvain Method on PCA-reduced data with a shared nearest neighbor graph [30]. The Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test was applied to discern genes with significant differential expression across the remaining clusters. Furthermore, the identification of cell types was facilitated by employing SingleR along with established marker genes (Table S2) [15, 32]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined with Seurat’s Find Markers function and a likelihood ratio test, considering genes with |log2FC|> 0.25 and Q ≤ 0.05 as DEGs. Functional enrichment analysis using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) identified DEGs enriched in specific pathways, with a Bonferroni-corrected P ≤ 0.05 compared to the transcriptome background. To quantify the senescent state of individual cells, a “senescence score” was calculated using the AddModuleScore function in the Seurat package (v4.0), based on a curated set of 591 senescence-associated genes (Table S3) identified by DEGs of bulk RNA-seq on uterine tissues from young and aging hens with the AgeAnno database.

The pseudotime trajectory analysis of gene expressions

The proposed chronological analysis can remodel the process of cellular change over time by constructing trajectories of intercellular change. The DDRTree algorithm in the Monocle2 software was used to learn the sequence of gene expression changes that each cell had to undergo to find the proper location in the trajectory [33]. The DEGs were first downscaled and a minimum spanning tree was constructed. The single-cell expression data were then projected into a low-dimensional space, and the fitted optimal trajectory of cellular gene expression was obtained. In addition, branch expression analysis modeling was employed to identify gene expression patterns that are dependent on branch characteristics.

Cell–cell communication analysis

CellPhoneDB (v.2.0) was used to analyze cell-cell communication, which was a Python-based analytical platform for exploring interactions between individual cell clusters [34]. It boasted a publicly accessible database of refined ligands, receptors, and their interactions. Initially, we pinpointed genes linked to aging in each cell type, proposing them as potential candidates for ligand-receptor interactions. Then, the CellPhoneDB statistical methodology was used to pinpoint ligand-receptor pairs, considering only those expressed in over 5% of cells for the next analysis. This approach ensures a focused examination of the most prevalent cellular communications implicated in aging processes.

Microbial 16S rDNA sequencing in the uterus

Uterine microbiota was sampled using CLASSIQ sterile disposable swabs (Coppan) and subsequently deposited into sterile cryovials containing 2 mL of PBS. DNA was extracted following the protocol of the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA kit (Omega Bio-Tek). The integrity and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the analysis of the V4 region of the microbial 16S rDNA gene, PCR amplification was conducted using primers 5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′ and 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′. The amplicon PCR protocol, executed on an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (Applied Biosystems), consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The purified PCR products were sequenced utilizing the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Microbial 16S rDNA data processing

Raw microbial sequencing data were analyzed, subjected to quality control, and filtered employing QIIME 2. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were refined and denoised using the DADA2 platform. Subsequently, ASVs were taxonomically classified by alignment with the SILVA (v.138) database across various phylogenetic levels. To evaluate microbial β-diversity, principal-component ordination was conducted, while the Analysis of Similarities was utilized to detect statistical disparities based on the Bray–Curtis distance metric. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was deployed to assess inter-group statistical differences, alongside the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test. Furthermore, linear discriminant analysis effect size was applied to discern microbes significantly enriched within each group. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Absolute quantification of total bacteria and Rhodococcus DNA

The abundance of total bacteria and Rhodococcus in uterine samples was estimated through absolute quantification. The 16S rRNA gene was targeted using primers Eub338 (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and Eub518 (5′-ATTACCGCGGCTGCTGG-3′), while Rhodococcus DNA was identified using Rho-F (5′-GGGTCTAATACCGGATAKGACC-3′) and Rho-R (5′-CCCGTATCGCCTGCAAGC-3′). For the preparation of standards, the target sequences were cloned into the pMD18-T vector following PCR amplification and purification. After transformation into competent cells and subsequent extraction, the identity of the recombinant plasmids was confirmed by sequencing. The plasmid concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and was utilized to calculate the corresponding copy numbers. Linear regression equations (standard curves) were generated using a decadic serial dilution of these plasmids. The final amplification was carried out on an ABI 7300 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA), and the absolute bacterial concentration was determined based on the established standard curve. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Co-occurrence network analysis

CoNet plugin within the Cytoscape (v.3.8.2) graphical interface was employed to build microbial co-occurrence networks, a technique designed for uncovering non-random microbial co-occurrence patterns [35]. This process resulted in the development of two separate networks, each characterizing the microbiota of young and aged uteri. Spearman’s correlation, setting a stringent filter threshold at 0.001, and optimization methods were applied to ascertain the most significant correlation threshold. The construction of these networks was predicated on a P-value threshold of 0.05. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Neutral community model analysis

The Neutral Community Model (NCM) has proven to be a versatile tool in the analysis of a broad spectrum of ecological phenomena. Its strength lies in its ability to deduce the stochastic processes that underlie the mechanisms of community assembly [36]. The getopt (v.1.20.3) package was used to analyze this model. The parameter Nm, which is a measure of dispersal among communities, plays a pivotal role. Nm shapes the correlation between the frequency of species occurrence and their regional relative abundance. Furthermore, the parameter R2 is a metric of how well the data align with the neutral model. The higher R2 value suggests a closer alignment with the neutral model and implies more random and less deterministic factors influencing the microbial construction [37]. To ensure the robustness of these fit statistics, we calculated 95% confidence intervals through bootstrapping, with 1000 replicates to enhance the accuracy of our assessment. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Normalized stochastic ratio

The Normalized Stochasticity Ratio (NST) analysis serves as a robust tool for the quantitative assessment of ecological stochasticity based on a universal null model framework. The NST index employs a 50% boundary to demarcate the transition between deterministic (< 50%) and stochastic (> 50%) community assembly. The robustness of this index was validated using simulated communities across diverse ecological drivers, including abiotic filtering, interspecific competition, environmental noise, and varying spatial scales. NST was calculated to determine the contribution of the stochastic process to the community assembly of microbes using R software with the package of “NST” (v3.1.10) [38]. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Metagenomic analysis

Uterine microbiota was sampled using CLASSIQ sterile disposable swabs (Coppan) and subsequently deposited into sterile cryovials containing 2 mL of PBS. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the E. Z. N. A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the DNA extract was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific). The resulting products were fragmented to an average size of approximately 400 bp on a Covaris M220 ultrasonicator (Covaris). The NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq (Bioo Scientific) was used to construct the paired-end library and Sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq system at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Low-quality reads with length < 50 bp, quality value < 20, and N bases were removed using FLASH (v.1.2.7). MEGAHIT assembled data into contigs based on the succinct de Bruijn graph [39]. Furthermore, MetaGene was used to predict the open reading frames (ORFs) from each contig [40]. Additionally, ORFs with lengths ≥ 100 bp were retrieved and translated into amino acid sequences. Then, cluster database at high identity with tolerance was used to construct a non-redundant gene catalog [41], and mapping with high-quality reads (95% identity) using Short Oligonucleotide Analysis Package to evaluate the gene abundance of each sample [42]. Finally, for the taxonomy and function of the species, the contigs were annotated using Diamond [43] against the Non-Redundant database and KEGG database with an e-value cutoff of 1e−5. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Bulk transcriptomic sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the uterine tissues of laying hens using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequent to DNA contamination removal from the genome with DNase I, the integrity of the purified total RNA was ascertained utilizing the RNA Nano 6000 kit on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies). A total of 1 μg of RNA per sample was selected for library construction, which was executed with Illumina’s TruSeqTM RNA Library Preparation Kit (New England Biolabs) in accordance with the provided guidelines. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) employing a paired-end methodology. Then, sequence trimming was performed using Fastp to eliminate low-quality (< 20 nt) bases and to discard reads with a high N-content (> 10%), thereby yielding clean sequences [44]. The quality-controlled reads were then aligned to the chicken reference genome (Gallus gallus 5.0) using HiSat2 software [45]. Gene expression levels were quantified employing the FPKM method, and differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 [46]. The calculated P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with |log2FC|> 1 and FDR < 0.05 were designated as DEGs. Ultimately, DEGs were subjected to GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to elucidate their biological significance. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Eggshell quality and ultrastructure

An Egg Force Reader (Israel Orka Food Technology) was used for the detection of eggshell strength. Eggshell thickness and color were evaluated using the Eggshell Thickness Gauge (Israel Orka Food Technology) and Colorimeter (NH310), respectively. Eggshell fragments from the equatorial region were prepared by removing inner membranes, dried, and gold-sputtered for scanning electron microscopy (SU8020, Hitachi, Japan). Cross-sectional images were acquired at 180 × magnification. The effective layer thickness (cuticle top to palisade layer bottom) and mammillary layer thickness (palisade layer bottom to membrane top) were measured. The calcified layer thickness was calculated as their sum, with each layer’s ratio expressed as its percentage of the total calcified thickness. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded uterine sections (5 μm) of laying hens were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. After blocking with 10% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γH2AX, PCNA, and PARP1. Subsequently, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1h, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescence signals were captured using a Nikon Ti2-E and quantified via ImageJ software. PBS-replaced primary antibodies served as negative controls to ensure specificity.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Uterine levels of IL-1β and SIRT1 were determined via chicken ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Briefly, tissue homogenates were centrifuged, and supernatants were collected. After protein normalization using a BCA assay, ELISA procedures were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm. For mitophagy assessment, cells (1 × 106 cells/mL in PBS) were lysed via three freeze-thaw cycles. The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min (4 °C) to harvest the supernatants. The expression levels of PINK1 and Parkin were then quantified using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., China) strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols. Each treatment group consisted of six or eight replicates.

Mitochondrial complex activity assay

The activity of mitochondrial complexes II, Ⅲ, and IV was quantified using the commercial assay kit (BC3230, BC3240, and BC0940, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Briefly, cell samples (approximately106 cells per sample) were resuspended in 200 uL of the provided extraction buffer and thoroughly homogenized on ice. The homogenate was first centrifuged at 600×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then transferred to a new 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and subjected to a second centrifugation at 11,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C to precipitate the mitochondria. The enriched mitochondrial pellet was subsequently resuspended in the extraction buffer and subjected to three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles to ensure complete disruption of the mitochondrial membranes. Finally, the enzymatic activity of the complexes was measured spectrophotometrically strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each treatment group consisted of six or eight replicates.

Morphological examination

Approximately 1 cm of uterine tissue from laying hens was harvested and subsequently fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for a duration of 24 h. Following fixation, the tissue underwent deparaffinization using xylene, anhydrous ethanol, and an alcohol solution, prior to being rinsed with distilled water. The tissue sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin and were dehydrated and mounted with neutral gum. The morphological characteristics of the uterine tissue sections were subsequently examined and analyzed using a light microscope (NIKON CI-S), which was equipped with a medical image analysis system. Each treatment group consisted of eight replicates.

Antibiotic intervention in oviduct microbiota of aged laying hens

Vancomycin was administered via oviductal infusion to laying hens at a 0.5% concentration in 2.0 mL over 7 days. The antibiotic solution was prepared on the day of use by dissolving the powder in sterile PBS. Laying hens were placed in a supine position, and their cloacae were disinfected with 75% ethanol before the procedure. The oviductal opening was accessed using a gentle pressure technique, mimicking artificial insemination, to introduce the vancomycin. A single-use sterile inseminating tube was then used to infuse a neomycin solution into the uterus. Post-treatment, laying hens were kept inverted for five minutes to facilitate solution retention before being returned to their cages. The laying hens showed a high degree of tolerance to the antibiotic regimen, with no signs of stress or diarrhea, contrasting with the control group.

Isolation of Rhodococcus in the uterus of hens

Under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet, hen uterine tissue samples were aseptically placed in a petri dish and minced thoroughly using a surgical scalpel. The minced tissue was homogenized in 20 mL of sterile aerobic preservation buffer and filtered to remove debris. The filtrate was serially diluted up to 10−5, and a 200-µL dilution was spread onto three types of aerobic agar plates: R2A (HB0167, Haibo Biotechnology), TSA (HB0177, Haibo Biotechnology), and Columbia agar (HB0124, Haibo Biotechnology). All plates were incubated aerobically at 28 °C for 3–9 days. Distinct single colonies were selected and inoculated into TSB liquid medium (HB4114, Haibo Biotechnology), followed by aerobic incubation at 28 ℃ for 2–3 days. Bacterial isolates were preliminarily identified using MALDI-TOF-MS (Autoflex III LRF 200-CID, Bruker). Target strains were then streaked onto TSA plates for purification and incubated under the same conditions. A second round of streaking was performed to ensure purity, after which single colonies were transferred again to TSB broth and cultured aerobically at 28 °C for 2–3 days. Final isolates were preserved for further whole-genome sequencing.

Whole-genome analysis of Rhodococcus

Genomic DNA was extracted from Rhodococcus using a commercial bacterial DNA kit with three replicates (TO7-100, China). After quality assessment, sequencing libraries were constructed and subjected to whole-genome sequencing on both PacBio SequelIIe and Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platforms. For Illumina sequencing, DNA was fragmented to 400 bp, and libraries were prepared using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit, followed by 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing. Bioinformatic analyses were performed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Raw Illumina reads were quality-trimmed using fastp (v0.23.0), and high-quality reads were combined with PacBio HiFi data for hybrid assembly with Unicycler (v0.4.8) [47]. The assembly was polished with Pilon (v1.22). Gene prediction included coding sequences with Prodigal (v2.6.3) [48], tRNA genes with tRNAscan-SE (v2.0) [49], rRNA genes with Barrnap v0.9 (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap), and plasmid genes with GeneMarkS [50]. Functional annotation was performed against NR, Swiss-Prot, Pfam, GO, COG, KEGG, and CAZy databases using BLASTP, Diamond, and HMMER. Secondary metabolite gene clusters were identified using antiSMASH (v5.1.2). Moreover, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA 6.0 software. Based on the phylogenetic tree of housekeeping genes (Table S4), the isolate showed 99% sequence similarity to Rhodococcus ruber and was consequently designated as Rhodococcus ruber NML-01.

Identification of microbial metabolites

The metabolic profile of the uterine microbiota and R. ruber NML-01 was analyzed with six replicates by LC–MS. Briefly, 100 μL of supernatant was combined with 400 μL of pre-cooled methanol:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). The solution was vortexed, sonicated at 5 °C for 30 min, and then held at − 20 °C for 30 min to deposit proteins. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred for analysis. LC–MS analysis was conducted on a UHPLC-Q Exactive platform. Separation was performed on a Waters HSS T3 column with data acquisition in both positive and negative ESI modes. Data processing included peak alignment, normalization via the sum-normalization method, and filtration to remove low-abundance features. Metabolites were annotated by comparing the MS data to the KEGG and HMDB repositories. The analysis was performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform from Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using Statistical Analysis Software (v.9.2), and two-tailed t-tests were employed to assess statistical differences between the two groups. The analyses for more than two groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. The data were expressed as mean ± SD, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

The specific colonization of uterine microbiota in aged hens involves the regulation of tissue aging and eggshell quality

Significant changes in the uterine cell structure were observed in aged hens, emphasizing cellular edema and membrane damage, particularly membrane damage, intramembrane matrix lysis, and cristae disintegration in mitochondria (Fig. 1b). The level of β-galactosidase was significantly increased in the aged uterus, suggesting a characteristic of uterine aging. This observation was further verified by increased inflammatory cytokine IL-1β in the aged uterus (Fig. 1c). The eggshell quality of aged laying hens decreased significantly compared to young laying hens, including eggshell thickness and breaking strength (P < 0.05, Fig. S1a, b). Ultrastructural analysis revealed that the thickness of the calcified layer, effective layer, and their proportions were significantly lower in aged laying hens, thereby affecting eggshell quality (P < 0.05, Fig. S1c, d). The uterus of laying hens, as the organ responsible for eggshell formation, harbors a diverse microbial community. Uterine microbiota underwent significant compositional changes in aged laying hens (P < 0.05, Fig. 1d). Specifically, the uterus of aged laying hens is characterized by a high relative abundance of Rhodococcus (Fig. 1e, f), which was found to be significantly positively correlated with eggshell quality (Fig. S2).

To validate the functional role of Rhodococcus, a combined antibiotic perfusion was administered to eliminate the uterine microbiota of laying hens, which resulted in a significant reduction in the uterine bacterial load (Fig. 1g). The depletion of the uterine microbiota led to a significant deterioration in eggshell quality (P < 0.05, Fig. 1h). Concurrently, the expression levels of β-galactosidase and rH2AX in uterine tissue were significantly elevated, accompanied by a decrease in PCNA (P < 0.05, Fig. 1i, j). A significant increase in the absolute abundance of Rhodococcus was observed in the uterus with the backfill of Rhodococcus (Fig. 1g), which significantly restored eggshell quality and mitigated the uterine aging and DNA damage caused by microbiota ablation (P < 0.05, Fig. 1h). Particularly, the prediction accuracy of Rhodococcus for eggshell quality reached 0.88, suggesting its potential regulatory function (Fig. 1k). Collectively, the specific colonization of uterine Rhodococcus in the aged uterus plays a critical role in maintaining physiological homeostasis and eggshell quality.

The remodeling of the uterine microbiota in aged laying hens is driven by the microenvironment of uterine aging

The underlying factors that contribute to the alterations in the microbial composition of the uterus remain inadequately understood. Here, the effects of uterine aging on the construction and stability of microbial interaction networks were found. The microbial network of the young uterus comprised 48 nodes and 361 edges (R >|0.70|, P < 0.05, Fig. 2a). The aged-uterus microbial network consisted of 49 nodes and 227 edges (R >|0.70|, P < 0.05, Fig. 2b), indicating that the microbial interactions in the young uterus were more intricate. The investigation into the NCM was performed to identify the factors that influence the microbial interaction and stability in the aged uterus. The higher value suggests a closer alignment with the neutral model and implies more random factors influencing the microbial construction. The NCM showed a goodness of fit for the microbial communities in the young uterus compared with the aged uterus (Fig. 2c, d). Furthermore, most of the NST values were higher than the 50% boundary point for the microbial communities in the young uterus, while they were significantly lower than the 50% boundary point for the aged uterus (Fig. 2c, d). Therefore, the community assembly in the young uterus was predominantly shaped by stochastic processes. In addition, a more important role of deterministic processes in contributing to the assembly of the microbiota in the aged uterus was observed, which might be linked to alterations in the uterine microenvironment [51].

We further characterized the transition in uterine microbial functions during uterine aging using metagenomics. In total, 117 GB of data and an average of 98,518,659 high-quality reads per sample were obtained from metagenomic sequencing, which further generated 4,165,740 contigs from the MEGAHIT assembly. The functional profiles of the genes were initially annotated using the Clusters of Orthologous Groups database (Fig. 2e). The predominant functions of the uterine microbiota in laying hens involved intracellular transport and secretion, gene replication and repair, protein modification, and signal transduction, as well as the metabolism of amino acids, carbohydrates, and lipids. In the uterus of laying hens, microbial functions can be classified into two distinct functional categories (Fig. 2f). In contrast to the young uterine microbiota, the aged microbiota categorized as Functional Type 2 were involved in the regulation of energy metabolism, as well as aging, neurodegenerative and infectious diseases (Fig. 2g). Consequently, the function of uterine microbiota undergoes a directional shift with the progression of uterine aging.

To investigate the adaptability of the uterine microbiota in aged individuals, we modulated the microbial community using vancomycin (Fig. S3a). The administration of antibiotics induced alterations in the microbial composition (R2 = 0.417, P = 0.001, Fig. S3b). A notable increase in the Shannon index, as well as an enhancement in the abundance of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, was found in the aged uterus (P < 0.05, Fig. S3c, d). Further, the microbial disruption exacerbated the infiltration of lymphocytes and granulocytes observed in the aged uterus, accompanied by a significant elevation in the levels of IL-1β and IgA (P < 0.05, Fig. S3e, f, h). Conversely, there was a decrease in the content of Mucin-2 in the uterus with antibiotics (P < 0.05, Fig. S3g). Concurrently, the eggshell quality as measured by eggshell thickness and breaking strength exhibited a notable decline in the aged uterus following microbial shifts (P < 0.05, Fig. S3i, j). These findings highlight that the native microbiota has adapted to the environment in the aged uterus, and altering it will exacerbate uterine damage and aging. Overall, the aging process of the uterus drives the directed succession of microbiota, thereby establishing a homeostasis of microbe-host interactions.

Single-cell transcriptomic landscape reveals changes in cell-type specificity during uterine aging in laying hens

Although aging of uterine tissues in aged laying hens has been identified, changes in cellular resolution remain unclear due to cellular heterogeneity. Single-cell sequencing from the uterine tissues of laying hens was applied using the 10 × Genomics Chromium platform in this study. We obtained 57,689 cells from uterine samples of young and aged laying hens, which were partitioned into 28 clusters by the nonlinear dimensionality reduction analysis. It revealed similar cellular landscapes and differences in the proportions of several clusters (Fig. S4). Based on the expression of cell-specific marker genes (Figs. S5 and S6), a total of 13 distinct cell types were identified, including fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, epithelial cells, ciliated epithelial cells, non-ciliated epithelial secretory cells, epithelial progenitor cells, endothelial cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, B cells, plasma cells, erythroblasts, and lymphatic endothelium (Fig. 3a). There was a significant difference in the proportion of these cell types in the young and aged uterus (Fig. 3b). Epithelial cells were increased in the aged group compared to the young group, which was accompanied by a decrease in the number of CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and B cells.

Enhanced DNA damage repair by PARP1 leads to decreased OXPHOS of epithelial cells during uterine aging

To elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms in the aging process of the uterine epithelium, we constructed the pseudotime trajectory of single cells. The cell trajectory was divided into five states and two branching points (Fig. 4b), which were further analyzed for the dynamic alterations in gene expression throughout the whole lineage (Fig. S7a). Overall, a shift in energy-related metabolism was found during cellular senescence. The expression of NDUFA4, ATP6AP1, CYCS, COX6C, NDUFB2, ND4, ND5, and ND6 was suppressed to reduce the cellular OXPHOS pathway (Fig. S7b). In contrast, pathways associated with glycolipid metabolism, including glycerolipid, fructose, and mannose metabolism, were enhanced by the up-regulation of DGKH and GMDS (Fig. S7b), thereby altering the pattern of cellular energy metabolism. Furthermore, the GeneSwitch responsible for biological alterations was elucidated in epithelial cells during uterine aging. The enhancement of functions related to ion transport and cilium movement led to a transition of uterine epithelial cells towards state 2, indicating that early senescent uterine cells were predisposed to ciliated epithelial cells (Fig. S8a). As the initial trigger, the signal transduction in response to DNA damage and suppressed regulation of histone H4 acetylation might contribute to the senescence of uterine epithelial cells (Fig. S8d). Subsequently, epithelial cells exhibiting up-regulation of genes AGR2, HMOX1, EGR1, ATF3, F3, and SOX9 would progress towards state 5 as a result of regulation of cell proliferation, communication, apoptosis, and response to stimulus (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, epithelial cells demonstrated the down-regulation of the precursor metabolites and energy, NADH dehydrogenase activity, and oxidoreductase activity in the electron transport chain (Fig. 4c). Therefore, state 5 indicated that the epithelial cells had progressed to a late stage of senescence, mainly due to the accumulation of cellular damage ultimately leading to a shift in energy metabolism.

Next, we conducted a comparative analysis of the scores for DNA damage and repair of uterine cells between young and aged laying hens. Our findings indicated an increase in the DNA damage score in the aged uterus, accompanied by a higher DNA damage repair score (Fig. 4d). As a key enzyme in DNA damage repair, PARP1 consumes large amounts of NAD+ through hydrolysis to repair break sites [53]. The current study revealed elevated PARP1 expression and markedly reduced NAD+ levels in the aged uterus, leading to insufficient substrates for mitochondrial respiration and ATP production (Fig. 4d). Consequently, the significantly decreased respiratory capacity of aged uterine mitochondria further compromises the energy supply required for Ca2+ transport in epithelial cells, thereby impairing eggshell biomineralization and eggshell quality. Taken together, PARP1-mediated enhancement of DNA repair results in a decline in energy metabolism within epithelial cells during uterine aging.

We hypothesized that inhibiting PARP1 would rescue mitochondrial energy metabolism of epithelial cells during uterine aging. In vitro treatment of senescent uterine epithelial cells from laying hens with PARP1 inhibitors significantly elevated intracellular NAD + content (Fig. 5a, b). Subsequently, the expression of SIRT1 was significantly enhanced (Fig. 5c), leading to enhanced mitochondrial OXPHOS and ATP production (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, this pathway was further supported by the observation that the supplementation of NAD + precursors in senescent cells also activated SIRT1 and promoted ATP production (Fig. 5b, c). In vivo experiments demonstrated that inhibiting PARP1 in the uterus of aged laying hens improved eggshell thickness and breaking strength (Fig. 5d, e). Treatment with PARP1 inhibitors significantly elevated NAD + level and SIRT1 expression and increased complex IV activity, thereby enhancing mitochondrial respiration and ATP production in the uterus of aged laying hens (Fig. 5f). Further, the transcriptomic analysis reveals remodeling of uterine energy metabolism in aged laying hens (Fig. 5g). This may account for the promoted cell proliferation following PARP1 inhibition in the aged uterus, as evidenced by the increased expression of PCNA (Fig. S9a). Consequently, SIRT1 enhanced mitochondrial energy metabolism and cell proliferation activity, supporting uterine calcium transport and ultimately increasing calcium content in eggshells (Fig. 5h).

Spermidine derived from Rhodococcus ruber induces mitophagy of senescent epithelial cells to enhance uterine energy metabolism

Previous studies have identified benzamide, a microbial metabolite that inhibits PARP1 activity, in aged uteruses [16]. Based on the established interactions between uterine microbiota and the aging microenvironment, Rhodococcus may influence eggshell quality by regulating mitochondrial energy metabolism through modulation of PARP1. First, a strain of Rhodococcus ruber NML-01 was isolated from the uterus of aged laying hens, and its metabolites were collected following in vitro culture (Fig. 6a). Subsequently, the collected metabolites produced by R. ruber NML-01 were reintroduced into the uterus after microbial clearance was achieved via a combined antibiotic regimen (Fig. 6a). This approach was employed to validate the role of R. ruber NML-01 metabolites in modulating energy metabolism and eggshell quality in the aged uterus. R. ruber NML-01 metabolites ameliorated the decline in eggshell quality caused by antibiotic intervention, specifically improving eggshell thickness and ultrastructure (Fig. S9b, c). Concurrently, mitochondrial respiration was significantly enhanced, promoting ATP production and cellular proliferation in the uterus (Fig. 6b). Although PARP1 expression and activity remained unchanged, autophagy-related signaling pathways were significantly enriched and were accompanied by a significant decrease in rH2AX (Fig. 6c, d). Meanwhile, transmission electron microscopy confirmed that R. ruber NML-01 metabolites triggered mitophagy in the aged uterus (Fig. 6e). Thus, R. ruber NML-01-derived metabolites may improve mitochondrial quality control, which in turn enhances OXPHOS.

In addition, to further uncover the specific metabolite mediating R. ruber NML-01-driven modulation of uterine cellular homeostasis, a R. ruber NML-01 recolonization experiment in the hen uterus was conducted. The uterine microbial metabolome of aged laying hens was characterized across three groups: the control, antibiotic-induced depletion, and R. ruber NML-01 rescue groups. PCA revealed that metabolites produced by R. ruber NML-01 in the uterus of aged laying hens were significantly distinct from those in the control and antibiotic-treated groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 6f). Spermidine, ranked among the top 10 metabolites in terms of importance (Fig. S10), exhibited significantly higher levels in both the control and R. ruber NML-01 groups compared to the antibiotic group. Given its role in regulating mitochondrial quality, spermidine may serve as a pivotal metabolite mediating the modulation of uterine cellular homeostasis by R. ruber NML-01. (Fig. 6g). Furthermore, whole-genome sequencing revealed that the R. ruber NML-01 possesses a genome of 5,942,729 bp with 70.38% GC and 5485 genes (Fig. 6h). Notably, a complete spermidine biosynthesis pathway was identified R. ruber NML-01 (Fig. 6i). Subsequently, the role of spermidine in regulating cellular homeostasis was validated in uterine epithelial cells of laying hens. The results of vitro showed significantly upregulated expression of Parkin and PINK1 proteins (Fig. 6j), indicating that spermidine promoted mitophagy in senescent epithelial cells. Meanwhile, spermidine increased NAD + levels in senescent uterine epithelial cells and significantly enhanced mitochondrial respiration and ATP production by enhancing the activity of complex IV (Fig. 6k). Therefore, spermidine derived from R. ruber NML-01 maintains energy homeostasis in senescent uterine epithelial cells by inducing mitophagy, which facilitates the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria [54].

Discussion

Uterine aging mechanisms remain poorly understood due to cellular heterogeneity, despite existing bulk transcriptomic profiles in laying hens [55–57]. Single-cell transcriptomics addresses this by providing high-resolution cellular atlases, allowing for the precise identification of critical aging-related cell clusters and the reconstruction of cell pseudotime trajectory. This study elucidated the biological mechanisms underlying uterine aging in laying hens via single-cell transcriptomic sequencing, identifying epithelial cells as the core population driving functional decline. Our results demonstrated that the significant accumulation of DNA damage in uterine epithelial cells triggers the hyperactivation of the DNA repair enzyme PARP1. The persistent activation of PARP1 consumed excessive amounts of the intracellular NAD+ pool, leading to a precipitous decline in NAD+ levels [58, 59]. As NAD+ is an essential substrate for the deacetylase SIRT1 [60], its depletion directly inhibits SIRT1 activity, thereby blocking PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis and OXPHOS [61]. Single-cell pseudotime analysis further confirmed a metabolic reprogramming in senescent epithelial cells, characterized by a shift from efficient OXPHOS to inefficient glycolipid metabolism and glycolysis. This remodeling of energy metabolism resulted in ATP insufficiency, which failed to support the energy demands required for active calcium transport in the uterine epithelium, ultimately impairing eggshell biomineralization [26, 62]. Notably, we found that inhibiting PARP1 or supplementing with NAD+ precursors effectively restored SIRT1 activity and mitochondrial function, reversing uterine aging phenotypes and improving eggshell quality. These findings clarify the critical role of the PARP1-NAD+-SIRT1 axis in regulating uterine aging in laying hens.

In addition, cellular senescence is a cellular state triggered by stress injury or certain physiological processes [63], primarily characterized by its proliferation arrest, resistance to apoptosis, and the secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASP) [64]. In the aged uterus of laying hens, a notable finding was the molecular alterations linked to cellular senescence pathways across various cell types. These changes included oxidative damage, inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, and apoptosis, accompanied by elevated cellular senescence scores. Previous studies have demonstrated that P53-P21-RB and P16-RB are activated in cells to regulate E2 transcription factor and histone deacetylases, which consequently lead to the proliferation arrest of these two pathways into senescence [65, 66]. The production of SASP in senescent cells primarily mediates the activation of NF-κB through the P38 MAPK signaling pathway, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [64]. Currently, numerous studies have linked reproductive aging to a chronic inflammatory microenvironment [67–69]. Activation of inflammation-related signaling pathways (T-cell receptor, Chemokine, Th17) and cell proliferation and apoptosis-related signaling pathways (MAPK, PI3K-Akt, ECM-receptor, ROS) in the aged uterus was observed in our results. Furthermore, senescent epithelial cells further induced uterine aging by diverse patterns of ligand-receptor interactions with other cell types in the cell-cell communication network, which could enhance our understanding of cell interactions in reproductive aging. Collectively, these results provide support and novel insights regarding the accumulation of inflammatory microenvironment-induced cellular damage driving uterine senescence, as well as the interactions among senescent cells.

Notably, uterine aging further drived deterministic assembly of the microbial community in the aged laying hens. Our study reveals a fundamental ecological restructuring of the uterine microbiome during the aging process, moving beyond simple descriptions of dysbiosis to uncover the forces shaping community dynamics. In young hens, the microbial network was characterized by higher complexity and a stronger adherence to the neutral community model, indicating that stochastic processes predominantly shape the community [13]. This stochastic assembly is typical of healthy, resource-rich ecosystems where host immune tolerance and intact epithelial barriers allow for a diverse, transient colonization without strong selection pressure. This is because healthy microbiota often exhibit stochastic stability, while stressed microbiota diverge into specific states driven by the stressor [14]. However, we observed a marked shift towards deterministic assembly in the aged uterus, evidenced by a lower fit to the neutral model, reduced network complexity, and increased phylogenetic clustering. This suggests that the aged uterine microenvironment in terms of oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and cellular senescence imposes rigorous environmental filtering. This selective filter precludes transient colonizers while promoting the enrichment of specific taxa adapted to withstand or ameliorate these stringent conditions [51].

This transition parallels findings in other ecosystems under stress, such as soil succession following disturbance or gut microbiome development under nutrient restriction, where extreme conditions enforce deterministic selection [70]. In the context of the reproductive tract, this deterministic shift challenges the prevailing view that aging is simply a passive breakdown of mucosal barriers leading to random pathogen invasion [71]. Instead, it represents an active, adaptive recruitment of an aging-associated microbiota. The significant enrichment of Rhodococcus in the aged uterus is not an accidental infection but a deterministic outcome of this selection pressure. Rhodococcus species are renowned for their metabolic versatility, vast enzymatic repertoire, and exceptional stress tolerance in environmental reservoirs [72, 73]. These characteristics facilitate their persistence in the inflamed, oxidatively stressed uterine niche. Crucially, the positive correlation between Rhodococcus abundance and eggshell quality suggests that this deterministic assembly is functionally compensatory rather than purely pathogenic. A pivotal finding of this work is the identification of Rhodococcus ruber as a microbial cell factory for spermidine production, which effectively alleviates uterine aging. Genomic analysis of the isolated R. ruber NML-01 strain confirmed the presence of a complete and robust spermidine biosynthesis pathway, which allows for efficient production even under nutrient limitation or environmental stress [74]. The reintroduction of R. ruber metabolites into the antibiotic-treated aged uterus significantly reversed the decline in eggshell quality and reduced DNA damage markers (rH2AX), establishing the causality of this microbial metabolite.

Spermidine is widely recognized as an anti-aging molecule, primarily through its induction of systemic autophagy and lifespan extension [75]. Our data extended the current understanding of spermidine to the reproductive system, demonstrating its supportive role in uterine function. This is consistent with recent reports where spermidine supplementation was shown to rejuvenate oocyte quality in aged mice [76]. Critically, we demonstrated that Rhodococcus-derived spermidine facilitates mitophagy via the activation of the PINK1/Parkin signaling pathway. The targeted clearance of damaged mitochondria not only limits oxidative stress but also addresses the chronic DNA damage [77]. By enhancing the metabolic efficiency of the residual healthy mitochondria, mitophagy indirectly mitigates NAD+ exhaustion, which is essential for restoring SIRT1-dependent pathways. This mechanism is consistent with findings in neurodegenerative models where NAD+ precursors and mitophagy inducers have been shown to mitigate PARP1-induced bioenergetic failure [78]. Consequently, our study introduces a distinct ecological insight: rather than depending solely on dietary or systemic sources, the aged host relies on commensal-derived symbionts [79]. The fact that antibiotic treatment leads to Mucin-2 deficiency and exacerbates aging phenotypes highlights the significance of the symbiotic relationship. Consequently, maintaining the functional integrity of this microbe-host metabolic axis is a prerequisite for healthy reproductive aging. These results offer significant insights for both poultry science and human reproductive medicine, highlighting the potential of the reproductive microbiota as a therapeutic target for reproductive health.

From the perspective of poultry production, our findings provide a mechanistic blueprint for extending the productive lifespan of laying hens, a primary objective for global poultry sustainability. The identification of the Rhodococcus-spermidine-mitophagy axis offers tangible targets for the development of next-generation precision probiotics or postbiotic feed additives. By restoring the NAD+/ATP supply chain in the aged uterus, these solutions can significantly mitigate the age-related decline in eggshell thickness and strength. This not only reduces the economic burden of cracked eggs, but also supports the commercial goal of a 100-week laying cycle, thereby reducing the environmental footprint per egg produced. Although our work provides a template for targeting reproductive aging via the microbiota in poultry, the bidirectional causal relationship between specific microbes and aged tissue in other animal species requires further elucidation to establish more generalizable therapeutic principles.

Conclusion

This study uncovers a previously unrecognized role of the microbiota in reproductive aging: specifically, that resident microbes enhance OXPHOS in the aged uterus. We demonstrate that PARP1-driven DNA damage repair leads to NAD + depletion and a systemic decline in the energy metabolism of epithelial cells during uterine aging. The aging process of the uterus drives the directed shift in the function of uterine microbiota toward a protective state, thereby establishing a homeostasis of microbe-host interactions. Microbial disruption exacerbates aging in the aged uterus, while R. ruber or its metabolite restores cellular energy homeostasis and enhances ATP synthesis. Mechanistically, R. ruber-derived spermidine induces mitophagy to enhance mitochondrial OXPHOS and spare NAD + pools in senescent uterine epithelial cells (Fig. 7). These findings challenge the prevailing perspective concerning microbial colonization in the aged tissue is merely a sign of decay, instead revealing a symbiotic role for microbe-host interactions in supporting healthy aging.
