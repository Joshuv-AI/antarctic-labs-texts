Global spread of carbapenem-resistant Providencia driven by high-risk lineages and plasmid co-evolution
Authors: Qian Liu, Xi Chen, Chunxia Yang, Li Gu, Hui Yuan, Wentao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbapenem-resistant Providencia (CRP) is a formidable opportunistic pathogen with extensive drug resistance, posing an increasing threat to human, animal, and environmental health. However, its global genomic epidemiology and resistome-plasmidome co-evolution within a One Health context remain poorly understood. Here, we conducted a comprehensive population genomic and plasmid analysis of 1197 CRP isolates from 35 countries spanning 2012–2025. We reclassified the CRP population into nine Providencia species, identifying P. stuartii and P. rettgeri as established epidemic species alongside the rapid emergence of P. hangzhouensis and P. huashanensis. We pinpointed 2019 as a critical inflection point, marking the transition from endemic stability to rapid global epidemic expansion (80.8% isolates in 2019–2025). Ten intercontinentally disseminated high-risk sequence types (e.g., ST46, ST79), each with strict species-ST specificity, were responsible for global spread. The blaNDM-1 gene (62.4% prevalence) was the predominant carbapenem gene, exhibiting strong geographic and species specificity and mutual exclusion with other major carbapenem genes. Plasmids encoded approximately 80% of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, with 78.0% of these plasmids belonging to 75 stable clusters. These clusters evolved a dual specialist-generalist strategy, with the broad-host-range cluster 72 mediating interspecies AMR transmission and cluster 7 acting as a super-vector carrying six carbapenem genes. Importantly, CRP circulated across interconnected human, animal, and environmental reservoirs, with distinct host-associated species distributions suggesting ecological niche adaptation and potential cross-host dissemination of resistance determinants. These findings demonstrate that the global expansion of CRP is driven by the combined evolution of high-risk clones and mobile genetic elements across interconnected ecological sectors, underscoring the need for integrated One Health surveillance and coordinated interventions spanning clinical, veterinary, agricultural, and environmental settings.Keywords: One health, Carbapenem-resistant Providencia, Genomic epidemiology, Antimicrobial resistance, Plasmid evolution, Integrated genomic surveillance

Introduction

The rapid emergence and global dissemination of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) represent one of the most pressing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) challenges worldwide [1], [2], [3]. Increasing evidence indicates that AMR is not confined to healthcare settings but is sustained through interconnected human, animal, food production, and environmental ecosystems, making it a quintessential One Health issue [4]. In this context, the genus Providencia, particularly P. stuartii and P. rettgeri, has emerged as an important opportunistic pathogen. Owing to their intrinsic resistance to colistin and reduced susceptibility to tigecycline, the acquisition of carbapenem resistance frequently results in extensively drug-resistant or even pan-drug-resistant phenotypes, leaving clinicians with very limited therapeutic options [5], [6].

Historically viewed as localized clinical occurrences, CRP is now recognized for its structured global spread across multiple continents [7]. Although the population structure and phylogeographic dynamics of pathogens such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli have been extensively characterized, our understanding of Providencia remains fragmented [8], [9], [10], [11]. This underscores the need for large-scale genomic surveillance to elucidate its patterns of long-distance transmission and localized clonal expansion. In addition, the genus Providencia has recently undergone a significant taxonomic revisitation and reclassification, driven by the increasing resolution of whole-genome sequencing [12]. Such taxonomic refinement is crucial for genomic epidemiology, as it reveals that carbapenem resistance is not uniformly distributed across the genus but is instead often confined within specific, newly redefined taxonomic species that may possess unique ecological fitness and pathogenic potential [13].

Antimicrobial resistance is increasingly recognized as a One Health challenge, driven by the continuous exchange of resistant bacteria and mobile genetic elements among humans, animals, food production systems, and the environment [14], [15]. Providencia species have been isolated not only from clinical infections but also from livestock, companion animals, wildlife, wastewater, and aquatic environments, indicating their remarkable ecological adaptability. Consequently, CRP should not be regarded solely as a healthcare-associated pathogen, but rather as a multidimensional ecological threat whose dissemination may involve interconnected reservoirs across multiple sectors. Understanding its evolutionary dynamics therefore requires a comprehensive One Health framework integrating genomic surveillance from human, animal, and environmental sources.

The evolutionary success of CRP is intrinsically linked to the sophisticated interplay between its resistome and plasmidome [16]. The dissemination of critical carbapenem genes, such as blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaOXA, is not merely the result of stochastic horizontal gene transfer. Rather, it is driven by a profound process of co-evolution between these genes and specific plasmid scaffolds, including IncC, IncX3, and hybrid megaplasmids [17]. This co-adaptive evolution involves structural refinements and modular recombination, which minimize the fitness costs associated with carrying large resistance determinants while maximizing stability within the Providencia host [18]. Deciphering whether specific Providencia species have evolved as specialist hosts for these plasmids, or whether the plasmids themselves have acquired promiscuous host-range determinants, is essential for understanding the global success of this pathogen [19].

In this study, we applied a One Health genomic surveillance framework to characterize the global evolution of CRP. By integrating clinical isolates with publicly available genomes derived from multiple ecological sources, we investigated species composition, population structure, phylogeographic dissemination, resistome–plasmidome co-evolution, and host-associated ecological adaptation. This integrated approach provides new insights into the evolutionary mechanisms driving antimicrobial resistance dissemination across interconnected human, animal, and environmental reservoirs.

Materials and methods

Study design and isolate collection

The study integrated two distinct genomic datasets to provide a comprehensive view of Providencia evolution. The local clinical cohort: a total of 86 clinical Providencia isolates were collected from Beijing Chao-yang hospital, a tertiary hospital in China, over a 13-year period (2012–2025), of which nine met the definition of CRP and were included in this study. Only one isolate per patient was included. Carbapenem-resistant Providencia isolates were defined as those with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ≥ 4 mg/L. The global database cohort: all publicly available Providencia genomes were retrieved from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) as of September 2025. Genomes with more than 100 scaffolds and sizes less than 4 Mb or greater than 5.5 Mb were excluded from further analysis. Isolate metadata, including source, geographical origin, and collection date, were standardized for downstream epidemiological analysis. For publicly available genomes lacking antimicrobial susceptibility data, carbapenem resistance was inferred from the presence of acquired carbapenemase genes, including blaNDM, blaKPC, blaIMP, blaVIM, and blaOXA-48-like variants.

Whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were constructed according to the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB), and whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 250-bp paired-end reads. Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed from the raw sequencing data using fastp v1.0.1 (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp). High-quality reads were then de novo assembled with SOAPdenovo v2.04, employing various K-mer values [20]. Contigs shorter than 500 bp were excluded from the final assembly.

Taxonomic reclassification

To ensure consistency across heterogeneous data sources, all genomes (both local and public) were re-analyzed using a unified bioinformatic pipeline. Initial species identification was rigorously re-evaluated using the FastANI v1.34 to calculate average nucleotide identity (ANI) against the type strains of the genus Providencia (Table S1). Genomes sharing an ANI value of ≥95% with the corresponding type strain were assigned to the same species, consistent with the commonly accepted genomic species boundary [21], [22]. Subsequently, taxonomic status was further evaluated using ribosomal multi-locus sequence typing (rMLST) [23].

Phylogenomic analysis and resistome characterization

For phylogenomic analysis, genomes in GFF3 format were annotated using Prokka v1.15.6 [24]. Core genes were then identified using the Roary v3.13.0 pipeline [25]. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed with IQ-TREE v2.1.2, employing the best-fit substitution model (GTR + G4) with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [26]. Sequence type (ST) was determined according to the PubMLST scheme for Providencia spp [27] Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene was characterized by screening assemblies against the AMRFinderPlus database, considering only hits with ≥95% identity and ≥ 90% coverage as significant. AMR gene was further clustered and grouped into drug-classes based on abriTAMR v1.0.20b (https://github.com/MDU-PHL/abritamr).

Mobile genetic elements characterization

Insertion sequences (ISs) were identified and annotated using the ISfinder database with an E-value threshold of <10−5 (https://isfinder.biotoul.fr/). Transposons (Tns) were characterized using the Transposon database from BacAnt v3.3.3 [28]. The genomic sequences of ISs and Tns were extracted based on their location information using Abricate v1.2.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate). Plasmids replicons were typed using PlasmidFinder [29]. For all assembled genomes (both local and public), plasmid sequences were reconstructed using MOB-recon [30]. Briefly, contigs were assigned to putative plasmid units based on similarity to reference plasmids in the MOB-suite database. Circular plasmids were identified and separated into independent units when appropriate, while clusters consisting solely of repetitive elements without identifiable replicons or relaxases were discarded. Reconstructed plasmids were subsequently compared against reference plasmids using Mash, and plasmids with a Mash distance >0.05 from known references were classified as novel groups. To investigate plasmid evolution and dissemination, reconstructed plasmids were clustered using MGE-cluster v1.1.0 [31]. A perplexity value of 100 was selected because the dataset contained thousands of plasmid sequences, allowing improved preservation of global population structure while reducing fragmentation into small clusters. Plasmids were grouped based on a Mash distance threshold of ≤0.05, representing highly similar plasmid backbones and enabling the identification of stable plasmid lineages disseminated across different geographical regions. Plasmid synteny and structural conservation of carbapenem-carrying plasmids were visualized using BLAST ring image generator (BRIG) v0.95 [32].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables (e.g., prevalence of carbapenem genes across species, source of isolates) were presented as frequencies and percentages, with differences assessed using Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0.

Results

Global landscape and taxonomic redistribution

Between 1947 and 2025, an integrated epidemiological analysis of 2244 Providencia isolates identified 1197 CRP cases (53.3%) between 2012 and 2025, revealing a dramatic longitudinal escalation and complex species succession. While nine species were identified, the population structure was dominated by P. hangzhouensis (26.1%), P. stuartii (24.7%), P. huashanensis (16.1%), and P. rettgeri (13.1%) (Fig. 1A). Temporally, the study identified a critical inflection point in 2019, marking a transition from a period of endemic stability (2012–2018, 16.1% of isolates) to a phase of rapid epidemic expansion (2019–2025, 80.8% of isolates). This surge was characterized by a 401.0% increase in total CRP detection, peaking in 2023 (n = 445), with P. rettgeri and P. stuartii exhibiting the most aggressive growth rates at 1533.3% and 1073.9%, respectively (Fig. 1B). Providencia species exhibited a distinct low-to-high growth trajectory, with five out of six primary species following an expansionary pattern (Fig. S1). Exceptionally significant growth was observed in P. rettgeri and P. stuartii. In contrast, P. huaxiensis and P. huashanensis demonstrated moderate expansion, evolving from sporadic detection to stable, high-prevalence populations.

From a One Health perspective, CRP demonstrated high ecological plasticity, circulating across human (55.0%), animal (36.4%), and environmental (1.3%) reservoirs (Fig. 1C). P. stuartii emerged as the primary human clinical threat, whereas P. hangzhouensis served as a major animal-associated lineage, highlighting distinct host-species preferences. Globally, Asia (45.9%) and North America (33.1%) served as primary epicenters, with high endemicity observed in both China and the USA (Fig. 1D). Significant regional clusters were also identified in Nigeria (Africa) and Romania (Europe), suggesting the parallel emergence of resistant clones across diverse geographical landscapes.

Population structure

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of 1197 Providencia isolates revealed a highly diverse yet polarized population structure, with 99.5% of isolates successfully assigned to 213 distinct STs (Fig. 2). While 66.7% of STs were singletons, the top ten most prevalent STs that were identified as high-risk STs (ST79, ST46, ST44, ST346, ST41, ST27, ST117, ST233, ST35, and ST95) accounted for 38.6% of the population (Fig. S2A). Geospatial analysis showed that all high-risk STs exhibited intercontinental dissemination, each found in 4 to 8 countries, and ST46 displayed the broadest global reach spanning four continents. ST79 and ST44 were predominantly distributed in 6–7 countries, with high prevalence in China, Nigeria, and Mexico (Fig. S2B). The high-risk STs also exhibited decadal persistence (7–12 years) across three distinct evolutionary phases. The early emergence (2012–2018) and stable expansion (2019–2021) periods were characterized by low-to-moderate prevalence, with ST79, ST44, and ST95 establishing foundational niches. In contrast, the explosive growth phase (2022–2025) accounted for 70.0% of all isolates, representing a 4.5-fold increase relative to earlier periods. Notably, ST46 emerged as the dominant ST following a 1420% expansion, while ST79 maintained its prevalence. This synchronized upward trajectory across all ten high-risk STs, exemplified by the rapid proliferation of ST41 and ST27, underscored an accelerating epidemiological shift in ST dominance (Fig. S2C). These prevalent STs exhibited absolute species-ST specificity; for instance, ST79 and ST44 were exclusively associated with P. hangzhouensis, while ST46, ST27, ST233, and ST35 were specific to P. stuartii (Fig. S2D).

Resistome profile

A total of 26 distinct carbapenem-encoding genes were identified, which could be classified into six major gene families (blaNDM, blaIMP, blaVIM, blaKPC, blaOXA, and blaPER) (Fig. 3A). The blaNDM family was the primary resistance driver, with blaNDM-1 acting as the absolute dominant determinant globally (62.4%) (Fig. S3A). Notably, blaNDM-1 exhibited genus-wide adaptability, being detected across all nine Providencia species and 15 countries, with particularly high prevalence in China (87.9%), Romania (100%), and Mexico (100%). Temporally, while blaNDM-1 maintained stable dominance, both blaIMP-27 and blaNDM-7 experienced explosive growth in the recent phase (2019–2025), rising by 86.4% and 79.0%, respectively (Fig. S3B). Geospatial analysis identified strong regional signatures. For example, the USA presented a unique pattern dominated by blaIMP-27 (58.7%), contrasting with the NDM-centric profiles of other regions (Fig. S3C). Furthermore, a high degree of species-specificity was observed. blaIMP-27 was primarily associated with P. huashanensis, whereas blaNDM-7 showed a preference for P. stuartii (Fig. S3D). Interestingly, major carbapenem genes demonstrated mutual exclusion (particularly between blaNDM-1 and blaIMP-27), suggesting that single-gene carriage provides sufficient fitness for carbapenem resistance without the metabolic cost of co-carriage.

Analysis of 1197 isolates revealed significant interspecies heterogeneity in resistance gene carrying capacity, ranging from 10 to 17.3 average genes per isolate (Fig. 3B). The population bifurcated into two tiers: a high-capacity tier (≥ 15 genes), led by P. xihuensis and P. huaxiensis, and a moderate-capacity tier, which included P. stuartii and P. huashanensis. Notably, P. huashanensis exhibited a unique bimodal distribution, suggesting divergent evolutionary lineages. Although AMR gene counts varied, a consistent high-resistance phenotype persisted across all species (minimum 10 genes). Population size strongly correlated with total resistance gene burden (R2 = 0.87, p < 0.05), identifying P. hangzhouensis and P. stuartii as the primary reservoirs for dissemination (Fig. S4).

Resistance profiling of CRP across nine Providencia species against 29 antibiotics/drug combinations revealed a stratified landscape of susceptibility (Fig. 3C). High-risk classes, including sulfonamides (90.4%), fluoroquinolones (79.7%), and trimethoprim (77.8%), showed nearly universal resistance with minimal interspecies variation (CV < 20%), suggesting their limited clinical utility. Conversely, fosfomycin emerged as the most viable empirical candidate, exhibiting a genus-wide resistance rate of only 2.5%. Significant interspecies heterogeneity was observed in tetracyclines (CV = 69.8%), where resistance rate ranged from 6.3% in P. pseudovermicola to 100% in P. huashanensis. These findings emphasize that, while fosfomycin and certain Beta-lactams remain effective, species-level identification is critical for optimizing targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Among the 86 clinical Providencia isolates collected during the study period, nine were identified as carbapenem-resistant Providencia (CRP) and included in subsequent genomic analyses. These isolates consisted of five P. hangzhouensis (55.6%) and four P. huaxiensis (44.4%) isolates. Six STs were identified, including ST4, ST32, ST79, ST116, ST227, and ST346. All nine isolates carried blaNDM-1, whereas two isolates additionally harbored blaIMP-4. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes were detected in seven isolates, including blaCTX-M-3, blaCTX-M-55, blaCTX-M-65, and blaPER-4. (Table S2).

Plasmid clusters

To decipher the fine-scale genetic architecture driving AMR dissemination, we conducted a comprehensive characterization of mobile genetic elements. Plasmids accounted for nearly 80% of all AMR genes in CRP, far exceeding the contributions of transposons and insertion sequences (Fig. 3D). A total of 2820 plasmids from nine Providencia species were identified, revealing a structured plasmidome, with 78.0% of plasmids categorized into 75 valid clusters (Fig. 4A). The remaining 22.0% were categorized as unclustered, with the highest sequence diversity observed in P. manganoxydans (34.4% unclustered) and P. hangzhouensis (25.7%), whereas P. huaxiensis exhibited the most conserved sequences with an 86.6% clustering rate. The distribution of 75 clusters across human, animal, environmental, and plant hosts were further investigated. The top 20 plasmid clusters showed high prevalence in Homo sapiens and animals, with sporadic distributions in environmental and plant sources, except for cluster 40 (Fig. 4B and Fig. 4C), which highlighted frequent horizontal transmission. While 76.0% of clusters exhibited high (e.g., 100% of cluster 40 in Homo sapiens and 100% of cluster 10 in animals) or moderate (66.2% of cluster 61 in Homo sapiens and 93.9% of cluster 60 in animals) host specificity, the remaining 24.0% were low-specificity generalists that facilitated cross-sectoral antimicrobial resistance spread. Despite a low abundance of plasmids, environmental sources harbored the greatest cluster diversity and unique lineages, acting as a genomic reservoir for novel threats.

The comprehensive analysis of 176 records revealed a highly significant and balanced relationship between nine Providencia species and plasmid clusters (Fig. 5 A). All recorded associations were statistically significant (p < 0.05), with a notable 42.6% classified as extremely significant (p < 0.001), establishing a robust foundation for identifying genomic evolutionary signatures. The correlation landscape was nearly evenly divided between positive (51.1%) and negative (48.9%) interactions, indicating a population structure characterized by a balance of synergetic and inhibitory biological pressures. The intensity of these associations was predominantly weak, with 71.6% of records showing an absolute correlation coefficient |r| below 0.1. At species level, P. stuartii stood out as the most broadly associated hub, linked to 45 distinct clusters. From a cluster-centric perspective, cluster 72 emerged as the critical generalist node, facilitating associations across eight different species despite its moderate average correlation strength. In contrast, the rare strong correlations (|r| ≥ 0.3), found exclusively in P. pseudovermicola, highlighted unique evolutionary specializations.

Plasmid-mediated carbapenem gene dissemination

Analysis of carbapenem-resistant plasmids revealed a highly skewed distribution, with the IncA/C2_1 plasmid type being overwhelmingly dominant (86.9%), followed by IncT_1 (6.21%) and IncQ1_1 (2.29%) (Fig. S5). Four major plasmid types accounted for 96.6% of the total distribution; however, the detection of six minor plasmid types indicated some level of diversity. Carbapenem-resistant plasmids in CRP belonged to 43 plasmid clusters, with the top five clusters accounting for 30% of the total samples. Cluster 50 was the largest cluster, and distributed across multiple countries (Fig. S6). The plasmid backbone structures of cluster 50 were almost identical, indicating a high degree of conservation (Fig. 5B). Overall, 39.5% of clusters were country-exclusive, while 60.5% exhibited multi-country distribution. China and the USA held a dominant role, together accounting for 88.2% of all exclusive clusters. In contrast, high-coverage clusters 41 and 67 were found in eight countries across five continents, indicating a high potential for cross-border transmission (Fig. S6).

We further investigated the complex landscape of carbapenem resistance gene distribution across 43 plasmid clusters, 36 of which showed significant associations (Fig. 5C). A distinguishing finding was the dominance of NDM-type genes, which exhibited both the greatest distribution breadth and the highest average association intensity across plasmid clusters (Fig. S7A). In particular, blaNDM-1 was the most versatile gene, and presented in 17 different plasmid clusters, suggesting an extraordinary capacity for horizontal transfer. Conversely, the majority of carbapenem genes (73%) were restricted to a single plasmid cluster, indicating a high degree of evolutionary specialization. While distribution breadth varied, the intensity of these associations was equally significant; blaNDM-7 showed the strongest specific association with any cluster (correlation coefficient of 0.8808 in cluster 40), followed by blaIMP-27 and blaKPC-2 (Fig. S7B). From the perspective of the plasmid clusters, cluster 7 emerged as a super-vector with a conserved backbone structure (Fig. S7C, and Fig. 5D). It was the most versatile cluster identified, capable of simultaneously carrying six different carbapenem genes from the IMP, KPC, and VIM families (Fig. S7D). While 72.2% of clusters carried only one gene type, the multidrug carrying capacity of cluster 7 marked it as a high-risk vehicle for public health.

Discussion

Our study provides the first comprehensive global genomic framework describing the evolution of CRP within a One Health context. By integrating newly sequenced clinical isolates with publicly available genomes originating from human, animal, and environmental sources, we demonstrate that the contemporary global expansion of CRP is driven by the combined evolution of high-risk genomic lineages and mobile genetic elements rather than by isolated local outbreaks. These findings extend current understanding of Providencia epidemiology beyond hospital-associated infections and highlight the importance of considering interconnected ecological reservoirs when investigating the emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance.

One of the most notable findings of this study is the pronounced taxonomic turnover within the genus Providencia. Previous epidemiological surveillance has mainly focused on P. stuartii and P. rettgeri [5], [7]. However, through high-resolution whole-genome sequencing and reclassification [33], we found that emerging species, particularly P. hangzhouensis (accounting for 26.1%) and P. huashanensis (accounting for 16.1%), have already exceeded the traditional clinical pathogenic species in quantity, becoming the major reservoirs of global carbapenem genes within genus Providencia. This numerical takeover suggests that these emerging species are not merely sporadic opportunistic pathogens but have become the primary global reservoirs for carbapenem resistance genes. Notably, it was only in 2023 that Dong et al. first identified P. hangzhouensis as a new species associated with urinary tract infections, pointing out its frequent misidentification as P. rettgeri in routine biochemical testing and its frequent association with carbapenem resistance genes [34]. In addition, CRP exhibits extremely high ecological plasticity, circulating widely among humans (55.0%), animals (36.4%), and the environment (1.3%). As recently observed in carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli, frequent host-switching is a core driving force in the global expansion of multidrug-resistant clones [35]. In our analysis, P. stuartii remain the main threat in human clinics, while P. hangzhouensis is primarily represented in animal-associated lineages. The adaptation of different species to specific ecological niches indicates that distinct Providencia species may occupy different ecological niches while remaining connected through broader One Health transmission networks.

A major implication of our findings is that CRP should be considered an emerging One Health pathogen rather than a microorganism confined to healthcare settings. More than one-third of the isolates analyzed originated from animal-associated sources, while additional isolates were recovered from environmental reservoirs, demonstrating extensive ecological dissemination. The marked differences in host preference among Providencia species further suggest ecological niche specialization, with P. stuartii predominating in human clinical settings and P. hangzhouensis showing stronger associations with animal reservoirs. Such ecological partitioning may facilitate the long-term maintenance of antimicrobial resistance genes within different sectors while simultaneously creating opportunities for cross-sector dissemination.

Our population structure analysis identified 10 high-risk STs (ST79, ST46, ST44, etc.) with notable potential for global expansion within CRP [36]. These STs not only show absolute species-ST specificity but also the ability to spread across multiple continents. In particular, P. stuartii ST46 showed the widest geographical distribution, spanning four continents in our analysis [5]. This finding aligns closely with recent outbreak surveillance in Europe. Călin et al. have reported that the NDM-1-producing P. stuartii ST46 high-risk ST has caused years-long interhospital transmission in healthcare systems across Romania and nine other European countries [5]. Other high-risk STs, such as the P. hangzhouensis-specific ST79 and ST44, have maintained high prevalence and persistence through long-term evolution. Similar to the recent emergence of multidrug-resistant Clade 2.5.6 in Acinetobacter baumannii [37], and the rapid expansion of the hypervirulent, highly resistant KpnK48 subclone within E. coli ST167 [38], the success of CRP high-risk STs is driven not only by antibiotic resistance but also by significant fitness advantages and enhanced horizontal transmission potential under selection pressure [39]. The rise of these dominant, multidrug-resistant clones underscores a critical shift in the Providencia landscape, necessitating targeted global surveillance to mitigate their escalating public health threat.

In terms of resistance mechanisms, the blaNDM family constitutes the primary driver of resistance in CRP, with blaNDM-1 holding absolute dominance across all nine species and 15 countries analyzed. Contrary to the conventional view of random horizontal transfer of antibiotic resistance genes, the distribution of resistance genes in CRP shows strong geographic and species specificity. For example, the United States displays a unique blaIMP-27-dominant profile, with a marked preference for P. huashanensis. An evolutionarily significant phenomenon is that major carbapenem genes (especially between blaNDM-1 and blaIMP-27) display mutual exclusion. This indicates that carrying a single highly efficient carbapenem gene can provide sufficient selective advantage, thus obviating the need to bear the considerable metabolic cost associated with harboring multiple large resistance loci. This strategy of optimizing resistance gene combinations by reducing adaptive costs is the key for pathogens to maintain a highly resistant phenotype, even in antibiotic-free environments.

To decipher the micro-genetic architecture driving AMR transmission, we performed a deep clustering analysis of Providencia plasmids [40]. Our results showed that plasmids carry nearly 80% of AMR genes. In examining the association between plasmids and host species, we observed an obvious dual evolutionary strategy of specialist and generalist. Nearly 60% of plasmid clusters show high species specificity (such as cluster 34, 100% confined to P. stuartii), representing a path of deep co-evolution between plasmids and specific hosts. However, the largest plasmid genotype, cluster 72 (accounting for 11.7% of effective plasmids), displays a low-specificity distribution across eight Providencia species. As a generalist vector, cluster 72 spans human, animal, and plant ecological realms, suggesting that successful plasmids may function as ecological bridges connecting otherwise distinct bacterial populations. This pattern of large-scale cross-boundary transmission of resistance genes driven by a few dominant mobile genetic elements (such as IncA/C2_1 in Providencia) is similar to the mechanism in E. coli, where the shift from IncX3 to IncF plasmids facilitates the dissemination of resistance [38], and echoes the emergence of super-epidemic lineages through recombination networks in Acinetobacter baumannii [37]. In addition, we identified cluster 7 as a super-vector capable of carrying six different carbapenem genes from the IMP, KPC, and VIM families, thus posing an extremely high public health risk. Such generalist plasmids likely facilitate long-term maintenance and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes across ecological boundaries, complementing the role of clonal expansion in driving global resistance dissemination.

Our findings have important implications from a One Health perspective. Although our genomic analysis was not designed to reconstruct direct zoonotic transmission events, the widespread distribution of genetically related high-risk sequence types and conserved plasmid clusters across human and animal sources strongly suggests that resistance determinants circulate through interconnected ecological networks. In particular, the identification of broad-host-range plasmid cluster 72 across multiple host species highlights the potential role of promiscuous plasmids in bridging ecological barriers and disseminating carbapenem resistance beyond individual host populations. These observations emphasize that plasmid-mediated horizontal gene transfer may represent an equally important driver of antimicrobial resistance dissemination as clonal expansion.

From a One Health perspective, surveillance strategies should move beyond hospital-based monitoring to include veterinary medicine, food-producing animals, companion animals, wastewater, and environmental ecosystems [41]. Integrated genomic surveillance across these sectors will improve early detection of emerging high-risk clones and mobile genetic elements, clarify potential transmission pathways, and facilitate coordinated interventions aimed at interrupting resistance dissemination before widespread establishment.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of original clinical isolates was relatively limited, which restricted the availability of detailed phenotypic and clinical data. Second, the global genomic analysis relied largely on publicly available genomes, which may be subject to sampling bias due to differences in surveillance intensity, sequencing capacity, and genome submission practices across countries and years. Consequently, the observed temporal and geographic patterns may not fully represent the true epidemiology of Providencia. Therefore, the observed global patterns reflect the currently available genomic surveillance data rather than the true global prevalence of CRP. Countries with more active sequencing programs are likely to be overrepresented, whereas regions with limited genomic surveillance remain underrepresented.

In conclusion, this study provides the first comprehensive global genomic framework describing the evolution of CRP from a One Health perspective. Beyond redefining the taxonomic and epidemiological landscape of the genus, our analyses demonstrate that the global dissemination of carbapenem resistance is jointly driven by the expansion of high-risk sequence types and the evolution of specialist and generalist plasmid lineages circulating across interconnected human, animal, and environmental reservoirs. These findings highlight the importance of integrating genomic surveillance across clinical, veterinary, agricultural, food production, and environmental sectors to identify emerging resistance reservoirs and interrupt cross-sector dissemination. As CRP continues to expand globally, coordinated One Health surveillance and intervention strategies targeting both high-risk clones and mobile genetic elements will be essential to mitigate the future spread of CRP.
