Listening to children's voices: Psychosocial and behavioral experiences and identity-related changes during the cancer trajectory
Authors: Yi-Pei Cheng, Hsiang-Yun Lan, Yi-Yen Lee, Hsiang-Ping Wu, Yun-Chen Fu, Chiao-Hsuan Lin, Jen-Jiuan Liaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the psychosocial and behavioral experiences of children undergoing cancer treatment, as well as their perceived identity-related changes, including changes in roles, social relationships, and self-perception.MethodsThis phenomenologically informed qualitative study used thematic analysis. Purposive sampling was used to recruit 12 children diagnosed with brain tumors or leukemia from pediatric oncology units at three medical centers in northern Taiwan. Data were collected through in-depth, face-to-face semi-structured interviews. Each interview lasted 30–40 minutes and was conducted in a private setting. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke's six-phase thematic analysis within a phenomenological orientation.ResultsFour interrelated themes were identified: (1) psychological turmoil, characterized by fear, anxiety, and emotional distress; (2) changes in social interactions and identity-related experiences, including social isolation, peer disconnection, changes in family roles, and shifts in self-perception; (3) disruption of normalcy, involving interruptions in schooling, adjustments to learning, and reduced participation in usual daily activities; and (4) behavioral coping and responses, including overt expressions of distress and more implicit coping strategies such as withdrawal.ConclusionsUnderstanding these multifaceted experiences provides important insights for family-centered care. Health care professionals should remain attentive to children's psychosocial and behavioral experiences and actively listen to their voices to better understand perceived changes in self-perception, social roles, and identity-related experiences during the cancer trajectory. Tailored supportive interventions may help promote social engagement, preserve a sense of normalcy, and support a positive self-perception throughout cancer treatment.

Introduction

Globally, approximately 400,000 new cases (aged 0–19 years) of childhood cancer are diagnosed each year.1 In Taiwan, according to statistics from the Ministry of Health and Welfare, approximately 500 new cases of cancer are diagnosed annually in children and adolescents under the age of 18, with leukemia and brain tumor being the most prevalent types.2 Childhood cancer has a significant impact on the entire family, often leading to heightened anxiety, uncertainty, and emotional burden. Marked disparities exist between high-income and low- and middle-income countries, where survival rates exceed 80% in well-resourced settings but remain substantially lower in resource-limited contexts.3 In Taiwan, the 5-year survival rate of childhood cancer has now exceeded 82.1% and the 10-year survival rate is more than 79.2%.4 These survivors frequently face multidimensional challenges across physical, psychological, behavioral, and social domains, which may significantly hinder their reintegration into everyday life and the resumption of pre-diagnosis roles and routines.5

Children undergoing cancer treatment may experience a wide range of physical symptoms, including fatigue, pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and loss of appetite. These symptoms may arise from both the underlying disease and treatment-related side effects.6 In addition, some children, particularly those undergoing surgical interventions (e.g., for brain tumor or other solid tumors) may experience visible physical alterations (e.g., scarring) or functional impairments, which may adversely affect body image and self-perception. Previous studies of pediatric brain tumor survivors have shown that treatment-related physical and neurological sequelae are associated with poorer psychosocial adjustment and health-related quality of life, which may also influence children's self-perception.7,8 Concurrently, the diagnosis initiates a trajectory of profound psychological distress, with initial reactions of fear and shock often evolving into persistent conditions such as depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD).9 This elevated risk for psychiatric morbidities is well-documented, stemming from the cumulative stress of the diagnosis itself, alongside the complex interplay of stressors during treatment, such as its prolonged duration, persistent side effects, and extended hospitalizations.10,11

Furthermore, these challenges often extend into survivorship, where pediatric cancer survivors may experience significant social isolation compared to their peers, accompanied by an increased risk of adverse long-term psychosocial outcomes12 and face ongoing medical demands, including follow-up surveillance and the increasing risk of relapse or secondary malignancies. Critically, over two-thirds of survivors will experience at least one chronic health condition or late effect, including physiological, psychological, or neurocognitive consequences.13 These long-term sequelae can profoundly impact the survivors' academic and occupational performance, peer relationships, the attainment of developmental milestones, and overall mental health.14,15 Beyond general psychosocial outcomes, identity represents an important developmental construct that reflects how children understand themselves, their social roles, sense of belonging, and future aspirations.16,17 During cancer treatment, disruptions in school attendance, peer interactions, physical functioning, and participation in age-appropriate activities may alter children's perceptions of who they are and how they relate to others. Such experiences may extend beyond emotional distress or psychosocial adjustment and influence children's self-perception.18 Therefore, identity-related experiences warrant focused investigation as a distinct aspect of adaptation among children undergoing cancer treatment. The importance of identity-related experiences may be particularly pronounced during childhood and adolescence, a developmental period characterized by ongoing psychosocial growth and self-understanding.19 According to Erikson's psychosocial theory, this stage is characterized by identity versus role confusion, in which individuals seek to understand who they are and how they fit within their social world.16 Through interactions with family, peers, and broader social environments, children develop an understanding of their roles, sense of belonging, and future orientation.19

In the present study, children diagnosed with leukemia and brain tumor were included because these conditions represent two of the most common childhood cancers in Taiwan and worldwide.1,2 Although both diagnoses involve prolonged treatment and substantial disruptions to children's daily lives, they may differ in treatment trajectories and potential long-term consequences. These differences may influence children's psychosocial and identity-related experiences in distinct ways and therefore warrant consideration when interpreting study findings. For children undergoing cancer treatment, perceived changes in self-perception, social roles, and social relationships may occur.20,21 The experience of serious illness can alter daily routines, restrict social participation, and reshape interpersonal relationships, potentially affecting how children perceive themselves and their roles in relation to others.22 Pediatric psychosocial oncology research has consistently demonstrated that children with cancer experience multifaceted challenges, including emotional distress, family disruption, and social isolation, all of which may influence their self-perception and identity-related experiences.23 In addition, the impact of illness extends beyond physical symptoms, affecting children's subjective understanding of self, including how they perceive their “body” and “self” relationships, and future possibilities. Despite growing recognition of these psychosocial challenges, limited research has explored how children themselves interpret these experiences and perceive changes in their roles, social relationships, and self-perception during active cancer treatment. Addressing this gap requires a deeper understanding of children's own perspectives on their illness experiences and perceived identity-related changes during active cancer treatment.

Therefore, this qualitative study aims to explore children's psychosocial and behavioral experiences during cancer treatment, including their perceptions of illness, social interactions, and coping responses. By examining these experiences, the study seeks to gain a deeper understanding of identity-related changes as perceived by children, including changes in their self-perception, social roles, and relationships during the cancer trajectory. In this study, identity is conceptualized as children's self-perception and their perceived social roles and relationships with others. Findings from this study may contribute to a richer understanding of how children make sense of their illness experiences and perceive identity-related changes throughout the cancer trajectory.

Methods

Research design

This phenomenologically informed qualitative study used thematic analysis to explore the lived psychosocial and behavioral experiences of children with cancer during treatment through face-to-face semi-structured interviews.24 This qualitative study aimed to explore how children perceived, interpreted, and made sense of their experiences during cancer treatment.25 In this study, emphasis was placed on children's own narratives regarding how cancer treatment affected their emotions, behaviors, social interactions, and self-perceptions.

A phenomenological orientation guided the researchers to attend closely to participants’ lived experiences while minimizing preconceptions through reflexive awareness and bracketing.26 This approach supported an in-depth exploration of how children perceived and made sense of their experiences during diagnosis and treatment.

Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis, which served as the primary analytic method for identifying, organizing, and interpreting patterns within the data. Although thematic analysis was used as the analytic framework, the analysis remained informed by phenomenological principles, with particular attention to preserving participants' subjective meanings and lived experiences.27 Phenomenology informed the study orientation, interview process, and interpretation of findings, whereas thematic analysis guided coding, theme development, and the identification of patterns across participants' narratives.

Sampling and recruitment

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling from three medical centers in northern Taiwan. Efforts were made to include children with varying demographic and clinical characteristics, including differences in age, sex, diagnosis, educational level, disease status, time since diagnosis, and sibling status, to capture a broad range of perspectives and experiences. Children were included because they are generally considered to possess sufficient cognitive development and verbal proficiency to provide meaningful descriptions of their illness experiences and associated emotional responses. Eligible participants were required to be enrolled in elementary school or above and have sufficient cognitive and language abilities to understand the interview questions and express their experiences. In accordance with Sandelowski's recommendations for qualitative sampling aimed at ensuring informational richness,28 the inclusion criteria were: (a) aged 8–18 years, (b) diagnosed with leukemia or a brain tumor, (c) currently receiving active treatment, and (d) able to communicate in Mandarin or Taiwanese. Exclusion criteria included critical illness or palliative care status.

A total of 16 eligible children and their parents were approached for participation. Two families declined to participate due to the child's poor physical condition or the demands of ongoing treatment, with parents expressing concerns about additional burden. Two additional families initially consented; however, each child's condition subsequently deteriorated suddenly, necessitating transfer to the intensive care unit. Written informed consent was obtained from parents, and assent was obtained from the children prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and both parents and children had the right to decline or withdraw at any time. Final decisions regarding participation were made by parents or legal guardians, with children's willingness and comfort respected throughout the process. Ultimately, 12 children (7 boys and 5 girls) were enrolled in the study. The sample size in this study was determined based on data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerged from the data analysis. Setting and data collection

Data were collected from October 2024 to June 2025 in the pediatric oncology wards of three medical centers. Prior to the interviews, socio-demographic information was collected, and participants were informed about confidentiality and voluntary participation. To establish rapport and foster trust, the first author conducted regular visits with hospitalized children receiving cancer treatment throughout their treatment course. In-depth, one-on-one semi-structured interviews were conducted either at the bedside of the pediatric oncology units or in a quiet playroom, depending on the child's clinical condition and mobility. To facilitate open expression and minimize the potential influence of parental presence, interviews were conducted individually with the child whenever possible. For children who were able to leave their hospital rooms, interviews were conducted in a private playroom. For children who required bedside interviews, parents were politely asked to leave the interview area temporarily whenever appropriate and with the child's agreement. All interviews were scheduled at times convenient for the participants and were conducted only when the children demonstrated readiness and comfort in sharing their experiences. The researchers encouraged participants to share their experiences freely within a supportive, non-judgmental environment. Interviews consistently lasted between 30 and 40 minutes, beginning with rapport-building introductions before transitioning to the core research questions. Although pilot interviews were not undertaken, the interview guide was developed based on the study objectives and reviewed by experts in pediatric oncology to establish its content relevance, clarity, and suitability for use with children. Particular attention was given to ensuring developmental appropriateness by using age-appropriate language, straightforward wording, and open-ended questions. This approach facilitated participants' ability to articulate their illness experiences, emotional responses, coping strategies, and perceived sources of support in their own words. The semi-structured interviews facilitated open-ended exploration using a set of core guiding questions, including: (1) During the course of your illness, what specific aspects caused you discomfort, and how did you typically respond to those feelings at the time? (2) What kinds of situations or experiences tend to make you feel unwell or cause discomfort? (3) When you are feeling unwell, what strategies or actions do you typically employ to cope or manage your discomfort? (4) Who provides support to you when you are feeling unwell, and what specific ways do they help or assist you? (5) What else would you like to share with me regarding your experiences or feelings since becoming ill? The interview questions encouraged children to describe their emotional responses, interpersonal relationships, daily experiences, coping strategies, and perceptions of themselves, thereby providing insights into psychosocial experiences and identity-related processes during cancer treatment. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. To enhance reflexivity and minimize researcher bias, bracketing was employed throughout the research process. The first author, a doctoral candidate with clinical experience and formal training in qualitative interviewing, conducted all interviews and maintained reflexive field notes. The research team included experienced qualitative researchers and a pediatric oncology specialist, providing methodological and clinical expertise. Due to the clinical condition of the participants undergoing cancer treatment, data collection was conducted through brief and flexible interviews, with each participant typically involved in one to two sessions to minimize fatigue and burden.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis within a phenomenological framework.30 While thematic analysis provided a systematic approach for identifying and organizing patterns in the data, the analytic process was guided by phenomenological principles, with an emphasis on understanding and preserving participants' lived experience and subjective meanings.

The analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six-phase framework: (1) immersion and familiarization with the data, (2) systematic generation of initial codes, (3) searching for and developing potential themes, (4) reviewing and refining themes, (5) defining and naming final themes, and (6) producing the final report.30

The analytic process was carried out by a doctoral candidate and a nursing graduate student, both trained in qualitative research and experienced in pediatric oncology settings, in collaboration with two principal investigators. The research team first engaged in immersion through repeated reading of transcripts to gain a comprehensive understanding of participants’ narratives.

During the coding process, particular attention was given to capturing participants' original expressions and meanings. Codes were generated inductively, with efforts to remain close to the data and avoid imposing pre-existing theoretical frameworks. In line with phenomenological principles, the analysis prioritized individual participants’ perspectives before identifying shared patterns across cases. In the development of themes, “meaning units”, defined as segments of data relevant to the research question, were identified and grouped into potential themes representing recurring experiences. Themes were iteratively reviewed and refined to ensure coherence and strong grounding in the data. The research team engaged in regular discussions to compare interpretations and reach consensus on the final thematic structure.

Reflexive practices, including bracketing and ongoing self-reflection, were employed throughout the analytic process to minimize researcher bias and preconceptions. To facilitate bracketing, the researchers documented their assumptions, clinical experiences, and preliminary interpretations through reflexive field notes and reflexive journaling throughout data collection and analysis. These reflections were regularly discussed during research team meetings to increase awareness of potential biases and pre-understandings. This process supported ongoing reflexivity and helped ensure that interpretations remained grounded in participants' accounts rather than researchers’ prior assumptions. In this study, saturation was defined as the point at which no substantially new codes or themes emerged across participants and interviews.31 This determination was supported by ongoing data analysis, constant comparison of codes, and regular discussions among the research team. Additional interviews were conducted to confirm thematic redundancy and ensure the robustness of the findings. Saturation was observed after approximately the tenth participant, after which no new meaningful codes emerged, and two additional participants were included to confirm redundancy.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure analytical rigor, the entire data analysis was collaboratively executed by a doctoral candidate, a graduate student and the principal investigators, all possessing extensive experience in qualitative inquiry. Data were systematically organized and analyzed using analytical tables, a manual process supported by iterative discussions among the research team. This strategy was integral to maintaining interpretive consistency and ensuring a focused approach aligned with the study's central aim.

Trustworthiness was established according to Lincoln and Guba's framework,32 addressing credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement, iterative team discussion, and continued analysis until saturation. Although the researchers had pediatric clinical backgrounds, they were not directly involved in participants' current treatment. No prior clinical relationship existed between the researchers and participants. This reduced potential power imbalance during recruitment and interviews. Transferability was supported through detailed descriptions of participants, settings, and analytic procedures. Dependability and confirmability were strengthened through maintenance of an audit trail, reflexive journaling, and team-based review of coding decisions.

Ethics considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committees of National Taiwan University Hospital (Approval No. 202403047RINE), Taipei Veterans General Hospital (No. 2024-05-003A), and MacKay Memorial Hospital (Approval No. 24MMHIS084e). The research was conducted in full accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.33 Written informed consent was obtained directly from participants aged 18 years. For participants younger than 18 years, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and written assent was obtained from the child. In addition, all participating children (aged 8–18 years) provided written assent using an age-appropriate assent form. All participants and their parents or legal guardians were fully informed about the study purpose, procedures, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

A rigorous and ethical procedure was followed for all interviews. Prior to each interview, the interviewer reiterated the study's objectives, focused on building rapport to foster a relationship of trust, and verified each participant's continued understanding of the research. All interviews were conducted by the first author. Data analysis was undertaken collaboratively by a graduate student, and the first, second and corresponding author with experience in qualitative research. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study; all data were pseudonymized, and quotations were reported using pseudonyms without directly identifying information.

Results

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. A total of 12 children (n = 12) participated in this study. The mean age was 13.25 ± 3.47 years, and 58.3% were male. The majority of participants were diagnosed with brain tumors (66.7%), whereas the remaining participants had leukemia (33.3%). Regarding educational level, nine participants (75.0%) were elementary or junior high school students, whereas three participants (25.0%) were senior high school students. Most participants had primary disease (91.7%), whereas one participant (8.3%) had recurrent disease. The average time since diagnosis was 13.97 ± 21.59 months. Seven participants (58.3%) had siblings, and all participants were receiving chemotherapy at the time of the interviews.

Main findings

In this study, four main themes were identified to capture children's psychosocial and behavioral experiences during cancer treatment (Fig. 1). Although these themes are analytically distinct, they reflect interconnected aspects of children's lived experience. Theme 1, psychological turmoil, describes children's emotional responses to cancer diagnosis and treatment, including fear, anxiety, and negative emotional responses. These emotional challenges were closely linked to Theme 2, Changes in social interactions and identity-related experiences, which encompassed social isolation, peer disconnection, and changes in family roles and interactions. In response to these cumulative challenges, Theme 4, behavioral coping and responses, emerged, encompassing a range of coping strategies, including both overt expressions of distress and more implicit responses such as withdrawal. Although the themes are presented sequentially for clarity, they are dynamic and interconnected, and may influence one another. While some experiential elements (e.g., social isolation and reduced peer interaction) may appear across themes, these are interpreted differently within each theme: Theme 2 focuses on relational and identity-related changes, Theme 3 reflects disruptions to daily routines and normal life, and Theme 4 captures children's behavioral responses to these challenges.

Theme 1: psychological turmoil

Children diagnosed with cancer experience significant psychological turmoil throughout their treatment trajectory. The initial cancer diagnosis frequently precipitates intense emotional distress, including a range of negative emotional responses. As treatment progresses, these emotional responses evolve into specific fears and anxieties related to medical procedures and the hospital environment. This sustained psychological distress may profoundly impact children's self-concept, emotional regulation, and overall psychological well-being, potentially affecting treatment adherence and long-term psychosocial outcomes.

Subtheme 1: fear, anxiety, and emotional distress

Children undergoing cancer treatment frequently experienced acute anxiety and tension in anticipation of or during specific invasive procedures, such as biopsies, injections, or procedures requiring sustained immobility. The hospital environment itself serves as a source of distress, eliciting aversive emotional responses and contributing to heightened emotional and psychological distress during treatment procedures.

Subtheme 2: negative emotional responses and existential concerns

Beyond the acute anxiety associated with medical procedures, the children with cancer frequently experience pervasive negative emotions and existential concerns throughout their treatment trajectory. This emotional struggle often begins with profound grief and a sense of injustice at diagnosis, evolving into fundamental existential questioning. As treatment progresses, initial shock may transition into chronic dysphoria and persistent desire for treatment completion. This sustained emotional and cognitive burden may compromise children's hope, diminish capacity for positive affect, and contribute to psychological distress and emotional exhaustion.

Theme 2: changes in social interactions and identity-related experiences

A cancer diagnosis profoundly affects children's social experiences and identity-related experiences, extending beyond the physical illness to influence their relationships, roles, and self-perception in daily life. The diagnosis often separates children from their usual routines and peer interactions, while their roles as students, friends, or siblings may change during illness and treatment. As treatment progresses, prolonged hospital stays and physical changes may further contribute to social isolation and challenges in maintaining peer relationships.

These experiences reflect not only disruptions in social interactions but also perceived changes in children's self-perception, roles, and relationships. Children described feeling different from their peers and experiencing changes in their roles within both peer and family contexts. Such experiences suggest perceived changes in how children view themselves and their place within their social world during illness.

Subtheme 1: social isolation and peer disconnection

Beyond the physical sequelae of serious illness, children with cancer frequently experience social isolation and peer disconnection. This disconnection is particularly pronounced within the school environment, where perceived differences may result in peer exclusion and social stigmatization, disrupting normative peer relationships and recreational interactions.

Theme 3: disruption of normalcy

Cancer treatment disrupted children's normal daily lives and developmental experiences. During treatment, children may experience interruptions in schooling, reduced participation in usual daily and recreational activities, and changes in their usual routines. These disruptions reflect the challenges children face in maintaining a sense of normalcy during cancer treatment.

Subtheme 1: interrupted schooling and learning adjustments

A significant consequence of pediatric cancer is the disruption of formal education, often resulting from prolonged treatment-related absences and recovery periods. To address educational gaps, alternative instructional modalities such as individualized bedside tutoring are frequently implemented. However, this educational transition presents considerable challenges, including managing substantial academic deficits and maintaining academic engagement despite fluctuating physical capacity and treatment-related symptoms such as fatigue.

Discussion

The findings of this study can be understood within pediatric psychosocial oncology perspectives and a developmental lens focusing on children's self-perception. According to Erikson's theory, developing a sense of self is an important developmental task during childhood and adolescence.16 In the context of pediatric cancer, illness-related challenges may influence children's self-perceptions, social roles, and identity-related experiences. Consistent with psychosocial oncology frameworks, cancer introduces multifaceted disruptions affecting emotional well-being, family dynamics, and social functioning.23 Within this context, children's behavioral responses, including emotional distress, withdrawal, and resistance, can be understood as adaptive reactions to ongoing medical stress, consistent with trauma-informed perspectives. Additionally, experiences of social isolation and perceived difference may reflect stigma-related processes that are associated with changes in children's self-perceptions and social engagement. This study elucidates children's voices by capturing their multifaceted experiences during cancer treatment, revealing four primary themes that reflect their psychosocial and behavioral impacts: psychological turmoil, changes in social interactions and identity-related experiences, disruption of normalcy, and behavioral coping. These findings highlight the complex interplay of emotional distress, social disruption, and adaptive responses across the trajectory of illness and treatment.

Psychological turmoil

The psychological turmoil theme captures the acute and pervasive distress that permeates a child's cancer journey, from initial shock to sustained anxiety. Children frequently articulated intense fear and stress tied to invasive procedures like biopsies and injections, compounded by the alienating hospital milieu, which evokes loneliness and bodily discomfort. These accounts align with established evidence that procedural anticipation and the sterile, unpredictable hospital setting amplify anxiety in pediatric patients, often manifesting as somatic complaints or anticipatory nausea.34 Beyond procedural fears, a deeper layer of negative emotions emerged, including profound sadness, injustice, and existential questioning such as “Why me?” that eroded hope and fostered chronic unhappiness. This existential dimension, where children grapple with the perceived unfairness of their illness and yearn for normalcy, mirrors reports from other studies emphasizing how cancer prompts young patients to confront mortality and life meaning prematurely, potentially leading to long-term psychological weariness if unaddressed.35 Children diagnosed with cancer experience profound disruptions in their lives, often accompanied by significant emotional turmoil. One recent study reported that nearly half of children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia undergoing chemotherapy had low psychological well-being. Factors associated with psychological distress include age at diagnosis, treatment duration, family support, and hope.36 Regardless of whether children expressed or suppressed their emotions, the findings suggest that nurses should consider supporting children in expressing their emotions, and highlight the critical role of pediatric nurses in providing holistic, family-centered care, with an emphasis on psychosocial support to enhance emotional well-being.

Changes in social interactions and identity-related experiences

Children's self-perceptions and social roles are shaped through interactions within meaningful family, peer, and social relationships.37 While young children’s self-perception is strongly influenced by family relationships, peer interactions become increasingly important in shaping self-perceptions and social roles as children grow older.37,38 Ongoing cancer treatment reshaped children's and families' routines and roles. Changes in peer interactions and family relationships may influence how children perceive themselves and their roles within social relationships.39 Previous research has illustrated how cancer may disrupt a child's interpersonal world, shift children's roles within peer and family contexts, and contribute to feelings of marginalization. Social isolation and peer disconnection were recurrent, with children describing avoidance by classmates who viewed them as “weird” due to their illness, resulting in solitary play and diminished companionship. This echoes broader literature documenting heightened loneliness among pediatric cancer survivors, where treatment-related absences and visible changes exacerbate stigma and hinder peer bonds, often persisting into adolescence.15,40 Our findings suggest that changes in family dynamics and peer disconnection may contribute to perceived changes in children's self-perception and sense of social belonging. These findings also suggest that children may experience uncertainty in how they perceive themselves, particularly in relation to their roles within peer and family contexts. The findings echo previous literature suggesting that childhood cancer survivors may experience changes in their self-perceptions and social identities following treatment.41

Furthermore, the redefinition of family roles emerged as a pivotal shift, with parents assuming intensified caregiving duties such as coordinating bedside tutoring or providing physical comfort while children navigated altered interactions, including personality changes or enforced routines. Participants noted evolving dynamics, like increased maternal involvement in daily soothing. Comparable patterns in family-centered research reveal that childhood cancer prompts role reversals and communication barriers, potentially amplifying parental anxiety and sibling resentment if not proactively managed.42 These findings suggest the ongoing challenges children face in maintaining peer relationships, with children experiencing loneliness due to visible illness changes.21 These findings indicate that nurses should recognize children's interpersonal relationship difficulties and perceived changes in self-perception, and provide appropriate interventions to support their psychological adaptation and identity-related adjustment during this challenging period.41 Children in this study frequently described social isolation and peer disconnection, often linked to perceived differences associated with illness. Experiences of being avoided, misunderstood, or labeled as “different” suggest the presence of illness-related stigma, which has been identified as a key factor affecting psychosocial adjustment in pediatric cancer populations. Stigmatization may contribute to withdrawal from peer interactions and reduced willingness to disclose illness experiences, further reinforcing social isolation.43 These experiences may also influence how children perceive themselves in relation to others, contributing to changes in their sense of belonging, social roles, and self-perceptions during cancer treatment. These findings align with trauma-informed perspectives, which emphasize that children's behavioral responses should be understood within the context of repeated medical stress.44 Rather than viewing these behaviors as problematic, health care providers should recognize them as meaningful signals of distress and provide supportive interventions that promote emotional regulation, social engagement, and a sense of safety.

Disruption of normalcy

The disruption of normalcy theme underscores cancer's insidious interruption of childhood milestones, redirecting children from predictable growth toward fragmented, illness-dominated paths. Educational disruptions were prominent, with prolonged absences necessitating bedside tutoring amid mounting backlogs and fatigue-induced scheduling conflicts, leaving children overwhelmed by “insane” workloads and inconsistent learning.45 This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that treatment-related school absences, together with cancer- and treatment-related cognitive difficulties, may negatively affect academic progress and school reintegration among children with cancer.46 Complementing this, the loss of daily activities and past roles evoked profound boredom and disconnection, as physical limitations curtailed sports, scouting, or outings, blurring memories of prior vitality into “fuzzy” recollections.47,48 Such enforced inactivity not only fosters idleness in monotonous hospital settings but also correlates with broader psychosocial risks, including deferred aspirations and broader psychosocial challenges.49 The findings are consistent with a qualitative study by Savaşa et al., which reported that family and social support resources, along with the maintenance of daily routines, play a critical role in facilitating social reintegration and a return to normalcy among pediatric cancer survivors.21

Behavioral coping and responses

This study shows that children with cancer display two distinct behavioral patterns when navigating these upheavals. Behavioral coping and responses emerged as complementary dimensions of adaptation, reflecting children's agency even amid feelings of powerlessness. Overt expressions of distress, such as shouting refusals of chemotherapy or expressing strong aversion to hospitalization, functioned as visible expressions of frustration and attempts to regain a sense of control.50 This behavioral defiance parallels documented patterns in which procedural distress prompts noncompliance as a means of communicating distress and attempting to regain a sense of control.51,52 Conversely, implicit coping via withdrawal such as napping, digital distractions, or appetite suppression offered quieter self-preservation, conserving energy through disengagement. These strategies resonate with mixed-methods analyses revealing a spectrum of pediatric coping, from active resistance to passive avoidance, which, while potentially adaptive in the short term, may signal unmet emotional needs if reliance on isolation persists.53 Our findings are consistent with Liu et al.'s report, which identified common behavioral changes in children undergoing cancer treatment, including acting out and withdrawal.54 Some researchers have found that children with cancer often exhibit withdrawal behaviors due to multiple factors, highlighting the need to support not only physical recovery but also psychological adjustment and opportunities for social engagement.55 These findings underscore the importance for caregivers of children with cancer to recognize subtle behavioral cues and provide tailored supportive interventions that facilitate social engagement, help re-establish daily routines, and support children's positive self-perception and psychosocial well-being. These findings also highlight the importance of equipping pediatric oncology nurses with competencies in psychosocial assessment and supportive care. Previous studies have emphasized the need for structured educational preparation for pediatric oncology nurses to effectively address children's emotional distress, social challenges, and developmental needs during treatment.56 Integrating these competencies into clinical practice may enhance the ability of health care professionals to provide timely psychosocial support, facilitate communication, and promote adaptive coping in children undergoing treatment.

In addition to the above findings, the study results should also be interpreted within the cultural context of Taiwan, where family-centered values and strong parental involvement may shape children's experiences during illness. In Taiwanese culture, parents often take on highly protective roles, prioritizing the child's well-being and closely managing daily activities and medical care. This pattern has been described in studies of Taiwanese families caring for children with cancer, where parents assume protective roles and actively maintain family stability throughout the treatment process.57 While this may enhance emotional support, it may also contribute to increased dependence and reduced autonomy for children during treatment. In addition, a strong cultural emphasis on academic achievement may intensify concerns related to school disruption and falling behind peers, as academic stress has been shown to be associated with negative emotional and psychological outcomes among adolescents in Taiwan.58 These cultural factors may influence how children perceive their illness, relationships, and roles within both family and social contexts. Furthermore, these cultural values may also shape children's coping strategies, as the emphasis on emotional restraint and maintaining harmony may encourage more internalized or less expressive forms of coping during treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This multi-center qualitative study provides in-depth insights into children's lived experiences during cancer treatment and highlights important psychosocial and behavioral dimensions that may provide insights relevant to clinical care. By including participants from multiple medical centers, the study captured a range of experiences across different clinical contexts, enhancing the richness and contextual depth of the findings within the specific study context. The use of children's own narratives offers valuable perspectives that are often underrepresented in clinically focused research and supports the development of more family-centered approaches in pediatric oncology care. From a clinical perspective, the findings suggest the importance of recognizing procedural distress, social disconnection, disruption of daily routines, and varied coping responses as integral components of the treatment experience. These insights may inform the development of supportive approaches, such as psychosocial screening, emotional support strategies, and programs that facilitate peer connection and school reintegration. However, these implications should be interpreted with caution, as the study was not designed to evaluate the specific interventions.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the relatively small sample size and qualitative design may limit the transferability of the findings, particularly to pediatric cancer populations with different clinical characteristics or treatment experiences. Second, the study focused on children with leukemia and brain tumors; therefore, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of children with other cancer types that involve different treatment trajectories, neurocognitive effects, long-term sequelae, and psychosocial challenges. In addition, differences between leukemia and brain tumor in treatment intensity and potential long-term consequences may have contributed to heterogeneity in participants' experiences and perceptions. Third, although participants were recruited from multiple institutions, all sites were located in northern Taiwan, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other regions with different health care systems, cultural contexts, or socioeconomic conditions. Finally, the study did not systematically examine variations in disease-specific clinical characteristics, such as leukemia risk classification, tumor stage, treatment intensity, or disease severity, which may influence children's psychosocial experiences. Despite these limitations, the study provides context-specific insights into how children experience cancer treatment and underscores the importance of integrating psychosocial support interventions into routine pediatric oncology care.

Conclusions

In conclusion, children undergoing cancer treatment face a wide range of interrelated psychosocial and behavioral experiences associated with invasive procedures and frequent hospitalization. These experiences include psychological distress, changes in social interactions and self-perception, challenges to maintaining a sense of normalcy, and various behavioral coping responses. The findings highlight the importance for nurses to recognize children's internal emotional struggles, perceived changes in their self-perception and disruptions to their sense of normalcy, and their behavioral coping or withdrawal during treatment.

Given the ongoing incidence of pediatric cancers and the expanding array of therapies that, while extending survival, also increase treatment burdens, it is essential for all health care professionals involved in children's care, including oncologists, nurses, social workers, psychologists, and educators, to remain attentive to these psychosocial and behavioral experiences. The findings suggest that tailored family-centered supportive approaches may help promote social reintegration, preserve a sense of normalcy, and support children's positive self-perception during cancer treatment.
