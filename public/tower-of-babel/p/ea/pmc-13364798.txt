Histological and radiographic evaluation of bone formation following alveolar ridge preservation using octacalcium phosphate collagen composite: A prospective non-randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Takahiro Miyazawa, Kenji Takeshita, Hiroto Sano, Yasuo Okada, Tetsu Takahashi, Kensuke Yamauchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo radiographically and histologically evaluate bone formation following alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) using octacalcium phosphate collagen composite (OCP/Col).Materials and methodsA total of 42 patients (48 sites) completed the study. The primary comparison was performed between an OCP/Col-treated molar group (OCP-M) and an atelocollagen-treated molar control group (Control-M). In addition, an OCP/Col-treated incisor–premolar group (OCP-IP) was included as an exploratory subgroup to assess tooth-type effects. Alveolar bone dimensional changes were assessed using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) immediately after ARP and at 6 months. Guided hard tissue biopsies were performed to quantify the proportions of mature bone and residual granules.ResultsChanges in buccal bone height were + 0.7 mm (± 2.2) in the OCP-M group, − 0.6 mm (± 0.7) in the Control-M group, and + 3.7 mm (± 3.9) in the OCP-IP group. The difference between the OCP-M and Control-M groups was significant (p = 0.041), as was that between the OCP-IP and OCP-M groups (p = 0.038). Histologically, the proportion of mature bone in the alveolar crest region was significantly higher in the OCP-M group (38.14% ± 20.96) than in the Control-M group (18.28% ± 16.52, p = 0.009).ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this study, ARP using OCP/Col resulted in increased buccal bone height and enhanced bone maturation at the alveolar crest compared with atelocollagen alone.Clinical RelevanceOCP/Col may serve as a stand-alone graft material for ARP and may facilitate minimally invasive, prosthetically driven implant therapy.Clinical Trial RegistrationUMIN000059637 (UMIN Clinical Trials Registry).

Introduction

Following tooth extraction, the alveolar bone experiences bundle bone loss, with the buccal bone particularly prone to resorption, resulting in progressive negative morphological changes over time [1, 2]. Consequently, many cases require bone augmentation during implant therapy. However, bone augmentation procedures are technically sensitive and involve considerable surgical invasiveness [3].

Against this background, alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) has been proposed to minimize post-extraction ridge resorption [4]. While autogenous bone remains the gold standard graft material, its use is limited by donor-site morbidity and the restricted amount that can be harvested, prompting the development of alternative biomaterials [5]. Numerous biomaterials have been successfully applied in alveolar ridge preservation (ARP), and recent systematic reviews have demonstrated favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes with a variety of grafting materials [6, 7]. However, differences remain in their handling characteristics, biological behavior, and the quality of newly formed tissue. From a practical standpoint, ease of handling is an important requirement for clinical application [8].

Octacalcium phosphate (OCP) is known as a precursor of biological apatite [9] and has been reported to promote bone formation because it serves as a scaffold for bone-related proteins and signaling molecules [10, 11]. Moreover, OCP exhibits excellent bioresorbability and can be gradually replaced by autogenous bone [12].

OCP/Col, a composite consisting of OCP and atelocollagen, promotes osteoblast proliferation and attachment through the addition of collagen [13], and offers the advantage of superior retention within extraction sockets compared with granular bone substitutes. In ARP, covering granular graft materials with a collagen membrane or sponge has been shown to be effective [6, 7], and there are also reports that open healing contributes to ridge preservation [14, 15]. These characteristics suggest that OCP/Col may function effectively as a stand-alone graft material for ARP without the need for additional barrier materials.

Although the biological properties of OCP/Col have been investigated in several experimental and clinical studies, evidence regarding its use as a stand-alone graft material for alveolar ridge preservation remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, no prospective controlled clinical trial has compared OCP/Col with atelocollagen alone and evaluated both radiographic and histological outcomes following ARP in humans. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate bone formation following ARP using OCP/Col through comprehensive radiographic and histological analyses.

Materials and methods

The clinical research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Japanese Society of Oral Implantology (Approval No. 2023-13). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1975 version and 2013 revision) and was performed between July 26, 2023, and May 31, 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, where applicable, and was registered in the UMIN clinical trials registry (ID: UMIN000059637). Registration of this clinical trial was completed after the initiation of the study. Nevertheless, the study protocol and outcome measures were established before enrollment and were not altered subsequently.

Study design and population

This study was a prospective non-randomized controlled clinical trial designed to evaluate tissue formation following ARP using OCP/Col (Bonarc® Toyobo Co., Osaka, Japan) through radiographic and histological analyses. The control group was treated with atelocollagen (TERUPLUG®, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The primary objective of this study was to evaluate bone formation following ARP according to the presence or absence of OCP in collagen by comparing the OCP molar (OCP-M) and the molar control (Control-M) groups. In addition, an exploratory subgroup analysis comparing the OCP-M and the OCP incisor–premolar (OCP-IP) groups was performed to assess the potential influence of tooth type on healing outcomes. Patients who underwent tooth extraction as part of planned implant therapy were recruited. All participants received detailed information regarding the study protocol and informed consent. Participants were provided with standardized information regarding the available ARP materials, including their characteristics, costs, and the potential advantages and disadvantages reported in previous studies of OCP/Col. Care was taken to present this information as objectively as possible and to avoid providing guidance that might favor one material over the other. The graft material was selected by the patient after a thorough discussion with the study investigators. Participants were allocated to study groups according to the selected graft material and the type of extracted tooth.

However, because treatment allocation was based on each patient’s informed decision rather than randomization, selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within this methodological limitation.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was conducted with reference to a previous study focusing on histological evaluation [16]. The study was designed to detect a 15–20% difference in the proportion of mature bone between the OCP-M and the Control-M groups, which constituted the primary comparison of this study. A significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% were assumed. Assuming a standard deviation of 15%, the required sample size was calculated to be 14 participants per group. To account for an anticipated 15% dropout rate, the target enrollment was set at 16 participants per group. Furthermore, considering the potential for unusable histological specimens due to sample damage, a larger number of participants was initially recruited.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows.

Eligible participants were patients aged 18 years or older who required tooth extraction in the maxilla or mandible and were scheduled to receive implant-supported prosthetic treatment.

Exclusion criteria were as follows.

(1) Patients with systemic diseases considered unsuitable for study participation, such as poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c > 7), (2) Current or past use of bisphosphonates or denosumab, (3) Patients with neurocognitive disorders, (4) Smokers, (5) Patients receiving corticosteroid therapy, (6) Patients receiving immunosuppressive medications, (7) Patients with hepatic or renal dysfunction, (8) Patients currently undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy, or with a history of head and neck radiation, (9) Pregnant or breastfeeding women, (10) Patients who declined participation in the study, (11) Patients who withdrew their consent during the study, (12) Patients who did not undergo implant placement and biopsy (13) Patients for whom histological evaluation could not be performed due to anatomical limitations, (14) Patients who experienced alveolar bone damage as a result of the extraction procedure, (15) Patients whose biopsy specimens were damaged or orientation was unclear, making analysis impossible.

Surgical procedure

Teeth were extracted atraumatically as follows: The periodontal ligament was severed using a scalpel, and teeth were carefully extracted with forceps or elevators to avoid damage to the surrounding mucosa. For multi-rooted teeth, sectioning was performed, and any remaining interradicular septum was preserved. The extraction sockets were curetted until the bone surface was exposed and subsequently irrigated with physiological saline.

In the OCP-M and OCP-IP groups, OCP/Col was placed into the extraction sockets up to the level of the gingival margin. In the Control-M group, atelocollagen was similarly placed. To stabilize OCP/Col, 5 − 0 PTFE filament sutures (BIO SOFTRETCH®, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) were used with cross-mattress suturing, and additional simple interrupted sutures were placed as needed to enhance stability. The Control-M group received the same suturing procedure. In both groups, tension-free suturing was performed, and the sockets were managed using an open-healing approach. For postoperative management, all patients were instructed to take 250 mg of amoxicillin and 60 mg of loxoprofen Na three times daily for 3 days. Sutures were removed 1 week after ARP.

Implant placement was performed 6 months after ARP. All implants used in this study were Astra Tech Implant System EV® (Dentsply Sirona, Sweden). At the time of implantation, the insertion torque values and the use of horizontal bone augmentation were recorded. When less than 2 mm of circumferential bone thickness was anticipated around the implant, horizontal bone augmentation was performed. In cases without bone augmentation, an appropriately sized healing abutment was placed, and the flap was positioned based on one-stage protocol. In cases with bone augmentation, a cover screw was used, and the flap was completely closed. Implant positioning was simulated using dedicated software (Simplant®, Dentsply Sirona, Sweden). CBCT DICOM data were superimposed with STL data obtained from the wax-up model to plan implant placement directly beneath the virtual prosthesis. Implant placement was performed using a dental electric surgical unit (Implant 1000®, Dentsply Sirona, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s recommended drilling protocol under copious irrigation. In all cases, the rotational speed was set at 25 rpm with a maximum torque limit of 45 Ncm. Insertion torque values were recorded electronically, and the final insertion torque value was used for analysis. Insertion torque values were classified and analyzed according to whether the amount of existing bone anchorage at the simulated implant position was ≥ 2 mm or < 2 mm, as determined by radiographic assessment described below.

During osteotomy preparation, tissue biopsies were conducted using a trephine bur with an outer diameter of 2.4 mm and an inner diameter of 1.6 mm. The surgical guides, designed in the simulation software, incorporated a sleeve matching the outer diameter of the trephine bur to ensure accurate bone sampling intraoperatively. During implant surgery, the completed surgical guide was used to insert the trephine bur to the planned depth and harvest tissue. To enhance the precision of histological analysis, soft tissue was included in cases without bone augmentation. In contrast, for cases requiring bone augmentation or with insufficient keratinized tissue, a mucoperiosteal flap was elevated, and only hard tissue was biopsied. In these cases, the thickness of the elevated soft tissue was measured and cross-referenced with the Simplant® plan to define the region of interest for histological analysis. Digital imaging reliability

To ensure data quality, radiographic and histological analyses were initially performed on 10 randomly selected patients by a blinded examiner. After confirming that the respective intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.91 and 0.92, full-scale data collection was initiated.

Radiographic analysis

To evaluate changes in alveolar bone morphology after ARP, CBCT scans were obtained immediately after ARP (t1) and 6 months later (t2). Open-source software (3D Slicer 5.8) was used for analysis.

The CBCT DICOM data were superimposed by referencing regions that exhibited no morphological changes over the 6-month period (e.g., the floor of the maxillary sinus or the inferior border of the mandible), and measurements were performed on identical planes at t1 and t2. Reference lines were established for both the maxilla and mandible: in the mandible, the tangent to the inferior border; in the maxilla, the tangent to the floor of the nasal cavity or to the lowest point of the maxillary sinus.

Although OCP/Col immediately after placement is radiolucent, its density tends to increase based on a previous study [17], and therefore did not affect the measurement of bone morphology at t1. The parameters assessed are listed below.

Bone anchorage was assessed by superimposing and comparing t1 and t2 CBCT images, allowing differentiation between pre-existing bone and newly formed tissue. Histological analysis

Sample preparation

The harvested tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and then decalcified in 10% EDTA-2Na solution at 4℃. After dehydration, the specimens were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. The sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining.

Histological evaluation

HE-stained sections were digitized into virtual slides using a NanoZoomer digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan). Analysis of tissue composition was performed using the image viewing software NDPView2 (Hamamatsu Photonics).

Definition of the “Crestal line”

A reference line perpendicular to the long axis of the specimen and in contact with the cut surfaces of bone or OCP granules was defined as “Crestal line”. The length of this line was aligned with the short axis of the specimen. It was also cross-referenced with the soft tissue thickness and hard tissue morphology in Simplant® to ensure there was no discrepancy between the two.

Definition of the region of interest (ROI)

To focus on the newly formed bone at the outermost layer of the tissue, the region of interest (ROI) was set within 2 mm from the alveolar crest. Specifically, a circle area with a diameter of 1 mm (area = 0.785 mm²) was centered at the midpoint of the crestal line. Another circle, touching the first circle, was placed on the implant apex side, and the interiors of both circles were defined as the analysis area. The circle closer to the alveolar crest was designated as “Area Upper (Area U),” and the circle immediately below it as “Area Lower (Area L)” (Fig. 3). Cases in which the ROI contained pre-existing bone were excluded.Fig. 3Definition of the region of interest

Definition of the region of interest

Analysis targets

The analysis focused on the areas of mature bone (MB) and residual granules (RG), and the percentage of each within the circular regions of interest was quantified. To identify the presence of lamellar structures and classify mature bone and fibrous bone tissue, a polarized filter (Olympus U-ANT, U-POT, Evident, Tokyo, Japan) was attached to an optical microscope (Olympus BX51, Evident, Tokyo, Japan), and images were captured using a digital camera (Olympus DP27, Evident, Tokyo, Japan). The criteria for identifying mature bone were areas that contained osteocyte lacunae and osteocytes, and exhibited a lamellar structure under polarized light.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS® Statistics version 21 (International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The primary comparison was conducted between the OCP-M and Control-M groups to evaluate the effect of OCP/Col on radiographic and histological healing outcomes. In addition, an exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted between the OCP-M and OCP-IP groups to assess the potential influence of tooth type on bone formation.

Baseline continuous variables are reported as median (range), and categorical variables as n (%). Radiographic outcomes (changes in bone morphology) and histological outcomes (tissue composition ratios) are reported as mean ± 1 standard deviation (SD).

Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For continuous variables with a normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent t-test. For variables not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study was designed to compare alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) using octacalcium phosphate collagen composite (OCP/Col) with atelocollagen alone. Atelocollagen was selected as the control material because OCP/Col consists of OCP incorporated into an atelocollagen matrix. Therefore, comparison with atelocollagen alone enabled evaluation of the specific contribution of OCP to bone formation while minimizing the influence of the collagen carrier.

This prospective non-randomized controlled clinical trial demonstrated that ARP using OCP/Col significantly enhanced buccal and crestal bone height following tooth extraction compared with atelocollagen alone, as assessed radiographically. Histological analysis further revealed a significantly higher proportion of mature bone (MB) in the superficial layer in the OCP/Col group, supporting the radiographic findings.

Among sites treated with OCP/Col, no significant differences in histological parameters were observed between the incisor–premolar and molar groups. In contrast, radiographic analysis showed significantly greater increases in buccal bone height and less horizontal ridge width reduction in the incisor–premolar group than in the molar group. These findings suggest that, in relatively small extraction sockets such as those in the incisor and premolar regions, bone formation may occur more coronally within the socket.

OCP is known to function as a scaffold for osteogenic proteins and signaling molecules, including BMP-2 [11, 18]. In addition, OCP exhibits favorable bioresorbability and has been reported to undergo gradual replacement by autogenous bone during healing [12]. OCP itself has been reported to promote angiogenesis [19], while OCP/Col, a composite of OCP and atelocollagen, enhances osteoblast proliferation, cell attachment, and angiogenic activity [13, 20]. Recently, Type-H vessels, a specialized vascular subtype characterized by high expression of osteogenesis-related markers and the ability to couple angiogenesis to osteogenesis, have been identified [21]. Type-H vessels have also been observed during extraction socket healing in animal models [22]. Furthermore, OCP/Col has been reported to promote the formation of such vascular networks [23]. Clinically, these biological properties may be advantageous as successful ARP should be evaluated not only by dimensional preservation but also by the quality of newly formed bone supporting subsequent implant placement.

In the present study, a significantly higher proportion of mature bone was observed even in the crestal region, which was distant from the existing bone surface. It is possible that cells and signaling molecules accumulating along the margins of OCP/Col through blood supply from the surrounding tissues contributed to bone formation in the crestal region. This observation is consistent with previous animal studies demonstrating vertical bone wall formation along the margins of OCP/Col [24].

The periosteum has also been suggested to contribute to bone formation at the alveolar crest [25, 26]. However, some studies have reported that periosteal tissue is not directly involved in OCP-induced bone formation [27]. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the formation of the outer bone wall observed in the present study remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

Following tooth extraction, rapid resorption of the alveolar ridge, particularly of the buccal bone wall, is well documented [2, 28]. Alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) using various biomaterials has been proposed to mitigate these dimensional changes. Among the available approaches, socket sealing with particulate graft materials covered by collagen membranes or collagen sponges is widely recognized as an effective strategy for reducing horizontal ridge resorption [6, 7].

More recently, surgical approaches involving open healing or minimal flap elevation have been reported to be effective in preserving both hard and soft tissue architecture [29–31]. In addition, several studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes with collagen-containing graft materials under open-healing conditions [15, 16, 32]. While most of these studies have primarily reported preservation of ridge width, the present study demonstrated relatively limited effects on horizontal ridge preservation, with differences mainly observed in the crestal region. In contrast, a notable finding of the present study was the enhancement of both buccal and crestal bone height.

To focus specifically on tissue formed following ARP, the region of interest (ROI) was strictly confined to the crestal region using guided surgery. Couso-Queiruga et al. [16] reported a mean mineralized tissue proportion of 33.33% (range, 0.49–56.21) after 6 months of healing following ARP using deproteinized bovine bone mineral with collagen (DBBM-C). Although direct comparison is difficult because of differences in the ROI and histological classification methods, the histological findings observed in the present study were generally within a similar range. These findings suggest that ARP using OCP/Col may provide a regenerative potential comparable to that reported for other collagen-containing graft materials.

On the other hand, considerable variability in new bone formation has been reported in previous studies [16, 33], and inter-individual variation in histological outcomes was also observed in the present study. This variability suggests that multiple factors, including surgical technique, local tissue conditions, and defect morphology, may influence bone formation. Further large-scale studies are therefore needed to clarify the relative contribution of these factors.

The radiographic improvement in buccal bone height observed in the present study may contribute to less invasive implant therapy. Immediate implant placement following tooth extraction has become increasingly common in clinical practice; however, the condition of the buccal bone wall is known to have a substantial influence on treatment success and long-term stability [34]. In teeth indicated for extraction, the buccal bone wall is often thin or partially deficient as a result of trauma or infection [35], which may limit the indications for immediate implant placement [34].

Furthermore, even when immediate implant placement is avoided and delayed implant placement following ARP is selected, greater horizontal ridge resorption has been reported in sites with a thin buccal bone wall [36], frequently necessitating additional bone augmentation procedures [37]. Therefore, the bone formation observed in the present study may contribute not only to the realization of less invasive implant therapy but also to the establishment of a more prosthetically favorable implant position.

Residual graft material is an important consideration in ARP using synthetic bone substitutes, and its persistence is known to depend on the composition and resorption characteristics of the graft material [33]. Although OCP/Col is classified as a resorbable biomaterial, residual granules were still observed in a proportion of cases after a 6-month healing period.

Histological observations revealed two distinct patterns of residual granules. One pattern consisted of granules located within fibrous connective tissue in the crestal region. As these granules showed no direct contact with bone tissue, they may have been present in an encapsulated state. The other pattern consisted of granules incorporated within bone tissue. A previous clinical study using OCP/Col demonstrated that the radiographic density of OCP/Col-derived tissue continues to increase after the initial 6-month healing period [38], suggesting ongoing mineral maturation and tissue remodeling. Therefore, residual granules incorporated within bone tissue may represent an intermediate stage of the bone replacement process.

In the present study, only the area fraction of residual granules was evaluated, and their histological characteristics were not investigated in detail. Further studies are required to clarify their biological behavior and long-term clinical significance.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.

Although this study was designed as a prospective controlled clinical trial, treatment allocation was based on each patient’s informed decision rather than randomization. Although standardized information regarding the available graft materials was provided as objectively as possible, selection bias cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting the present findings. In addition, the relatively small sample size and limited observation period represent further limitations.

Post-extraction ridge alterations are known to be influenced not only by the surgical procedure and graft material used but also by local anatomical conditions, particularly the status of the buccal bone wall [35]. Although information regarding extraction socket morphology was collected and evaluated as baseline characteristics, the influence of factors such as buccal bone wall thickness and defect morphology on treatment outcomes was not fully examined in the present analysis. These factors should therefore be considered when interpreting the observed treatment effects.

Further large-scale randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up periods that consider variations in extraction socket morphology are required to confirm the present findings.

Conclusions

Alveolar ridge preservation using OCP/Col as a stand-alone graft material resulted in significantly improved radiographic and histological outcomes, including increased buccal and crestal bone height and enhanced bone maturation at the alveolar crest. Within the limitations of this study, these findings suggest that OCP/Col may facilitate minimally invasive and prosthetically driven implant therapy.
