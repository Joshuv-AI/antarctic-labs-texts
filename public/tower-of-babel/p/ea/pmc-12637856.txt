Impact of Collateral Circulation on Poststroke Epilepsy After Mechanical Thrombectomy for Large Vessel Occlusive Stroke: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Peng Jiang, Zongzhi Jiang, He Sui, Mingyang Du, Qi Guo, Zhaoshi Zheng, Fuli Wang, Zhanhao Mo, Songyan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAcute ischemic stroke (AIS) represents a significant etiology of acquired epilepsy in adults. However, there remains a paucity of evidence concerning predictive factors for poststroke epilepsy (PSE) among patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy (MT). We aimed to evaluate the association between poor collateral circulation and the risk of PSE.MethodsThis multicenter cohort study included AIS patients who underwent MT (2017–2022) at three tertiary centers. Adjusted Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PSE risk. Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and competing risk analyses were also performed.ResultsWe included 1167 patients (mean age: 62 years; 46.5% female). PSE frequency was higher in the poor collateral circulation group compared to the control group (13.0% vs. 4.2%, p < 0.001). Patients with inadequate collateral circulation (ASITN/SIR < 2) showed significantly increased risks of both 12‐month PSE and 60‐month PSE development (HR > 1, p < 0.05) compared to those with adequate collateral status (ASITN/SIR ≥ 2) following MT for AIS.ConclusionsPoor collateral circulation is associated with PSE and may help identify individuals at high risk for PSE.

Introduction

Poststroke epilepsy (PSE) is a neurological sequela of stroke, accounting for approximately 55% of newly diagnosed epilepsy cases in older adults and 10% of all epilepsy cases [1]. PSE is divided into early seizures (within 7 days after stroke) and late seizures (beyond 7 days) [2]. Late‐onset seizures typically reflect ongoing neuronal damage and are associated with chronic inflammation, with a > 50% cumulative risk within 10 years [1, 2]. PSE incidence is increasing with the growing number of stroke survivors, with 2%–14% of patients following ischemic stroke and 10%–20% of patients following hemorrhagic stroke developing PSE [3]. The initial 12 months poststroke constitute the peak risk period for PSE, encompassing 40%–80% of all reported instances [3].

PSE profoundly affects patient prognosis and the broader disease burden, including a mortality risk 1.7 times greater than that of stroke survivors without epilepsy and a ~40% elevated likelihood of cognitive decline [4, 5]. Furthermore, PSE is associated with a higher risk of disability and dependence, particularly among older adults, many of whom require extended care after their first seizure [6]. Identifying risk factors associated with PSE and implementing early prediction and intervention strategies are crucial to improving the long‐term quality of life for stroke survivors.

Risk factors for PSE include stroke severity, cortical involvement, age, infarct size, and early poststroke seizures [7, 8]. The “SeLECT score,” which incorporates stroke severity, large‐artery atherosclerosis (LAA), early seizures, cortical involvement, and territory of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), has been proposed as a user‐friendly tool for identifying individuals at high risk of PSE [7]. Nevertheless, evidence is scarce on the predictors of PSE in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients with large vessel occlusion after mechanical thrombectomy (MT). Endovascular MT is the standard treatment for AIS caused by large‐vessel occlusion, markedly improving survival rates and functional recovery outcomes [9]. Patients undergoing MT account for 5%–10% of all stroke cases, and this proportion is increasing with the widespread adoption of medical technologies and improvements in stroke care systems [10, 11]. Consequently, clinical research specifically targeting the risk factors and prediction of epilepsy following MT for large‐vessel occlusion stroke remains lacking.

Angiography has become an indispensable component of MT [12], providing an opportunity to precisely investigate variations in cerebral collateral circulation and their impact on clinical outcomes and complications related to infarct development. Collateral circulation influences the success of reperfusion and recanalization during thrombectomy, alters the final infarct volume, and profoundly affects postoperative functional outcomes [13]. The gold‐standard American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology (ASITN/SIR) score for digital subtraction angiography is the most widely used scale for evaluating visual collateral vessel status [14]. Consistent evidence indicates that poor collateral circulation, as assessed by ASITN/SIR grading, is closely associated with larger infarct volumes, rapid infarct core growth, and hemorrhagic transformation (HT) following endovascular treatment [15]. Notably, infarct volume and HT are key risk factors for PSE [16]. Thus, collateral circulation status may be closely linked to PSE occurrence.

In this study, we analyze PSE in patients receiving MT, specifically aiming to assess the association between collateral circulation status and PSE occurrence in this treatment context.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Overview

This retrospective, multicenter study was conducted in the neurology departments of three hospitals. This study was approved by the China‐Japan Union Hospital (No. 2023120805), Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital (No. KY2025039), and ShengJing Hospital of China Medical University Ethics Committees (No. 2024PS1100K), and verbal informed consent was obtained from participants or their legal representatives over the telephone. Given the retrospective nature of this study, the minimal risk involved, and the absence of any interventions, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

This report complies with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [17]. This complies with the guidelines outlined in the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data extension [18]. Figure 1 illustrates the study workflow.

Study Population

We included all consecutive patients who underwent MT for AIS between January 1, 2017 and April 31, 2023, at China‐Japan Union Hospital, Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital, and Shengjing Hospital. This encompassed both inpatient and emergency cases.

The exclusion criteria were a documented history of epilepsy or other potential epileptogenic risk factors, such as intracranial neoplasms or traumatic brain injury, recurrent cerebrovascular events during follow‐up, and a prior stroke occurring within 2 years before MT.

Data Source

Demographic and clinical data were collected through a review of electronic medical records, including age; sex; stroke etiology (LAA, cardioembolism, stroke of undetermined etiology, stroke of other determined etiology, and small‐artery occlusion); stroke severity at admission (National Institute of Health stroke scale [NIHSS] ≤ 3, NIHSS 4–10, and NIHSS ≥ 11); medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disorders (e.g., atrial fibrillation and congestive heart failure), and behavioral factors, particularly smoking status; occurrence of early‐onset seizures; impairment of consciousness at stroke onset; baseline NIHSS and modified Rankin scale (mRS) upon hospital admission; surgical details related to the MT; and routine radiological data. HT was defined as the presence of intracranial hemorrhage on follow‐up imaging (either noncontrast computed tomography or MRI) when no bleeding was observed on the initial scan or as hemorrhagic infarction identified on the baseline images. For spatial localization, HT was categorized as follows: (a) intrainfarct hemorrhage (defined as blood confined within the infarct core as delineated by diffusion‐weighted imaging or computed tomography); (b) extrainfarct hemorrhage (defined as blood located outside the infarcted territory), including remote parenchymal or subarachnoid hemorrhage; and (c) mixed‐type hemorrhage characterized by the coexistence of intra‐ and extrainfarct bleeding. This binary classification was adopted to preserve statistical power while capturing clinically relevant spatial patterns. Confirmation of HT was achieved through consensus between two neurologists, both of whom were blinded to seizure outcomes. To ensure accurate documentation, the presence of blood within the infarcted region was required to be visible on at least two consecutive imaging slices. The Hounsfield unit scale was used to differentiate between hemorrhage, extravasated contrast material, or a combination of both. Two trained neurovascular imaging experts evaluated postintervention brain magnetic resonance imaging by incorporating T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, conventional T2‐weighted imaging, and diffusion‐weighted imaging sequences.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the incidence of PSE (Spontaneous seizures more than 7 days after the onset of AIS) within 60 months after stroke. The secondary outcome was PSE incidence within 12 months following stroke. PSE episodes were ascertained through comprehensive medical record evaluations and subsequently validated via telephone interviews using a modified standardized questionnaire (Table S1). To minimize false positives and determine the epileptic nature of these episodes, a positive answer triggered a face‐to‐face neurological consultation and an electroencephalogram. Any uncertainties related to PSE events or clinical parameters were resolved by consensus between two independent clinicians, with unresolved cases ultimately arbitrated by a senior neurologist.

Exposure Variables

Collateral vessel status was assessed using the ASITN/SIR collateral grading system (Table S2). The collateral vessel score is categorized into grades 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, with < 2 indicating poor collateral circulation and ≥ 2 indicating good collateral circulation before MT (Figure 2). The angiographic data were digitally transferred to the core laboratory for standardized assessment. Two blinded evaluators independently assessed baseline collateral circulation grading (Z.S.Z. and F.L.W., with > 15 years of angiographic rating experience).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables or as median (interquartile range) for skewed distributions. Categorical data are summarized as frequencies and percentages. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the one‐sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The PSE and non‐PSE groups were compared using an independent samples t‐test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test for non‐normally distributed continuous variables, and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. To address incomplete data entries, multiple imputation was performed. The detailed distribution of missing data across variables is presented in Tables S3–S5.

The follow‐up person‐years for each participant were calculated from the baseline survey date (conducted between 2017 and 2022) to either the date of PSE diagnosis or the end of follow‐up (May 2024), whichever occurred first. To investigate the association between the ASITN/SIR index and PSE development, a multivariable‐adjusted Cox proportional hazards model was used to account for the time‐to‐event framework, estimating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three models were constructed to analyze the data. Model 1 served as the unadjusted baseline model. Model 2 incorporated adjustments for demographic and clinical covariates, including age (continuous), sex (male/female), current smoking status (yes/no), medical history (hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and congestive heart failure), stroke etiology, occlusion site, early seizure (yes/no), stroke severity, and ASPECTS (continuous). Model 3 further adjusted for neuroanatomical involvement, specifically frontal lobe involvement (yes/no) and temporal lobe involvement (yes/no). Variance inflation factors were calculated to assess the potential influence of multicollinearity among variables. These variables were selected based on their established associations with unprovoked PSE following ischemic stroke and their representation as components of the SeLECT score, which is widely used in clinical practice to predict PSE risk after ischemic stroke.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to predefined stratification factors, including age, sex, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, followed by univariable and multivariable statistical analyses. The multivariate analysis was adjusted for age, sex, current smoking status, medical history (hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, and dyslipidemia), stroke etiology, occlusion site, stroke severity, early seizures, ASPECTS, frontal, and temporal involvement. In the subgroup analyses by age and sex, adjustment was omitted from the multivariate models due to inherent stratification. Participants were categorized into two cohorts (< 60 and ≥ 60 years). For each group, survival analyses were conducted using Cox regression models to estimate proportional hazards, with the corresponding HRs and 95% CIs graphically represented through forest plots.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. Individuals with MT failure were excluded, and then further adjustments were made for impairment of consciousness at stroke onset, ASM use after early seizure, mRS score at discharge, and parietal and occipital lobe involvement. The analysis was repeated among 1053 patients with a baseline mRS of ≤ 2. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by replacing early seizures with acute symptomatic status epilepticus as a covariate to further examine the association between collateral circulation status and PSE. Lastly, a competing risk regression model was applied to account for death as a competing event during follow‐up. The cumulative incidence function was utilized to estimate the time‐dependent risk of seizure development, incorporating death as a competing risk event. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant, and all reported p‐values are two‐sided. Statistical analyses were performed in R v.4.2.1.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of the Participants

The baseline characteristics of the study population are detailed in Table 1, consisting of 1167 participants with a mean age of 62 years (±16). The cohort predominantly contained men (53.5% men; 46.5% women). Over a maximum follow‐up period of 5 years, hypertension prevalence was noted in 52.1% of participants, whereas diabetes and dyslipidemia were present in 21.9% and 31.5% of participants, respectively. A history of current smoking was reported by 29.2%, and atrial fibrillation was observed in 33.0% of participants. Congestive heart failure was identified in 21.9%. Early seizure incidence was 3.9%, whereas the cumulative PSE incidence was 8.7%. Acute symptomatic status epilepticus occurred in 0.9% of patients (n = 10), with a significantly higher incidence in the ASITN/SIR < 2 group (1.5%) compared with the ASITN/SIR ≥ 2 group (0.2%). Discharge mRS scores were significantly worse in the ASITN/SIR < 2 group compared with the ASITN/SIR ≥ 2 group (p < 0.001). HT was identified in 43.6% of patients, with intrainfarct HT being the most prevalent subtype (39.2%). The ASITN/SIR < 2 group had significantly higher rates of both intrainfarct and mixed‐type HT (p < 0.001). In both subgroups, participants with an ASITN/SIR index < 2 were younger (p = 0.001) and had higher NIHSS scores (p < 0.001) and higher baseline mRS scores (p < 0.001) and a higher prevalence of frontal and temporal involvement (p < 0.001) compared to those with an ASITN/SIR index ≥ 2. Additionally, this group demonstrated a higher incidence of early seizures (p < 0.001) and PSE (p < 0.001).

The baseline characteristics of patients in the PSE and non‐PSE groups are summarized in Table 2. ASITN/SIR Index and PSE

Table 3 shows the correlation between ASITN/SIR scores and PSE incidence, including both 60 and 12‐month PSE rates. In model 1, patients with an ASITN/SIR index < 2 were associated with a 221% (HR 3.21, 95% CI: 2.03–5.08, p < 0.001) increased risk of PSE events. This risk decreased to 155% (HR 2.55, 95% CI: 1.56–4.16, p < 0.001) in model 2. In model 3, individuals with an ASITN/SIR index < 2 exhibited a 166% (HR 2.66, 95% CI: 1.62–4.37, p < 0.001) higher risk of PSE events.

A similar trend was observed for the 12‐month PSE incidence: patients with an ASITN/SIR index < 2 had an HR of 4.93 (95% CI: 2.05–11.85, p < 0.001) in model 1, 4.81 (95% CI: 1.89–12.24, p < 0.001) in model 2, and 5.38 (95% CI: 2.07–13.96, p < 0.001) in model 3. In the competing‐risk analysis, we adjusted for death as a competing event during follow‐up, and the results were consistent with those of the primary analysis (aHR: 2.23; 95% CI: 1.31–3.81) (Table S6). The cumulative incidence functions for competing events (death) and the outcome event (PSE) are presented in Figure S1. Factors that were significant in univariate analysis and the ASITN/SIR score were included in the variance inflation factor analysis (Table S7).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Subgroup analysis was performed to assess the impact of collateral circulation status on the overall risk of PSE (Figure S2). The association between poor collateral circulation status and PSE was significant across subgroups, including patients aged > 60 years, male and female patients, those with hypertension, and patients with or without diabetes mellitus, with no significant interaction effects observed.

The results of several sensitivity analyses were consistent with the initial findings (Table 4). Participants with unsuccessful recanalization were excluded to avoid confounding effects from MT failure, and poor collateral circulation remained significantly associated with PSE (aHR: 2.62; 95% CI: 1.55–4.43). After further adjustment for impairment of consciousness at stroke onset, mRS at discharge, use of ASMs after early seizures, and parietal and occipital involvement, poor collateral circulation remained significantly associated with an increased risk of PSE (aHR: 2.62; 95% CI: 1.60–4.30). Moreover, when “early seizure” was replaced with “acute symptomatic status epilepticus” as a covariate in the Cox regression model, the association between poor collateral circulation and PSE remained robust (aHR: 2.51; 95% CI: 1.55–4.06). Finally, re‐analysis of the data from 1053 patients with a baseline mRS score of ≤ 2 yielded consistent results (aHR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.66–4.79).

Discussion

Several studies have established the efficacy of endovascular thrombectomy for the management of AIS cases [19]. The impact of collateral circulation status on stroke prognosis and its predictive value in postinterventional adverse outcomes underscores the necessity of evaluating the relationship between collateral circulation status and PSE. In this study, poor collateral circulation (ASITN/SIR < 2) was identified as an independent risk factor for PSE. Even when compared with traditional PSE predictors, poor collateral circulation remained closely associated with PSE occurrence. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses further supported this relationship. Moreover, our findings suggest that poor collateral circulation is linked to the development of PSE and may serve as a novel biomarker for identifying stroke survivors at a high risk for PSE.

The correlation between the individual ASITN/SIR index, and stroke outcomes, and complications has been extensively evaluated in previous studies [20, 21]. Vascular imaging analysis from the Interventional Management of Stroke III trial demonstrates that insufficient collateral perfusion (ASITN/SIR < 2) correlates with multiple adverse prognostic indicators, including expanded initial ischemic lesions, accelerated progression of infarcted tissue, and poorer neurological outcomes following endovascular therapy (EVT) [13]. Among patients with similar occluded vessels, individual differences in collateral status significantly contribute to variations in clinical and radiological outcomes [15]. Several cohort studies have revealed that the ASITN/SIR index is associated with multiple clinical factors that influence stroke outcomes [22]. The California cohort study of EVT‐treated patients with AIS reported that superior collateral perfusion assessed by the ASITN/SIR classification system at presentation was linked to improved reperfusion rates after endovascular intervention [21]. Additionally, higher collateral grades were associated with reduced postoperative hemorrhage, smaller infarct volumes at both presentation and discharge, and better neurological outcomes at 90 days [13]. These factors are critical predictors of functional recovery in stroke survivors. Alexandre et al. demonstrated that insufficient collateral perfusion is a significant predictor of increased blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability in patients with AIS undergoing thrombectomy, highlighting the critical role of collateral circulation status in maintaining BBB integrity [23]. Building on these findings, our study also demonstrated that poor collateral circulation status significantly increases PSE risk. This observation supports the previously established link between inadequate collateral circulation and worse neurological outcomes.

The combined influence of HT and insufficient collateral circulation exacerbates the neurobiological environment conducive to epileptogenesis. In the setting of poor collateral circulation, endovascular recanalization may fail to achieve effective tissue‐level reperfusion. Even when technical recanalization is successful, brain regions with marginal perfusion remain highly vulnerable to secondary hemorrhage [20, 24]. Such hemorrhagic complications may elevate interstitial pressure, increase arteriolar resistance, and further restrict the recruitment of leptomeningeal collateral flow [25]. This cascade establishes a vicious cycle that exacerbates ischemic injury in previously hypoperfused regions and compromises the integrity of the BBB [26, 27]. Disruption of BBB function facilitates the infiltration of proinflammatory cytokines—such as interleukin (IL)‐1β, tumor necrosis factor‐α, and IL‐6—which promote glial activation and initiate inflammatory signaling pathways that enhance neuronal hyperexcitability [28, 29]. The resulting neuroinflammatory microenvironment—characterized by electrolyte disturbances, persistent immune activation, neurotransmitter dysregulation, and local iron accumulation—establishes favorable conditions for epileptogenesis [20, 30]. In cases of large‐vessel occlusion, robust collateral networks serve a compensatory role by maintaining cerebral perfusion, mitigating hypoxia, and preserving metabolic homeostasis in vulnerable brain regions. These hemodynamic benefits stabilize the neurovascular environment, protect against BBB disruption, and minimize the risks of both HT and PSE [23]. By contrast, inadequate collateral supply following revascularization may result in continued infarct progression, further deterioration of BBB integrity, and adverse remodeling of the neuronal environment [31]. Key pathological features under these conditions include dysfunctional ion channel activity, glial reactivity, cytokine infiltration, hemosiderin deposition, and glial scarring [32, 33]. Collectively, these processes enhance neuronal excitability and impair inhibitory regulation, thereby increasing the susceptibility of seizure development after stroke.

Emerging evidence further indicates that, in addition to collateral circulation status, acute‐phase therapeutic outcomes—such as futile recanalization following MT and the severity of neurological deficits at 72 h postonset—substantially improve the accuracy of PSE risk prediction [34, 35]. When combined with established predictors such as cortical involvement, acute symptomatic seizures, LAA, and infarction within the MCA territory, these parameters offer a more comprehensive assessment of treatment response and strengthen the discriminatory power of predictive models. These findings highlight the importance of integrating acute treatment effects into PSE risk stratification frameworks, supporting the development of more refined and individualized prediction models that incorporate vascular status, treatment efficacy, and early neurological recovery.

This study had some limitations. First, since this is an observational study, we cannot establish a causal relationship between collateral circulation status and PSE. However, the close association between collateral circulation status and PSE risk factors has been widely validated in previous studies. Our study aimed to assess the impact of collateral circulation status on PSE. Second, due to the retrospective design of this study, we cannot completely exclude residual or unmeasured confounding factors that may affect the effect estimates. Third, selection bias may exist due to a loss of follow‐up data among the enrolled participants. Fourth, the ASITN/SIR scale demonstrates limited interobserver agreement and operator dependency, representing a well‐established limitation of this scoring system. Lastly, we only included patients with stroke who underwent MT, which limits the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

The findings of our study may provide novel insights into PSE prediction. Accurate and early prediction of patients with high‐risk PSE could help reduce the risk of injury or mortality caused by unpredictable seizures in daily life. Further prospective studies are warranted to explore the causal relationship between poor collateral circulation and PSE. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis (EDAS) is well tolerated in adults with moyamoya disease and intracranial atherosclerotic steno‐occlusive disease, improving collateral circulation in high‐risk regions [36]. Future research should investigate whether EDAS could serve as a preventive strategy for PSE in survivors of stroke.
