Tuberculosis-related stigma and associated factors among people with pulmonary tuberculosis predominantly co-infected with HIV in Kampala, Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jonathan Izudi, Simon Kyazze, Francis Bajunirwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Data on TB-related stigma in Uganda are limited.•We studied adults with TB across five TB clinics in Kampala.•Stigma was higher among men and people with poor psychological well-being.•TB programs should integrate mental health to reduce stigma.

Background

In low- and middle-income countries, tuberculosis (TB) is still a major global public health problem. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that in 2024, 10.7 million people fell ill with TB globally, with the African Region accounting for about 24–25% of incident cases [1]. During the same period, an estimated 1.23 million people died from TB worldwide (comprising approximately 1.08 million deaths in people without HIV and 150 000 deaths in people with HIV), with the African Region accounting for around 29% of TB deaths among HIV-negative people and the majority of deaths among people with HIV [1]. Between 2010 and 2024, there has been a declining trend in the TB incidence (approximately 28% reduction), and TB/HIV-related deaths in the African Region [1], but TB-related morbidity and mortality remain significantly high. The TB burden is further exacerbated by social and psychological challenges, including stigma. TB-related stigma negatively impacts the well-being of people with TB (PWTB) and is an important social determinant of health [2]. Stigma contributes to both anticipated and experienced social exclusion, deters individuals from engaging in TB testing and follow-up care [3], contributes to diagnostic delays and treatment nonadherence [4], and negatively impacts the health-related quality of life of PWTB [5]. Additionally, qualitative studies in Uganda have shown that TB-related stigma is a barrier to TB diagnosis [6], [7], treatment initiation [6], [8], contact investigation [9], and preventive therapy initiation [10]. These challenges undermine effective TB control efforts.

Several factors contribute to TB-related stigma. Observational studies have reported age, rural residence, and lack of formal education [11], perceiving TB as a severe disease, lack of knowledge about TB, and the existence of depressive symptoms [11] as factors associated with TB-related stigma. Additionally, previous history of TB treatment and TB and HIV comorbidity [12], higher education levels, and inadequate knowledge about TB have been documented as being associated with TB-related stigma [13]. Although there is some evidence available on TB-related stigma, only a few studies have focused on the topic in Uganda [12], [13], [14]. Moreover, the few studies had limitations such as categorizing TB-related stigma scores instead of analyzing it on a continuous scale [12], thereby reducing statistical power and causing information loss. Also, the relatively smaller sample sizes of most studies [12], [14] limit the generalizability of the findings.

We examined the factors associated with TB-related stigma among individuals aged ≥ 18 years receiving treatment across five TB clinics in Kampala, Uganda. We hypothesized that persons with poor psychological well-being have higher TB-related stigma scores than those with good psychological well-being. Findings contribute to the evidence base on TB-related stigma and may inform the development of targeted stigma-reduction interventions to improve treatment outcomes and the quality of life among PWTB in Uganda and similar settings in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods and materials

Data source and study design

We analyzed data from a parent cross-sectional study that involved PWTB receiving treatment at five primary health facilities in Kampala, Uganda [15]. The study setting is described in our previous studies [16], [17], [18]. In brief, the data were collected between September 02, 2024, and November 29, 2024, to determine the correlates of under- and overnutrition among people with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB. The study population comprised adults aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB receiving a six-month TB treatment—2 months of Isonazid (H), Rifampicin (R), Pyrazinamide (Z), and Ethambutol, followed by RH for 4 months. Participants were consecutively sampled during the study period. The study excluded individuals with extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB), including forms like TB meningitis and skeletal TB, due to their extended treatment duration of 10 months or more. Such prolonged therapy may influence appetite, gastrointestinal function, and nutritional requirements, thereby complicating the consistent measurement of dietary exposures and related outcomes. A total of 818 participants were enrolled, reflecting a 99.5% response rate. The sample size was calculated to detect a clinically relevant difference in the prevalence of malnutrition between PWTB who smoke and those who do not, as previously reported [19]. Data were collected using interviewer-administered structured questionnaires in face-to-face interviews across the clinics. The questionnaire included sections on: 1) socio-demographic characteristics such as age, sex, education, employment, and marital status; 2) clinical factors such as history of TB treatment, and HIV serostatus; 3) social and mental health-related factors such as TB-related stigma, psychological wellbeing, alcohol consumption and smoking; and, 4) nutritional and dietary factors such as food insecurity, dietary diversity, and food access problems.

Variables and measurements: exposure, outcome, and covariates

The primary exposure was psychological well-being, assessed using the World Health Organization (WHO) Five Well-Being Index (WHO-5). The tool measures psychological well-being over the past two weeks. Scores ranged from 0 to 20, with ≥ 15 indicating good psychological well-being, and < 15 indicating poor psychological well-being.

The outcome variable, TB-related stigma, was assessed using an adapted version of the Van Rie TB Stigma Scale [20], [21], comprising 12 patient-perspective items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). Total scores ranged from 0 to 48, with higher scores indicating greater levels of perceived stigma. The Van Rie TB Stigma Scale has been validated in Uganda among PWTB and has been found to have excellent psychometric properties [22]. TB-related stigma was measured as a continuous outcome, as there is no universally agreed-upon cut-off for the Van Rie scale.

Covariates included age categories in years (<25 vs. ≥ 25), level of education, occupation, marital status, religion, type of employment, socioeconomic status (SES), HIV serostatus, smoking status, presence of chronic disease, and food access problems. SES was measured based on 15 binary household asset variables, and based on normalized tertile scores, participants were categorized into low, moderate, and high SES. Age was categorized as < 25 years and ≥ 25 years to distinguish younger adults from older adults, reflecting differences in social maturity, economic independence, and health-seeking behavior. Food access problems were defined as self-reported difficulties in accessing or consuming adequate food during TB treatment.

Statistical methods

The analysis was conducted in R version 4.2.1 (2022–06-23 ucrt). We summarized categorical variables as frequencies and proportions, and numerical data as means and standard deviations. In the bivariate analysis, differences in sex and psychological well-being across independent and outcome variables were assessed using the Chi-square test for tables with all cell counts ≥ 5, and Fisher’s exact test when any cell count was < 5.

For numerical data, the Student t-test was used to assess mean differences, as the data were normally distributed. Findings on psychological well-being differences with independent and outcome variables are also presented. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with robust standard errors, an exchangeable working correlation structure, a Gaussian family, and identity link were used to identify factors independently associated with TB-related stigma. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the unadjusted GEE analysis and those selected a priori based on existing literature and plausible social and biological links with stigma were included in the adjusted (multivariable) GEE model to identify factors independently associated with TB-related stigma. Thus, variable inclusion in the adjusted model was informed by both prior evidence and the unadjusted GEE analysis results. Collinear variables were excluded from the adjusted analysis. Model estimates were reported as regression coefficients (β) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. We did not report p-values associated with the beta-coefficients as they are less informative regarding clinical or statistical importance. Confidence intervals are more appropriate, as they indicate precision, establish statistical significance, and are currently recommended in reporting measures of association [23], [24], [25]. We assessed model diagnostics to evaluate the appropriateness of the GEE model fitted with an exchangeable working correlation structure and Gaussian family for TB-related stigma scores. Diagnostic plots, including residuals vs. fitted values, normal Q-Q plots, scale-location plots, and residuals vs. leverage, were examined.

Ethical issues

Ethical approval was provided by the Clarke International University Research Ethics Committee (CLARKE-2024–1100). Administrative clearance was provided by the Kampala Capital City Authority Directorate of Public Health and Environment (DPHE/KCCA/1301/01). All participants provided written informed consent, and all data were anonymized before analysis.

Factors associated with TB-related stigma in the unadjusted and adjusted analyses among PWTB in Kampala, Uganda

In the unadjusted analysis, TB-related stigma was more likely among individuals aged ≥ 25 years, males, and those with poor psychological well-being (Table 3). After adjusting for other factors (Table 2), we found that poor psychological well-being (adjusted β = 0.86, 95% CI 0.60, 1.13) and being a male (adjusted β = 0.77, 95% CI 0.53, 1.00) were significantly associated with higher TB-related stigma scores, with the association between age and TB-related scores being borderline significant (adjusted β = 0.83, 95% CI 0.11, 1.55). Moderate SES showed some marginally significant negative association with TB-related stigma compared with low SES (adjusted β = -0.79, 95% CI −1.57, −0.02), whereas high SES showed no association (adjusted β = -0.05, 95% CI −0.83, 0.72). Model diagnostics and assumption results

The residuals vs. fitted values plot indicated a random scatter around zero, suggesting that the linearity and homoscedasticity assumptions were reasonably met. The normal Q-Q plot showed that standardized residuals closely followed the theoretical quantile line, indicating approximate normality of residuals. The scale-location plot demonstrated a uniform spread of residuals across fitted values, supporting the assumption of homoscedasticity. The residuals vs. leverage plot revealed no influential observations, as Cook’s distances were low (<0.02), and no points exhibited extreme leverage. Overall, these diagnostics supported the adequacy of the model assumptions for the GEE analysis.

Discussion

This study investigated the factors associated with TB-related stigma among PWTB in Kampala, Uganda. The findings show that poor psychological well-being and male sex are associated with higher TB-related stigma scores among PWTB in Kampala, Uganda. However, persons aged ≥ 25 years had a marginally significant association with higher TB-related stigma. The finding of higher TB-related scores among individuals with poor psychological well-being underscores the potential link between mental health and stigma. This finding aligns with previous literature suggesting that stigma and mental health challenges often co-occur and reinforce each other in a cyclical relationship [26].

The findings also align with those of a cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia, which reported a higher likelihood of experiencing TB-related stigma among individuals with mental health challenges, such as high perceived stress and depression [27]. A study in Armenia also concurs with our findings [28]. TB-related stigma may exacerbate psychological distress, while poor mental well-being can heighten the perception or internalization of stigma [4]. These results emphasize the need for integrated approaches that address both mental health support and stigma reduction in TB control programs.

The study showed sex differences in TB-related stigma, with men experiencing higher scores than women. This result differs from prior studies that have reported higher stigma among women than men in Uganda [14], [29]. The differences may be explained by gender norms, whereby women tend to discuss health issues more openly with peers, while men often exhibit poorer health care–seeking behaviors and are less likely to consult others [30]. Women largely experience household-level stigma, while men primarily face community-level stigma [31]. To resolve the inconsistent associations between TB-related stigma and sex, a meta-analysis that systematically synthesizes findings across studies would help.

We found a borderline significant association between age and TB-related stigma, with PWTB aged ≥ 25 years experiencing higher TB-related stigma than those aged < 25 years. These findings concur with a previous study conducted in Uganda, which showed that individuals aged ≥ 20 years were more likely to experience TB-related stigma [13]. Similarly, a study in Armenia found that TB-related stigma scores increased with age [28]. This finding may reflect age-related differences in social roles and expectations, which could influence how individuals perceive or are affected by stigma. Additionally, it may suggest that stigma reduction interventions may benefit from age-specific strategies that address the unique needs and vulnerabilities of PWTB.

Our finding of a trend toward lower TB-related stigma scores among PWTB of moderate and higher SES, compared with those of lower SES, is consistent with evidence from Ethiopia [32], where individuals in the lowest and third wealth quintiles had nearly twice the likelihood of higher TB stigma scores compared with those in the highest quintile. Taken together, these findings suggest a gradient of declining TB-related stigma with increasing SES.

However, in the present study, the association was weak and did not reach statistical significance, warranting further investigation using larger or more diverse samples to establish a more robust relationship.

Implications of findings

The implications of these findings include underscoring the significance of integrating mental health services into TB care and targeted stigma reduction strategies that are sensitive to sex and age dynamics. Such integrated approaches may help to break the cycle of TB-related stigma and psychological distress, ultimately improving treatment outcomes and the psychological well-being of PWTB [33]. The high prevalence of HIV in our study population suggests a potential for HIV-associated stigma among PWTB. Although we did not directly measure HIV-related stigma, participants with TB/HIV showed a trend toward lower TB-related stigma scores compared with those without HIV. This finding may reflect the positive impact of routine psychosocial support and supportive HIV care. Previous studies have reported that TB-related stigma may be higher than HIV-related stigma in some settings. We restricted our analysis to PWTB to reduce clinical heterogeneity and allow a clearer assessment of stigma patterns within a comparable population.

Study strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include being one of the few to quantitatively examine the correlates of TB-related stigma in an urban population. Previous studies have focused on the influence of TB-related stigma on the uptake of TB screening, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention using a qualitative lens. We used validated tools to measure TB-related stigma and psychological well-being, adding strengths to the study’s internal validity. Limitations include the use of a cross-sectional study design that precludes causal inference, as it was impossible to determine the directionality of observed relationships. Although measured using validated tools, self-reported measures of TB-related stigma and psychological well-being may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. This may have influenced the accuracy of true stigma experienced among PWTB. The study was conducted in an urban setting characterized by different SES and cultural contexts, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other settings, especially rural areas.

Unmeasured confounders such as cultural beliefs, family dynamics, disease severity, and duration of TB symptoms, among others, may have influenced the observed associations but were not analyzed. Also, we did not record the exact TB treatment phase (intensive or continuation) at the time of the interview, which may have influenced participants’ perceptions of TB-related stigma. The Van Rie tool captured only patient-level, and not community-level, perspectives of TB-related stigma. Although our sample size was relatively large, the study’s statistical power in detecting associations between the covariates examined and TB-related stigma remains unknown, as the original study was designed and powered to examine factors associated with malnutrition. Our primary hypothesis is that persons with poor psychological well-being have higher TB-related stigma scores than those with good psychological well-being, but a reverse hypothesis is plausible from both social and biological perspectives. Such associations are better investigated in longitudinal studies rather than cross-sectional studies. Additionally, the high proportion of individuals with HIV in our study population and their unique sociodemographic characteristics (for example, SES and marital status) may limit generalizability to settings with lower HIV prevalence among PWTB. Also, the categorization of age into two groups (<25 years and ≥ 25 years) may oversimplify age-related differences and reduce variability. These limitations should be considered in the interpretation of the study findings.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study showed that poor psychological well-being and being male are associated with higher TB-related stigma scores among PWTB in Kampala, Uganda. PWTB aged ≥ 25 years experienced higher TB-related stigma compared to those younger than 25 years, although the association was only marginally significant. Future research should explore longitudinal designs to examine the temporal relationship between psychological well-being and TB-related stigma. Qualitative studies should be conducted to provide deeper insights into the gendered and age-specific experiences of TB-related stigma to inform tailored interventions. TB control programs should consider integrating mental health services into routine TB care and developing stigma reduction strategies that are sensitive to sex and age differences.
