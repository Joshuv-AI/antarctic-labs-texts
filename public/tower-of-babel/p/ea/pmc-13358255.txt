Transanal irrigation for the management of low anterior resection syndrome (LARS): protocol for a pragmatic, multicentre, crossover randomised controlled trial
Authors: Jessica Holland, Marie Demian, Sarah Sabboobeh, Camila L P Oliveira, Li Tang, Sahir Bhatnagar, A Sender Liberman, Sébastien Drolet, Carl Brown, Caitlin Cahill, Sepehr Khorasani, Terry Zwiep, Carol-Ann Vasilevsky, Nancy Morin, Allison Pang, Richard Garfinkle, Sami A Chadi, Shafic Abdulkarim, Gordon Best, Julio Faria, Gabriela Ghitulescu, Husein Moloo, Louise Samuel, Steven Wexner, Julio F Fiore, Marylise Boutros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdvances in surgical techniques and neoadjuvant care for rectal cancer have increased sphincter preservation, allowing restoration of bowel continuity through low anterior resection (LAR). However, up to 75% of patients experience low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), which is bowel dysfunction characterised by faecal incontinence, frequency, urgency and clustering of bowel movements, significantly impairing quality of life (QoL). Current management is largely empirical, using lifestyle or pharmacological strategies with variable success. Transanal irrigation (TAI) is a non-surgical intervention that allows controlled colonic washouts, improving symptom control. Evidence supporting TAI for LARS is limited by small sample sizes and is primarily observational, highlighting the need for a rigorous randomised controlled trial (RCT). This study aims to compare TAI with conventional LARS care on QoL, bowel function, faecal incontinence and satisfaction in adults with LARS following LAR.Methods and analysisThis is a pragmatic, multicentre, crossover RCT conducted at eight academic hospitals across Quebec, Ontario and British Columbia, Canada. Eligible participants are adults (≥18 years) with an LARS score >20, at least 6 months post-LAR and without an ostomy or active colorectal complications. Participants will be randomised to receive either 3 months of daily TAI or conventional LARS care, followed by a 1 month washout and crossover to the alternate intervention. The TAI intervention includes an irrigation system, access to a web-based educational platform and virtual training with a research team member. Primary outcome is difference in global QoL between interventions, assessed using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30. Secondary outcomes include bowel function (LARS score), faecal incontinence (Cleveland Clinic Faecal Incontinence Score) and satisfaction. Sample size (n=66, accounting for 10% attrition) was determined using Monte Carlo simulations to detect a 6-point difference in QoL with 85.8% power. Data will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models accounting for period, treatment and sequence effects. All study data will be collected via Research Electronic Data Capture and maintained in secure, password-protected systems; participant confidentiality will be ensured throughout. Safety monitoring will include training on proper TAI technique and reporting of adverse events.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received ethics approval from each participating site. The trial adheres to the Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement on the use of human participants in research. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conference presentations and scientific meetings, with authorship following the International Committee of Journal Editors guidelines.Trial registration numberNCT05007015.

Introduction

Advances in surgical techniques and neoadjuvant care for the treatment of rectal cancer have increased sphincter preservation rates, enabling restoration of bowel continuity following an oncological surgery, the low anterior resection (LAR). However, these advances are not without long-term functional consequences for patients.13 Following LAR, 50–75% of patients experience some degree of bowel dysfunction, known as low anterior resection syndrome (LARS). This syndrome refers to a constellation of distressing and disruptive symptoms that include incontinence to stool and gas, frequency, urgency and clustering of bowel movements.4 It has been shown to significantly impair quality of life (QoL) and may persist indefinitely.2 3 5 With the rising incidence of rectal cancer in younger patients, LARS can have a particularly profound impact on patients’ ability to return to work and regain preoperative functional capacity.68

Due to the personal nature of each patient’s symptom cluster,9 the current management of LARS is empirical, guided by self-management using an ad hoc combination of lifestyle and pharmacological strategies, with mixed success.9 10 Several promising interventional strategies have been recently proposed for LARS, including pelvic physiotherapy, sacral neuromodulation and transanal irrigation (TAI).1114 Transanal irrigation has been proposed as a potential treatment for incontinence after anterior resections since 1989.15 It offers a non-surgical approach for managing LARS symptoms, allowing patients to perform controlled colonic washouts at scheduled times. Transanal irrigation systems introduce water into the rectum, facilitating bowel clearance through a combination of mechanical lavage and reflex-mediated stimulation of colonic peristalsis, thereby improving bowel emptying and symptom control.16 17

The current evidence supporting TAI for the management of LARS remains limited, comprising primarily small-scale observational and interventional studies conducted in Europe1826 and Brazil.27 A recent meta-analysis of these studies reported that TAI was both safe and effective in ameliorating LARS symptoms, particularly among patients with a prolonged disease course.28 However, the authors cautioned that these findings should be interpreted carefully, as they predominantly reflect short-term outcomes derived from observational data.28 Despite its limitations, the existing evidence on TAI for LARS collectively supports its potential efficacy and provides a strong rationale for a large-scale randomised controlled trial (RCT) in North America. Therefore, the aim of this pragmatic, multicentre, crossover RCT is to compare TAI with conventional LARS care (control, CON) in adults with LARS who have undergone LAR on QoL, bowel function, faecal incontinence and satisfaction. It is hypothesised that participants assigned to the TAI group will experience better QoL, bowel function, faecal incontinence and satisfaction compared with the ones assigned to the conventional LARS care group. To our knowledge, this study represents both the first North American-based trial and the largest RCT assessing TAI to date. Furthermore, it will be the first to deliver patient education, TAI training and support primarily through a virtual platform, thereby enhancing accessibility for individuals who do not reside near specialised colorectal centres.

Recruitment strategy, enrolment and randomisation

Potential participants will be approached by their colorectal surgeon at one of the designated hospitals during a routine postoperative and/or surveillance visit, occurring at least 6 months after completion of rectal cancer treatment. Participants may also be identified among patients referred to a participating colorectal centre for LARS management. The site research coordinator will subsequently contact eligible participants to provide detailed information regarding the study and to obtain written informed consent (online supplemental appendix 2). Following confirmation of eligibility and enrolment, participants will be randomised using a stratified, blocked randomisation design with computer-generated, permuted blocks of randomly varying sizes (four or six). Randomisation will be stratified by hospital sites to account for potential site-specific differences. An online centralised randomisation system will be employed to maintain allocation concealment. The lead study coordinator will be responsible for both enrolling participants and assigning them to the intervention groups. Blinding of participants and outcome assessors is not feasible due to the nature of the intervention.

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs, ie, global QoL, bowel function and faecal incontinence) and a satisfaction questionnaire (online supplemental appendix 4) will be collected before the start and at the end of each 3-month intervention period (ie, TAI and CON), which is separated by a 1-month washout phase. Intervention and study discontinuation

Transanal irrigation arm

Participants randomised to the TAI intervention arm will undergo a 3-month intervention consisting of daily TAI while continuing conservative management, as per usual care provided by the treating healthcare team. On randomisation, participants will receive a mailed package containing the irrigation device (Coloplast Peristeen anal irrigation system) and instructional materials, as well as access to a web-based educational platform (https://www.larsapp.ca/). The platform, developed with input from a multidisciplinary team, provides educational content to support rectal cancer survivors with LARS, promotes self-management and enhances patient engagement and well-being.30 31 For this study, the application has been modified for TAI-specific use: peer-support tools have been removed, and content now includes videos and written instructions on performing TAI, patient testimonials and a portal for submitting questionnaires to the study team. It is accessible on any internet-enabled device.

Within the first 2 weeks following randomisation, participants will have a virtual training session with a trained research team member. This one-on-one session will review the instructional materials, demonstrate device use and address participants’ questions. Additional support sessions (by phone or in-person) can be scheduled as needed.

Participants will perform daily irrigation using a single-use catheter connected to the irrigation system, prefilled with tap water. An initial irrigation volume of 1000 mL is recommended, which may be adjusted between 500 and 1500 mL according to participants’ tolerance. Catheters are to be disposed of after each use as per manufacturer directions, while the reusable irrigation device is provided for the full 3-month intervention period. Participants will be instructed to irrigate daily or every 1–2 days, following manufacturer guidelines to discard the system after 90 uses. Each session is expected to take approximately 20–90 min, depending on individual tolerance. Participants may adjust the frequency of irrigation based on preference or tolerance; such modifications will not be considered protocol deviations, unless irrigation is discontinued entirely. During the intervention period, an irrigation diary will be completed daily using the web-based educational platform (online supplemental appendix 6).

Conventional LARS care arm

During the control phase, participants will receive an educational LARS booklet previously developed by our research group32 and usual LARS care of their respective hospital. Participants will be expected to continue any previously prescribed management strategies or medications for LARS.

Washout period

A 1-month washout period will be implemented between intervention arms. Minimal contamination between study phases is expected, as the effects of the intervention are limited to the period during which irrigations are performed. Participants will receive the irrigation materials for the 3-month intervention period and no additional materials will be provided to continue the irrigation by the study team. This approach is expected to minimise the risk of participants continuing irrigation during the CON phase.

Study discontinuation

Participants initially randomised to the TAI intervention arm who feel they are benefiting from the treatment and are unwilling to discontinue irrigation during the control phase may exit the study. Those wishing to continue irrigation will be asked to continue documenting their experience through the planned PROMs. All withdrawals, including reasons for withdrawal, will be reported.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimate

Monte Carlo simulations were performed to estimate statistical power. These simulations required specification of the fixed effects (baseline and period effects) and the variability of the data (SDs for both the random effect and the residual error). Parameter estimates were obtained from the literature: a baseline QoL (intercept) of 65, a period effect of 5 points, an SD for the random effect of 5 and an SD for the error term of 10.2 3 35 Using these parameters, 1000 datasets were simulated with a total sample size of 60 patients (6 sites×10 patients per site). The simulations indicated that the study would be able to detect a 6-point difference in QoL with 85.8% power (95% CI 83.48 to 87.91). According to consensus guidelines for the EORTC-QLQ-C30, a 10-point difference in global QoL is considered clinically meaningful for interventions targeting cancer-related QoL.3 36 Therefore, a planned sample size of 60 patients is expected to provide adequate precision. To account for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the final adjusted sample size is 66 patients.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics will be reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, with a complementary per-protocol analysis excluding participants who withdraw or cross over before completing the assigned intervention. Continuous outcomes (eg, global QoL) will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models with treatment (TAI vs CON), period (first vs second phase) and sequence (TAI followed by CON vs CON followed by TAI) as fixed effects and a subject-specific random intercept; carryover will be evaluated via a treatment-by-sequence interaction, and if significant, only first-period data will be analysed using a parallel-group framework. Count outcomes (eg, bowel frequency) will be modelled using mixed-effects Poisson regression with overdispersion assessed; if present, a negative binomial model will be used and zero-inflated variants will be considered if appropriate. Binary outcomes will be analysed using mixed-effects logistic regression with the same fixed and random effects structure. Ordinal outcomes (eg, LARS categories and Likert-type satisfaction) will be analysed using cumulative logit mixed-effects models; the proportional-odds assumption will be checked, with partial proportional-odds or multinomial mixed models used if violated. Model assumptions will be assessed using residual diagnostics and goodness-of-fit; transformations or non-parametric alternatives will be applied as needed. Missing data will be assessed for its mechanism. If less than 5%, a complete-case analysis will be performed; if 5% or more, or if patterns indicate data are missing at random, multiple imputation by chained equations will be applied, with sensitivity analyses comparing imputed and non-imputed results. Two-sided tests will use α=0.05. No interim analyses are planned given the trial’s duration and risk profile. Analyses will be conducted in R V.4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with relevant packages including lme4, glmmTMB, ordinal and mice.

Data management

Before participating in the study, all participants will be informed of the procedures and potential risks involved in the investigation and will be asked to provide written informed consent. Moreover, participants will be informed that all data collected during the study will be kept private, and no personal identifiable information will be shared. Participant confidentiality will be maintained in accordance with the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, the Civil Code of Quebec and other relevant provincial legislation. On enrolment, participants will be assigned a unique study identification number and the key linking participant identities to study data will be stored in a password-protected Excel file, accessible only to the principal investigators. For the purposes of TAI training, participants’ identities will necessarily be known to the research team conducting the intervention, while all other study personnel will access only de-identified data.

Study data will be collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap),37 a secure, web-based electronic data capture platform hosted and maintained by the female Davis Institute at the JGH. Participants who prefer to complete PROMs on paper will be mailed physical copies to complete and return to the lead study team. Electronic data will be stored on a password-protected JGH server, and paper forms will be secured in locked cabinets at the study site, accessible only to authorised research personnel. Each participant will be assigned a unique code number through REDCap, and all study data will be identified using this code to protect participant confidentiality. A separate document linking codes to participant identities will be stored on a password-protected computer on the JGH secure institutional server and will be accessible only to authorised members of the research team. Personal information will be maintained in separate, password-protected files on the same secure server.

In accordance with institutional policy, study data will be retained for 25 years. After 10 years, all data will be anonymised for indefinite storage, and consent forms will be securely shredded using the confidential disposal services provided by the CIUSSS du Centre-Ouest-de-l’Île-de-Montréal. A notification will also be provided to the Research Ethics Board (REB) certifying completion of secure data destruction. If participants decide to discontinue the study, no other information will be collected; however, the data already collected will be kept, unless participants specifically request that it be destroyed. Only the study team will have access to all hard copy and electronic datasets.

Data monitoring and safety

No formal data monitoring committee has been established for this study. Given the short duration of the trial, no interim analyses are planned. The study team will perform regular audits of the collected data to ensure that PROMs are being completed according to protocol; however, no independent audit is planned.

Rectal irrigation via enemas is routinely used during oncological surveillance following an LAR. In this study, however, the intervention involves daily irrigation with larger volumes, which carries a small risk of local trauma or, in rare cases, rectal perforation. No such complications have been reported in patients with LARS in the current literature.152325 27 35 38 Any perforation would be reported immediately as a serious adverse event to the JGH REB. Risk mitigation includes comprehensive participant training on proper technique and selection of a lower-risk population (patients at least 6 months post-surgery). Other potential adverse effects, which will be disclosed to participants, include minor bleeding, abdominal pain, nausea and discomfort during device insertion. These events are typically transient and resolve on discontinuation of treatment.

The irrigation device (Coloplast Peristeen anal irrigation system) was originally designed for faecal incontinence. Its use in patients with LARS has been demonstrated in other studies; however, manufacturer instructions recommend special caution in patients with prior anal or colorectal surgery.232543 In this study, TAI will be performed under medical supervision to ensure patient safety, particularly because alternative treatment options may carry higher risk. Large-scale audits indicate that TAI is a safe procedure, with bowel perforation rates estimated at 2–6 per million procedures.44 Nonetheless, the intervention should always be introduced with experienced healthcare personnel to provide appropriate guidance.

The irrigation procedure requires time commitment from participants. The duration of each session varies, with a reported mean of approximately 45 min (range 20–90 min).27 39 This time investment is expected to be offset by overall reductions in bowel movements and time spent managing LARS symptoms during the rest of the day.

Any incidental findings identified during the study will be disclosed directly to the participant by the principal investigator. With the participant’s consent, these findings will also be communicated to their primary care physician or relevant surgeon for follow-up outside the trial.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved from the inception of the research. The study originated from patient-identified priorities, as patients brought TAI to the attention of the research team as a management strategy of interest, which directly informed the development of the research question and overall study design. Five patient partners contributed to the design and conduct of the study, including the development, review and refinement of patient-facing study materials. Patient partners emphasised the importance of QoL as a primary outcome rather than bowel function alone, which informed the selection of the primary outcome measure. Patient partners also supported study recruitment by sharing information through patient networks, including social media platforms (eg, an LARS Facebook group) and by word-of-mouth. Patients will be involved in the interpretation of study findings and in agreeing dissemination plans, with results to be shared through social media, colorectal cancer support groups and patient communities.
