Suture stent insertion into an over draining tube shunt lumen via single paracentesis
Authors: Sukriti Mohan, Jessie Wang, Jonathan Eisengart, Mary Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glaucoma tube shunts, especially Baerveldt-350s, carry the risk of hypotony-associated complications. In a 2016 letter to the editor by Kuruvilla et al., a technique was described to secondarily stent Baerveldt tube lumen with 3-0 Supramid suture to minimize hypotony-associated complications. Others have further described or demonstrated this technique, which uses two paracenteses, in surgical videos. This case report demonstrates our adaptation of the aforementioned technique to insert a suture stent into an over-draining tube shunt via a single paracentesis. We also describe a case of a patient with severe primary angle glaucoma and a Baerveldt-350 who developed late hypotony with maculopathy and a decline in visual acuity. With this technique, her intraocular pressure rose, maculopathy resolved, and visual acuity improved.

Glaucoma tube shunts carry a risk of early or late hypotony-associated complications, and the risk is higher with the Baerveldt Glaucoma Implant-350 (BGI-350) than the Ahmed FP7 implant.1 With non-valved tubes such as the BGI-350, there are 3 distinct time points at which hypotony-associated complications can occur: (1) during the early postoperative period before spontaneous ligature dissolution; (2) when the ligature spontaneously dissolves between 5 and 6 weeks after surgery; and (3) later on if there is ultimately too much outflow through the tube relative to the eye's aqueous production.2

To prevent hypotony-associated complications during the early postoperative period, surgeons may take steps such as avoiding wicks or blade incision fenestrations.3 To enhance early intraocular pressure (IOP) control prior to ligature dissolution, surgeons can instead perform a concurrent goniotomy in qualifying eyes, in which case the IOP would not be expected to fall below the episcleral venous pressure.4 At the second time-point, a removable relatively thick ripcord suture, such as a 3-0 or 4-0 Prolene (Ethicon, Raritan, NJ), can be placed into the lumen of non-valved tubes at the time of surgery and left in place until after spontaneous ligature dissolution.2 This strategy, which is preferred by 55 % of the 37 % of recently surveyed American Glaucoma Society (AGS) members who routinely use a ripcord suture with non-valved tubes, is hypothesized to blunt the magnitude of sudden IOP-lowering that occurs when the ligature dissolves, thereby minimizing hypotony-associated complications.3 To minimize hypotony-associated complications at the third time point, the aforementioned ripcord can be selectively left in place, possibly indefinitely, to provide partial lumen obstruction in eyes with risk factors for late hypotony, for example in the setting of uveitis or prior cyclodestruction.2 Alternatively, a valved tube shunt can be used instead of a non-valved tube shunt in eyes with risk factors for late hypotony.1

Despite these available measures to reduce the risk of hypotony-associated complications, early or late hypotony-associated complications after tube shunt surgery still occur. In 2016, Kuruvilla et al. described using a 3-0 Supramid suture (S. Jackson Inc. Alexandria, VA, USA) to secondarily stent the tube lumen in two eyes with Baerveldts without ripcords that developed sudden hypotony at the time of spontaneous ligature dissolution.5 This report includes a composite figure with 4 still photos from the surgery demonstrating the technique, which is described as requiring two paracenteses: the 3-0 Supramid suture is inserted into a paracentesis 180° away from the tube using 23-gauge retina asymmetric peeling forceps (Bausch & Lomb Inc.), and the suture is fed into the lumen of the tube through a second paracentesis 60° to the right of the tube using TAN EndoGlide Loading Forceps (Network Medical Products Ltd). The anterior chamber (AC) is maintained with Healon GV during the procedure, which is partially removed at the end of the procedure. In 2019, Iyer et al. described a case series of 4 patients who underwent ab-interno 3-0 Supramid stent placement to manage hypotony-associated complications after tube shunt surgery (two Ahmed FP7s, one Ahmed FP7 followed by subsequent cyclophotocoagulation, and one BGI-350); this report did not include any surgical photos nor videos.6 There are currently two surgical videos available on YouTube.com by Dr. Keith Barton and Dr. Michelle Lim demonstrating this technique to perform ab-interno 3-0 Supramid stent insertion into an over draining tube shunt via two paracenteses,7,8 as described by Kuruvilla et al. in the aforementioned publication. A 2 paracentesis-technique with Prolene suture has also been demonstrated on Youtube. The Prolene suture can be directly docked into the tube bevel via the first paracentesis, but a second paracentesis is made to insert micro-forceps to grasp the suture and feed in into the tube.9

Herein, we demonstrate our adaptation of these technique to insert a suture stent into an over-draining shunt via a single paracentesis. This technique is demonstrated for inserting a 3-0 Supramid suture into a bevel-up anterior chamber tube by an attending glaucoma specialist (MQ).

Our patient is an 83-year-old pseudophakic African American woman with severe stage primary open angle glaucoma in both eyes and a BGI-350 in the left eye 2.5 years prior who developed late hypotony with visual acuity declining from 20/20 to 20/40, IOP of 4 mmHg on no IOP-lowering medications, and hypotony maculopathy, as seen in Fig. 1A. Video 1 and Fig. 2 show the ab-interno insertion of a segment of 3-0 Supramid into the lumen of the tube via a single paracentesis. The tube was inserted into the anterior chamber radially at 12:30 o'clock, so a paracentesis was made radially at 6:30 o'clock. Lidocaine 1 % was injected into the anterior chamber, followed by carbachol to constrict the pupil. The anterior chamber was filled with Healon Pro (Johnson & Johnson Vision, Jacksonville, FL). A segment of 3-0 Supramid was cut so that it would span approximately the distance between the tube tip and the tube-plate junction. The Supramid was inserted into the paracentesis by holding it perpendicularly with tying forceps, and the tip of the Supramid was able to be docked directly into the bevel of the anterior chamber tube. The Supramid was advanced into the tube by grasping it perpendicularly with tying forceps outside of the eye until the entire length of suture was in the eye. The tying forceps were switched to curved MicroSurgical Technology (MST) Micro-Holding Forceps (MicroSurgical Technology, Redmond, WA), and the Supramid was grasped at the trailing tip in an orientation that was parallel to the direction of the suture and tube. The MST forceps holding the trailing tip of the Supramid suture was pushed directly into the paracentesis, across the AC, until it reached the tip of the tube. In conclusion, this report demonstrates a novel adaptation of a previously well-described technique to perform ab-interno insertion of a 3-0 Supramid or 3-0 Prolene stent into the tube lumen of an over-draining tube shunt via a single strategically placed paracentesis. In order to perform this maneuver, the paracentesis should be located directly across from the tube (not necessarily 180° away) and angled in a direction that is parallel to the orientation of the tube. This facilitates being able to dock the tip of the suture directly into the bevel of the tube while grasping the suture on the outside of the eye with standard tying forceps without necessarily needing to stabilize or bend the tube with micro-forceps inside the eye via a second paracentesis. This maneuver to dock a straight suture into a bevel-up anterior chamber tube is similar in principle to a recently published technique to dock a straight guidewire directly into the bevel-up needle that is used to create the sclerotomy for a guidewire-assisted sulcus tube entry.10

This technique can also be performed when the tube is located in the sulcus and bevel-down, but in this scenario, a curved segment of suture should be used so the curved tip of the suture can be used to “hook” into the underside of the bevel-down tube while still holding the suture from outside of the paracentesis which is at a more anterior plane compared to the tip of a sulcus tube. Sutures are usually placked into rectangular packaging and have both straight and curved segments in them when they are first removed from the packaging, so a straight or curved segment can be fashioned depending on whether the intention is to dock it until a bevel-up or bevel-down tube tip, respectively.

Once the suture is in the tube lumen and has been advanced so that no length remains outside of the eye, MST forceps can be used to continue to push the suture straight into the tube. This eliminates torque and distortion at the paracentesis, compared to the alternative technique of using micro-forceps to grasp the suture in a perpendicular orientation in the AC to advance the suture farther into the tube with multiple pinch-and-release movements. This step to push the suture tail across the eye and entirely into the tube lumen can be performed with a variety of reusable multi-use micro-forceps and does not require opening expensive single-use micro-forceps. The viscoelastic chosen to keep the globe pressurized during the procedure should be one that can be rinsed out with balanced salt solution on a syringe and cannula; a bimanual irrigation and aspiration system is not required.

Video 1 was MQ's first attempt at this technique, having seen videos of the original technique with 2 paracenteses on YouTube.com, but having never performed the technique previously. This technique has also been performed by a postgraduate year (PGY)-4 resident and a glaucoma fellow; those videos will not be shown here in the interest of keeping this a single case report. The suture stent may also be removed if needed; Video 2 demonstrates stent removal in a different patient.

In conclusion, the purpose of this report is to demonstrate a novel and efficient adaptation of a surgical technique to insert a suture stent (3-0 Supramid or 3-0 Prolene) into the lumen of an over-draining tube shunt in the anterior chamber or ciliary sulcus via a single paracentesis. This technique has a quick learning curve and can be taught to trainees with basic familiarity with anterior segment surgery. This report does not aim to provide robust clinical outcomes data. Future studies are needed to elucidate the short-term and long-term IOP-raising effects of various types of stent sutures in eyes with over-draining tube shunts.
