AI learning dependence and interpersonal incompetence in EFL contexts: The moderating role of ethical awareness
Authors: Qinqing Zhang, Hung-Wei Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly integrated into English as a Foreign Language (EFL) education, concerns have arisen regarding its potential impact on learners’ social development. This study investigates the relationship between AI learning dependence and interpersonal incompetence among EFL university students, with a particular focus on the moderating role of AI ethical awareness. A survey of 608 non-English major university students was conducted. Using the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) and structural equation modeling (SEM), the research identified factors contributing to AI dependence in EFL learning. Additionally, the impact of AI dependence on learners’ interpersonal incompetence was examined through Media Dependency Theory. The findings indicate that factors such as effort expectancy, performance expectancy, facilitating conditions, and social influence are positively associated with learners’ AI dependence. This dependence, in turn, is positively associated with greater interpersonal incompetence. Furthermore, AI ethical awareness moderates this relationship: the positive association between AI dependence and interpersonal incompetence is weaker among learners with higher ethical awareness. The study confirms the applicability of UTAUT in explaining AI learning dependence and highlights the importance of fostering ethical awareness to mitigate the potential social risks of AI in education. Practical suggestions are also provided to support the sustainable integration of AI in EFL education.Keywords: UTAUT, AI learning dependence, Interpersonal incompetence, Ethical awarenessSubject terms: Education; Information systems and information technology; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), its application in education has become increasingly widespread, providing learners with innovative learning experiences and efficient educational resources1–3. Within the domain of AI in education (AIED), learners now frequently employ AI-driven tools to acquire knowledge and complete learning tasks. Capitalising on its robust capabilities—such as information retrieval and generation, efficient human–computer interaction, and personalised customisation—AI technology has demonstrated significant advantages, particularly in large-class EFL (English as a Foreign Language) teaching contexts. These benefits have contributed to its rapid adoption in such educational settings. Recent empirical studies have begun to systematically examine EFL learners’ engagement with AI technologies. For instance, Wu et al.4, developed and validated the AI-assisted Second Language (L2) Learning Attitude Scale for Chinese college students, establishing significant correlations between AI learning attitudes and L2 proficiency outcomes. Wu et al.5, further investigated the determinants of EFL learners’ behavioral intention to use AI in distributed learning contexts, identifying key factors that shape technology adoption in language education. These studies underscore the growing integration of AI in L2 learning and highlight the need for deeper understanding of its psychological and social implications for learners.

Despite the promising potential of AI in education, the growing ubiquity of AI learning tools may be associated with fewer opportunities for essential interaction and communication practice in social contexts, and this reduction in social practice may, in turn, be linked to learners’ interpersonal incompetence6,7. A pressing challenge in contemporary education lies in balancing the benefits of AI technology with the need to mitigate its potential associations with learners’ social functioning, particularly as manifested in interpersonal incompetence.

Existing research has extensively employed the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) to examine learners’ acceptance and attitudes towards emerging technologies. However, such applications have predominantly focused on initial adoption phases8,9. As AI becomes more deeply embedded in educational contexts, learners’ dependence on these technologies is markedly increasing. Yet, the underlying mechanisms driving this dependency and its consequences for social skill development remain underexplored.

Furthermore, while ethical considerations in AI applications are gaining prominence10,11, research examining ethical awareness within educational settings—particularly EFL contexts—remains nascent. Crucially, the role of learners’ AI ethical awareness in shaping their technology use and social skill development has not been sufficiently investigated.

This study seeks to address these research gaps by employing the UTAUT framework to investigate the formation mechanisms of AI learning dependence and its subsequent impact on learners’ interpersonal incompetence. Specifically, we examine how four key factors – effort expectancy, performance expectancy, facilitating conditions, and social influence – collectively contribute to the development of AI dependence among EFL learners.

Furthermore, the study explores two critical dimensions: (1) the positive association between AI dependence and interpersonal incompetence, and (2) the moderating role of AI ethical awareness in this relationship. Beyond examining these direct associations, this study also seeks to theoretically bridge technical adoption frameworks with socio-psychological perspectives on social development, recognizing that the relationship between AI dependence and interpersonal outcomes likely involves complex mechanisms such as social displacement, emotional substitution, and cognitive adaptation to AI-mediated communication patterns. By incorporating ethical awareness as a moderating variable, this research makes dual contributions: it not only elucidates the risks of AI dependence for social skill development but also provides theoretical foundations for how ethical education might mitigate these negative consequences. The findings offer critical insights for educational practitioners and policymakers by demonstrating how to strategically balance AI’s technological advantages with its ethical challenges, advocating for holistic AI-enhanced EFL pedagogies that integrate—rather than displace—human interaction, and providing a framework for targeted interventions that preserve interpersonal skill development in AI-mediated learning environments.

Literature review

Media dependence

As a primary variable in this study, AI learning dependence is derived from Media Dependence Theory. Originally proposed by Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur12, this theory aims to explain the dynamic relationships among individuals, media, and social systems. It posits that media dependence is a psychological and behavioural attachment that audiences develop towards media to fulfil their informational, emotional, or social needs. The degree of dependence is contingent upon the irreplaceability of media functions in society and the extent to which media facilitate individuals’ goal attainment. With the advancement of information technology, the connotations and applications of Media Dependence Theory have been further developed and expanded. The close and unique relationship between online media and audiences has enhanced the theory’s applicability in digital contexts13. In the era of intelligent media, Media Dependence Theory has been extended to the field of human–computer interaction, examining emotional interactions between humans and chatbots and their impact on human social interactions14,15. As human–AI interaction technologies evolve, new characteristics of media dependence have emerged: on one hand, users obtain emotional compensation through anthropomorphic interactions, such as alleviating loneliness and experiencing companionship16; on the other hand, over-reliance may be associated with the deterioration of real-life social skills and may perpetuate a cycle of “dependence–loneliness”17. Currently, scholars have begun to pay closer attention to learners’ dependence on AI technologies in AIED contexts and have raised concerns about potential declines in cognitive skills and student motivation18.

Building on Media Dependence Theory, this study conceptualizes AI learning dependence as a multidimensional psychological state defined by three interrelated characteristics: (a) perceived indispensability, where learners view AI tools as essential for successful learning outcomes; (b) affective withdrawal, characterized by negative emotional states (e.g., boredom, discomfort) when AI tools are inaccessible; and (c) behavioral substitution, whereby learners habitually turn to AI for problem-solving or task completion instead of utilizing their own cognitive resources or seeking human assistance. This conceptualization distinguishes dependence as a psychological attachment from mere routine use or preference, emphasizing a perceived lack of viable alternatives.

This multidimensional conceptualization of AI learning dependence aligns with emerging empirical research on learners’ psychological engagement with AI in language learning contexts. For instance, Wu and Wang (2025)explored Chinese college students’ flow experiences in generative AI-assisted informal digital learning of English, revealing how self-determination theory constructs—particularly autonomy and competence—shape learners’ optimal experiences with AI tools. Their findings suggest that the quality of learners’ psychological engagement with AI may influence not only learning outcomes but also the depth of their reliance on these technologies. Furthermore, Wu et al.19, developed and validated a GenAI competence scale for pre-service second language teachers, distinguishing AI-specific competence from general digital competence. This distinction is pertinent to the present study, as it implies that learners’ capacity to navigate AI technologies—and by extension, their potential to develop dependence—involves unique cognitive and social dimensions that warrant investigation. Together, these studies underscore the complexity of human-AI interaction in educational settings and provide empirical grounding for examining how AI dependence may extend beyond the learning context to affect learners’ interpersonal functioning.

Interpersonal incompetence

Interpersonal incompetence refers to an individual’s deficiency in establishing and maintaining healthy relationships within social interactions20. As Spitzberg21 articulated in his foundational work on interpersonal competence and loneliness, incompetence manifests as impaired empathic and role-taking abilities, often resulting in social withdrawal and isolation. Unlike the positive framing of interpersonal competence22, the construct of incompetence focuses on the absence or deficit of these critical social skills.

The development of interpersonal incompetence is influenced by a variety of factors. During late adolescence and early adulthood, individuals who lack opportunities for meaningful peer interactions—such as self-disclosure and conflict resolution—may develop patterns of interpersonal incompetence23. External environments and modes of social interaction are also considered important factors influencing interpersonal incompetence24,25.

With the advancement of technology, the widespread use of electronic devices has had a profound impact on interpersonal incompetence. Research by Sunhee et al.26, has shown that over-reliance on smartphones is positively associated with real-life interpersonal incompetence. In the context of AI applications, scholars have expressed concerns that highly efficient and convenient human-AI interactions may be associated with users’ interpersonal incompetence, potentially due to fewer opportunities for authentic social practice27. However, research in this area is still in its infancy, and studies on learners’ interpersonal incompetence in AIED contexts are particularly scarce28.

AI ethical awareness

Ethical awareness, a pivotal element of ethical sensitivity, denotes an individual’s capacity to identify potential ethical issues within a particular context29. It encompasses both cognitive and affective dimensions, such as perspective-taking and emotional contagion, with cognitive empathy playing a more pronounced role in ethical sensitivity30,31. Ethical awareness is crucial for making morally sound decisions in professional and academic settings, aiding individuals in recognising and addressing ethical dilemmas32,33. Moreover, it influences behaviour and decision-making processes, thereby contributing to more ethical outcomes34.

With the proliferation of digital technologies and the rapid advancement of AI, the scope of ethical awareness has expanded beyond traditional ethical considerations to include digital ethical awareness and AI ethical awareness. In the context of e-learning, numerous scholars have called for the integration of ethics education and the cultivation of digital ethical awareness within teaching practices35,36. The ethical dimensions of AI use in language education have become increasingly salient as AI technologies proliferate. (Wu et al.419,; series of studies on AI attitudes, competence, and engagement in L2 contexts implicitly raise ethical considerations regarding appropriate AI use, learner autonomy, and the boundaries between human and machine interaction in language learning. While these studies primarily focus on learning outcomes and technology acceptance, they provide a foundation for investigating how learners’ awareness of AI’s ethical implications may shape their patterns of AI use and, consequently, their social development. However, compared to traditional ethical awareness, research on digital ethical awareness, particularly AI ethical awareness, remains relatively underdeveloped37,38. In the domain of AI-enhanced EFL education, research on this topic is virtually rare.

UTAUT

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), proposed by Venkatesh et al.39,, serves as a comprehensive theoretical framework that integrates and simplifies various prior technology acceptance models (e.g., TAM, TPB) to more effectively explain and predict individual behaviour regarding the acceptance and use of information technology. The core of UTAUT comprises four key constructs: Performance Expectancy (the degree to which an individual believes that using the technology will improve job performance), Effort Expectancy (the degree of ease associated with the use of the technology), Social Influence (the extent to which significant others believe the individual should use the new technology), and Facilitating Conditions (the degree to which an individual believes that an organisational and technical infrastructure exists to support the use of the technology). These constructs influence Behavioural Intention to use the technology, which in turn affects Use Behaviour.

UTAUT has been widely applied across various domains, including e-commerce and educational technology, and is often extended to include additional variables such as trust and risk40. The inclusion of moderators such as age, gender, experience, and voluntariness further refines the model’s applicability and explanatory power. Since its inception, UTAUT has been extensively utilised to analyse individuals’ attitudes and acceptance levels towards technology due to its robust explanatory capacity.

In the context of the rapid development of AI in education, several scholars, including Venkatesh, have employed UTAUT to investigate learners’ willingness to adopt AI technologies41,42. However, research examining potential technology dependence among learners in AIED contexts from a UTAUT perspective remains limited.

The present study and hypotheses

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology provide a robust theoretical framework for examining individuals’ technological adoption intentions and usage behaviours. Recent scholarly work has increasingly employed UTAUT to analyse technology dependence phenomena in educational settings. Almashyakhi and Abozandah’s43study of Saudi teachers during the COVID-19 pandemic established that performance expectancy (PE), effort expectancy (EE), facilitating conditions (FC), and social influence (SI) not only predict learning management system (LMS) acceptance but also demonstrate significant positive correlations with technological dependence. Complementary findings by Dosaya et al.44, from their investigation of LMS adoption at an Indian engineering college further substantiate that technology dependence is shaped by key factors including perceived usability and peer/senior influence. Based on the above analysis, the following hypotheses are proposed in this study:

H1: EFL learners’ effort expectancy positively influences their AI learning dependence.

H2: EFL learners’ performance expectancy positively influences their AI learning dependence.

H3: The facilitating conditions that EFL learners face positively influence their AI learning dependence.

H4: The social influence that EFL learners perceive positively influences their AI learning dependence.

Drawing upon Media Dependence Theory, this study posits that technological reliance is positively associated with users’ interpersonal incompetence—a proposition empirically substantiated across multiple studies45–47. Within educational contexts, this dependence is conceptualized as involving a dual mechanism: (1) progressive displacement of human interaction needs, and (2) consequent attrition of social skills that may manifest as, or contribute to, interpersonal incompetence. Yuan et al.15, research provides empirical evidence consistent with this phenomenon, showing that excessive dependence on AI-mediated communication is significantly associated with deficits in learners’ emotional reciprocity and social adaptation. Based on the above analysis, the following hypotheses are proposed in this study:

H5: EFL learners’ AI learning dependence positively influences their interpersonal incompetence.

H6: EFL learner’ AI learning dependence positively mediates the relationship between EFL learners’ effort expectancy and their interpersonal incompetence.

H7: EFL learners’ AI learning dependence positively mediates the relationship between EFL learners’ performance expectancy and their interpersonal incompetence.

H8: EFL learners’ AI learning dependence positively mediates the relationship between EFL learners’ facilitating conditions and their interpersonal incompetence.

H9: EFL learners’ AI learning dependence positively mediates the relationship between EFL learners’ perceived social influences and their interpersonal incompetence.

Ethical awareness is inherently individualised, exhibiting substantial variability across individuals, which explains its frequent examination as a moderating variable in empirical research. Within business management studies, scholars have extensively investigated the mediating and moderating effects of ethical awareness, particularly regarding its associations with employee performance and organisational outcomes48,49. As AI technologies become increasingly prevalent in educational contexts, researchers have begun exploring the moderating role of learners’ AI ethical awareness in their learning behaviours. For instance, Zhu et al.50, demonstrated that university students with heightened ethical awareness in AI-enhanced learning environments tend to adopt more cautious approaches to AI technology utilisation while maintaining greater vigilance towards potential risks50. Based on the above findings, the following hypotheses are proposed in this study:

H10: EFL learners’ AI ethical awareness negatively moderates the relationship between AI learning dependence and interpersonal incompetence.

H11: EFL learners’ AI ethical awareness negatively moderates the mediated path of EE → AD → PI.

H12: EFL learners’ AI ethical awareness negatively moderates the mediated path of PE → AD → PI.

H13: EFL learners’ AI ethical awareness negatively moderates the mediated path of FC → AD → PI.

H14: EFL learners’ AI ethical awareness negatively moderates the mediated path of SI → AD → PI.

Method

Participants and procedure

The study sample comprised 608 non-English major undergraduate students from five universities in central and western China, where AI technology has been systematically integrated into foreign language instruction. These institutions employ intelligent teaching platforms that combine with personal mobile applications to facilitate English language learning. All participants were admitted to their universities through the National College Entrance Examination (Gaokao) and had completed at least nine years of formal English language education prior to university enrollment. This educational background indicates an intermediate to upper-intermediate proficiency level commensurate with their year of study. Students routinely utilise AI-powered features, including speech recognition for pronunciation correction, intelligent dictionaries, machine translation, information retrieval, voice-to-text transcription, and automated assessment tools. These technologies support various language learning activities (speaking, listening, reading, writing) while generating personalised learning analytics and individualised learning pathways. The participant pool included 382 female (62.8%) and 226 male (37.2%) students. Complete demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

This study intended to investigate EFL learners’ psychological dependence on AI technology in intelligent classroom environments, its potential impact on interpersonal communication, and the moderating role of AI ethical awareness. This research adhered to rigorous ethical standards. The study protocol received review and approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the School of Foreign Languages, Huainan Normal University (Approval Number: HNNUSFL202506190009). This formal approval was secured prior to the initiation of any research-related activities. The approval comprehensively covered all study components, including participant recruitment protocols, the informed consent procedure, and the data collection methodology (specifically, the survey). All aspects of the research were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines of the approving institution and in accordance with the principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Data were collected through questionnaires distributed by AI-utilising foreign language instructors over a four-week period. Participants received a full explanation of the study purpose, confidentiality assurances, and voluntary participation terms before completing the 30-minute survey. To express gratitude for their time, participants received a small commemorative keychain of nominal value (approximately $1 USD), which was deemed non-coercive and appropriate for voluntary research participation. From the initial 650 distributed questionnaires, 630 were returned (96.9% response rate). After excluding incomplete, inconsistent, or improperly completed questionnaires through rigorous quality control procedures, 608 valid responses were retained.

To provide a more concrete behavioral context for AI learning dependence, participants were asked to report their typical usage of AI tools for EFL learning. On average, students reported using AI-powered language learning tools for approximately 2.8 h per week (SD = 1.6), with usage ranging from less than 1 h to over 6 h weekly. The most frequently used platforms included Kimi (reported by 78.3% of participants) for writing assistance and idea generation, Doubao and other machine translation tools (85.7%) for translation and comprehension support, and Grammarly and Quillbot (52.4%) for writing revision and grammar checking. Other mentioned tools included speech recognition applications for pronunciation practice (34.2%) and AI-powered dictionary apps (67.8%). These descriptive data suggest that AI tools are extensively integrated into participants’ EFL learning routines, providing a behavioral foundation for the psychological construct of AI learning dependence examined in this study.

Instruments

To ensure the reliability and validity of the questionnaire, this study employed scales for the relevant variables that have been widely used in mainstream international journals. Participants’ responses to the relevant items were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates “strongly disagree” and 5 indicates “strongly agree”.

The study adapted the AI-assisted teaching scale designed by An et al.51, to measure foreign language learners’ Performance Expectancy, Effort Expectancy, Social Influence, and Facilitating Conditions. Specifically, Performance Expectancy comprises four items (e.g., “AI is very helpful for my EFL learning”), with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.882. Effort Expectancy includes four items (e.g., “AI learning systems are easy to operate for me”), with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.859. Facilitating Conditions consists of four items (e.g., “There are convenient conditions for me to use AI in EFL learning”), with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.839. Social Influence includes three items (e.g., “Students around me who are good at using AI will have more respect”), with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.842. It is important to note that the UTAUT framework was originally developed to explain technology acceptance and usage intention39. Adapting this framework to measure factors contributing to AI learning dependence represents a conceptual extension of the original model. To ensure the validity of this adaptation, we conducted rigorous psychometric assessments. As reported in Table 3, confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated excellent fit for the seven-factor model (RMSEA = 0.034, CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.973). All factor loadings for the UTAUT constructs (EE, PE, FC, SI) exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, ranging from 0.732 to 0.832. Composite reliability values ranged from 0.839 to 0.882, and average variance extracted values ranged from 0.572 to 0.651, all exceeding minimum acceptable thresholds(see Table 2)52,53.

To assess AI learning dependence, this study adapted the media dependence scale originally developed by Yuan et al.15, for AI-enhanced educational contexts. The modified scale comprises six items measuring learners’ reliance on AI technologies (e.g., “If I do not use AI in EFL learning, I would find the learning process dull and uninteresting”), demonstrating excellent internal consistency (α = 0.915).

To assess interpersonal incompetence., we employed Yuan et al.,’s15 validated scale comprising five items (e.g., “In real interpersonal communication, I often find it difficult to integrate into the group and often feel lonely”). The scale demonstrated high reliability in our study (α = 0.899), confirming its psychometric adequacy.

For measuring AI ethical awareness, we adopted Zhu et al.50, six-item scale, which evaluates learners’ understanding of ethical considerations in AI-enhanced education (e.g., “I realize that paying attention to the ethics of AI can bring many benefits and avoid potential risks associated with using generative AI products”). The instrument showed exceptional internal consistency (α = 0.945) in our sample.

All items in this study demonstrated factor loadings substantially exceeding the recommended cutoff of 0.7053. Furthermore, alpha and CR values for all constructs exceeded 0.70 (see Table 2), suggesting strong internal consistency reliability53.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using SPSS version 26.0 and Mplus version 8.3. Preliminary descriptive and correlational analyses were conducted to provide a summary of the data. Following this, confirmatory factor analysis was employed to validate the measurement model. Structural equation modelling was then utilised to evaluate the model fit and to examine the relationships through regression and mediation analyses. Finally, this study employed bootstrap analysis to examine the moderation effect and moderated mediation effects within the model.

Results

Test for common method variance

To minimize the potential for common method bias, several procedural remedies were implemented during data collection54. First, all participants were assured of complete anonymity and confidentiality to reduce evaluation apprehension and social desirability bias. Second, the questionnaire items were randomized to prevent systematic response patterns. Third, participants were explicitly informed that there were no right or wrong answers and were encouraged to respond honestly. Fourth, the survey was conducted in a calm classroom environment with sufficient time (approximately 30 min) to complete all measures, reducing time pressure and fatigue effects.

To evaluate potential common method bias, this study employed Harman’s single-factor test54, conducting maximum likelihood factor extraction on all measured constructs. The analysis revealed that a single factor accounted for only 32.06% of the total variance, which falls substantially below the 40% threshold recommended by Hair et al.55, as indicative of significant common method variance. Consequently, common method bias was not considered a substantive issue in the current investigation.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed using Mplus 8.3 with maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. The five-point Likert scale items were treated as continuous variables, which is common practice in SEM when the number of response categories is five or more56. The hypothesised seven-factor model (M1), specifying that each construct was measured by its respective indicators, demonstrated superior model fit compared to alternative configurations. As presented in Table 3, the single-factor model showed unacceptable fit indices (RMSEA = 0.159, TLI = 0.391, CFI = 0.431). In contrast, the seven-factor solution exhibited excellent fit to the data (RMSEA = 0.034, TLI = 0.973, CFI = 0.976), with all indices surpassing established acceptability thresholds. These results provide strong evidence for the discriminant validity of the seven measured constructs in our study.

To provide a more rigorous assessment of discriminant validity, we additionally examined the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations57. As shown in Table 4, all HTMT values were below the more lenient threshold of 0.9058, ranging from 0.212 to 0.885. While the highest value (0.885 between EE and FC) slightly exceeds the conservative threshold of 0.8557, it remains well below the 0.90 cutoff, indicating adequate discriminant validity. These results further confirm that the seven constructs are empirically distinct.

This study also examined descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations) and bivariate correlations among the seven key constructs (EE, PE, FC, SI, AD, EA, PI) in AI-enhanced EFL learning contexts. Mediation effect analysis

This study utilised the Bootstrap method, employing 5,000 bootstrap samples and a 95% confidence interval (CI), to evaluate the significance of the mediating effects. The determination of path significance via the Bootstrap method hinges on whether the 95% CI encompasses zero. A CI that includes zero suggests an insignificant path, whereas a CI that excludes zero indicates a significant path. The empirical results are detailed in Table 7.

Examination of moderation effects and moderated mediation effects

The structural equation model analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between EFL learners’ AD and EA on PI (β = −0.099, p = 0.005). This finding suggests that AI ethical awareness acts as a negative moderator, mitigating the positive relationship between AI learning dependence and interpersonal incompetence.

To further examine this moderating effect, a simple slope analysis was conducted by plotting values one standard deviation above and below the mean of AI ethical awareness (see Fig. 3). The results demonstrated that the positive impact of AI learning dependence on interpersonal incompetence was weaker among learners with higher AI ethical awareness (β = 0.271, p < 0.001) compared to those with lower awareness (β = 0.468, p < 0.001). This pattern indicates that AI ethical awareness functions as a protective factor, buffering the adverse effect of excessive AI reliance on learners’ social functioning. Consequently, Hypothesis H10 was supported.

Discussion

Firstly, in accordance with the UTAUT theory, EE, PE, FC, and SI are critical determinants of learners’ attitudes towards educational technologies in technology-enabled educational contexts. This study further identifies a positive relationship between these factors and AI learning dependence. Previous studies have also examined user technology dependence from the UTAUT perspective. For instance, Izhar et al.60, argued that effort expectancy may lead to over-reliance on AI technology among researchers and even result in plagiarism. In an empirical study of live streaming service users in India, Singh et al., (2021) confirmed that performance expectancy not only predicts users’ intention to use but also has a positive causal relationship with users’ addictive behaviour. Additionally, facilitating conditions have been identified as a cause of technology dependence in prior research61. For example, Cao et al.62, found that access to technical support and other facilitating conditions are crucial in driving users’ media dependence. Moreover, numerous studies have demonstrated that perceived social influences can lead to excessive dependence on social media, mobile phones, and other communication technologies4,63.

In practical teaching scenarios, intelligent tutoring systems enhance EE by precisely delivering targeted exercises and videos based on individual progress, eliminating the need for manual material searching and increasing system usability. This ease of use encourages regular system utilisation and reliance on AI for problem-solving, fostering dependency. Meanwhile, these systems boost performance expectancy by identifying knowledge gaps and providing targeted plans to efficiently improve academic performance. As students become accustomed to convenient resource access, they may develop a dependency on the platform, viewing it as essential for high-quality learning experiences. The simple operation and user-friendly interfaces of AI educational tools facilitate facilitating conditions, making it easy for learners to adopt and use these tools. Prolonged exposure to such convenient environments may gradually foster dependency and heighten trust in AI technology, leading learners to become overly reliant on AI due to extensive technical support and potentially neglecting their own capability development. In the context of AI-empowered foreign language learning, the frequent and high dependency on AI technology by teachers and peers serves as a behavioural model, influencing social influence. This exposure may lead learners to perceive AI use as “mainstream” or “advanced”, prompting them to overuse AI technology to conform to social norms and expectations.

Secondly, this study finds that dependency on AI learning is positively associated with learners’ interpersonal incompetence. Previous research has begun to examine the potential ethical risks associated with AIED, particularly its negative associations with learners’ social roles and social skills. For instance, Xie et al.64, have argued for the necessity of interventions to better leverage the positive influence of AIED while simultaneously promoting the development of social adaptability64. Tutton and Cohen65, have also cautioned that over-reliance on Intelligent Computer-Assisted Language Learning may lead to a reduction in learners’ interpersonal exchanges65. Additionally, concerns have been raised regarding the excessive use of Large Language Models (LLMs) and generative AI, which may give rise to interpersonal issues66.

While the UTAUT framework provides valuable insights into the antecedents of AI learning dependence, it is important to acknowledge that the pathway from AI dependence to interpersonal incompetence involves complex socio-psychological mechanisms that extend beyond technical adoption processes. The erosion of social skills in AI-dependent learners may be better understood through complementary theoretical lenses. First, social displacement theory suggests that time spent interacting with AI systems displaces opportunities for authentic human interaction, thereby reducing practice in essential social skills such as turn-taking, emotional reciprocity, and conflict resolution15,67. Second, emotional substitution may occur when learners turn to AI for companionship or emotional support—functions traditionally fulfilled by human relationships—potentially diminishing their motivation to seek out and maintain real-world social connections16. Third, cognitive adaptation to AI-mediated communication patterns, characterized by predictability and reduced ambiguity, may impair learners’ tolerance for the inherent uncertainty and complexity of human interaction68. These mechanisms collectively suggest that AI dependence does not merely correlate with interpersonal incompetence through simple displacement but fundamentally alters learners’ social expectations, emotional regulation strategies, and cognitive frameworks for interpreting social cues. Future research should directly examine these mediating mechanisms to build a more comprehensive model of how AI engagement shapes social development in educational contexts.

The positive association between AI dependence and interpersonal incompetence, and by extension its inverse relationship with interpersonal competence, carries particular significance in the EFL context, where language learning is inherently a social and communicative endeavor. Unlike content-based subjects where knowledge can be acquired individually, second language acquisition fundamentally depends on authentic interpersonal interaction for the development of communicative competence69–71. When EFL learners become excessively dependent on AI tools—particularly for tasks that traditionally required human interaction—several language-specific consequences may ensue. First, over-reliance on AI translation tools (e.g., DeepL, Google Translate) may circumvent the process of interlanguage development—the transitional linguistic system that learners construct as they progress toward target language competence. This short-circuiting of productive struggle deprives learners of opportunities to negotiate meaning, receive feedback, and modify their output in response to interlocutor cues—processes essential for developing spontaneous communicative ability72. Second, dependence on AI for conversation practice may reduce learners’ exposure to the unpredictability and variability of authentic target language interaction. Human interlocutors introduce hesitations, repairs, implicatures, and cultural references that AI systems, despite advances in natural language processing, cannot fully replicate. Learners who primarily interact with AI may develop rigid, formulaic language patterns that fail to prepare them for the fluid, context-dependent nature of real-world communication in the target language. Third, AI dependence may diminish learners’ willingness to communicate73 in English by providing a low-anxiety alternative to potentially face-threatening interactions with human speakers. While this avoidance may offer short-term comfort, it ultimately deprives learners of the authentic practice needed to build communicative confidence and competence. These language-specific mechanisms suggest that the interpersonal costs of AI dependence are not merely general social deficits but specifically manifest as impairments in target language communicative ability—the very competence EFL education seeks to cultivate.

While EE, PE, FC, and SI are not directly associated with interpersonal incompetence, this study examines the mediating role of AD in the relationships between these factors and this outcome. It empirically illustrates how the current favourable conditions supporting AI-based education may be negatively associated with the development of learners’ social skills and their ability to fulfil social roles, potentially leading to greater interpersonal incompetence. In AI-assisted educational contexts, the responses learners receive from intelligent systems are based on predefined algorithms and models, lacking the emotional, tonal, and expressive elements present in human interactions. Prolonged exposure to this singular mode of interaction may be associated with challenges in learners’ adaptation to the complexity and uncertainty of real-life social interactions. Additionally, becoming accustomed to simplified communication patterns with AI may be associated with difficulties in learners’ capacity to accurately interpret others’ intentions and to express their own thoughts and emotions clearly and comprehensively in complex interpersonal communications.

Ultimately, this study demonstrates that learners’ AI ethical awareness negatively moderates the positive association between AI learning dependence and interpersonal incompetence and exerts a negative moderating effect on the mediated paths of EE → AD → PI, PE → AD → PI, FC → AD → PI, and SI → AD → PI. To understand how this moderating effect operates, it is useful to conceptualize ethical awareness as a cognitive buffer that promotes mindful rather than mindless AI engagement. Learners with high ethical awareness are more likely to engage in metacognitive reflection about their AI use—questioning not only what AI can do for them, but also what over-reliance on AI might do to them. This reflective stance preserves learner agency in at least three ways. First, ethically aware learners maintain a clearer distinction between AI as a tool and AI as a substitute for human cognition and social interaction, resisting the tendency to offload social-communicative tasks entirely to technology. Second, ethical awareness prompts learners to set intentional boundaries around AI use—for example, using AI for initial idea generation but insisting on independent formulation of final written output, or using AI for vocabulary lookup while preserving authentic conversational practice with peers. Third, ethical awareness fosters critical evaluation of AI-generated content, which may translate into more critical evaluation of one’s own communicative performance and greater motivation to develop authentic interpersonal skills. In essence, ethical awareness transforms AI engagement from a passive, habitual dependency into an active, reflective practice—one that leverages AI’s capabilities while safeguarding the learner’s investment in human social connection. Previous research has also empirically demonstrated and emphasized the significant role of ethical awareness in online learning and AI-enhanced learning contexts74,75. In the context of AI-empowered foreign language learning, learners with a higher level of AI ethical awareness recognize AI’s auxiliary role in learning. They reflect on their interactions with AI and its impact on their own capabilities, thereby moderating their use of AI in terms of time and method. These learners actively seek collaborative and communicative opportunities with others, thus avoiding reduced interpersonal interactions due to over-reliance on AI. This approach may support the development of communication skills and emotional intelligence, potentially offsetting deficits in interpersonal competence that may be related to AI dependency.

Conclusion

This study investigates the effects of EE, PE, FC, and SI on AD among EFL learners in AIED contexts, based on UTAUT. The results indicate that EE, PE, FC, and SI are significantly and positively associated with AD, which in turn is significantly positively associated with PI. EA serves as a negative moderator, weakening the positive relationship between AD and interpersonal incompetence. EA also moderates the indirect effects of EE, PE, FC, and SI on PI through AD. These findings elucidate the intricate dynamics between AI learning dependency, technology acceptance, and social skill development, underscoring the pivotal role of ethical awareness in AI-assisted learning environments.

By integrating the UTAUT framework into AI-assisted foreign language learning, this study extends the theoretical application of UTAUT to emerging educational technologies. Furthermore, it empirically supports the critical moderating role of learners’ AI ethical awareness, advancing scholarly understanding of ethical considerations in AI-driven education.

From a practical standpoint, the findings offer actionable insights for EFL educators. Conceptualizing ethical awareness as a cognitive buffer suggests that cultivating mindful AI engagement should be an explicit pedagogical goal. Rather than simply exhorting students to “use AI ethically,” educators can design reflection-based tasks where learners compare AI-generated output with their own formulations, heightening awareness of AI’s role as a tool rather than a substitute for independent thinking. Structured peer interaction following AI-assisted tasks ensures that AI serves as preparation for, rather than replacement of, authentic human communication. Additionally, boundary-setting activities where students intentionally define when and how they will use AI can transform AI engagement from passive habit into active, reflective practice. Given the language-specific mechanisms identified—particularly regarding interlanguage development and willingness to communicate—EFL educators should be especially attentive to how AI use may impact target language social interaction, providing structured opportunities for unassisted production in low-anxiety settings. These strategies collectively support the dual goals of language proficiency and the mitigation of interpersonal incompetence, thereby fostering healthier social development in technology-enhanced EFL environments.

This study has several limitations. First, while all participants had completed at least nine years of formal English education and were enrolled in university-level EFL courses—suggesting an intermediate to upper-intermediate proficiency level—their English proficiency was not formally assessed through standardized tests. As learners with different proficiency levels may rely on AI tools for varying purposes and with different intensities4, the absence of measured proficiency data represents a limitation. Future research should incorporate objective measures of English proficiency to examine its potential role as a covariate in the relationship between AI dependence and interpersonal outcomes. Second, the study examined only key variables (EE, PE, FC, SI, and AD), without accounting for learners’ emotional factors or AI-related technical aspects. Future research should consider incorporating individual differences (e.g., motivation, self-efficacy, learning styles) and AI-specific technical factors (e.g., functionality, interactive design) to enrich the theoretical framework. Finally, while Harman’s single-factor test suggested that common method bias was not a major concern in this study (with a single factor accounting for 32.06% of total variance), it should be acknowledged that this test has recognized limitations as a diagnostic tool54,76–79. Although procedural remedies—such as anonymity assurance, item randomization, and reduced evaluation apprehension—were implemented during data collection to mitigate potential bias, future research could employ more rigorous statistical remedies, such as the unmeasured latent method factor approach or marker variable techniques, to further validate the findings.
