More than a medication: The lived meaning and value of long-acting injectable buprenorphine
Authors: SR Donaldson, JF Dillon, A Radley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople who use substances often experience stigma in healthcare settings. Opioid agonist treatment (OAT), while effective, is criticised for rigid structures that limit autonomy and reinforce stigma. Long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB) offers an alternative that may reduce these barriers. This study explores the perceived meaning and value of LAIB in supporting recovery.MethodsA longitudinal qualitative study was conducted in community drug treatment services in Tayside, Scotland. Semi-structured interviews were undertaken with 47 patients (LAIB: n = 29; traditional OAT: n = 18) and 9 clinicians. Patient participants were invited to up to three interviews over 18 months. Data were analysed using framework analysis informed by the Social Identity Model of Recovery, recovery capital, and locus of control.ResultsFour themes emerged: (i) avoiding unwanted contacts, (ii) pursuing a “normal life,” (iii) planning for the future, and (iv) service-related challenges. LAIB reduced exposure to stigmatising environments and supported autonomy, enabling participants to engage in employment, family life, and travel. Many described renewed hope and proactive future planning. However, reduced service contact and administrative pressures limited holistic, person-centred care.ConclusionsLAIB can enhance recovery by reducing stigma and increasing autonomy, but its benefits depend on service delivery models. To realise its potential, implementation must prioritise continuity of care and psychosocial support alongside pharmacological treatment.Keywords: Long-acting injectable buprenorphine, opioid agonist treatment, recovery, stigma, qualitative research

Introduction

A substantial body of literature describes stigma and marginalisation experienced by people who use substances, both in the wider community and within healthcare settings (Biancarelli et al., 2019, Earnshaw et al., 2013, Radley et al., 2017, Simon et al., 2020, van Boekel et al., 2013). People who use substances are often perceived to live outside of accepted social norms. A consequence of this is that they are viewed as having a tainted identity and treated as second class citizens (Treloar et al., 2013). Such marginalisation can erode agency, limit choice and restrict social inclusion (Donaldson et al., 2023). The structured and often rigid nature of supervised Opioid Agonist Treatment (OAT) can reinforce perceptions of control, influencing the social, psychological and physiological dimensions of treatment relationships (Bilodeau and Potvin, 2018, Duff, 2013). Visible routines associated OAT, most notably the daily presence in the “methadone queue” at community pharmacies have been critiqued as stigmatising. Such practices may hinder identity transformation and obstruct pathways to recovery by publicly marking individuals as patients, thereby perpetuating social exclusion (Alexandrescu, 2017, Fraser, 2006, Fraser and Valentine, 2008). Although OAT is clinically effective, it is often criticised for its perceived emphasis on normalisation and social control, and many patients report limited improvements in their broader social and personal circumstances (Mayock and Butler, 2021, Moran et al., 2018, Valentine, 2007).

UK clinical guidelines recommend methadone or buprenorphine as first-line pharmacological treatments for opioid dependence, delivered within a framework of supervised dosing, psychosocial support and ongoing clinical review (Clinical Guidelines on Drug Misuse and Dependence Update 2017 Independent Expert Working Group, 2017). More recently, the introduction of long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB), Buvidal® being the licensed UK product, has expanded treatment options by providing an alternative to daily supervised consumption for suitable patients, with weekly and monthly dosing options (Scottish Intercollegiate Guideline Network (SIGN), 2022).

Emerging evidence suggests that LAIB has the potential to disrupt the restrictive routines associated with daily OAT. Qualitative studies indicate that LAIB can reduce treatment visibility, mitigate stigma, restore a sense of normalcy, and offer greater flexibility for patients (Gendera et al., 2025, Lancaster et al., 2023, Martin et al., 2022, Neale et al., 2025, Neale and Strang, 2024, Treloar et al., 2022). Recent UK research further highlights LAIBs capacity to reshape treatment experiences by altering patterns of service engagement, reducing stigma, and producing varied impacts across different stakeholder groups (Fish et al., 2025).

For many, LAIB facilitates engagement with employment, education, and caregiving responsibilities previously hindered by strict OAT regimes, and reduces contact with high-risk social networks (Neale and Strang, 2024). However, the literature also highlights that these benefits are not universal: some patients experience a loss of valued routines, diminished support, or reduced autonomy, and there are concerns about replicating negative discourses that have historically surrounded other forms of OAT (Nordgren et al., 2024, Treloar et al., 2022). Theoretical frameworks such as the social identity model of recovery (SIMOR), recovery capital, and locus of control provide important lenses for interpreting these findings and identifying gaps in the current evidence. SIMOR posits that recovery is driven by a shift in self-identity and increased engagement with social networks where substance use is not the norm (Best et al., 2016, Best et al., 2012; Dingle and Stark, 2015; Dingle and Cruwys, 2015; Frings and Albery, 2015; Mawson et al., 2015; Praharso et al., 2017). Existing LAIB studies show that reduced clinic attendance and less visible treatment can support identity reconstruction for some, but may also risk social isolation or loss of recovery-orientated connections for others (Lancaster et al., 2023, Treloar et al., 2022). Recovery capital, which encompasses personal, social, and community resources, has been shown to underpin long-term wellbeing and facilitate engagement with networks that support recovery (Best and Laudet, 2010, Cloud and Granfield, 2008, Granfield and Cloud, 1999, Hennessy, 2017, Neale and Brown, 2016). LAIB may enhance recovery capital by freeing up time and reducing stigma, but may also reduce access to supportive services if not carefully implemented (Neale et al., 2025, Nordgren et al., 2024). Locus of control, or the extent to which individuals feel able to influence their own lives (Rotter, 1990), is also relevant: while LAIB can increase autonomy for some, others may feel a loss of control over dosing or treatment decisions (Laycock, 2023, Treloar et al., 2022). Despite these theoretical insights, there is limited research that explores patient experiences of LAIB through a more nuanced lens. Existing studies highlight that satisfaction and retention are dynamic, context-dependent processes shaped by changing life circumstances, treatment expectations, and service delivery models (Gendera et al., 2025, Neale et al., 2025, Parkin et al., 2023). Yet little is known about how LAIB intersects with SIMOR, locus of control and recovery capital, factors that profoundly influence recovery trajectories and wellbeing. This study will examine patient and clinician experiences of LAIB through these lenses to provide insights into how LAIB can best support the broader social processes of recovery to inform service models that are person-centred and responsive to the diverse realities of people’s lives.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This longitudinal qualitative study was conducted in partnership with patients and staff from NHS Tayside’s integrated drug and alcohol recovery services, covering Dundee, Angus, and Perth & Kinross. While Dundee is urban, the latter two include both urban and rural areas. Semi-structured interviews were used as they have been shown to collect flexible yet focused data, as demonstrated in prior research (Adams, 2015, Adeoye-Olatunde and Olenik, 2021, McGrath et al., 2019). The interview guide was developed drawing on the research team’s prior experience and was further informed by the theoretical frameworks identified for the study, which shaped the focus of the questions. Demographic data provided context and characteristics or patient participants.

Sample and recruitment

Participants comprised two groups: those receiving LAIB and those on traditional OAT (oral buprenorphine or methadone). Eligible participants were adults (18 +) with a history of problematic substance use, currently receiving treatment via NHS Tayside. Grouping was based on current OAT type, though some had prior experience with other formulations. Target sample sizes (15–30 per group) were informed by existing qualitative research (Sim et al., 2018). A convenience sampling approach was used, with recruitment via flyers and routine care pathways. All participants were provided an information sheet explaining the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of the study, confidentiality procedures, and that their treatment or employment would not be affected by participation. Nursing staff introduced the study to potential participants and, with consent, referred interested individuals to the researcher to further explain the study, answer any questions, obtain informed consent and arrange interviews. At interview participants were reminded of their right to withdraw and that their data would remain confidential unless a risk of harm was disclosed.

Staff with a mix of roles and responsibilities (e.g. key worker, non-medical prescriber, consultant) were recruited by targeted emails and snowball sampling to capture multidisciplinary perspectives on LAIB.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted by SRD between June 2022 and January 2024, either in person, by phone, or for staff via MS Teams. Acknowledging the non-linear nature of recovery, we attempted to follow up with patient participants on two further occasions over the course of an 18-month period (visit 1 (baseline), visit 2 (9 months (+/- 6 months), visit 3 (18 months (+/- 6 months)) to investigate how views may shift and evolve over time. Supermarket vouchers were provided as recompense for participants’ time, set at £ 10 for the first and second interview and £ 20 for the final interview. Staff were interviewed once during work hours and were not compensated.

An interview guide informed by SIMOR and recovery capital ensured consistency while allowing exploration and follow-up questions (Supplementary material Table S1). The same guide was used in follow-up interviews to explore changes over time. Interviews lasted a median of 24 min (range 18–39 min), were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and accuracy checked. Participants could withdraw at any time.

It is important to consider the context, characteristics and experiences of people participating in the research to add meaning and understanding to the thematic analysis. To do this information was gathered that describe individuals’ social and personal circumstances using the following tools. The Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (SWEMWBS) provided a measure of wellbeing (Warwick, 2024). The Social Identity Map- Ascertaining identity resources (SIM - AIRing) was used to describe the participant’s social network (Best et al., 2014). Patient participant information also captured age, gender, ethnic background, and Scottish Survey’s Core Questions were utilised to capture financial inclusion, household income and employment data (Scottish Government, 2012).

Ethical considerations & data management

Ethical approval was granted by the Northwest Greater Manchester East Research Ethics Committee, 21/NW/0358 on 10/01/2022. R&D approval and Caldicott approval was given by NHS Tayside.

Participants were anonymised using study identification numbers. Demographic data were stored in a password protected excel file on University of Dundee secure servers. NVivo 12 was used to support qualitative analysis.

Data analysis

This paper adopts a critical realist perspective. Thematic framework analysis was used to identify patterns and generate insights aligned with the research questions. A conceptual model was established through discussions between SRD, AR & JFD, based on existing literature to guide the analysis (Best et al., 2016, Best et al., 2012, Best and Laudet, 2010, Cloud and Granfield, 2008; Dingle and Cruwys, 2015; Frings and Albery, 2015; Granfield and Cloud, 1999; Hennessy, 2017; Laycock, 2023; Rotter, 1990) (Fig. 1). Coding followed the five stages of framework analysis (Table 1) (Gale et al., 2013, Ritchie and Spencer, 2002, Ritchie et al., 2003). SRD and AR held regular meetings to discuss coding, explore divergent cases, and agree on recurring themes.

Reflexivity

As researchers we are not neutral observers in using the method outlined in this paper, we are influenced by our social, cultural and professional backgrounds. The primary researcher has many years of experience of working in drug treatment services as a specialist pharmacist. She acknowledges that this experience and identity are influential in this research, shaping the design phase, data collection and analysis, utilising prior knowledge of the field as a resource for interpretation and finding meaning in the data (Braun and Clarke, 2021, Braun and Clarke, 2022).

Themes

To synthesise the findings, we present the themes collectively to reflect patterns observed across all interview stages and participant groups. Themes showed no meaningful variation over time, and credibility checks, including comparisons across waves and attention to divergent cases, confirmed the stability of our interpretations. This consistency indicates that the themes capture enduring issues rather than time-specific concerns, enabling a clear and coherent account of participants’ experiences.

Four main themes were identified relating to the views, meaning and value that people place on LAIB: (i) Avoiding unwanted contacts, (ii) pursuing a “normal life”, (iii) planning for the future, and (iv) service-related challenges. Each theme is discussed in detail below.

Theme 1: avoiding unwanted contacts

In the UK, OAT is typically delivered via supervised dispensing at community pharmacies. This aims to ensure correct dosing, reduced diversion risk, and foster therapeutic relationships to promote well-being (Clinical Guidelines on Drug Misuse and Dependence Update 2017 Independent Expert Working Group, 2017). However, participants described pharmacy attendance as problematic, creating environments where change is difficult to achieve. The visibility of the “methadone queue” marked individuals out, leading to feelings of judgement and concerns about disclosure and othering. This tension was especially acute when work and private lives collided:

“Yeah, yeah, I've been there before and there has been people in there from my work and they didn’t know I was on methadone so I just had to walk out and the pharmacy were like “[name] are you leaving” and I just had to walk out and leave it.” Participant 29, male, 43 years, traditional OAT arm, interview 1

Pharmacy visits also exposed participants to running the gauntlet of dealers. The environment was seen as a hotspot for unwanted encounters, undermining recovery efforts. Achieving the stability required for relaxed dispensing, and thus reduced pharmacy visits, was difficult:

“And the thing is, you know that that environment is not good for somebody off drugs who wants to stay off drugs, you know, you go to the chemist and listen, I mean, it's literally where everybody congregates. It's actually where everybody meets, you know, so you want to stay clean and away from debts, going to the chemist every day is not the best place to be going. You know you go there and there’s people going “do you want Valium? do you want diazepam?” all sorts of stuff, it’s just constant.” Participant 04, male, 37 years, LAIB arm, interview 1

LAIB provided relief from these contacts by reducing pharmacy visits. A small number of participants had LAIB administered at home, avoiding unwanted contacts entirely. The majority attended monthly appointments at the drug treatment service for administration, but this was not viewed as problematic suggesting frequency of contact may be the key issue.

The SIMOR explains how group membership influences the recovery process, and how this network frames your own identity (Bathish et al., 2017, Best et al., 2016; Dingle and Cruwys, 2015). A shift in social connections is often necessary, as one participant described:

“You hang by who you hang with (…) if you hang around with shoplifters then you’ll be a shoplifter, if you hang around with drug dealers, you’ll be a drug dealer. If you hang about with car thieves, you’ll be a car thief. If you hang about with millionaires, you’ll be a millionaire”. See those 3–4 people you surround yourself with that’s who you are. When I come out of prison, basically I hung around with my good friends, stayed away from all the associates I used to have, and my life changed so dramatically.” Participant 09, male, 43 years, LAIB arm, interview 1

Participants valued LAIB’s role in enabling control over social contacts, shifting the locus of control from enforced to chosen associations, and the difference this makes in everyday life:

“Just going to work and things and you don’t have to hang around with druggies. You don’t feel horrible, you don’t have to go to the chemist every day, yeah. I can just get on with family whereas before when I was on the methadone I couldn’t work ‘cause I’d have to go to the chemist everyday ken. it just put you off even trying to work …you just get into that cycle. I can function now, I can do things with the kids, I can go out with the kids.” Participant 16, male, 42 years, LAIB arm, Interview 2

Strengthening connections within the community, particularly with networks not associated with substance use, may further support the recovery journey. Services could play a proactive role in fostering these connections across all forms of OAT.

Theme 2: pursuing a “normal life”

Recovery capital refers to the resources that an individual can draw on to initiate and sustain recovery from substance use. It includes resources such as health, employment, relationships, connection to communities, housing, self-efficacy and coping skills. Participants described the influence of LAIB in relation to these resources allowing participants to experience a “normal life”, a life returning to the way it was before using substances:

“Everything came back. I’ve went back to the way that life was before now, if you know what I mean? (….) If this had come out like five years ago. I would have stopped doing this a long time ago, so, it’s a really good thing” Participant 01, male, 33 years, LAIB arm, Interview 1.

LAIB was overwhelmingly seen as a “game changer”, having a positive impact on the lives of those who are prescribed it:

“Everything was affected by it [substance use]. And since I've been on this injection, I feel absolutely amazing to be honest it’s the best thing that has ever happened to me.” Participant 07, female, 40 years, LAIB arm, interview 1.

LAIB provides people with control over their lives which is experienced to be lacking with other forms of OAT. One participant reflected on their time receiving methadone, highlighting how the drug treatment service’s control extended beyond daily life and fostered deep anxiety that OAT might be abruptly withdrawn if expectations were not met. This exercise of power echoes broader patterns of control and the essence of stigmatisation. In contrast, the delivery of LAIB offers reassurance, reducing the sense of precarious dependence at the mercy of a service provider:

“Oh aye, yeah, see when you are on a methadone prescription. The chemist have got you by the, excuse my French, the balls. The DPC [drug problem centre] at that time, 48 h DPC they had, like they could tell you…” Oh you’re not getting it tomorrow”, ken [do you know, do you understand]? (…) They treat you like different…. To me, in my eyes, they treat you like a scumbag” Participant 3, male, 47 years, LAIB arm, interview 1.

This control allowed people in receipt of LAIB the freedom to structure their daily routine to include caring responsibilities for children and family members and engaging with education, training and employment, affording them greater financial security. A notable difference that LAIB provides is the opportunity to travel, with many participants stating how this brings a sense of normality and enjoyment in life:

“Oh, my life has changed so much for the better, I am away to book a holiday and I’m doing my house up with the money that I’ve saved. You know what I mean? I'm just really a different woman.” Participant 19, female, 54 years, LAIB arm, interview 1

Narratives describing opportunities afforded by OAT to build recovery capital were not unique to LAIB, though were less frequently described in the traditional OAT arm. The right OAT choice for the individual can make a dramatic difference to their life as demonstrated by one participant in receipt of methadone:

“Hundred, hundred percent it works. Methadone saved ma’ life. As I've said this to many people like it's actually changed, ah have a couple of holidays a year I've got like a really good relationship with my kids and my family and stuff and it's right for me. I'm not saying it's right for everybody, but it's right for me, you know.” Participant 34, male, 47 years, traditional OAT arm, interview 1

However, in other circumstances methadone can hinder “normal” life, preventing individuals from participating in family life as they would wish:

“It affects a lot because I can’t go on holiday or anything. But like I can’t go away with family. Like my son he stays wi’ ma’ mum and he goes away and I just can’t go” Participant 33, male, 38 years, traditional OAT arm, interview 1

Recovery from substance use looks different for different people. We have moved beyond defining recovery solely as abstinence and now recognise that for some people, recovery may include continued substance use, in ways that reduce harm and support overall wellbeing. Many participants on LAIB described reducing or stopping all substance use, a consequence of this was more money in their pocket and the ability to choose to spend this money on other activities that contributed to their wellbeing:

“Brand new. I'm totally like, ken like when I first got put on it, the Buvidal [LAIB] I was still dabbling like, but totally no drugs in the last nine months. No bad drugs bar anything but weed.

Since ah managed to stop everything, I managed to save up an’ buy a new puppy (….) this is the first time in twenty odd years that I’ve been drug free, ken. (…) I do what I want to do ken, not what the drugs tells me, not what methadone tells me, not what heroin tells me, it’s what I want to do ken? (…) I’m happy with what I’ve got in my life.” Participant 03, male, 47 years, LAIB arm, Interview 2

Theme 3: planning for the future

A shift in the balance of control from an external locus to an internal locus is evident in themes 1 and 2 for the participants in receipt of LAIB. This continued to be evidenced when the participants looked to the future and described plans and goals. The LAIB group were more likely to shift towards an internal locus of control, setting goals and describe proactive steps towards the future, such as having clear plans to reduce LAIB dose, regularly describing a future without OAT. These plans often appeared realistic, with motivation and persistence in achieving these long-term goals.

“Yeah.I am still on the Buvidal [LAIB], I mean I was wanting to come off of my Buvidal [LAIB] like in prison and the place I was in, the hostel, it wasn’t the best of situations so I just stayed on it for safety purposes but when I went to get my injections, two or three weeks ago, I mentioned that I wanted to drop from 96 to 64 and go on the 64 for a couple of month and then come off it altogether. It’s working for me.” Participant 23, male, 42 years, LAIB arm, Interview 2

Many of the participants in the traditional OAT group could also describe future plans and a life after OAT prescribing, though these plans were more abstract and without detail. A prevailing sense among many participants was that future outcomes were beyond their control, perceiving themselves as needing to wait for external circumstances to shift before they could commit to any personal change. This sense of passivity was closely tied to feelings of inadequate support from service providers and anxiety about an uncertain future:

“No…no… I am away to start with a psychologist with the drug treatment place. I’ve got a referral, but I have been waiting for a long time for it. I don’t know….my drug worker asked me yesterday if I wanted to get better … obviously, I said aye but I don’t even know what better is…I said at the time how do you get better from something you’ve been since 4-year-old, things you’ve learned from such a young age? Who are you going to be after…ken it is hard for me to say what the future is like …it’s hard for me…I have been wondering what that would even look like so without methadone I just don’t know….” Participant 40, male, 41 years, traditional OAT arm, Interview 3

Many of the traditional OAT participants were aware of LAIB and a number of them discussed LAIB as an option that they would like to explore at a future date. LAIB was seen as a potential tool towards detoxification at some point in the future.

“I was talking to him about maybe going onto an injection or something once I get down to a certain dose.errmm.I don’t know I think I'll just kind of talk to them more about it because. I'm still… I don't really know what to do. I have just kind of come off methadone before and then ended up back on it. So, I don’t want to repeat it, so I'll definitely be listen to them.” Participant 43, female, 43 years, traditional OAT arm, Interview 3

Staff participants were able to add a different perspective and described the differing experiences of reducing OAT doses. Reflecting on the psychological effect that reducing methadone has on an individual, seeing the reducing dose of the green liquid in the cup, questioning if they can do this reduction. A reduction in LAIB dose isn’t visible to the patient, and so the reduction isn’t visually reinforced daily, potentially supporting the psychological aspect of detoxification.

“Whereas with the Buvidal [LAIB] injection because they're coming in every week. You know, they're …they're getting praise and motivation, and you know that work around them that they're doing really well, but they're not seeing, yeah, it's the same injection. However, they don't feel it because it… it is a reduction, but it's a reduction in the amount, but they're not seeing that. But they do see it in a cup of methadone and to me, I think there probably needs to be a wee bit more psychology around that for them (…) I really think it's about what they see. Because if you think that someone spent, what, 10 years of their life drinking this paper cup of methadone and then suddenly it's getting less and less, and they're at this part where they're sitting with 20 ml. Can I really do this?” Staff participant 08, female.

Theme 4: service-related challenges

In Scotland the Medication Assisted Treatment standards are a nationally agreed set of 10 standards, developed to improve quality, consistency and access to treatment for opioid dependency. Standard two states that people should be offered a range of treatment options, this includes making LAIB widely available (Scottish Government, 2021). Interestingly, participants provided caution about making LAIB universally accessible and questioned the suitability of LAIB for some people:

“Like say, this injection I…. I wouldn't say it's for a’body [everybody]….I would say people that want to stop…. or people who are forced in, I would say. Ken like, that's two different ken sorts of people.” Participant 03, male, 47 years, LAIB arm, Interview 1

As reflected by this participant, LAIB on its own cannot be expected to do the heavy lifting of turning someone’s life around. There needs to be support and services available to address the holistic needs of the individual. The partial agonists effects of buprenorphine can result in some patients experiencing emotions that were previously blunted by full agonists such as heroin or methadone. Whilst this is an issue for both oral and injectable formulations of buprenorphine, the experiences of those receiving LAIB highlight that it is a particular issue for some patients receiving LAIB, which may be linked to gaps in service provision:

“Oh yeah, it's good. But like emotionally, they don't prepare you for that. That's the biggest drawback to it. and I've said this to people since I’ve been on the injection, it's like when you go to see [NHS drugs service], you're just going there for the injection. There's not much counselling.” Participant 08, male, 42 years, LAIB arm, Interview 1.

Those in receipt of LAIB felt that there was a shift in focus of the service, with many reporting a lack of continuity in care and quality, to the detriment of the holistic needs on the individual:

“I just think that it's different nurses every time I go, there is no quality, there is no consistency, you are speaking to different people every time you go to clinic there is someone else giving you your injection….” Participant 22, male, 51 years, LAIB arm, Interview 1

There was a recognition that the opportunities to pursue a “normal life” came with challenges. Some participants in receipt of LAIB found the change in structure and routine left hours of the day unfilled, recognising that they needed to find something else to do once attendance at the pharmacy is removed:

“I need to find something else to be doing….” Participant 12, female, 34 years, LAIB arm, Interview 3

The pressure of workload on services means that many participants (both groups) were not currently allocated a keyworker. This is detrimental to people’s recovery journey as the participants recognised that a good therapeutic relationship with a keyworker strengthens their recovery capital:

“Aye, aye it is [names keyworker] I see, and he is a qualified CPN, so he helps me out with benefits, doctor’s appointments, dental appointments, everything you need. He helps me out with it; he is really good.” Participant 39, male, 49 years, traditional OAT arm, Interview 2

Staff participants reflected that many people are requesting LAIB due to word of mouth on the street, with people sharing their experiences of LAIB with one another. This is seen as both a positive, as people have greater choice in treatment options, and as a negative due to the pressures that this is placing on an already stretched system:

“I’m not surprised at how quickly it's taken off (…) it started off quite small and just… just a small a selection of patients were interested in it (…) it's kind of exploded from there. So that's great to see (…) now what we're finding is that it's actually taken up quite a lot of our resources to… to be able to facilitate the injections (…) So that’s… that's good. But it just brings its challenges, I suppose, in being able to facilitate that for our patients.” Staff participant 05

Discussion

This study examined the lived meaning and value of LAIB through the combined lenses of the SIMOR, recovery capital, and locus of control. These frameworks emphasise how shifts in social identity, access to social and personal resources, and perceptions of agency interact to shape recovery trajectories. Interpreting our findings through these perspectives highlights the ways in which LAIB can facilitate, yet sometimes constrain, the social and psychological processes underpinning sustained recovery.

Four interrelated themes characterised participants’ experiences: avoiding unwanted contacts, pursuing a “normal life,” planning for the future, and service-related challenges. Consistent with SIMOR, participants described LAIB as creating distance from the negative social interactions often associated with the daily pharmacy attendance required by traditional OAT. These interactions, situated within what Fraser (2006) terms the “chronotope of the queue”, were experienced as stigmatising, lacking confidentiality, and exposing individuals to high-risk networks. Removing these routine pharmacy contacts afforded autonomy and dignity, reinforcing movement towards non-using identities and reducing exposure to triggers associated with relapse. These changes also supported the accrual of recovery capital, which participants linked to leading a more “normal life.” This included freedom to travel, take holidays, or pursue employment without seeking permission or planning around rigid service structures.

Participants described renewed hope, increased self-efficacy, and a shift in locus of control, with several noting that reduced reliance on daily medication management fostered greater autonomy in their recovery. For some, LAIB supported progress toward goals such as dose reduction or life beyond treatment. However, these benefits were not universal. Although moving from pharmacy-based to clinic-based administration reduced stigma for some, it also reduced opportunities for holistic, person-centred care. This underscores that LAIB reconfigures, rather than simply reduces, service contact with implications for therapeutic engagement and continuity of psychosocial support.

These findings reinforce that LAIB is more than a pharmacological intervention. By reducing service contact frequency, it creates opportunities for enhanced autonomy and dignity, often missing in traditional OAT. However, realising these benefits requires careful attention to service design and delivery. In Scotland, where LAIB prescribing is increasing (The Scottish Public Health Observatory, 2024), understanding both its therapeutic potential and the structural conditions that support or hinder its implementation is essential.

These findings align with a growing literature showing that LAIB offers practical and psychosocial benefits, including reduced stigma, avoidance of high-risk environments, and identity transformation (Barnett et al., 2021, Lancaster et al., 2023, Treloar et al., 2022). For many, disrupting the daily pharmacy routine created space to reconnect with family, explore employment opportunities, and build a future outside of treatment. As other studies have noted, this restoration of agency and normalcy is highly valued (McMaster and Abeysundera, 2025, Neale et al., 2025, Parkin et al., 2023). Yet concerns noted in the literature were also evident here. Barnett et al. (2021) caution that reduced service contact may undermine access to social and practical supports, while Treloar et al. (2022) highlight the risk of replicating negative methadone narratives. Recent UK multistakeholder work (Fish et al., 2025) similarly points to variability in suitability and service pressures during implementation.

A central ethical obligation is ensuring that LAIB enhances, rather than limits, autonomy by maintaining all OAT options and supporting informed choice, recognising that both LAIB and traditional OAT carry distinct benefits and drawbacks. Reclaiming control over daily life emerged as a central thread across participant narratives. LAIB offered an alternative to the highly regulated, often dehumanising experiences within the pharmacy ecosystem, which many felt undermined their recovery journey. The fundamental right to health extends beyond access to medication to include conditions that promote wellbeing and social participation. Current OAT structures often fail to meet this broader definition (Ritter and Barrett, 2024).

LAIB, by contrast, was seen as offering the chance to influence social identity. Participants frequently discussed the influence of their social networks on recovery trajectories, with one participant reflecting, “you hang by who you hang with”. This sentiment strongly aligns with SIMOR’s position that recovery is shaped by shifts in group membership and social norms (Bathish et al., 2017, Best et al., 2016; Dingle and Stark, 2015; Dingle and Cruwys, 2015; Donaldson et al., 2023). Interestingly, the social identity mapping did not reveal major compositional changes in network structure, this may reflect that unwanted pharmacy contacts were peripheral acquaintances rather than meaningful network members. Parkin et al. (2023) similarly describe an initial contraction of social networks for those receiving LAIB during the adjustment phase, followed by transformation with new identities and the repair of social connections. Further support to link people with wider community connections may aid this journey.

LAIB’s benefits were partly contingent on service design. Participants voiced concern that key worker interactions, once opportunities for holistic support, had become narrowly focused on injection administration. For those experiencing heightened emotions due to the pharmacological action of buprenorphine, this lack of support can be particularly challenging. This shift was experienced by some as a perceived loss of mattering, where reduced relational engagement signalled that their broader wellbeing was no longer a priority. Such perceptions risk undermining the recovery capital LAIB might otherwise enhance (Bahl et al., 2023, Charles and Alexander, 2014). These findings echo recent UK evidence showing that increasingly task-oriented service models can diminish patients’ experience of being valued, heard, and supported (Neale et al., 2025, Neale and Strang, 2024).

Participants emphasised that reducing the burden of pharmacy attendance should not equate to reducing meaningful therapeutic contact. Resources in the drug treatment sector are under pressure, yet findings suggest that reducing the frequency of pharmacy visits, not reducing all service contact, is the key to improving outcomes. A hybrid model where community pharmacies deliver monthly injections while specialist services maintain responsibility for relational, recovery-oriented care may better balance efficiency with patient wellbeing (Scottish Government, 2021).

This study adds to the UK evidence base demonstrating that LAIB can enhance autonomy, reduce stigma, and support identity change, however it also presents practical and structural challenges. Consistent with multistakeholder research (Fish et al., 2025), our findings underline the need for delivery models that preserve informed choice, attend to individual preferences, and protect the relational elements of care foundational to recovery.

This further suggests that LAIB is not merely a change in medication delivery. By reshaping social identity processes, strengthening recovery capital, and supporting a stronger internal locus of control, LAIB can help build the social and psychological foundations for sustained recovery. However, realising these benefits requires service structures that maintain continuity, therapeutic relationships, and meaningful patient choice. As treatment options expand under Scotland’s MAT standards (Scottish Government, 2021), services must remain attentive to individual needs. LAIB cannot, alone, carry the burden of recovery.

Implications for policy and practice

Implementation strategies should integrate LAIB within recovery-oriented systems rather than as a standalone intervention. Models that combine monthly injections with proactive psychosocial support, potentially through partnerships with community and third-sector organisations could optimise outcomes. Workforce planning is essential to prevent administrative pressures from eroding person-centred care. Policymakers should view LAIB as part of a broader toolkit under medication-assisted treatment standards, ensuring informed choice and equitable access. While findings are rooted in the UK context, they resonate internationally. Health systems worldwide face similar challenges: balancing clinical efficiency with recovery-oriented care, addressing stigma, and expanding treatment options. LAIB offers promise in diverse settings, but its success depends on structural conditions that uphold autonomy, dignity, and social inclusion. Future research should evaluate integrated models to inform global best practice.

Limitations

This study’s qualitative design captured rich lived experiences across time points. However, many participants were lost to follow-up due to inactive or changed phone numbers. Despite this, themes remained consistent among those completing follow-ups. Recruitment for the traditional OAT group was slower, with LAIB recipients more eager to share experiences. Clinician interviews suggested experiences with LAIB were widely shared in communities, possibly driving interest and willingness to participate. Participants were offered a choice of interview modality (telephone, in-person at a mutually convenient location, or, for staff participants, via MS Teams). Although the use of multiple interview formats may introduce some variability in the data, enabling participants to select their preferred mode of participation likely enhanced their comfort and engagement by allowing them to take part in a manner and setting most conducive to open and authentic discussion.

Conclusion

While LAIB may offer meaningful support for recovery in some cases, its potential is shaped by the broader treatment context. It cannot, in isolation, address complex social and structural challenges faced by patients. To realise its benefits, service delivery must prioritise holistic, person-centred care over administration demands. This may require a review of how LAIB has been implemented in services.
