Using machine learning to predict future foster care admission
Authors: Ari Ne’eman, Alex Brooks, Kellie Hans-Green, Arpit Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFoster care admissions are highly traumatic for children and their families, often causing serious adverse outcomes. We seek to assess the viability of machine learning methods to identify children at risk of future foster care admission to facilitate diversion.MethodsWe use claims data for children enrolled in a Medicaid health plan in Ohio as well as for linked adults, along with data on individual and geographic social determinants of health (SDOH) factors. We test the performance of a gradient-boosted tree machine learning algorithm as compared to logistic regression. Of the children, 85% have SDOH data available.ResultsUsing a gradient-boosted tree machine learning algorithm, we built a model that identifies 2408 children (1.32%) as at risk of foster care admission in a sample of 181 841, of whom 1599 entered foster care within 1 year, resulting in a positive predictive value (PPV) of 66.4% (F1 = 55.5%, specificity = 99.5%, sensitivity = 47.67%), outperforming logistic regression. Introduction

Although needed in specific contexts to address serious instances of maltreatment, abuse, and neglect, transitions into foster care can be highly traumatic for children, resulting in higher healthcare utilization and adverse health consequences. A growing body of evidence suggests that foster care transitions are in part the result of social and contextual factors. Children in economically insecure households face dramatically higher rates of identified maltreatment and neglect. The majority of children investigated by Child Protective Services (CPS) are below 200% of the federal poverty line.1 Although such investigations are in many instances justified in their proximate cause, the circumstances that lead to CPS intervention becoming necessary are often amenable to intervention.

A growing literature supports this premise. For instance, Beland et al. document that Medicaid expansion decreased foster care admissions related to neglect.2 Gihleb et al. show that the adoption of mandatory Prescription Drug Monitoring Programs reduced child removals by 10%.3 Bullinger et al. use annual variation in Alaska's unconditional cash transfer program to show that an additional $1000 received by families in the first few months of a child's life reduces by 10% the likelihood they will be referred to CPS by age 3.4 Rostad et al. found that state adoption of a refundable Earned Income Tax Credit was associated with an 11% reduction in foster care entries compared to states without such a policy.5 These policy interventions take place on a large scale and impact children at risk of foster care by shifting the broader landscape of health care provision.

However, for some families more targeted and resource-intensive interventions may be required to divert a child from future foster care placement. Some potentially promising interventions, such as intensive respite care services, may be too costly to offer on a broad basis without limitation. As such, identifying children at the greatest risk of future foster care admission could enable the provision of additional resources to families, potentially enabling families to stay together by diverting children from removal.

To that end, in this paper, we seek to test the viability of making use of machine learning algorithms to predict future foster care admission as a possible tool for providing families additional resources to facilitate diversion and avoid child removal. We emphasize that this objective is wholly distinct from other applications of machine learning in child welfare focused on using predictive analytics to identify families for CPS investigation. We make use of a gradient-boosted tree algorithm, a machine learning ensemble technique, to attempt to identify those children at greatest risk from a large sample of Medicaid-enrolled children in the state of Ohio. We compare its performance to a simple logistic regression to assess the degree to which the gradient-boosted tree algorithm can improve upon standard regression-based approaches. We also test the ability of models with and without social determinants of health (SDOH) data and compare the relative predictive accuracy to examine the role of SDOH data in predicting foster care admissions and then fit a composite “combined” model making use of the SDOH data for the majority of children for which it is available and applying the model without SDOH variables for those children for whom no linked SDOH information is present.

Methods

Data

We make use of data from CareSource Ohio, a nonprofit health plan with the largest Medicaid enrollment in the state of Ohio. Our data consists of children less than 18 years of age enrolled in CareSource's Ohio plan from January 2019 through June 2022. For children in foster care, our sample consists of those children enrolled in CareSource from the Adoption and Foster Kids (AFK) eligibility pathway who have prior eligibility in a different program within 90 days of their enrollment in Medicaid via the AFK pathway. For non-foster care members, we subset to members who have continual eligibility from any pathway and CareSource enrollment for at least 12 months after the index date from which their data are used and who have no prior entrance into foster care as measured by Medicaid eligibility coding during their prior Medicaid enrollment history. Index dates were calculated by looking 12 months prior at currently enrolled children as of the date of the analysis (June 2023). This produces a sample of 606 001 children, from whom we hold back 181 841 children (30%) to use as an out-of-sample (OOS) test and use the remainder for training/validation. We randomly split the training/validation set into 339 328 children (80%) to use as training data and 84 832 children (20%) as a validation set (for parameter tuning).

We use a wide range of variables to predict foster care admission, including clinical diagnoses and utilization of children, diagnoses and utilization of linked parental adults, data from the Johns Hopkins ACG® System health analytics software, and 21 SDOH risk scores constructed for use in CareSource's broader analytics activities using data from third-party vendors who acquire individual-data from public records and construct geospatial data on the socioeconomic gradient of specific neighborhoods of residence. The list of each of the risk scores and an explanation of their details is available in Appendix A. To address multicollinearity across predictor variables, we iteratively removed variables with the highest Variable Inflation Factor score until all candidate variables were below a threshold of 10.

Analysis

We seek to predict future foster care admission using a gradient-boosted tree algorithm, which we then compare to a standard logistic regression to test the value of the gradient-boosted tree approach relative to a more standard regression-based approach to predictive modeling. Gradient-boosted trees consist of a series of “weak” models (each of which is an individual decision tree) which work together to create a “strong” model. The method by which they work together is called gradient boosting, under which at each step a new weak model is trained to predict the error of the current strong model. The weak model is then added to the strong model with a negative sign to reduce the error of the strong model. Each iteration of weak models thus incrementally improves the strong model.6 Our algorithm is set to estimate a binary outcome using a logistic function. The maximum depth of each tree is set at 3, meaning that each tree can use only 3 different classification conditions. Our maximum number of trees are 100, meaning that the gradient-boosted tree can produce as many as 100 weak models in the process of creating a strong model. Gradient-boosted trees avoid overfitting through a process known as “early stopping” under which each version of the model developed from the training data is applied to validation data in an iterative process that stops when performance on the validation data begins to decrease at the same time as performance on the training data continues to improve, suggesting overfitting. In our implementation, we selected area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) as our performance measure for purposes of assessing overfitting.

We also make use of a shrinkage factor to reduce the influence of any single tree, also sometimes referred to as a learning rate. Before adding the output from each weak model to the strong model, it is multiplied by the shrinkage factor, effectively reducing the speed at which the strong model learns from the weak models (and thus reducing the risk of overfitting). In our implementation, the shrinkage factor is set to 0.1.

530 951 children (87.6%) in our overall sample have SDOH data. To classify the remaining children without SDOH data (and to yield insights on model effectiveness without SDOH information), we fit a parallel model on all data without SDOH variables, then classify those children with SDOH data using the SDOH model and those children without SDOH data using the non-SDOH model. In the OOS test set, 159 277 children (87.6%) were analyzed using the SDOH model and 22 564 children (12.4%) were analyzed using the non-SDOH model. When reporting accuracy for the non-SDOH model alone, we report accuracy for all 181 841 children in the OOS test set. For the overall sample, 1.84% of children enter foster care. The percentage is slightly lower (1.52%) for only those children who have SDOH data.

We identify children as at risk through 2 distinct pathways. First, we designate a cutoff point based on maximizing the F1 score [the harmonic mean of the positive predictive value (PPV) and sensitivity statistics] in the SDOH model. For those above the cutoff point, we then classified them into low risk, medium risk, high risk, and intense risk categories based on after-the-fact percentile analyses to determine at what percentiles there were sizable differences in future admissions. Children were classified as intense risk if the model assigns them a greater than 95% probability of foster care admission, high risk at a 70%-95% probability, medium risk at a 20%-70% probability, low risk at a 9%-20% probability and no risk at below a 9% probability.

To aid in interpretability of the output of our machine learning model, we produce plots showing the variable importance and Shapley values of each variable. Variable importance is defined as the average information gain across all splits the feature is used in. Shapley values provide an interpretation of how each value of a variable contributes to the outcome of interest.7 Both of these tools are common mechanisms for facilitating the interpretability of machine learning algorithms, including in health care applications. We show the top 20 variables for each model, ranked in order of variable importance. Next to each variable's name is the distribution of Shapley values, with the x axis representing the degree of change in the log-odds each variable provides at different levels of the variable. Colors represent the value of the variable for a particular individual in our data set on a spectrum from red to blue, with red representing higher values and blue representing lower values. As a robustness check, we also test a modeling procedure using only the top 20 variables.

Finally, to illustrate the predictive accuracy of the SDOH and non-SDOH models, we display a precision–recall curve for each model, plotting the precision (also known as the PPV, defined as the proportion of observations identified as positive that are actually positive) on the y axis and the recall (also known as the sensitivity, defined as the proportion of actual positives identified as such by the algorithm) on the x axis. Precision–recall curves are often used as an alternative to ROC Curves in the context of rare outcomes, as the latter would be misleadingly optimistic as specificity is innately high in rare outcomes. We report the average precision across all cutoff points in the precision–recall curve.

Results

In the first panel of Table 1, we show a confusion matrix table showing the number of true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives from our combined model. Of the 181 841 in our OOS test data, our algorithm classifies 2408 as at risk of a foster care admission using the cutoff calculated by maximizing the F1 value. Of these, 1599 (66.4%) transitioned into foster care. Of the 179 433 our model did not identify as at risk of foster care admission, only 1755 (0.98%) transitioned. In the second and third panel of Table 1, we also provide confusion matrix tables split between the 159 277 children who were classified using the SDOH model and for all children classified using the non-SDOH model. Crucially, the SDOH model has substantially better PPV (the percentage of positives that are true positives) than the non-SDOH model, with the former having a PPV of 84.72% and the latter having a PPV of only 27.44%. Results are substantively similar when re-estimating the gradient-boosted tree models with only the top 20 variables (see Appendix Table SB.1). Appendix Table SB.2 shows that the gradient-boosted tree substantially outperforms simple logistic regression. When not using SDOH variables, logistic regression was unable to correctly predict a single child's future foster care transition, yielding a PPV of 0%. Although the logistic regression performed slightly better when using SDOH variables—yielding a PPV of 12.7%—this was still substantially worse than the 84.72% achieved by the gradient-boosted tree algorithm.

These show the spectrum of values across different levels of the variable next to each variable plotted in terms of each's impact on the log-odds of our outcome. To illustrate this, consider the impact of age in the SDOH model, which shows higher values of age (denoted by the red dots next to the AgeAtMemberMonth variable) far to the right side of the Shapley value, indicating that their impact on model output is positive and large in terms of log-odds. This means that older children in Ohio are more likely to transition into foster care in our model.

In the SDOH model, 8 of the 20 most important variables are SDOH related. Of the rest, 4 relate to a linked household adult. Only the remaining 8 relate to the child themselves—and for some of these variables, such as homelessness and county of residence, they are likely driven primarily by socioeconomic determinants. In the non-SDOH model, diagnoses and utilization patterns play a greater role. However, as discussed above, the non-SDOH model has much poorer predictive performance than the SDOH model.

Figure 3 shows precision–recall curves for both models. The precision–recall curves show that the SDOH model maintains high levels of precision across a broad range of the recall/sensitivity spectrum, while the non-SDOH model sees precision drop dramatically relatively early in the recall/sensitivity spectrum. This means that the SDOH model can still accurately identify children at risk of foster care admission as the threshold for sensitivity increases, while the non-SDOH model quickly loses PPV as a user seeks to increase sensitivity to identify a larger portion of future foster care admissions. Consistent with this, Average Precision (the average proportion of positives that are true positives across all possible cutpoints) is 0.75 for the SDOH model and 0.16 for the non-SDOH model.

Discussion

Limitations

Although we work to identify foster care transitions using claims data, it is possible that some children have prior or future foster care admissions not identified within the data, either by virtue of missing data or because of enrollment with a different health plan. However, we believe that our data is likely to capture the majority of foster care transitions among Medicaid-enrolled children given CareSource's large market share in Ohio, representing the majority of Covered Families & Children Medicaid enrollment. Although our model displays strong predictive performance when applied to a random subset of children enrolled in CareSource's Ohio plan—the population on which it was trained—it may perform less well in different populations. Data users must train models on samples representative of the populations they seek to apply their model to rather than applying models built in other contexts to demographically and clinically distinct populations. Information was not available on sibling status within our data set, preventing us from ensuring that children from the same household were not split across the training and validation data sets.

Policy and practice implications

The high levels of positive predictive accuracy present within the gradient-boosted tree models relative to logistic regression—particularly when using SDOH data—suggest that machine learning can be an effective tool for identifying children at risk of foster care admissions in order to provide families with additional resources to facilitate diversion. The removal of SDOH data substantially reduced the predictive accuracy of our model, reinforcing the importance of socioeconomic factors in increasing the risk of future foster care admission and suggesting that additional investments in linking SDOH data to Medicaid claims where not yet available may yield important benefits to payers seeking to better target scarce resources to persons with the greatest need. Notably, the SDOH variables that proved most important in the SDOH model include both contextual factors defined by geography (such as local spending on education, the most influential variable in the SDOH model) and individual-level factors defined at the household level (such as household members” prior liens, bankruptcies, and evictions as well as the degree of criminal justice system involvement of household members, the second and third most influential variables, respectively). However, even without SDOH data, machine learning tools still offer a means to target a smaller population with a much higher risk of foster care admission than the overall sample.

Poverty, parental substance use and other behavioral health challenges, and children's mental health challenges substantially predict foster care admission. The provision of additional resources, such as assistance in accessing substance use disorder treatment for parents and enhanced respite care for families of children with substantial support needs, could help to prevent such admissions. Existing research documenting a causal effect of expansions of public insurance and cash assistance in significantly reducing child removals and CPS investigations supports this premise.2,4 However, intensive interventions are often too costly to offer to all Medicaid-enrolled children. Our results indicate that machine learning can be used to enable payers and policymakers to offer families and children resources at a level more likely to reduce the risk of unnecessary foster care admission. In contexts in which the modeling objective is to enable payers to offer more costly services (such as respite care or enhanced case management) to enable diversion, even relatively modest sensitivity rates (such as the 60.98% of our SDOH model or even the 13.24% of our non-SDOH model) offer a substantial targeting advantage relative to the counterfactual of no predictive model in which payers must offer the same resources to all children regardless of relative risk.

Ethical issues related to the use of predictive analytics

In recent years, machine learning methods have also been used by child welfare agencies to assess future child maltreatment risk.9,10,11 Some have argued that machine learning methods have the potential to predict future child maltreatment and should thus be a useful tool for child welfare investigators in deciding how to allocate investigatory resources. However, applications of machine learning for purposes of surveillance and investigation raise different ethical considerations from those implied in our anticipated use case of foster care diversion. Identifying families for additional scrutiny and potential child removal should require a much higher evidentiary standard than identifying families for the provision of additional resources. The predictive accuracy of our model is—in our opinion—impressively high for the diversion use case while not sufficient for targeting investigation resources. In addition, because machine learning algorithms rely on identified foster care transitions in the training data to ascertain which children are at risk, they run the risk of replicating racial, disability and socioeconomic biases in the existing child welfare system. This risk is not implicated to the same degree when using machine learning algorithms to provide families with additional resources. In contrast, the use of the same methods and models for targeting families for additional scrutiny should raise serious ethical concerns. The use of parental disability as a predictor may also raise legal concerns when a model is used to target families for investigation, given growing concerns regarding disability discrimination in child welfare agencies’ child removal decisions.12

Even interventions that do not come with any intent for investigation or removal still present some risk. For instance, Stritzel shows that increases in public insurance rates were associated with rates of foster care entry due to parental substance use, suggesting the possibility that greater contact with mandatory reporters among low-income parents may increase the risk of child removal.13 Design and evaluation of future targeted interventions to divert children from the foster care system should carefully assess the risk that such interventions may have unintended consequences in the absence of careful program design. Nonetheless, the use of machine learning to identify families in need of additional assistance and provide it to them presents an important opportunity to provide intensive interventions that are too costly to offer on a broader basis.

Conclusion

Our analysis shows that machine learning can be an effective tool in predicting future foster care admission and that SDOH variables are important predictors for use in identifying children at risk. As this technology continues to develop and be deployed, it will be important for payers, policymakers, and regulators to distinguish between appropriate and inappropriate use cases for machine learning in the child welfare context. Although machine learning presents serious ethical and legal risks when used to target families for investigation and child removal, other use cases offer a greater potential to leverage the predictive abilities of this technology with fewer risks of iatrogenic harm. As a means of providing families with additional resources, machine learning can be a powerful tool capable of reducing the rates of unnecessary child removals by providing families with the resources necessary to address the socioeconomic roots of many (though not all) instances of child neglect and maltreatment.
