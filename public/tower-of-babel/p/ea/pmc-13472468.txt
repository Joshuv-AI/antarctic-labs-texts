Evaluating the therapeutic potential of Salacca zalacca peel ethanol extract on probing depth and gingival recession in Wistar rats with periodontitis
Authors: Aprilia Yuanita Anwaristi, Nur Ariska Nugrahani, Afifah Khoirun Nisa, Annisa Rachmah Afrizal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Specific germs produced periodontitis, a destructive inflammatory disease of the tissues supporting the teeth that destroyed the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, and the creation of pockets, gingival recession, or both. Adjunctive therapy and mechanical scaling were used to treat periodontitis. Because of its anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and wound-healing properties, the ethanol extract of snakefruit peel (Salacca zalacca var. pondoh) included phytochemicals that were helpful as an alternate supplementary therapy for periodontitis.Objective:This study aimed to evaluate the effect of snake fruit peel ethanol extract on probing depth and gingival recession in Wistar rats with experimentally induced periodontitis.Materials and Methods:A posttest-only control group design was used in a real experimental laboratory investigation. Twenty-four Wistar rats were used as research subjects and divided into six groups. On days 1, 7, and 14, the treatment group received the ethanol extract of snake fruit peel, while the positive control group received chlorhexidine gluconate. The One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Post hoc least significant difference tests were used for data analysis.Results:One-way ANOVA showed significant differences in probing depth and gingival recession among the groups (P < 0.05). The treatment groups showed lower probing depth and gingival recession scores than the positive control groups across all observation periods.Conclusion:Snake fruit peel ethanol extract was associated with lower probing depth and gingival recession scores in Wistar rats with experimentally induced periodontitis. Further studies are required to confirm its biological mechanisms and therapeutic potential as an adjunctive periodontal therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal disease is a condition that affects the periodontium, the supporting structures of the teeth, including the gingiva, alveolar bone, cementum, and periodontal ligament.[1] Periodontal disease is broadly classified into gingivitis and periodontitis.[23] Periodontitis is a destructive inflammatory disease of the supporting tissues of the teeth caused by specific microorganisms, including Porphyromonas gingivalis, Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans, Prevotella intermedia, Tannerella forsythia, and Fusobacterium nucleatum.[4] These microorganisms induce progressive degradation of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, leading to periodontal pocket formation, gingival recession, and clinical attachment loss, ultimately culminating in tooth loss if left untreated.[567]

Periodontitis can be further classified into aggressive and chronic forms.[89] Chronic periodontitis is the most common form. It is generally associated with plaque and calculus accumulation, with slow disease progression punctuated by intermittent periods of accelerated tissue destruction and horizontal alveolar bone loss.[10] Clinically, chronic periodontitis is characterized by gingival inflammation, bleeding, periodontal pocket formation, attachment loss, radiographic bone loss, and, in advanced cases, tooth mobility and tooth loss.[1112]

Clinical evaluation of periodontal disease is commonly performed using parameters such as probing depth, clinical attachment level, and bleeding on probing.[13] Probing depth represents the distance from the gingival margin to the base of the periodontal pocket. In healthy gingival tissues, probing depth is typically ≤3 mm, whereas inflamed or diseased periodontal tissues exhibit probing depths ≥3 mm.[14] In periodontitis, the periodontal probe penetrates through the junctional epithelium into the connective tissue compartment, reflecting connective tissue breakdown.[15] Gingival recession, which distinguishes periodontitis from gingivitis, is defined as the apical displacement of the gingival margin resulting in exposure of the root surface.[1617]

The management of periodontitis follows a systematic approach consisting of initial, surgical, restorative, and maintenance phases. Initial therapy focuses on eliminating etiologic factors through mechanical debridement, such as scaling, root planing, and curettage.[181920] Adjunctive systemic or local therapies may be employed to suppress periodontopathogenic bacteria and enhance treatment outcomes.[2122]

Mechanical therapy is often combined with chemical agents such as chlorhexidine gluconate, povidone-iodine, and fluoride. Chlorhexidine gluconate is considered the gold standard in periodontal therapy due to its effectiveness in suppressing plaque microorganisms,[2324] including Streptococcus mutans and P. gingivalis. However, prolonged use of chlorhexidine-containing mouthwashes may lead to adverse effects, including xerostomia, hypogeusia, calculus accumulation, and discoloration of the teeth and tongue.[2526]

As a result, increasing attention has been directed toward herbal-based adjunctive therapies, which have demonstrated promising outcomes in periodontal management.[272829] Snake fruit peel (Salacca zalacca) is one herbal material currently under investigation.[30] Natural herbal products, such as snake fruit peel, contain bioactive phytochemical compounds, including flavonoids, saponins, tannins, alkaloids, and chlorogenic acid.[31] These compounds have been reported to exhibit antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and wound-healing properties.[32]

Despite the known biological activities of snake fruit peel, no previous studies have evaluated the effect of ethanol extract from S. zalacca peel as an adjunctive therapy in periodontitis-induced Wistar rats, specifically focusing on probing depth reduction and gingival recession outcomes. The use of snake fruit peel as an alternative herbal therapy also adds value by maximizing the use of S. zalacca fruit beyond its role as a food source.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of ethanol extract from snake fruit peel (S. zalacca var. pondoh) on probing depth and gingival recession in Wistar rats with experimentally induced periodontitis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study employed a true experimental design with a posttest-only control group methodology.[333435] Observations were conducted on both control and treatment groups. The study titled “Evaluating the Therapeutic Potential of Salacca zalacca Peel Ethanol Extract on Probing Depth and Gingival Recession in Wistar Rats with Periodontitis” was conducted from January to March 2024. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of Indonesia (ethical clearance number 2.373/XII/HREC/2023) and were performed in accordance with international guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Ethanol extract of snake fruit peel (S. zalacca) was an extract obtained from snake fruit peel from Turi Village, Sleman, Yogyakarta. The plant material was authenticated and subjected to phytochemical determination in a laboratory specializing in traditional health services. The peel was extracted in a pharmaceutical laboratory using the maceration method with 70% ethanol. The extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator and stored at 4°C until use. The final ethanol extract concentration used in this study was 40%.

P. gingivalis was cultured in a molecular medicine and therapy research laboratory under anaerobic conditions. A total of 24 male Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus) were used as experimental subjects. The rats were 3 months old, weighed 150–200 g, and were clinically healthy. Male rats were selected to minimize hormonal variability that could influence periodontal inflammation and healing responses.

The sample size was determined based on Federer’s formula for experimental animal studies (t = 1) (n = 1) ≥15, where t represents the number of groups and n the number of animals per group. With six experimental groups, a minimum of four rats per group was required, resulting in a total of 24 rats. The animals were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 4 per group), as summarized in Table 1.

The rats were anesthetized via intramuscular injection of ketamine HCl and xylazine (0.2 ml/kg body weight) in the thigh region.[36] Experimental periodontitis was induced by placing a silk ligature around the subgingival area of the right mandibular incisor, followed by topical application of P. gingivalis suspension (0.01 ml) to the buccal and lingual subgingival areas using a micropipette once daily for 7 consecutive days. Clinical signs confirming the induction of periodontitis included plaque and calculus accumulation, gingival erythema and inflammation, attachment loss, gingival recession, periodontal pocket formation, and spontaneous bleeding.[7]

After periodontitis induction was confirmed, scaling was performed to remove supragingival and subgingival plaque. In the treatment groups, rats received 0.1 ml of S. zalacca peel ethanol extract applied subgingivally once daily for 14 days using an oral probe. The dosage of 0.1 ml/day was selected based on: (1) Preliminary toxicity and safety data from herbal periodontal studies indicating local tolerance, (2) The limited subgingival space in rat incisors, and (3) previous in vivo studies using comparable topical application volumes. The positive control groups received 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate in the same volume and application method.[37]

Probing depth and gingival recession measurements were performed on days 1, 7, and 14 using a UNC-15 periodontal probe. The probe was gently inserted into the gingival sulcus or periodontal pocket, with the probe tip maintained parallel to and in contact with the tooth surface. Measurements were initiated from the base of the papilla and continued along the mesial to distal surfaces of the tooth.

The walking stroke technique was used, moving the probe up and down, resembling a nodding motion, advancing 1 mm at a time. The probe tip remained in the pocket throughout the measurement. Pocket depth was recorded using a six-point system on each tooth: mesiofacial, midfacial, distofacial, mesiolingual or palatal, midlingual or palatal, and distolingual or palatal points. If multiple pocket depths were measured on one side, the deepest pocket depth was recorded. Gingival recession was quantified from the cementoenamel junction to the gingival margin.[3839] The data were subjected to parametric analysis, namely one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a significance level of 95% (P ≤ 0.05), followed by an Least Significant Difference (LSD) test to assess significant intergroup differences.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the therapeutic potential of S. zalacca peel ethanol extract on periodontal probing depth and gingival recession in Wistar rats with experimentally induced periodontitis, using 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate as the positive control. The results demonstrated that both the positive control and treatment groups showed progressive improvement in periodontal parameters over the observation period, with the extract-treated group exhibiting a greater reduction in probing depth and gingival recession. Periodontitis was initiated and sustained by periodontopathogenic bacteria, particularly P. gingivalis.[40] P. gingivalis destroys the periodontal ligament by expanding subgingival plaque into the gingival sulcus, disrupting the epithelial attachment to the tooth surface, and leading to tissue destruction. This imbalance between bacterial load and host response leads to the formation of periodontal pockets and the loss of gingival attachment.[41] Reduction of bacterial burden and modulation of inflammation are therefore essential for periodontal tissue healing. The greater reduction in probing depth observed in the extract-treated group suggests that S. zalacca peel ethanol extract may support periodontal healing through combined antimicrobial and host-modulatory effects.[42]

The treatment group exhibited a notably greater decrease in probing depth scores when contrasted with the positive control group. This effect can be attributed to the compounds in snake fruit peel ethanol extract, such as flavonoids, tannins, saponins, alkaloids, and chlorogenic acid, which possess anti-inflammatory properties.[3143] Rather than acting through a single pathway, these compounds appear to exert complementary actions, including suppression of inflammation, inhibition of bacterial growth, antioxidant activity, and promotion of tissue repair. These compounds inhibit cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase enzymes, accelerating the inflammatory phase,[44] minimizing tissue damage,[45] and promoting faster regeneration and healing of periodontal tissue.[46] The inflammatory phase is characterized by the presence of leukocytes, including neutrophils, lymphocytes, and macrophages. These cells facilitate the granulation process, efficiently eliminate bacteria and necrotic tissue through phagocytosis, and initiate the proliferative phase, leading to the formation of new tissue.[20] Snake fruit peel ethanol extract also exhibits antibacterial activity by damaging bacterial cell membranes, inhibiting metabolism, and interfering with bacterial growth, further aiding in the healing of periodontal tissues.[15]

The proliferative phase is characterized by the formation of granulation tissue and new collagen fibers, with fibroblasts transforming into myofibroblasts, which contract the tissue and aid wound maturation. This phase promotes re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, and fibroplasia.[21] The flavonoid content in snake fruit peel extract, with its antioxidant properties, stimulates the proliferation of cells such as fibroblasts and collagen, which are crucial for tissue regeneration. In addition, the chlorogenic acid in snake fruit peel accelerates wound healing by increasing hydroxyproline content in the tissue.[4748] Saponins and tannins, known as “proangiogenic” compounds, enhance the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, stimulating new cell growth and promoting fibroblast formation.[4950]

The reduction in probing depth scores indicates the maturation phase during periodontal tissue healing.[51] During this phase, the proliferation and migration of fibroblasts promote collagen synthesis, resulting in the formation of the extracellular matrix. The formation of fibroblasts and collagen in periodontal tissue enhances resistance to probing pressure, resulting in clinically observed reductions in probing depth. The potential anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and wound-healing effects of snake fruit peel ethanol extract contribute to this reduction in probing depth through various mechanisms.[47] It is well established that preventing bacterial contamination and infection is a crucial factor in wound healing, as it facilitates normal cell proliferation to close the wound.[1852]

Chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% is the positive control and the gold standard for periodontitis therapy due to its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. However, long-term use can lead to side effects such as dry mouth (xerostomia), altered taste sensation (hypogeusia), calculus formation, and tooth and tongue discoloration.[53] Chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% can induce oral tissue inflammation through local irritation, hypersensitivity reactions, disruption of oral microbiota, and cytotoxic effects.[54] Moreover, its effectiveness against Gram-negative bacteria is limited due to the more complex cell wall structure of these bacteria compared to Gram-positive bacteria, reducing its target sites. The significant decrease in chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% efficacy at the end of the observation period is attributed to its peak effect occurring 2–4 weeks after initial administration, showing a sustained impact on microorganisms. The side effects of chlorhexidine gluconate can be mitigated with natural remedies, such as snake fruit peel extract, which has minimal side effects. Snake fruit peel extract is a promising alternative due to its lack of side effects when used correctly.[5556]

The reduction in gingival recession observed in both groups reflects normal periodontal wound-healing progression following bacterial control and scaling. However, the consistently lower recession values in the extract-treated group indicate enhanced soft-tissue repair.[575859]

Statistical analysis indicated that the group receiving snake fruit peel ethanol extract exhibited a lower average gingival recession score than the positive control group on days 1, 7, and 14. The observed effect results from the phytochemicals in the ethanol extract of snake fruit peel, including flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and alkaloids, which function as antioxidants, anti-inflammatories, and antibacterial agents. The ethanol extract of snake fruit peel promotes angiogenesis, the process of new blood vessel formation, which is critical for delivering nutrients and oxygen necessary for the acceleration of periodontal tissue healing.[434760]

Gingival healing involves epithelial migration, angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation, and collagen remodeling. The phytochemical constituents of S. zalacca peel may accelerate these processes by improving local microcirculation, supporting extracellular matrix formation, and reducing inflammatory-mediated tissue breakdown.

The anti-inflammatory activity of the compounds in snake fruit peel ethanol extract works by inhibiting the COX and lipoxygenase enzymes, thereby accelerating the inflammatory phase, minimizing tissue damage, and speeding up tissue healing or regeneration.[23] The flavonoids in snake fruit peel serve as antibacterial agents through three mechanisms: preventing nucleic acid synthesis, disrupting cell membrane function, and inhibiting energy metabolism.[4761] Tannins in snake fruit peel extract inhibit protein synthesis, leading to incomplete cell wall formation and the death of P. gingivalis bacteria. Saponins act as antibacterial agents by inducing protein and enzyme leakage from P. gingivalis cells and increasing membrane permeability, leading to hemolysis.[62] Alkaloids exhibit antibacterial activity by disrupting the peptidoglycan components of bacterial cell walls, preventing proper cell wall formation, and causing bacterial death.[6364] Snake fruit peel ethanol extract also enhances myofibroblast activity and collagen synthesis, demonstrating its ability to accelerate wound healing.[48]

Chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12%, used as the positive control, remains the gold standard for chemical plaque control due to its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity.[65] Its mechanism involves binding to bacterial cell membranes, increasing permeability, and causing intracellular leakage, leading to bacterial death.[66]

While effective, chlorhexidine does not actively promote tissue regeneration and is associated with adverse effects when used long-term, including tooth discoloration, altered taste sensation, mucosal irritation, xerostomia, and disruption of oral microbiota balance.[6768] These limitations highlight the clinical relevance of exploring natural alternatives such as S. zalacca peel extract, which may provide comparable antimicrobial effects with fewer side effects.[252629] Overall, the findings of this study suggest that S. zalacca peel ethanol extract has the potential to serve as an adjunctive or alternative therapeutic agent for periodontal disease management by combining antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing properties.

This study has several limitations. First, it employed an animal model, and the results may not fully represent periodontal healing in humans. Second, the observation period was relatively short, limiting assessment of long-term periodontal stability. Third, only a single extract concentration was evaluated, preventing dose–response analysis. In addition, histological and molecular evaluations were not performed to confirm tissue-level mechanisms directly.

Future studies should investigate multiple concentrations of S. zalacca peel extract to establish optimal dosing and therapeutic thresholds. Long-term studies are needed to assess sustained periodontal stability and safety. Further research incorporating histopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses would help clarify the underlying biological mechanisms. Ultimately, controlled human clinical trials are required to confirm the clinical applicability of S. zalacca peel ethanol extract in periodontal therapy.

In addition, histological and molecular evaluations were not performed to confirm tissue-level mechanisms directly. Clinical attachment level was calculated from periodontal probing depth and gingival recession values; however, it should be interpreted cautiously because it was not supported by histological or radiographic evaluation. Furthermore, alveolar bone loss was not evaluated in this study. Since alveolar bone destruction is a key feature of periodontitis, the absence of bone loss measurement limits the ability to determine whether the extract influenced periodontal bone preservation or regeneration. Future studies should include radiographic, micro-CT, or histomorphometric assessment of alveolar bone loss.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this study, S. zalacca peel ethanol extract was associated with lower probing depth and gingival recession scores than 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate in Wistar rats with experimentally induced periodontitis. However, this study did not directly evaluate tissue healing, inflammatory biomarkers, collagen formation, angiogenesis, or periodontal regeneration; therefore, conclusions regarding superior healing properties cannot be drawn. Further histological, molecular, microbiological, dose-response, and long-term studies are needed to clarify the therapeutic potential of S. zalacca peel ethanol extract as an adjunctive agent in periodontal therapy.
