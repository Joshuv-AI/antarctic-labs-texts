Understanding occupational self-efficacy for health and meaningfulness at work: a qualitative study among home care workers with threatened work ability
Authors: Stina Wallin, Linda Estman, Malin Andtfolk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetaining home care workers who are experiencing increasing job demands alongside deteriorating occupational health has become a growing concern. Previous research has found occupational self-efficacy, that is belief in one’s own capabilities to successfully fulfil work-related responsibilities, associated with work wellbeing and meaningfulness. The aim of this study was to explore how home care workers who experience threatened occupational health perceive the importance of occupational self-efficacy for their occupational health and their sense of meaningfulness at work.MethodsEight home care workers with threatened work ability, defined as having accumulated 30 days or more of sick leave in the past year, were interviewed. The semi-structured interviews were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsBased on the analysis, an overarching theme—“Occupational self-efficacy, health and meaningfulness at work walking hand in hand”—was generated, together with two main themes. The main themes were “Holding on to myself through the balancing act of health, worth and work”, and “Protecting the value of meaningfulness in the face of organisational challenges”. The findings highlight a reciprocal relationship between occupational self-efficacy, health and meaningfulness at work, however, influenced by work environment.ConclusionsSupporting the inner health and sense of worth of home care workers experiencing ill health enhances sustainable occupational self-efficacy. A balanced sense of meaningfulness at work further contributes to this process. Although occupational self-efficacy is a personal resource, it is strongly influenced by the psychosocial and organisational work environment, underscoring the importance of developing a healthy work environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04989-y.

Background

The deteriorating occupational health and wellbeing of home care workers is causing growing concern at both societal and individual worker levels [1–3]. Concurrently, their workload has increased in recent years [2–5]. Home care workers have reported, among other concerns, rising stress levels, increased physical and mental exhaustion, and sleep disturbances [1, 4, 6, 7]. These trends coincide with increasing time pressure, a growing number of clients, and more complex care needs, often associated with multimorbidity. It is indispensable to aim for reduced work demands and increased resources in home care services. Yet beyond this, personal resources are crucial in managing work-related demands. The personal resource occupational self-efficacy, defined as the belief in one’s own capabilities to successfully fulfil work-related responsibilities [8], plays a crucial role in managing job demands [9], even in the face of adversity [10] and changes [11]. Supporting the occupational self-efficacy of home care workers may contribute positively to their health and well-being at work [12]. In previous research, newly qualified care workers with high occupational self-efficacy were better equipped to cope with work-related demands, adapted more effectively to demanding work environments, experienced lower levels of occupational stress, and demonstrated greater perseverance [13]. Among younger employees [14], positive associations have also been observed between occupational self-efficacy and work ability, similar to findings among older home care workers [15]. Enhancing workers’ self-efficacy beliefs may also increase the perceived meaningfulness of work [16], while meaningfulness may, in turn, strengthen occupational self-efficacy [7]. According to previous research, meaningfulness at work has a positive impact on, for example, work ability [17], work wellbeing, work engagement, job satisfaction and life satisfaction, and is negatively correlated with turnover intentions and sick leave [18].

Due to the increasing workload, a substantial proportion of home care workers are considering changing jobs [4, 5, 7], exacerbating the already existing workforce shortage in the home care sector [19, 20]. At the same time, there is a rising demand for home care services, as many European countries [21], including Finland [22], are prioritising home care over residential care. Furthermore, the major 2023 social and health care reform in Finland, which transferred responsibility for health care and social services from municipalities to larger wellbeing services counties, followed by changes in politics and decision-making affecting home care [23], has prompted concerns regarding the capacity of home care services to meet increasingly complex client needs [24].

According to the WHO definition, occupational health aims to prevent health deterioration caused by working conditions; to “promote and maintain the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being of workers in all occupations” [25]. However, the perceived time pressure experienced by home care workers [3, 26] during client visits, as well as the insufficient number of staff in relation to clients’ needs have raised concerns about home care workers’ ability to cope with the workload [3]. Therefore, prioritizing the occupational health of home care workers is essential to ensure adequate staffing and the provision of safe home care [27]. Nevertheless, the health of home care workers should also be regarded as a priority in its own right, and not merely as a means of sustaining service delivery.

A substantial amount of quantitative research has been conducted on this topic, whereas fewer qualitative studies have explored how workers themselves experience the importance of occupational self-efficacy for health and meaningfulness at work, particularly in the context of home care. Furthermore, research is needed on concrete applications of occupational self-efficacy to strengthen occupational health [28–30]. Understanding these relationships is particularly important in home care work, where meaningfulness is often described as a key factor for motivation and retention despite high job demands. Based on previous research, the aim of this study was to gain a deeper understanding of how home care workers with threatened occupational health perceive the significance of occupational self-efficacy for their occupational health and for their meaningfulness at work.

Theoretical perspectives

The occupational self-efficacy theory

In line with previous research, we assert that occupational self-efficacy may serve as an important personal resource in promoting occupational health. According to self-efficacy theory, individuals’ belief in their capabilities vary across different activities and situations [10, 31, 32]. Thus, the domain specific occupational self-efficacy refers to one’s belief in their own capabilities to successfully fulfil work-related commitments [8]. Individuals with high self-efficacy act proactively and believe they possess the capability to produce desired outcomes. They are motivated, can cope with affective states, facilitate needed thought patterns, and are resilient and persevere when facing obstacles and difficulties [10, 31, 32].

According to the theory, self-efficacy can be strengthened in four ways [10, 31, 32]. The most powerful source is mastery experiences—successful experiences of overcoming difficulties and managing failures through perseverant effort develop resilient self-efficacy. The second source is vicarious experiences—observing others similar to oneself succeed. The third involves receiving constructive feedback and social support that reinforce one’s belief in having necessary capabilities to succeed, which helps to maintain perseverance in the face of obstacles. The fourth source concerns the interpretation of and coping with physiological and affective states in challenging situations, thereby reducing stress levels [10, 31, 32]. Self-efficacy is essential in home care work, since home care workers are required on a daily basis to manage unforeseen events and primarily work alone, which requires a belief in their own capabilities.

The job demands-resources model

The role of occupational self-efficacy in relation to occupational health can be understood in light of the comprehensive framework of the Job Demands-Resources (J D-R) model [33, 34]. The J D-R model emphasises the importance of personal resources. Personal resources refer to an individual’s psychological capacities, such as self-efficacy, resilience, optimism and hope [33, 34]. Job demands, in turn, refer to physical, mental, social, or organisational job-related strains that require physical and/or mental effort from the worker to cope with—such as work overload, organisational changes, and interpersonal conflicts. Job resources are positive physical, psychological, social, or organisational aspects of work that help reduce job demands, facilitate the achievement of work goals, and promote personal development. Examples include job control, effective communication, and support and feedback from others. Job demands and resources initiate two processes: a health impairment process and a motivational process. Job demands primarily lead to strain-related outcomes, while job and personal resources buffer the negative effects of work-related demands [33, 34]. Although workers with sufficient personal resources do not necessarily perceive fewer demands, they tend to be more resistant to adverse conditions and more confident in their abilities to cope with emerging demands [33]. Moreover, personal resources can positively influence job resources, and vice versa; job resources have been shown to predict the development of personal resources.

Together, the perspectives of occupational self-efficacy and the J D-R model can serve as a complementary framework for understanding how the personal resources of home care workers interact with the work environment and affect occupational health. In this study, these frameworks informed both the development of the interview guide and the interpretation of the findings.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This study was a qualitative interview study with an inductive approach. The invitation to participate was sent via email conveyed by the management in the organisation where the participants worked. Participation was voluntary, and management had no information regarding who chose to participate. Inclusion criterion was employment in home care services within a purposively selected wellbeing services county in Finland. The county is one of 21 wellbeing services counties responsible for organising public health care, social welfare, and rescue services. It comprises 14 rural and urban municipalities and serves approximately 180,000 residents, with around 8,000 employees. The organisation was chosen because it had reported high levels of sick leave among home care workers. A further inclusion criterion was having threatened work ability, operationalised as having been on sick leave for a minimum of 30 days during the past year. The criterion of sick leave lasting 30 days was chosen because this is the level that must be reported to the organisation’s occupational health provider in order to ensure that appropriate support can be provided to the worker [35].

A pre-tested, semi-structured interview guide with open-ended key questions was developed in Swedish for this study, based on previous research [7, 15, 30]. This ensured that the same themes were addressed in all interviews while still allowing flexibility to follow participants’ accounts [36]. The interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1. To reach a shared understanding of the concept of ‘occupational self-efficacy‘, a description of the concept was presented to the participants at the beginning of the interview, and they were able to return to it during the interview. Presenting this description of the concept ensured that all participants understood the concept in the same way. This strengthened the dependability of the data collection by ensuring consistency while still allowing participants to articulate their experiences freely. Eight interviews were conducted, in Swedish, in December 2024 by the first author, either face-to-face or digitally, according to the participants’ preferences. The interviews lasted between 45 and 75 minutes and were audio recorded.

The authors of this study acknowledge that eight interview participants do not constitute a large sample size. Nonetheless, the authors determined that these interviewees generated rich and nuanced data to meet the study objectives. In reflexive thematic analysis, depth and meaning are prioritised over numerical saturation, and this dataset provided sufficient depth and richness to support the development of coherent and analytically robust themes [37].

Finnish context of home care work

In Finland, home care includes day-to-day assistance and nursing services aimed to support individuals with reduced functional capacity—due to aging, illness, or disability—in coping with their everyday activities and needs [38, 39]. The aim is to enable them to live at home for as long as possible. Home care includes personal and health care, provided based on an assessment of health status, functional capacity, and individual needs, followed by a care plan. In Finland, home care is primarily provided by the public sector through the wellbeing services counties, although services may be outsourced to private providers [38, 39]. Most home care workers are licensed practical nurses with a three-year vocational education, supported by registered nurses, home support workers, and, to a lesser extent, therapists such as physiotherapists and occupational therapists [1, 40].

In this article, the term “home care workers” will be used to refer to all personnel working in home care.

Participants

The participants interviewed comprised six practical nurses with a vocational education degree and two registered nurses with a bachelor’s degree. All were employed in home care services within the same wellbeing services county. The participants included both women and men, aged between 29 and 49 years. On average, they had 13 years of experience working in home care. During the past year, six of them had been on sick leave for between 30 and 180 days. When two of the participants arrived for the interview session, it emerged that they had a maximum of nine days of sick leave during the past year. Nevertheless, their experiences of perceived strain and threatened work ability were consistent with the study focus, and their interviews were retained to enhance analytical depth. The variation in age and gender, sick leave duration, and the extensive experience of home care work, strengthened our study.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [41–43]. This approach was chosen as it allows for an in-depth exploration of participants’ experiences and meaning-making processes, while acknowledging the active interpretative role of the researchers. All interviews were transcribed verbatim by the first author (SW), which facilitated early familiarisation with the data. The analysis followed the six-phase process outlined by Braun and Clarke [41–43].

In the first phase, all authors familiarised themselves with the data through repeated readings of the transcripts to gain a holistic understanding of the material. Initial reflections and analytic notes were documented to capture early impressions and potential patterns of meaning. In the second phase, initial codes were generated inductively from the data. The first cycle of coding was conducted separately by the authors, with each coding a subset of the interviews. Coding focused on identifying meaningful segments related to occupational self-efficacy, occupational health, and meaningfulness at work. The analysis was primarily data-driven, although the conceptual framework informed our attention to issues related to occupational self-efficacy and job resources [43].

In the third phase, codes were brought together and discussed among the authors to explore patterns of shared meaning across the dataset. Through iterative and reflexive dialogue, codes were clustered into preliminary themes that captured broader interpretative patterns. Differing in interpretations among the researchers were discussed reflexively and used to deepen the understanding of the data rather than to achieve coding agreement [42, 43]. During the fourth and fifth phases, themes were reviewed, refined, and defined in close engagement with the entire dataset. This involved returning repeatedly to the transcripts to ensure that themes were internally coherent, clearly distinguishable from one another, and meaningfully grounded in participants’ accounts while offering interpretative depth. Through this iterative process, the thematic structure was clarified and organised into one overarching theme, two main themes and four subthemes. The two main themes represented overarching patterns of meaning in the dataset, while the subthemes supported and exemplified these by illuminating more specific aspects of participants’ experiences.

In the final phase, the analytic narrative was developed, and the themes were contextualised in relation to occupational self-efficacy theory and the Job Demands–Resources model. Throughout the analysis, reflexive discussions were conducted to critically consider how the researchers’ professional backgrounds in caring science and occupational health might influence interpretation. Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, the process emphasised reflexivity, transparency, and interpretative depth rather than inter-rater reliability or coding consensus as indicators of rigour [42, 43].

Holding on to myself through the balancing act of health, worth and work

Participants in this study stressed the importance of respecting individuals living with ill health and supporting a healthy work-life balance in order to maintain occupational self-efficacy beliefs.

Breaking the silence: permission and courage to speak about ill health

The participants stressed the importance of open workplace dialogue about ill health and its consequences. They expressed that such an open dialogue climate would strengthen their sense of security and confidence when discussing health-related concerns and their impact on both work and private life. The participants also expressed a need for support in maintaining their self-esteem when experiencing poor health. They wanted to feel valued—equally to their healthier colleagues—and appreciated for who they are. They also wanted to feel safe disclosing when they were having a bad day and to be allowed to take sick leave without feeling blamed when genuinely ill, enabling them to prioritise their health and recover more effectively. On the other hand, taking sick leave sometimes also served to protect the clients and colleagues from becoming ill. To maintain their energy and self-efficacy beliefs, they needed to change the way they worked. Such strategies included limiting their focus to client-related tasks, reducing their work performance and extent to which they helped colleagues, coping with stressful situations, and omitted work-related information they did not consider particularly important. Boundary-setting was also described as essential for maintaining a sustainable balance between work and leisure. When the workload was manageable, participants had sufficient energy to engage in leisure activities and enjoy a fulfilling personal life. Meaningful leisure time enabled them to mentally detach from work, restore their energy, and cultivate a positive view of life. Feeling well in their private lives was perceived to positively influence their occupational self-efficacy. Protecting the value of meaningfulness in the face of organisational challenges

Even though participants described home care work as deeply meaningful, the growing work‑related burden posed a threat to their sense of meaningfulness. As meaningfulness at work enhanced their occupational self-efficacy, this increasing burden also threatened their self-efficacy beliefs.

The power of client meetings: mutual giving and receiving

The participants emphasised that experiencing a sense of meaningfulness at work was highly important. They described home care work as deeply meaningful, both in terms of giving and receiving. They particularly highlighted the relationships with clients and supporting their well-being, enabling them to remain in their own homes for as long as possible. Engaging with clients and responding to their unique needs through support, care and conversations—and witnessing their ability to develop and cope with daily challenges—formed the foundation of work meaningfulness. Although home care work was sometimes perceived as demanding, especially due to the home care workers’ own health issues, interactions with clients were generally described as compensating for these challenges. Feeling needed further contributed to a deeper sense of meaningfulness.It really is about the clients. They are the ones you live for. They are the ones who have built the society for me. So of course they should get the best of me. … And that is truly meaningful for me (P1)

Receiving appreciation, consideration, and expressions of gratitude further enhanced the participants’ sense of meaningfulness at work. While appreciation was mostly received from the clients, recognition from clients’ relatives, colleagues, and home care supervisors was also considered important. Moreover, a strong sense of community with colleagues also contributed to the overall sense of meaningfulness at work. Nonetheless, the participants acknowledged that they did not always fully internalise the positive feedback they received.It is the positive feedback. The threats of organisational changes: staying for the meaningfulness

The organisational changes and cost-saving measures implemented in recent years were perceived as placing considerable demands on home care staff, resulting in sustained pressure and stress. These conditions, along with the uncertainty brought about by the organisational changes considering how to address practical issues in client care, further undermined this sense of meaningfulness at work. The participants felt stressed when routines no longer functioned as smoothly as before. Being able to focus solely on the clients’ care again would increase their sense of meaningfulness. Although some participants had considered changing occupation due to health-related challenges, the perceived meaningfulness at work –and the joy it brought– served as strong incentives to remain in the profession.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to gain a deeper understanding of how home care workers who experience threatened occupational health perceive the significance of occupational self-efficacy for their occupational health and for their meaningfulness at work. The results showed a fundamental reciprocal interrelationship between occupational self-efficacy, health and perceived meaningfulness at work. At the same time, these interrelationships should be understood within the broader organisational and psychosocial context in which home care work is currently situated.

The participants emphasised that a strong sense of occupational self-efficacy improved their ability to cope with health issues as well as with challenging job demands. Conversely, their perceived health influenced their belief in own capabilities to manage daily work tasks and work-related challenges. This reciprocal relationship between health and occupational self-efficacy becomes particularly critical, as home care work has become more complex, demanding and burdensome [4, 5, 7], is mostly provided alone [44], and therefore relies on the home care worker’s own confidence, skills and knowledge. Demographic changes and the growing demand for home care services [22] call for home care workers who both wish to remain in the sector and are able to continue working in good health. Therefore, maintaining the health of home care workers is not merely an individual matter; it is an issue of significance for society.

A central finding in this study was the participants’ expressed need to feel equally worthy despite their health challenges. They wanted to be treated with dignity by their leaders and colleagues, to be recognised and respected for their intrinsic worth, without being devalued or judged due to their ill health. The participants’ emphasis on dignity and equal worth can be understood as the essence of health, a force of inner energy and vitality [45]. Inner health includes core values of dignity, self-respect, personal excellence and quality of life [46]. These core values are crucial for occupational health. Without inner health, health and well-being will be incomplete. Experiencing poor health entails suffering for the human being. A reduced sense of self-worth because of health issues adds further to this burden. Individuals suffering from chronic conditions sometimes have the validity of their symptoms questioned [47]. This can further drain their already limited energy, thereby reducing occupational self-efficacy and work ability [48]. Thus, the findings of this study reinforced the importance of supporting workers’ inner health when aiming to strengthen their occupational self-efficacy. This goes beyond the regulations, procedures and practices to ensure a safe and healthy daily practical work [46]. To support the entire health, work and organisations must support the whole human being.

An open dialogue and a respectful and safe discussion climate in the workplace were asked for by the participants in this study. Such dialogue may enhance the understanding of the consequences of poor health, including how it affects the whole human being, and thereby help to alleviate their suffering [45]. An open dialogue may also facilitate the ability to set the boundaries the participants perceived as necessary, without feeling quilt. The participants needed boundary setting in order to prioritise work tasks and duties appropriately to protect their health and maintain a sustainable work-life balance. This open dialogue could facilitate support in the workplace. Support from both leaders and colleagues was asked for. Understanding and supporting leaders and colleagues have been found essential for home care workers’ occupational self-efficacy and their belief to continue working in their profession in previous research as well [7]. To prevent home care workers from leaving their work and profession, improved support from leaders is a key factor [5]. Thus, leaders’ abilities to provide support must be targeted, both in terms of their supporting skills and needed time and resources. Good relationship with colleagues also contributes to well-being at work [49]. Such relationship includes understanding, trust, friendship and a permissive atmosphere where care workers offer help to each other, which are in concordance with the participants’ needs in this study.

Experiencing meaningfulness at work was considered highly important by the participants in this study. Home care work was perceived as meaningful in terms of giving and receiving. Giving reflected meaningfulness through the characteristics of home care work—providing meaningful tasks and appropriate care to the clients and their contribution to clients’ health and to their ability to continue living in their homes. Receiving, in turn, reflected the perceived meaningfulness at work, including social relationship with clients and colleagues, perceived support, appreciation, and feelings of competence. This mutual exchange requires opportunity and time to connect with the clients, to communicate and identify needs. When time pressure is high, this connection may suffer, as may the perceived meaningfulness. These aspects of both giving and receiving can be understood as job resources within the framework of JD‑R theory [33]. An imbalance between these job resources and perceived job demands may lead to a conflict between home care workers’ desire to provide sufficient care (meaningfulness through giving) and their experienced sense of meaningfulness when the workload no longer allows for meaningful relationships and mutual exchanges with clients and colleagues (meaningfulness through receiving). When job demands and workload become so high that workers no longer experience meaningfulness, their burden may increase even further. However, in this study, when workload was high, sense of meaningfulness emerged as an important factor influencing home care workers’ decision to remain in the profession. Meaningfulness thus appears as a double-edged resource: sustaining retention while simultaneously risking self-sacrifice.

In this study, completing meaningful tasks enhanced the participants’ occupational self-efficacy through a sense of successful accomplishment. Experiencing success by completing (challenging) demands is one of the cornerstones of self-efficacy theory [10, 33]. Previous research has shown that feeling skilled in the demanding home care work also strengthened home care workers’ belief in their capabilities to retain in home care work, whereas the constant sense of hurry posed a threat [7]. Feeling inadequate—because the workload prevents workers from providing proper care to clients—has been reported by one third of care workers in Nordic countries [5]. Home care in which only mandatory care needs are met increases home care workers’ worries about the clients and thereby erodes their work ability [50]. Home care workers who are required to act against their values, professional competence and ethics have an increased intention to leave the work and profession [50]. Previous quantitative research among Nordic home care workers has identified burden—expressed as mental and physical exhaustion, pain and sleep difficulties—as the strongest predictor of leaving work and the profession [5]. Lack of time and resources for client work causes an ethical burden for home care workers [50] and affects the quality of care [5], thereby increasing their intentions to leave the work [5, 50]. Workers’ previous experiences of how their physical and affective reactions to burden and stress affected their performance influence their current reactions and, consequently, their self-efficacy [10]. Improved coping with overly elevated reactions enhances self-efficacy beliefs [10]. In turn, enhancing self-efficacy may improve workers’ abilities to cope with adverse job demands [33]. However, strengthening personal resources should not diminish the importance of creating healthy workplaces. Retaining care workers requires that the burden and stress they experience be adequately addressed [5].

In this study, there was also an interplay between work environmental aspects and organisational changes that affected home care worker’s perceived occupational self-efficacy, health, and their ability to cope with the challenges they encountered in working life. To retain home care workers in their work and profession alleviating perceived workload and burden is crucial [4, 5, 7]. An adequate physical and mental work environment is required to maintain well-being [7, 51, 52]. An EU policy recommendation underscores that workers have frequently been forgotten in the shadow of projections concerning future development and assessment of staffing levels [52]. The recommendation emphasises the responsibility “to care for those who are caring”. According to the recommendation, building sustainable and resilient workforces and systems are required. Improving working conditions is essential to maintain and promote workers’ health and well-being, as well as to ensure a healthy work-life balance [52]. Meaningful work, support from leaders, social relationship and positive work atmosphere embody values that contribute to a healthy workplace [46].

Methodological considerations

A strength of our study is that interview data were collected from participants with a variation in age and gender, and with extensive experience of home care work. Most of the participants had long-term experience of ill health, i.e., more than 30 days of sick leave during the previous year, which was important for the study aim. Two of the participants had shorter sick leave periods, which may have influenced their experiences of working despite ill health and, consequently, the credibility of the study. Although they did not fully meet the predefined sick leave criterion, their accounts reflected perceived strain and experiences of threatened work ability that were closely aligned with the study aim. Including these interviews contributed to the richness and variation of perspectives in the dataset.

The researchers who performed the analysis consisted of one professor (LE) and two post-doctoral researchers (SW and MA), all with experience in research and qualitative analysis. The researchers also had experience in care work across different settings. One researcher had expertise in occupational health and work environment issues, as well as from rehabilitation work with home care workers. To enhance credibility, the researchers engaged in continuous reflexive discussions throughout the analytic process, examining how their professional backgrounds and pre-understandings might influence interpretation [42, 43]. The researchers each carried out the initial coding of a few interviews, followed by reflexive discussions within the entire research group to explore and refine interpretations of the results of the data analysis. Divergent interpretations were treated as valuable analytic resources that contributed to refining and strengthening the thematic structure [42, 43].

The transferability of the findings is strengthened by participants’ rich descriptions of their occupational self-efficacy, health, experiences of meaningfulness, and work environment. However, transferability may be limited by the specific organisational context and the purposive selection of a single wellbeing services county. Finally, although remote interviews may limit the ability to capture non-verbal cues, offering both digital and face-to-face participation options likely increased participation and enabled the inclusion of workers who might otherwise have been unable to attend, thereby enhancing the study’s inclusiveness.

Conclusions

Taken together, the findings of this study suggest that home care workers’ occupational self-efficacy is not merely an individual belief but is relationally and organisationally embedded. It is sustained through dignity, meaningful relationships with clients, colleagues and leaders, and supportive workplace dialogue, yet simultaneously threatened by excessive job demands and structural constraints. Strengthening occupational self-efficacy may therefore benefit from interventions that address both personal and organisational resources. Practical strategies may include supportive leadership practices, opportunities for dialogue about health challenges, employee participation in decision making, manageable workloads, and organisational structures that allow home care workers to maintain meaningful relationships with clients.
