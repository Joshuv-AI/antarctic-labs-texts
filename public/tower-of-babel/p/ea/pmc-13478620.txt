Propagation effects in high harmonic generation media driven by bright squeezed vacuum light
Authors: Javier Rivera-Dean, Deeksha Kanti, Philipp Stammer, Stefanos Carlström, Nikolaos Tsatrafyllis, Misha Yu Ivanov, Maciej Lewenstein, Paraskevas Tzallas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent developments in quantum light engineering have enabled the use of infrared bright squeezed vacuum (BSV) femtosecond pulses in nonlinear optics, including strong-field physics and high-harmonic generation. However, theory has focused on single-atom interactions, neglecting the crucial macroscopic aspect of light propagation through media. This raises a key question: How does BSV propagate in nonlinear media, and how this affects the nonlinear signals and the radiation’s quantumness? We address this with a fully quantized framework for propagation in gas media undergoing high-harmonic generation. We find that ionization and the associated photon losses cause decoherence, which limits the propagation length, degrades radiation’s quantumness, and reduces the emitted harmonics. However, these effects are not detrimental. We identify conditions that minimize propagation-induced decoherence while preserving detectable harmonics with super-Poissonian photon statistics. Our results establish fully quantized basis for exploring BSV in nonlinear optics, strong-field-quantum optics, and ultrafast science in all states of matter.Subject terms: Quantum optics, High-harmonic generation, Atomic and molecular interactions with photons

Introduction

Squeezed states of light, first developed decades ago, are one of the key resources for quantum technologies1,2. Recent advances in quantum light engineering have dramatically increased their brightness, enabling the generation of bright squeezed vacuum (BSV) states in the infrared (IR) regime3–5. Together with recent advantages in generating high-photon-number non-classical states and multimode entangled light6–23, these sources form key pillars of a rapidly emerging field often referred to as strong-field quantum optics. Born out of the synergy between quantum optics and strong laser-field physics, this field aims to merge tools and methods of these disciplines to both develop new approaches for fundamental research and novel applications in quantum information and ultrafast science at the fully quantized level17,24–27.

BSV has already been employed in nonlinear optics for the generation of low-order harmonics in optical crystals4, multiphoton28 and tunneling-induced29 electron emission, electron motion30, atomic ionization31,32, and to probe the role of statistical properties of light in atomic spectroscopy33. High-photon-number squeezed states, and in particular BSV states, have also emerged as promising drivers of extreme nonlinear processes, in particular high-harmonic generation (HHG)34–45, where theoretical studies have shown that IR squeezed light can produce harmonics with photon energies beyond those of conventional laser pulses34,36,39. Moreover, it has been recently proposed that squeezed coherent states over a wide spectral range can be engineered via laser interactions inducing strong ground-state depletion46, through atoms resonantly excited or initially prepared in excited states17,47, or by driving HHG in strongly correlated materials16,20,48.

So far, however, theoretical investigations of BSV-driven HHG have focused on the single-atom response. As emphasized in refs. 27,49, assessing the applicability of such light sources requires incorporating the interplay of essential mechanisms, such as ionization, ground state depletion, and field propagation through the medium. While these aspects have been extensively studied for intense coherent femtosecond laser drivers using semiclassical approaches50–53, and for low photon number light sources using fully quantized approaches54–58, they remain largely unexplored for intense BSV light sources due to the lack of fully quantized approaches capable of describing the propagation of intense BSV light in highly nonlinear media.

Here, we fill this gap by introducing a fully quantized framework that accounts for the propagation of intense BSV in strongly driven nonlinear media. Focusing on a gas medium, we analyze conditions under which the BSV quantum features get degraded due to fundamental processes occurring during propagation: strong-field ionization (SFI), whether in multiphoton (MPI) or tunneling (TI) regimes, scattering, and high harmonic generation (HHG) (Fig. 1a). We consider the interaction of Argon (Ar) atoms with intense, linearly polarized BSV light carried at λ = 800 nm, for multi-cycle femtosecond pulses. Using HHG as a nonlinear observable, we reveal the central role of decoherence, the probabilistic nature of nonlinear interactions, the atomic ionization and ground-state depletion induced by the BSV pulse. Under conditions that preserve the quantum properties of BSV, we determine the number of high-harmonic photons generated and benchmark the results against those obtained with conventional coherent laser light sources. We find that the considered intensity observables are compatible with those produced by classical stochastic fields with similar intensity statistics and, by themselves, cannot reveal non-classical features59,60. Accordingly, we analyze how the photon statistics of the emitted harmonics, as well as the phase-space fluctuations of the driving field, are modified after the interaction with the Ar gas.

Results

Atomic ionization in a BSV light field

Our starting point is a fully quantum description based on a complete phase-space representation, the generalized positive-P formalism34,61, which exactly represents the density operator (“Methods”) and employs the canonical commutation relation of the field operators.

Equivalently, Q(Eα) gives the probability of having an electric field amplitude Eα (with Eα ∝ α) and intensity Iα=cϵ02Eα2 (where c is the speed of light in vacuum and ϵ0 is the vacuum permittivity) within ρ^. Hereafter, for reasons of simplicity, we omit the subscript α from the amplitude distribution, i.e., IαequivI and Eα ≡ E.

One of the defining features of Q(E) for BSV light is its remarkably broad nature (Fig. 1b for 〈I〉 = 1.3 × 1014 W/cm2), which reflects the intrinsic field fluctuations of high-intensity squeezed light along the antisqueezed quadrature. This behavior contrasts sharply with that of coherent states, whose distribution under the same mean intensity conditions is narrowly peaked around E ≈ 3 × 108 V/cm. Such a distinction has direct experimental implications: while coherent light sources yield nearly identical intensities across successive laser shots, BSV pulses exhibit much larger shot-to-shot fluctuations, as encoded in Q(E). On average, these fluctuations follow ⟨I⟩∝sinh2(r), where r is the squeezing amplitude, roughly corresponding to r ≈ 17 for obtaining 〈I〉 ≈ 1.3 × 1014 W/cm2 under experimental conditions. This pronounced broadening of the field distribution is therefore expected to strongly influence atomic ionization.

To illustrate this effect, Fig. 1c shows the total ionization yield for both coherent (blue) and BSV (in black) light interacting with Ar atoms. The driving field is modeled as a pulse E(t)=Ef(t)cos(ωt+θ), with f(t) taken as a sin2-envelope of duration τ = 13 fs. For interactions with coherent light states, the ionization yield 〈Yi〉 ≈ 2% occurs at intensities ≈ 1.3 × 1014 W/cm2, while for interactions with BSV, due to the enlarged field fluctuations, the ionization is substantially higher at much lower intensities, providing the same ionization yield at intensities ≈9 × 1012 W/cm2. In the following sections, we discuss how this strong ionization induced by the BSV significantly affects both the quantum properties of the BSV field and the HHG process during light propagation in a medium.

We address these effects in two stages. First, independently of harmonic propagation, we analyze the squeezing degradation of the driving BSV field using an effective beam-splitter model for a Gaussian loss channel62, which preserves canonical commutation relations and accounts for vacuum noise introduced by absorption. This allows us to determine the absorption threshold at which squeezing degrades by a given amount. Second, we study the field propagation using macroscopic quantum electrodynamics, focusing on mean values of observables, specifically intensities of the generated light. In this case, the medium effectively acts as a classical energy sink, and the propagation can be solved using classical nonlinear wave equations, with the quantum properties of the driving field entering through Q(E). Our approach (see SI) is based on the positive P-representation61, which fully incorporates the canonical commutation relations for the electromagnetic field operators. The evolution intrinsically includes deterministic drift terms describing Hamiltonian dynamics, damping, as well as fluctuations. In this sense, it is equivalent to rigorous quantum Langevin approaches63, where drift terms correspond to the Hamiltonian evolution and fluctuating Langevin forces encode quantum noise64. For the mean field observables considered here, quantum diffusion terms vanish on average and play no role in the results.

Decoherence effects of the BSV field in nonlinear media

Quantum features of non-classical light fields are highly sensitive to decoherence effects arising from photon losses caused by scattering and absorption as light propagates through a medium. For interactions of gaseous media with light fields of intensities reaching the strong field region, the majority of IR photon losses originate from absorption due to strong ionization, while those caused by scattering and the HHG process itself can be considered negligible (“Methods”). The strong ionization modifies the quantum properties of the BSV state, transforming the initial pure state of the driving field BSVin to a generally mixed state ρ^out (Fig. 1a). To quantify this impact, we analyze the evolution of the Wigner function W(X1, X2) (Fig. 2a) and the deviation of the product of the optical quadrature variances from the Heisenberg uncertainty limit, i.e., (ΔX12)(ΔX22)−1 (Fig. 2b) under photon losses62 (see SI and Supplementary Figs. S1 and 2). The optical quadratures are defined as X^1=(a^†+a^) and X^2=i(a^−a^†). The product reaches the Heisenberg limit, with ρ^out corresponding either to a pure BSV (no absorption A = 0) or a vacuum state (maximum absorption where all the IR photons are lost, A = 1). For intermediate losses, the state is noisy: although the quadrature distributions retain some stretching reminiscent of squeezing, these features do not correspond to genuine quantum squeezing below the vacuum level. Propagation of BSV in nonlinear media and HHG

Having established the interplay between BSV driving fields and SFI, we now turn to the HHG response. For single-atom interactions, Fig. 3a shows the harmonic yield 〈Nq〉 of the 15th harmonic as a function of 〈I〉 for both types of fields. In the case of BSV-driven interactions, 〈Nq〉 reaches its maximum within the intensity range ≈6 × 1013 to ≈2 × 1014 W/cm2. For coherent light, the corresponding range is narrower, exhibiting a peak around ≈1.3 × 1014 W/cm2. Hereafter, we define Isat = 1.3 × 1014 W/cm2 as the harmonic saturation intensity, i.e., the value yielding the maximum harmonic output for both fields. This intensity range is commonly employed in HHG and attosecond experiments using the interaction of Ar atoms with intense coherent light52,53,65, as it provides maximum harmonic yield while keeps the ionization at low levels 〈Yi〉 ≈ 2% (Fig. 1c), conditions which is important for maintaining the phase matching conditions and attosecond pulse formation. For a BSV field at this intensity range, although the harmonic yield is maximum, the ionization level is also high 〈Yi〉 ≈ 50% (Fig. 1c), an issue that substantially affects the quantum properties of the BSV and the harmonic yield exiting the medium. We also restrict our analysis to this regime, since we can roughly consider that for both fields (BSV and coherent), at higher intensities the yield decreases due to ground-state depletion, while at lower intensities is suppressed by the HHG nonlinearity.

In this analysis, the mean photon number of the qth harmonic 〈Nq〉 is evaluated as ⟨Nq⟩=∫dEQ(E)∣FT[⟨ψE(t)∣d^∣ψE(t)⟩]∣2, where d^ is the dipole operator, FT[⋅] denotes the Fourier transform, and ψE(t) is the electron state driven by E(t), which incorporates ground-state depletion via the ionization rate Γ (“Methods”). This procedure amounts to tracing over the relative phase coherence between realizations, which is appropriate for intensity-based observables but does not apply to phase-sensitive measurements. For BSV drivers, it effectively neglects quantum coherence between the different coherent-state realizations of the BSV, especially in the case of strong absorption of the driving field by the medium. This absorption inevitably entangles light with the medium during propagation. Thus, as long as the observation traces out the final state of the generating medium, the quantum coherence between the different BSV realizations will be lost.

At low intensities 〈I〉 ≪ Isat the BSV-driven harmonics dominate, thanks to the enhanced field fluctuations which allow the BSV to sample higher intensities. At 〈I〉 ~ Isat the single-atom harmonic yield is already strongly suppressed by the strong ground-state depletion, again due to the high intensity fluctuations of the BSV. As a result, around Isat, the single-atom HHG yield for the BSV driver is over an order of magnitude lower than that of the coherent state driver. Similar behavior was observed for all plateau harmonics (not shown in Fig. 3a for simplicity). Once 〈I〉 > Isat, depletion effects dominate, leading to a rapid reduction of the HHG field. The suppression is much faster for the coherent-state driver, as the BSV-driven atoms benefit from sampling lower intensities. Notably, while HHG driven by coherent states exhibits an oscillatory behavior in the yield due to quantum path interference66, these interferences are washed out by the intensity fluctuations of the BSV driver.

The broadening of the electric field amplitudes also explains the small extension of the highest harmonics observed for BSV compared to coherent light sources34. Figure 3b shows the HHG spectrum for both fields at 〈I〉 = Isat, with a cutoff at the 29th harmonic for coherent light and at the 33rd for BSV. We note, however, that these spectra would correspond to multi-shot averages over single-atom responses and, at this stage, do not include propagation or decoherence effects. Importantly, while Nq(I) and Yi(I) at a specific intensity I cannot be observed experimentally for a BSV driver, they play a crucial role in the propagation effects and the resulting decoherence. These effects are unavoidable in any experimental configuration and significantly influence the emitted HHG radiation.

The qualitative difference between the two regimes originates from the interplay between the strong nonlinearity of HHG, depletion effects, and the Husimi distribution. The dependence of g(2)(0) on 〈I〉 is shown in Fig. 4c. For low intensities 〈I〉, depletion effects are negligible over the entire Husimi distribution, leading to correspondingly large values of g(2)(0). As 〈I〉 increases, depletion becomes progressively more significant, particularly in the tails of the Husimi distribution. As a result, harmonic emission is effectively confined to its central region, which in turn leads to a reduction of g(2)(0). At 〈I〉 ≈ 5 × 1013 W/cm2, depletion reaches almost its maximum, and g(2)(0) reduces to its minimum value, reflecting the fact that the harmonic signal is predominantly generated from the central part of the Husimi distribution. For 〈I〉 > 5 × 1013 W/cm2, g(2)(0) slightly increases again, reaching values slightly above its minimum. This behavior can be attributed to the strongly nonlinear nature of the HHG process, which enables contributions from regions of higher Husimi distribution values, while ionization-induced depletion remains approximately constant (see Fig. 1c). Because in these Husimi regions, the field fluctuations are confined to high values, they can be significantly narrower than those of the BSV field. Under such conditions, quantum interference between electron pathways or channel-closing effects may arise—an effect typically observed for coherent light states50,51,66. Although this issue requires further investigation beyond the scope of the present work, such effects could lead to a periodic low-amplitude dependence of g(2)(0) on 〈I〉, as shown in Fig. 4a at high 〈I〉. Finally, we note that the intensity dependence of g(2)(0) for low-order harmonics generated by coherent light, as well as their entanglement, has been theoretically studied in ref. 69. While these results are not directly comparable to those in Fig. 4 (which involve nonclassical driving fields), the framework of ref. 69 could be extended to the present case to enable entanglement certification of high-order harmonics generated by BSV light, including propagation effects.

Discussion

In recent theoretical studies involving the ideal case of interaction with a single atom, it has been shown that the harmonic yield and the number of generated harmonic frequencies increase dramatically when using BSV pulses compared to intense coherent light states34. However, our findings show that propagation effects in the medium limit the practical use of intense BSV sources in strong-field physics and HHG compared to intense coherent light. These limitations arise from decoherence due to photon losses during propagation—primarily from enhanced ionization in the medium—as well as from the broader intensity distribution of BSV states. Consequently, the effective propagation length in the medium is reduced, and the probability of observing events with sufficiently high intensities to generate high harmonics is lower than for coherent states. Moreover, the nonlinearity of the processes driven by intense BSV light, combined with atomic ground-state depletion, further constraints the HHG yield. As a result of the above, when the HHG process is driven by BSV light in a gas medium, the number of emitted harmonic frequencies is very similar to those produced by coherent light, while the harmonic yield is more than 50 times lower than that of the coherent light states.

Nevertheless, the ability to detect a highly nonlinear observable, such as high harmonics, remains of significant interest for applications in ultrafast science and quantum technologies25–27. Thus, it is crucial to address the conditions at which the BSV harmonics are detectable. To quantify this, we used experimentally and theoretically established HHG conversion efficiencies for Ar atoms (“Methods”). For plateau harmonics driven by a BSV driving field of mean photon number ~1013 photons per pulse5, a medium length of Lm(BSV), yields ~5 × 102 harmonic photons per pulse exiting the Ar medium. Such photon numbers are detectable.

An advantage of BSV light over standard coherent states emerges in the intensity range 〈I〉 < Isat—a regime in which coherent light states cannot produce harmonics. In this way, we can minimize or even eliminate decoherence effects, since photon losses caused by atomic ionization are significantly reduced (Fig. 2c, d), i.e., A ≪ 12%. For example, it can be estimated (“Methods”) that for BSV light with 〈I〉 ~ 1013 W/cm2, the emitted photon number is in the range of 20 photons per pulse, which remains detectable. Therefore, this intensity regime can also be considered suitable for the generation of high harmonics without BSV light being affected by decoherence effects. It is furthermore important to emphasize that none of the results presented here can be taken as proof of the quantum nature of a high-photon-number BSV source, or as evidence that quantum features are imprinted on the HHG observables. Demonstrating this would require methods capable of characterizing quantum states at high photon numbers while minimizing decoherence from passive optical elements—an issue beyond the scope of this work. Nonetheless, we find that the generated harmonics exhibit strong super-Poissonian photon-number statistics, with features sensitively depending on the interplay between the initial squeezing of the driving field, the absorption it experiences in the medium, and the depletion of the atomic system during the interaction. This provides a versatile control knob for obtaining light with extreme super-Poisonnian statistics70 in the extreme ultraviolet regime.

Since ionization, harmonic generation, and light scattering are ubiquitous in light–matter interactions, our study provides a foundation for understanding the propagation of intense BSV light across all states of matter, such as the recent works conducted in solids 40,41. This underlines its relevance for future investigations in strong-field physics, nonlinear optics, and ultrafast science26,27. Finally, these findings demonstrate the need to account for propagation effects when examining the phase locking of harmonics generated by BSV states, a consideration that is particularly critical for attosecond science applications.

For ϱat ~ 1018 atoms/cm3, the photon losses induced by IR scattering are on the order of 10−8 photons/cm and can thus be considered negligible. Comparing the ionization probabilities (Fig. 1c) with the harmonic generation probabilities (Fig. 3a), we see that the major part of the IR photon losses is associated with the atomic ionization. Also, for the gas densities used in the present work, the IR photon losses associated with the Inverse Bremsstrahlung process (c.f. 76,77) are negligible.

Photon number estimation of high harmonics generated by BSV

To estimate the photon number of the high harmonics generated by BSV light, we use the experimentally and theoretically demonstrated values of conversion efficiency (defined as CES = Nq/NIR ∝ S) of the HHG process in Ar atoms (S is the focal spot area of the laser beam in the HHG medium). For interactions of Ar atoms with ≈60 fs IR laser pulses of NIR(coh.)~2×1016 photons per pulse, I ~ 1014 W/cm2, ρat ~ 1018 atoms/cm3 and Scoh. ~ 5 × 10−4 cm2, it has been found65 that the CEScoh.~5×10−6. Considering that the available BSV sources deliver pulses with mean photon number ~1013 photons4,5, we have to focus a 60 fs BSV pulse into the focal spot area of SBSV ≈ 2.5 × 10−7 cm2 in order to reach an intensity of ~1014 W/cm2. Although such BSV intensities have not yet been demonstrated experimentally, in this case the CESBSV~2.5×10−9 and the estimated harmonic photon number per pulse will be ~2.5×104×⟨N15⟩(BSV)/⟨N15⟩(Coh.). This, for a Lm≈Lm(BSV)≈2La long medium length, results that ~5 × 102 harmonic photons per pulse exiting the Ar medium.

For BSV of mean photon number ~1013 photons and mean intensity ~1013 W/cm2, the photon losses due to ionization can be considered negligible i.e., A ≪ 12%, the focal spot size can be increased to SBSV ≈ 10−6 cm2. Considering that for this BSV intensity, the ⟨N15⟩(BSV) is an order of magnitude lower than the corresponding value at Isat, we obtain that CESBSV~10−9 and ⟨N15⟩(BSV)/⟨N15⟩(Coh.)~2×10−3. Therefore the estimated photon number exiting the medium is ~104×⟨N15⟩(BSV)/⟨N15⟩(Coh.)=20 photons per pulse.
