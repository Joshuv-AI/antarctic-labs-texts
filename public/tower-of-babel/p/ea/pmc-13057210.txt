Advancing green mobile networks
Authors: Antonio De Domenico, Nicola Piovesan, Xiaoyi Zhou, Xiaowen Li, Magnus Olsson, Xin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mobile communication is a cornerstone technology in our society, facilitating personal relationships, fostering cooperation, and advancing education. Each generation of this technology has brought new services, from voice calls and messaging to internet access, and now Artificial Intelligence (AI). This transformation has steadily augmented the mobile network resource requirements. Driven by the societal and economical needs to increase the mobile network energy efficiency, reduce its energy consumption and the associated environmental footprint, this comment surveys standardized metrics to assess energy-related network performance and overview data-driven state-of-the-art solutions to accelerate the energy transition of mobile networks.

Governments and industries worldwide have set ambitious targets to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, mitigate global warming, and meet the United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDG)1. Energy efficiency has become an essential requirement in the telecommunication sector, alongside strategies to reduce energy consumption and minimize waste. This shift not only aligns with SDG but also supports economic sustainability, particularly in light of ongoing energy challenges, including price volatility, supply instability, and increased demand – especially low-carbon energy –2 due to the growth in data volume that must be processed and transmitted3.

In this context, the telecommunication sector, which accounts for 2-3% of the worldwide energy use4, is under increasing pressure to reduce energy costs by securing and stabilizing the available renewable energy. Indeed, energy costs today represent between 20% and 40% of a telecoms company operational expenditure5. Moreover, the impact of the information and communication technology on the global GHG emissions has doubled in the last 15 years. Finally, while renewables account for only 20% of the network power supply6, operators in Southeast Asia, India, and Africa continue to rely largely on diesel in off-grid areas5,6. Therefore, crucial components of the energy transition of the telecom industry involve reducing network power consumption, increasing network energy efficiency, and accelerating the shift to renewable energy sources to reduce GHG emissions, in line with the goal of achieving net-zero emissions by 20507.

Historically, Radio Access Networks (RAN) have been designed and optimized to meet growing demands for coverage and capacity. However, to address the pressing environmental and energy challenges, a methodological shift was required, and significant progress has been made in 5G standardization in this context8, resulting in the emergence of various standards. In fact, recently, several metrics and measurement solutions related to mobile network energy consumption, energy efficiency, and carbon footprint have been standardized by key bodies such as the Telecommunication Standardization Sector of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU-T) Study Group 5, the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) Environmental Engineering technical committee, and the Third Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) Radio Access Network Technical Specification Group9.

Despite these advancements, the complexity of modern 5G networks poses significant challenges. The intricate trade-offs between energy consumption and network performance, measured by traditional Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) such as coverage quality, throughput, and latency, require a systematic, data-driven approach to utilize these standardized metrics effectively. In this work, we present a path that harmonizes existing solutions to accelerate the energy transition of mobile networks while managing the increasing demand for high data rate mobile services (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1A Path Towards Green Mobile Networks.In this path, we have highlighted four phases interacting together: data collection, resource optimization, hardware refreshment, and green deployment.

Overview of green metrics for sustainable and efficient mobile networks

In the past, energy efficiency was the most commonly used metric to evaluate the sustainability of mobile networks10. However, this metric alone is no longer sufficient to address the growing environmental and societal challenges faced by the mobile industry. Specifically, energy efficiency alone fails to capture the ever increasing network requirements, and the usage of different energy sources.

In response to these challenges, the mobile industry has developed a set of energy-related metrics and measurement methods that account for the various layers of a telecommunication network and the multiple axes of relevant performance requirements. Energy efficiency evaluation

A RAN consists of three principal components: the site infrastructure, the BS, and the User Equipment (UE), with a fourth component being the radio channel between each BS and UE. The site infrastructure typically includes rectifiers for converting Alternating Current (AC) to Direct Current (DC) power, power backup systems, and cooling mechanisms like air conditioners. The BS is composed of baseband and radio units, which may be co-located or distributed within the RAN, and are responsible for transmitting data to and from the UEs. Moreover, each BS transmits data to the nearby UEs using one or multiple carrier frequencies, often denoted as cells.

An illustrative breakdown of the RAN setup and its associated components is shown in Fig. 2, which also outlines the flow of energy from the AC input through DC conversion, to its distribution across the BS, and finally to the transmission over the air interface to the UE. The energy efficiency of the RAN can be analyzed at three distinct levels: the network level, which evaluates the interactions between various BSs operating within a given geographical area; the site infrastructure level, to assess energy losses from support equipment like cooling; and the BS level, which captures the impact of sustainable hardware and energy-saving mechanisms.Fig. 2RAN energy consumption.Illustration of a typical Radio Access Network (RAN) site with energy flow from the main Alternating Current (AC) input to reception at the user equipment (top figure), along with a breakdown of the RAN site and macro base station power consumption (bottom figure)12.

At the site infrastructure level, the energy consumption of the support equipment plays a significant role, reaching up to 40% of the total energy consumption in traditional sites12. Support equipment includes backhaul and site facility equipment like rectifiers, power backup, air conditioning, batteries, and monitoring systems. To assess the impact of support equipment on site energy consumption, Recommendation ITU-T L.1350 defines principles for calculating the site energy efficiency (SEE)14.

Finally, at the BS level, energy consumption is influenced by factors such as the BS configuration (e.g., maximum transmit power and number of antennas), traffic volume, radio environment, and the implementation of energy-saving mechanisms: the BS energy efficiency metric (EEBS) has been defined by ETSI15.

Carbon footprint evaluation

Recommendation ITU-T L.1333 defines the carbon emission intensity of a network by relating carbon emissions from network energy consumption to the volume of delivered data traffic16. This recommendation introduces the Network Carbon Intensity energy (NCIe) and provides a methodology for measuring it. The NCIe is essential for assessing the overall “greeness” of the network. It not only encourages reducing electricity consumption but also promotes the use of low-carbon energy sources.

In addition to the NCIe, the Renewable Energy Factor (REF)17, as defined by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), can be used to assess the share of renewable energy in the network total energy consumption.

Sustainable communication networks

State-of-the-art solutions for sustainable communications relies on the metrics presented in Overview of green metrics for sustainable and efficient mobile networks, and can be classified in four optimization phases, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These phases interact and are combined together, as a cycle, to achieve long-term sustainability goals for the network. Therefore, upon completion of the fourth phase, the cycle does not end; rather, it restarts to evaluate network-wide performance improvements, adapt the optimization roadmap, and define new strategies. In the following, we provide more details on these four phases, and using results from tests on real networks, we highlight recent progress on green mobile networks. These results are mainly related to macro cell deployments and include different radio access technologies, environment, and power equipment. This provides a general analysis of the most common deployments in different regions and countries. However, our manuscript does not consider small cell networks, which have distinctive power consumption profiles and performance characteristics18. In fact, due to deployment issues, including site acquisition and management19, globally, small cell accounts for only around 10% of the overall RAN market20, which makes macro cell technologies the main target for energy saving solutions in mobile networks21,22.

Phase 1: data collection

The mechanisms in the first phase focuses on collecting and processing site-level statistics to assess the mdNEE and the NCIe metrics presented in Overview of green metrics for sustainable and efficient mobile networks. Data is collected at each site and every hour, for at least one week to capture slow variations in traffic patterns driven by human behavior, as well as the effects of energy-saving mechanisms that may be activated during off-peak periods, such as nighttime or weekends23. Based on this data, an annual improvement roadmap can be created, and, as described in ref. 12, sites clustered according to their behavior to determine the most effective improvement strategies for each of them.

Phase 2: resource optimization

The second phase includes strategies focusing on sites that have a large amount of inefficiently used communication/energy resources. The lifetime of these strategies typically lasts a few weeks, during which software mechanisms are implemented to optimize network energy usage and capacity in response to fluctuating service demand, with the goal of reducing both costs and carbon footprint. At the end of this period, the performance variation can be evaluated and the strategies updated. These mechanisms are typically vendor-specific solutions, supported by standard enablers24 that utilize rule-based approaches or more advanced optimization tools such as AI. Such solutions need to manage a large number of optimization variables (a typical metropolitan network consisting of 1000 + BS cells and 20 + parameters to be configured per cell25) and stochastic fluctuations of both the wireless channel and network traffic.

The telecom industry has defined various solutions to optimize network resources for more sustainable operations, focusing on both energy saving and energy management features.

Network energy saving features

These aim to adapt network capacity to the time-space variations in traffic demand, improving energy efficiency. They can operate across different domains: time, frequency, space (antenna), and power9,21, and are typically implemented following the self-organizing network (SON) paradigm, where each cell dynamically configures its status based on the network condition26. Time-domain solutions operate in the time scale of hundreds of microseconds to hundreds of milliseconds and quickly activate or deactivate BS hardware components in response to traffic presence or absence. Frequency-domain solutions allows switching off BS hardware components for long periods of time, e.g., time scales of minutes, even hours, based on the long-term traffic load variations of a network (for instance during night-time). Space-domain techniques optimize the BS configuration by using only a subset of antennas and transceivers when traffic load is low. Finally, power-domain techniques dynamically adjust downlink transmit power to match fast traffic variations.

A recent work demonstrated the benefit of SON energy saving features applied in a real network in a metropolitan area comprising 134 sites, including 400 4G cells (operating in multiple frequency bands) and 155 5G cells (operating at 2.6 GHz and 700MHz)27. This experiment comprises four phases, and for each of them, coverage, downlink user rate, and energy consumption measurements were collected each hour at each site during one entire week, i.e., for four weeks in total. After measuring the baseline network energy consumption with all energy-saving solutions deactivated, traditional rule-based approaches were applied by expert engineers to configure the energy saving mechanisms, resulting in a 10.5% energy saving gain. Then, these mechanisms, including mobility management parameters, were tuned using a stochastic optimization framework able to model the influence of noisy measurements and changes in the stochastic network environment. This approach results in an additional 4.6% energy saving; in the final stage, dynamic service quality adjustments across different zones and times of day provided a further 3.1% energy saving gain. The collected measurements confirmed that 1) the average network traffic conditions were comparable during the four phases of the experiment, and 2) the implemented energy saving mechanisms did not impact the average downlink user rate and the average strength of the user received signal power. Indeed, during the four weeks, the average downlink user rate was stable around 18 Mbps, and the percentage of users with good coverage was above 96%. To facilitate the reproducibility of these results, we made available a dataset related to network energy saving28.

Network energy management features

Historically, MNOs energy management strategies focused on securing low-carbon energy tariffs and long-term contracts to ensure a stable and cost-effective energy supply. However, the energy market landscape is shifting from centralized, non-renewable sources to more decentralized systems incorporating renewable energy sources. In this context, MNOs are investing in energy resources to enhance network resilience and reduce operational costs while maintaining QoS. As reported by GSMA29, in the last 5 years mobile operators invested 109 billion dollars, and although capital investment in infrastructure can vary from year to year, this has been quite stable in the last decade. When these investments are allocated to projects that contribute to environmental sustainability, they are often balanced by significant OPEX saving, since energy costs today represent a large part of a telecoms company operational expenditure5. BS hardware refresh

With a hardware upgrade, old equipment can be replaced with more energy-efficient, multi-RAT solutions, reducing the needed number of RUs and BBUs31. Results from the literature present performance improvement for a setup of two BS sites with 3 RUs operating at 1.8 GHz (4G), 2.1 GHz (3G), and 900 MHz (2G/3G) and 3 BBUs (2 × 4G and 1 × 2G/3G). This setup was replaced with a more efficient system, including one RU operating at 1.8 MHz (4G) and a multi-band RU operating at 900 MHz (2G/3G) and 800 MHz (4G) together with a single BBU (2G/3G/4G). Overall, this resulted in an average improvement in energy efficiency at the two sites of 70%.

Site support equipment refresh

Site energy consumption is determined by both BS and support equipment, as detailed in Energy efficiency evaluation. By reducing the footprint of site support equipment, significant energy savings can be achieved31. Traditionally, support equipment is housed indoors, while telecom equipment (e.g., RUs and antennas) is placed outdoors, which results in an average SEE of 74%. However, transitioning to outdoor cabinets with compact battery and power systems, coupled with the removal of air conditioners (commonly used in indoor shelters), can lead to an average SEE of 92%. Finally, the site support equipment can been further compacted and moved closer to the RU, further reducing cooling and cable losses, resulting in an average SEE of 96%.

Phase 4: green deployment

In the fourth phase, the focus shifts to green deployment, where MNOs address the demand for new mobile services and stringent performance requirements by gradually transitioning to more energy-efficient RATs. In fact, each new generation of mobile communication systems is significantly more energy efficient than its predecessor when carrying the same amount of traffic31. This phase consists of two key steps:First, identifying network hotspots –areas with high traffic volumes– and deploying new BSs to offload traffic from legacy technologies and shift it to 5G.Second, shutting down 2G and 3G networks wherever feasible and refarming the 2G and 3G spectrum to support more energy-efficient RATs (e.g., 4G and 5G).

5G technology not only incorporates energy-saving features that adjust capacity based on varying traffic demand, but it is also four times more energy efficient than 4G, owing to its broader spectrum resources and enhanced multiplexing capabilities22.

Summary

This comment has reviewed the challenges related to the energy transition of mobile networks towards low-consumption, low-cost, and low-carbon requirements. To achieve this threefold goal, the industrial and research communities have developed solutions that can be categorized in four phases: data collection, resource optimization, hardware refreshment, and green deployment. Together, with the combination of these solutions, the energy consumption growth rate due to the expected growth in traffic demand will drop to 2−3% per year and pave the way towards more sustainable mobile communications that meet the UN SDG. In the coming years, we foresee a rapid proliferation of AI solutions to further enhance the capabilities of mobile networks. Future research and industrial efforts should prioritize a comprehensive analysis of AI impact on networks32. Moreover, for each of the four optimization phases, it is crucial to identify and recommend the most resource-efficient AI solutions that allow to meet or exceed the target network performance requirements.
