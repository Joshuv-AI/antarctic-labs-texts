Dosimetric superiority of proton radiosurgery in liver cancer: an in silico comparative dosimetric study
Authors: Jiaran Li, Tao Ma, Hao Zhou, Changxing Feng, Lu Liu, Lei Liu, Li Li, Chao Shan, Dongdong Zheng, Shuanghu Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in silico dosimetric study aimed to evaluate a potentially more efficient approach by assessing the dosimetric advantages of single-fraction 35 Gy proton radiosurgery relative to photon therapy.MethodsThis retrospective study included 98 patients with liver cancer who had undergone radiotherapy. Simulated treatment plans were generated for each patient to deliver a single fraction of 35 Gy using both photon and proton techniques. Patients were defined as eligible if all organs at risk (OARs) met the preset dose constraints. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare dosimetric parameters. The Youden index was used to identify the optimal cutoff for the safety margin between the OARs and the gross tumor volume.ResultsProton therapy demonstrated a higher overall compliance rate with dose constraints compared to photon therapy, with notable improvements for key OARs, including the liver (96.9% vs. 88.8%), duodenum (93.9% vs. 91.8%), and heart (91.8% vs. 89.8%). Proton therapy plans provided superior sparing of OARs by significantly reducing the maximum dose to all organs (p < 0.0001) and the optimal cutoff for satisfying dose constraints was generally < 1 cm. Furthermore, proton therapy significantly reduced the irradiated liver volume (V5Gy and V11.6 Gy, both p < 0.05), and this benefit was consistent across tumor sizes (≤ 3 cm or > 3 cm) and locations (dome, caudal, left medial, or central).ConclusionSingle-fraction 35 Gy proton radiosurgery offers enhanced sparing of OARs and a broader therapeutic window for patients with liver cancer compared with photon therapy from a dosimetric perspective. Whether these dosimetric benefits can translate into actual clinical efficacy requires prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16539-5.Keywords: Proton therapy, Radiosurgery, Stereotactic body radiotherapy, Liver cancer, Dosimetric analysis

Introduction

Proton therapy (PT) exploits the physical characteristics of the Bragg peak to deliver higher radiation doses to tumors while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues, representing a significant advance in radiotherapy [1]. However, the substantial capital investment required for implementation has limited its widespread clinical adoption. Consequently, a key research priority for optimizing PT and enhancing its accessibility has been to increase the dose per fraction while reducing the number of treatment sessions [2].

Recent studies have increasingly recognized local ablative therapies, such as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), radiofrequency ablation (RFA), and high-dose-rate brachytherapy (HDR-BT), as integral components in the multidisciplinary management of primary and metastatic liver tumors [3–7]. Clinical studies have confirmed that this non-invasive modality can achieve long-term survival outcomes comparable to surgery or RFA by delivering tumoricidal doses in a limited number of sessions [3, 8, 9]. However, a major challenge is that the 3–5 fraction regimens recommended by the current ASTRO guidelines [10] may limit patient access and compliance because of prolonged hospitalization and machine utilization, particularly in resource-limited settings. Radiosurgery has emerged as a potential alternative. This concept was first introduced in the 1950s by a Swedish neurosurgeon [11], and initially applied to intracranial functional disorders and tumors [12]. Advances in radiobiology and technology have since extended its application from intracranial lesions to extracranial primary and metastatic tumors [13, 14].

An analysis of multiple radiobiological models identified 35 Gy as the optimal single-fraction isocenter dose for liver tumors [15]. Furthermore, Meyer et al. [16] demonstrated that a single 35 Gy fraction of photon SBRT achieved favorable local control with acceptable toxicity in selected patients with liver metastases. These findings established the preliminary clinical feasibility of this regimen by demonstrating that the liver could tolerate such a high focal dose. However, a major limitation of the photon-based approach is the unavoidable dose dispersion and penumbra effects, which expose surrounding healthy tissue to potentially harmful radiation [17]. In contrast, proton radiosurgery exploits the “Bragg peak” to confine high doses more precisely within the target, providing a stronger physical rationale for the safe delivery of a single-fraction 35 Gy dose. This dosimetric superiority is complemented by evidence from multi-fraction SBRT studies, which highlight the potential of proton therapy to reduce clinical toxicity [18, 19].

While proton radiosurgery offers clear theoretical advantages, there remains a lack of direct comparisons between single-fraction proton and photon therapy for liver tumors. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted an in silico planning study to quantify the dosimetric advantages of single-fraction 35 Gy proton irradiation and to establish a strong evidence base for its clinical application.

Materials and methods

Study population

We retrospectively collected a cohort of patients with primary or metastatic liver cancer treated with radiotherapy between January 2024 and April 2025. Eligible patients were required to have pathologically confirmed liver cancer with a solitary tumor lesion measuring ≤ 7 cm in maximum diameter. We excluded patients with multifocal liver cancer or those with poor-quality imaging unsuitable for treatment planning. All patient clinical and tumor characteristic data were extracted from the electronic medical record system.

Simulation and target delineation

Patients were positioned supine and immobilized using a vacuum bag, after which a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) simulation was performed in the treatment position. All patients were assumed to have employed breath-hold techniques for respiratory motion management. The simulation CT images were imported into the Eclipse treatment planning system (version 16.1, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was contoured slice by slice on the planning CT images, with MRI or PET-CT scans incorporated, when available, to refine delineation of viable tumor tissue. The internal target volume (ITV) was generated by applying a uniform 5-mm expansion to the GTV, and the planning target volume (PTV) was subsequently defined by a uniform 3-mm expansion of the ITV. Normal tissues, including the liver (whole liver minus GTV), stomach, duodenum, spinal cord, skin, and ribs, were delineated as organs at risk (OARs). The minimum distance between each OAR and the GTV (OAR-to-GTV) was calculated using the CalculateMinDistance function in the commercial Cura treatment planning system (Raynaissance Technologies, Hefei, China).

Radiotherapy treatment planning

In this study, we generated simulated treatment plans delivering a single 35 Gy fraction using both photon intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and proton intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT) for each patient. The photon plans employed a 6 MV X-ray technique with a six-field arrangement, reflecting the standard clinical practice at our center. To enhance dose conformity and optimize OAR sparing, these six fields were initially distributed at equidistant intervals. Subsequently, the beam angles and collimator settings were manually fine-tuned based on the specific anatomical relationships among the beam, target volume, and OARs. Optimization was performed using a photon golden beamlet algorithm on a 0.25 cm calculation grid, and the final dose distribution was calculated with the anisotropic analytical algorithm. For comparison, a four-field pencil beam scanning (PBS) proton plan with multifield optimization (MFO) was generated for each patient. Energy layer spacing was set to “variable, 2 sigma”, spot spacing to 1 sigma, and robust optimization was applied to the PTV with a 3% range uncertainty. Setup uncertainties were geometrically accounted for by the 3-mm expansion from the ITV to the PTV. We excluded an additional setup uncertainty parameter during robust optimization. This approach was chosen to prevent double-counting of setup margins and to minimize unnecessary dose spill to surrounding healthy tissues. These proton plans also used a 0.25 cm calculation grid, with final dose calculation performed by the proton convolution superposition algorithm. For both modalities, the prescribed dose of 35 Gy was normalized such that at least 95% of the PTV received 100% of the prescription dose. Optimization objectives prioritized target coverage while strictly adhering to the OAR constraints established by Timmerman et al. (Table 1) [20]. A relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of 1.1 was assumed for all proton plans.

Treatment plan evaluation and statistical analysis

After all treatment plans were optimized, we compared them using dose-volume histograms (DVHs) and three-dimensional isodose distributions. For the dosimetric evaluation, patients were considered eligible if all OARs met the preset dose constraints and ineligible if any constraint was exceeded. We also analyzed the irradiated liver volume receiving 5 Gy (V5Gy), as this parameter is strongly associated with the risk of radiation-induced liver disease (RILD) [21]. To define a safety margin, we applied the Youden index to identify the optimal OAR-to-GTV distance cutoff required to satisfy dose constraints. To investigate the influence of tumor location, we performed a subgroup analysis in which tumors were categorized as dome, caudal, left medial, and central, according to established anatomical classifications [22, 23]. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare the dosimetric parameters between the paired proton and photon plans for each individual OAR and anatomical subgroup. All statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS version 25, and all graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and compliance rates with dose constraints

We retrospectively analyzed 98 eligible patients with liver cancer, with a mean age of 61.5 ± 1.06 years. The cohort comprised 65.3% males, and the majority of tumors (90.8%) measured ≤ 5 cm, with a median diameter of 3 cm and a median GTV volume of 13.5 cm³. The most common tumor locations were the hepatic dome (32.7%), caudal (25.5%), and central area (23.5%) (Table 2). The overall constraint compliance rate was higher with proton therapy than with photon therapy (75.5% vs. 69.4%). This advantage was notable for critical organs such as the liver (96.9% vs. 88.8%), duodenum (93.9% vs. 91.8%), and heart (91.8% vs. 89.8%), whereas both techniques achieved 100% compliance for the skin and spinal cord (Table 3). A detailed comparison of OARs constraint compliance across all structures is presented in Figure S1.

OARs sparing and safety margins between proton and photon therapies

Our dosimetric analysis confirmed the superiority of proton therapy in sparing OARs. Proton plans achieved a significantly lower Dmax to all evaluated OARs—including the heart, stomach, duodenum, ribs, skin, and spinal cord—compared to photon plans (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1; Table 4). This advantage was particularly evident at close ranges; although OAR dose decreased with distance for both modalities, proton therapy demonstrated a markedly steeper dose fall-off gradient in proximity to the GTV (Fig. 2). This intrinsic dosimetric precision allows for a significantly smaller spatial dosimetric clearance between the target volume and adjacent OARs to meet strict single-fraction constraints. Based on the Youden index, the optimal OAR-to-GTV cutoff distances required for meeting constraints were generally smaller in proton plans for the heart (0.83 cm vs. 1.25 cm) and duodenum (0.90 cm vs. 1.11 cm), equivalent for the stomach (0.83 cm vs. 0.83 cm), but larger for the ribs (0.63 cm vs. 0.55 cm) (Table 5). Notably, the optimal safety margin for all OARs in the proton plans was generally < 1 cm, whereas some OARs in the photon plans required larger margins. In other words, when the OAR-to-GTV distance exceeded 1 cm, proton plans were more likely to meet the dose constraints.

Assessment of liver dose-volume parameters based on tumor location and size

To further assess the impact of tumor location on dosimetry, we analyzed the 66 patients for whom both proton and photon plans satisfied all dose constraints. These patients were divided into four groups by tumor location: dome, caudal, left medial, and central, and their detailed characteristics were presented in Table S1. The irradiated liver volume increased with tumor diameter, and proton therapy demonstrated superiority in reducing liver irradiation (Figure S2). As shown in Fig. 3; Table 6, proton plans significantly reduced liver V5Gy and V11.6 Gy across all tumor locations (all p < 0.01). Dose distributions for representative tumor locations are illustrated in Fig. 4. A second subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate previous reports suggesting that protons confer no dosimetric advantage for liver tumors < 3 cm [22]. Using a 3 cm diameter as the cutoff, our analysis demonstrated that proton therapy significantly reduced the irradiated liver dose for tumors both smaller and larger than 3 cm, with all differences reaching statistically significant (all p < 0.05, Figure S3 and S4).

Discussion

Based on a relatively large-sample dosimetric analysis, this study demonstrates that single-fraction 35 Gy proton radiosurgery confers significant dosimetric advantages over photon therapy in patients with primary or metastatic liver cancer. Our results demonstrated that proton plans achieved higher overall constraint compliance with dose constraints and showed clear superiority in sparing key organs like the liver, stomach, duodenum, and heart. Although absolute differences in constraint compliance between proton and photon plans may seem marginal for certain organs, these incremental gains are clinically meaningful. For single-fraction ablative radiotherapy, meeting stringent dose constraints remains a strict prerequisite for treatment eligibility. Thus, even a modest rise in compliance rates dictates that a subset of patients intolerant to photon SBRT can now safely access potentially curative proton radiosurgery. Notably, the optimal cutoff for distinguishing between eligible and ineligible with dose constraints was generally < 1 cm, underscoring the clinical feasibility of proton therapy for lesions near critical structures. Moreover, the proton advantage in sparing normal liver tissue was consistent across all tumor sizes and locations. These findings provide a strong dosimetric rationale for adopting a single-fraction radiosurgery model in the management of liver tumors.

Currently, various comparative dosimetric studies focusing on liver-directed radiotherapy modalities have sought to identify interventions that minimize radiation exposure to the uninvolved liver [19, 23–26]. Our results are consistent with the existing literature. The dosimetric superiority of protons over photons in sparing healthy liver tissue and OARs has been well-documented in multi-fraction SBRT studies [19, 23, 24]. The key contribution of our work is the systematic demonstration that this dosimetric advantage persists even in the challenging setting of a single 35 Gy ultra-hypofractionated dose. The translation of these dosimetric gains into clinical practice holds significant promise. As demonstrated by Bilski et al., the exceptional conformity and dosimetric advantages of HDR-BT have already successfully translated into excellent clinical outcomes and minimal toxicity in real-world cohorts of liver oligometastases [7, 25]. Proton radiosurgery emerges as a highly promising, non-invasive addition to the multidisciplinary management of liver tumors.

Building on prior work that established the feasibility and efficacy of high-dose single-fraction photon SBRT [16, 27], our study addresses a key dosimetric question: whether protons can provide a superior safety profile for this efficient regimen. Notably, we demonstrate that proton plans not only improve OAR constraint compliance but achieve this with smaller spatial dosimetric clearance. This represents a critical advantage that may expand the indications for radiosurgery to patients with tumors adjacent to vital structures, who are often considered unsuitable for conventional radiotherapy. Our findings provide theoretical justification and practical impetus for initiating clinical trials of single-fraction proton radiosurgery.

Gandhi et al. [22] proposed a treatment decision model that recommends selecting photon or proton SBRT based on a comprehensive consideration of tumor size and location. According to their model, protons provide a dosimetric advantage primarily for tumors ≥ 3 cm in the hepatic dome or central region, with limited benefit for smaller or less critically situated lesions [18]. However, our study found that proton therapy significantly reduced the irradiated volume of normal liver tissue regardless of tumor size or location. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, Gandhi et al.‘s study employed a 50 Gy in 5 fractions regimen, whereas ours focused on a single 35 Gy ultra-hypofractionated protocol. A massive single-fraction dose essentially maximizes the dosimetric benefits derived from the proton Bragg peak. The inherent absence of an exit dose mitigates the prohibitive integral dose that would otherwise occur with photon SBRT, thereby making ultra-hypofractionated 35-Gy ablation clinically feasible. Second, our study included 98 patients, a substantially larger sample size than the 10-patient validation cohort in Gandhi et al.’s work, thereby providing greater statistical power to detect subtle yet clinically meaningful dose differences. Finally, differences in the dosimetric metrics employed may also account for the inconsistent conclusions: Gandhi et al. evaluated liver V15Gy and mean dose, whereas we primarily assessed V5Gy and V11.6 Gy.

This study possesses several strengths. First, the sample size of nearly 100 patients encompassed tumors of various sizes and locations, thereby enhancing the generalizability and robustness of our conclusions. Second, the focus on a single 35 Gy dose is highly relevant, as it aligns with contemporary explorations in radiosurgery and provides a solid basis for future translational research. Third, we introduced and quantitatively assessed the OAR-to-GTV safety margin derived via the Youden index. It should be emphasized that these cutoffs represent exploratory dosimetric metrics derived specifically from our cohort’s anatomical and planning data, and they require further external validation across different patient populations and delivery techniques.

However, as an in silico planning study, it has inherent limitations. It cannot provide direct clinical outcomes such as local control, survival, or toxicity. A prospective clinical trial is required to determine whether these dosimetric advantages translate into tangible clinical benefits. Additionally, we note that the in silico comparison between proton and photon plans presented here was limited to static targets. First, assuming consistent breath-hold execution across this retrospective cohort remains an unverified assumption. Second, a uniform 5-mm ITV expansion cannot fully substitute for a motion-inclusive dosimetric evaluation. Residual motion induces interplay effects that disproportionately degrade proton PBS plan quality relative to photon IMRT. Consequently, this asymmetric uncertainty may affect the observed magnitude of dosimetric superiority. Furthermore, although our inclusion criteria permitted lesions up to 7 cm, the applicability and safety for these larger lesions remain to be fully determined because the actual analyzed patient cohort consisted predominantly (90.8%) of tumors ≤ 5 cm. Another limitation pertains to the representativeness of the photon baseline. We utilized a 6-field IMRT approach. the magnitude of proton superiority observed herein inherently depends on this specific planning strategy and may not fully generalize to all contemporary photon techniques. Finally, to prioritize accurate GTV delineation, dose calculations were performed on contrast-enhanced CTs without the application of density overrides. While a shared CT baseline was used for both modalities, we acknowledge that this introduces an asymmetric dosimetric bias. Unlike photon dose calculation, which is relatively insensitive to contrast agents, the artificially elevated Hounsfield unit can systematically underestimate the proton range. This directional bias represents a methodological limitation that should be considered when interpreting the dosimetric comparison.

Based on the strengths and limitations of this study, future work should focus on the following directions. First, a prospective clinical trial is urgently needed to validate the real-world safety and efficacy of single-fraction 35 Gy proton radiosurgery, comparing it against parallel photon SBRT cohorts, with a particular focus on the incidence of RILD and long-term survival outcomes. Second, building on evidence that single doses up to 40 Gy are tolerable with photons [16], a promising avenue of research is to explore whether proton therapy permits safe dose escalation beyond 35 Gy to achieve higher local control. Finally, the development of an individualized patient selection model for single-fraction proton therapy is warranted. Such a model should integrate clinical factors like liver function, tumor anatomy, and underlying liver disease with health economic data to optimize patient selection and facilitate implementation in resource-limited regions [28].

In summary, this large-scale dosimetric study demonstrates that single-fraction 35 Gy proton radiosurgery provides superior dosimetric sparing of organs at risk and a broader therapeutic window compared with photon therapy in patients with liver cancer. Notably, this dosimetric advantage was independent of tumor size and location, and was observed across a broad spectrum of lesions. This finding provides a theoretical rationale for applying proton therapy in the single-fraction ablative setting. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the dosimetric feasibility of single-fraction 35 Gy proton therapy and support its potential for clinical translation. Nevertheless, the true clinical efficacy and toxicity profiles await further prospective validation.
