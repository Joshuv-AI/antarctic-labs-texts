Step-by-step clinical protocol for the fabrication and titration of a bibloc mandibular advancement device: a practical guide for sleep physicians
Authors: Raphaël Wakam, Didier Maurice, Caroline Gorin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAdult obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is a major public health concern requiring multidisciplinary management, from diagnosis to therapeutic decision-making. While continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) remains the standard treatment for severe OSAHS, long-term adherence is often suboptimal. Mandibular advancement devices (MADs) are now recommended as first-line therapy for mild to moderate OSAHS without comorbidities, and increasingly considered for patients with more severe forms who are intolerant to CPAP. Custom-made bibloc MADs are among the most commonly prescribed oral appliances, and their effectiveness depends greatly on the accuracy of the clinical steps involved in their fabrication and adjustment. However, many sleep physicians and prescribers are unfamiliar with these steps, which can compromise treatment outcomes and device tolerance.MethodsThis article provides an illustrated, step-by-step clinical protocol for the design, fabrication, insertion, and titration of a custom-made, titratable bibloc MAD, illustrated with the Somnodent™ Flex as a representative example. Emphasis is placed on technical accuracy, reproducibility, and collaboration between sleep and dental professionals. ResultsThe protocol details each stage of clinical implementation, from impression taking to follow-up adjustments, highlighting key parameters influencing device comfort, tolerance, and therapeutic efficacy.ConclusionThis guide aims to help both dental practitioners and non-dental prescribers better understand MAD workflows, thereby improving interdisciplinary management, and treatment outcomes for patients with OSAHS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11325-025-03514-w.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is a frequent and potentially serious condition, characterized by repeated episodes of partial or complete upper airway obstruction during sleep. These events result in intermittent hypoxemia, apneas, hypopneas, sleep fragmentation, and micro-arousals, and have been strongly associated with increased cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurocognitive morbidity.

Although tracheostomy was historically the first effective treatment, the development of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) has long since become the standard of care for moderate to severe OSAHS. However, CPAP therapy is often limited by low long-term adherence: nearly 50% of patients discontinue its use within five years [1], prompting growing interest in alternative therapeutic strategies.

In response to these limitations, mandibular advancement device (MAD) has gained increasing attention as a non-invasive, well-tolerated alternative. These intraoral appliances function by repositioning the mandible in a forward position, thereby increasing the upper airway space and reducing collapsibility during sleep. MADs have demonstrated significant improvements in respiratory indices and clinical symptoms, particularly in patients with mild (5 < AHI < 15) to moderate (15 < AHI < 30) OSAHS, and in those with severe OSAHS (AHI >30) who are intolerant to or refuse CPAP therapy [2].

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) supports the use of custom-made, titratable MADs, citing higher efficacy and better tolerance compared to prefabricated or non-adjustable models. While CPAP remains more effective in reducing the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), MADs offer comparable benefits in selected populations, particularly younger female patients with lower Body Mass Index and retrognathic profiles [3]. In all cases, the goal of therapy remains the same: to restore airway patency, improve oxygenation, alleviate clinical symptoms, and prevent long-term complications [4].

As MADs become increasingly prescribed in the management of OSAHS, it is essential for all prescribers – whether physicians or dentists – to understand the oral prerequisites for their safe and effective use. A recent and thorough dental evaluation must be conducted before initiating MAD therapy, in order to confirm that the dentition is healthy, stable, and capable of supporting the appliance. Common contraindications – such as advanced periodontal disease, carious lesions, or tooth mobility – can often be treated beforehand, but may compromise outcomes if overlooked [5].

In partially or fully edentulous patients, MAD therapy may remain feasible, but it requires more complex impression techniques and mucosal registration that should be handled by a trained dentist.

Beyond initiation, regular dental follow-up is critical, especially after titration, to detect and manage side effects such as occlusal changes, increased tooth mobility, or periodontal stress. These complications are often progressive and asymptomatic at early stages, yet they can significantly impact long-term tolerance and therapeutic success. Equally important is the monitoring of patient-reported outcomes, including reduction in snoring, sleep quality, daytime alertness, or morning symptoms [6]. Such feedback is essential to guide titration in routine practice, to improve adherence, and to detect early signs of intolerance before they compromise long-term outcomes.

Regardless of who delivers the device, ensuring dental supervision before and during MAD therapy is key to maintaining oral health and optimizing treatment efficacy. This reinforces the need for close interdisciplinary collaboration in the care of OSA patients.

A wide variety of MADs are available, differing in their retention mechanisms, titration systems, materials, and overall comfort. Titratable bibloc appliances, in particular, allow for progressive and bilateral mandibular advancement, offering precise titration and better adaptation to individual anatomical features. One widely used model is illustrated in this article as an example of clinical workflow, but the principles described are applicable to other custom-made bibloc MADs.

This article provides a standardized, step-by-step clinical protocol from initial evaluation to insertion, titration, and follow-up of bibloc devices illustrated with the Somnodent™ Flex (SomnoMed®) as an example of one of the most widely prescribed appliances worldwide. The primary aim is to provide physicians involved in sleep medicine with a clear and practical understanding of the clinical workflow required for effective MAD therapy. By detailing each step of the process, from impression taking to titration, it also facilitates communication with dental professionals and supports coordinated, patient-centered care.

General concept of the device

The Somnodent™ Flex is a custom-made titratable bibloc MAD, composed of two separate splints – one for the upper and one for the lower dental arch – designed to allow progressive and controlled mandibular advancement.

The maxillary splint (Fig. 1A) includes two lateral movable wedges equipped with a micrometric advancement screw (Fig. 1D-F), activating using a dedicated titration key (Fig. 1C). This mechanism enables precise bilateral advancement of the mandible, with a clear directional guide engraved on the appliance (yellow arrow). The mandibular splint (Fig. 1B) features two lateral wings that interlock with the movable wedges of the upper splint when the patient is in mandibular rest position, when the mouth is closed. As the screw are activated (Figs. 1E and F), the upper wedges slide forward, and push against the mandibular wings, thereby advancing the lower jaw.

The initial protrusion is generally set at 70% of the patient’s maximal protrusive capacity, as determined during the clinical examination (Fig. 1G), allowing for an effective yet comfortable starting position. Each activation increment is small and gradual, allowing the device to be titrated progressively to improve tolerance and efficacy: each quarter-turn (90°) of the key corresponds to an incremental advancement of 0.1 mm.

If advancement beyond 5 mm is needed, the internal mechanism can be reset and extended by the dental laboratory. The process is straightforward, and the practitioner is guided throughout by the dental laboratory. They simply need to return the models and the device to the lab, which will carry out the necessary modifications. This process includes repositioning the jack and, if not initially incorporated, adding metal pins and elastics (Fig. 1H). These additions are intended to stabilize the mandible and prevent downward tilting, which could otherwise compromise the therapeutic effect by allowing mandibular retroposition during sleep.

Several optional design features can enhance the effectiveness, comfort, or durability of MADs, but must be anticipated during the initial fabrication, as they may affect the overall design and thickness of the appliance.

Importantly, all of these customizations can be incorporated at no additional cost if requested at the time of the initial order. However, if they are added later – requiring the device to be returned to the manufacturer – additional charges apply, and the process may involve production delays or adjustments that alter the original fit. For this reason, early identification of patient-specific needs and close communication between the prescribing clinician and the dental laboratory are essential to optimize outcomes and avoid unnecessary costs.

Clinical workflow: from impression to titration of the Somnodent™ Flex device

Depending on national regulations, and once the dentist has confirmed clinical feasibility of using a MAD and provided adequate information to the patient, the therapeutic protocol can be initiated.

Dental impressions of the upper and lower arches

Accurate dental impressions are essential to ensure proper fit, retention, and initial stability of MADs. Two impression techniques may be used: conventional physical impressions or digital intraoral scanning, depending on equipment availability and practitioner expertise.

Conventional impressions

Commercially available impression trays (Fig. 2A), whether plastic or metal, perforated or not, must be carefully selected based on the width of the patient’s dental arches (Figs. 2B and C). The tray should be wide enough to avoid contact with the teeth, but not excessively large, to prevent material distortion. Once selected, the trays must be coated with adhesive compatible with the impression material to ensure retention and prevent detachment during removal. Materials such as Class A alginate, addition silicone, or polyether are commonly used, selected according to the expected time before processing. After setting, the impression is removed (Figs. 2D and E), disinfected, and sealed in a protective transport pouch.

Note

Impression quality is critical. Inaccuracies at this stage can compromise the precision fit and progressive titration of bibloc MADs such as Somnodent™ Flex. It is essential to strictly follow material handling protocols, tray selection, adequate adhesive and insertion/removal technique to avoid distortions.

Practical tip

(i) Regardless of the material used, a thin, uniform layer of tray adhesive should be applied and allowed to dry completely before placing the impression material. Excess adhesive must be avoided, as it can interfere with material adaptation and setting. (ii) Material selection should take into account the expected delay before model casting and transport conditions. Alginate is often preferred due to its low cost, ease of use, and high accuracy, but it is also highly sensitive to dehydration. To preserve its dimensional stability, the impression must be immediately sealed in a plastic pouch inflated with humid air, creating a saturated environment. Wrapping in a moist towel or gauze is contraindicated, as excess moisture leads to expansion and deformation by imbibition. Alginate impressions should be poured within 24 hours.

Alternatively, addition silicone or polyether materials may be used, especially if longer transport times are expected. These materials offer greater dimensional stability, but they are more rigid and may pose risks in cases of tight interdental spaces or fixed prostheses (e.g., bridges), where the material may flow under the restoration and lock the tray in place. In such cases, extreme caution must be exercised.

Digital impressions

When using intraoral scanners (Fig. 2F), it is essential to ensure full capture of all dental surfaces (Figs. 2G-J), especially: the distal surface of the most posterior teeth, and the gingiva up to 5 mm apical to the cervical margin, to allow proper appliance seating and comfort.

To enhance scan accuracy, the teeth must be thoroughly dried, and highly reflective areas (e.g., metallic restorations like amalgams) may need to be lightly powdered to reduce glare. The quality of the scanner also influences the ability to capture fine detail.

One advantage of digital impressions is that the dental laboratory can validate the scan in real-time, and request immediate corrections if areas are missing or distorted – reducing errors and fabrication delays.

Given the high cost of intraoral scanners, most non-dental practitioners do not have access to digital impression systems. Conventional impressions thus remain the most commonly used method in non-dental settings. Mastering their proper execution, evaluation, and storage is critical to avoid distortions and ensure a precise fit of the MAD.

Regardless of the device used, begin by loosening the lower screw of the gauge (Figs. 3A and G, orange arrow). Advance the slider to correctly position the mandibular incisors within their notch (Figs. 3A and G, black arrow), then retighten the lower screw to secure the position (Fig. 3H).

Next, loosening the upper screw (Figs. 3A and G, yellow arrow) to insert the fork (Fig. 3C), and verify that the fork can slide within the gauge without friction. Replace the gauge on the mandibular incisors in their notch, and invite the patient to close their mouth to insert their maxillary incisors in their notch (Figs. 3B and I, green arrow). The maxillary incisors must fit precisely into their notches (Figs. 3D and J). This step is critical to ensure fork stability, which directly impacts the accuracy of mandibular protrusion registration.

If this setup is insufficient – for example, if a gap is visible behind the maxillary central incisors -loosen the lateral screw (Fig. 3I, grey arrow) to adjust the lingual tilt by increasing the ramp’s slope of the fork. Slide the ramp (Fig. 3I, purple arrow) forward until the maxillary incisors are securely locked in place, with contact on both the palatal and labial surfaces, and retighten the lateral screw. This ensures a stable and reproducible registration (SomGauge™). Engraved numerical markings on the fork provide visual guidance for precise positioning (Fig. 3I, purple arrow).

If you are using a fork that does not offer this precise adjustment (e.g., Fig. 3B), it can be manually adapted by trimming the plastic or adding resin to properly support the maxillary incisors (George Gauge™).

Once the fork is properly positioned and stable, it is essential to ensure that there is at least 5 mm of interocclusal space between the posterior teeth. This clearance is necessary to accommodate the thickness of the future MAD.

With the upper screw still loosened, the clinician instructs the patient to repeatedly move the mandible from maximum retrusion (Figs. 3E and K) to maximum protrusion (Figs. 3F and L), ensuring that the movements are smooth and stable. Three measurements are taken to ensure reproducibility, and the retrusive and protrusive positions - as well as the total range of motion -are recorded on the laboratory order form (Suppl. 1).

Note

Fork instability may occasionally occur depending on incisor shape. If instability persists despite proper fork positioning and slope adjustment, it may be necessary to modify the fork by trimming excess plastic or adding resin to improve contact with the maxillary incisors. However, such adaptations usually require dental equipment and materials, and may not be feasible in non-dental settings. In these cases, referral to a dental practitioner is recommended to ensure accurate and stable registration.

Digital bite-registrations

After setting the therapeutic mandibular position at 70% (Fig. 5A: 0.7 × 9 ≈ 6 mm, and (−7) + 6 = −1 mm), the bite registration material is applied only in the molar regions (Fig. 5A). This approach facilitates spatial repositioning of the arches by allowing digital processing of the material-free anterior teeth.

Once the material has fully set, the lateral regions (left and right) are scanned digitally (Figs. 5B and C). The digital files are then exported to the manufacturer’s platform and merged with the previously acquired scans of the upper and lower arches taken separately (Figs. 2G–J), enabling accurate three-dimensional alignment in the initial protrusive position of the MAD (Figs. 5D–E).

Note

Before validating the intermaxillary registration, three critical elements must be verified: (i) frontal alignment: the registration fork must be properly aligned with the facial midline, particularly with the nasal axis; (ii) tooth positioning: the maxillary and mandibular incisors must remain in close contact with the positioning notches, ensuring a stable and reproducible position; (iii) the incisal midlines must be aligned, any deviation must be reported to the laboratory in the comment section; and (iv) impression continuity: the silicone registration material must provide continuous contact between the upper and lower arches, with no gaps or discontinuities.

Initial delivery and adaptation of the device

The custom-made Somnodent™ Flex device is delivered pre-set at 70% of the patients’ maximal mandibular protrusion. At the time of delivery, the clinician must verify the individual fit and comfort of each splint, ensuring they can be inserted and removed without discomfort. Insertion follows a defined sequence: the maxillary splint is positioned first, followed by the mandibular splint, which is guided into place by its lateral wings. Maintenance instructions are provided, including daily cleaning with a denture brush and neutral soap, air drying, and weekly disinfection with a product recommended by the manufacturer.

Note

Each splint must be perfectly adjusted before the patient leaves the clinic. Patients should be reassured regarding common initial adaptation symptoms (e.g., tightness, mild discomfort, articular pain, or excessive salivation) and should be encouraged to wear the device consistently. Titration generally begins 2 to 3 weeks after delivery, once the device is well tolerated throughout the night.

In some cases, minor adjustments may be required – for example, if the splint induces excessive pressure on specific teeth. These adjustments involve selective reshaping or relief, which must be performed using appropriate rotary instruments and clinical expertise.

Most non-dental clinicians are not equipped to perform such modifications. Therefore, collaboration with a trained dental professional is essential to ensure proper appliance adaptation and prevent early discontinuation of treatment.

Titration phase

Titration should only begin once the patient is able to wear the device comfortably and pain-free throughout the entire night. In the absence of contraindications, an initial mandibular advancement of 1 mm is typically recommended. Each subsequent adjustment should be spaced 2 to 3 weeks apart to allow for neuromuscular and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) adaptation. If discomfort or TMJ pain occurs, the advancement must be reversed on the affected side until symptoms fully resolve. Titration can then be resumed gradually, using smaller increments (e.g., 0.3 to 0.5 mm), to allow for better joint adaptation and patient comfort. The final titration position may vary depending on the clinical response and tolerance thresholds. It is important to note that final advancement may remain asymmetrical, especially in cases of unilateral TMJ sensitivity.

Note

Beyond objective indices such as AHI, arousal index or oxygen desaturation, patient-reported outcomes are crucial in guiding titration. Improvements in snoring, perceived sleep quality, daytime alertness, or morning symptoms often provide early and practical indicators of treatment efficacy. This is particularly relevant in daily practice, where access to full polysomnography may be limited and where clinical decisions often rely on patient feedback during the first weeks of therapy [6, 7].

Stabilization and confirmation of therapeutic position

The final titration point is determined either by complete resolution of clinical symptoms or when further mandibular advancement is no longer tolerated due to joint-related limitations, such as temporomandibular discomfort or fatigue. The effectiveness of mandibular advancement should ideally be evaluated by full-night polysomnography, which allows for a comprehensive assessment of residual respiratory events, sleep architecture, and oxygen saturation. In contexts where access to polysomnography is limited, home-based ventilatory polygraphy may offer a reasonable alternative, although it presents a lower diagnostic yield. In selected cases, additional advancement may remain possible, depending on the patient’s tolerance and the persistence of residual symptoms, when the AHI remains too elevated.

Long-term monitoring and maintenance

MAD therapy is a long-term, nightly treatment, and its efficacy and tolerance may evolve over time. Regular follow-up is essential to maintain clinical benefit and detect potential complications.

It is recommended that patients be re-evaluated at 6 months after initiation of therapy, and then annually, through coordinated follow-up between the prescribing sleep physician and a dental professional. Medical follow-up should focus on symptom control, treatment adherence, and overall sleep quality, while dental evaluation should assess oral health status, occlusion stability, and device integrity.

Beyond clinical monitoring, particular attention must be given to potential side effects. Short-term issues such as transient discomfort, dry mouth, excessive salivation, difficulty chewing in the morning, gum irritation, headaches or TMJ discomfort are relatively common during the first weeks of therapy and usually resolve spontaneously or with minor adjustments of the device [2, 7]. Long-term effects are predominantly dental and skeletal, including progressive reduction in overjet and overbite, changes in molar relationship, and mild tooth mobility [8–10]. These modifications are often asymptomatic at an early stage but may compromise tolerance and long-term efficacy if not anticipated [11].

Preventive strategies are therefore critical. Patients should be systematically instructed to perform daily morning jaw exercises (occlusal repositioning movements performed for a few minutes upon awakening) or to use a morning repositioning device immediately after removing the appliance. These measures have been shown to help restore mandibular and occlusal position, and to slow down the progression of occlusal changes [11]. Regular reinforcement of these instructions during follow-up visits is essential to maintain adherence.

Note

Patients must be clearly informed of the importance of seeking prompt evaluation in the event of persistent discomfort, TMJ pain, mucosal lesions, or any malfunction of the device. In case of MAD deterioration or maladjustment; or significant dental changes – such as tooth loss, malocclusion or the placement of new restorations – the device may need to be returned to the manufacturer for modification, or in some cases replaced entirely.

Discussion

MADs are now recognized as a validated, non-invasive therapeutic option for OSAHS, especially in patients with poor CPAP adherence or intolerance. Their advantages include individualized design adapted to dental morphology, precise and progressive titration, biocompatible materials, favorable tolerance, and measurable efficacy. Among the different types of MADs, bibloc appliances – notably the Somnodent™ Flex – are preferred over monobloc models due to their adjustability and patient comfort [2].

The AASM recommends the use of custom-made, titratable MADs over prefabricated or non-adjustable models [2]. These devices allow for incremental mandibular advancement, enabling optimization of the therapeutic position while minimizing discomfort and side effects. Efficacy must be verified through follow-up sleep testing, typically based on improvements in AHI, oxygen saturation, and patient-reported outcomes.

A growing body of evidence supports the clinical efficacy of MADs, including Somnodent™ Flex, in improving AHI and oxygen saturation, particularly in moderate OSAHS [12]. Despite variability in cohort sizes and follow-up durations, the reported side effects are generally mild, often reversible, and rarely necessitate discontinuation [2, 8–10, 13–15]. Nevertheless, as MADs are usually prescribed for long-term or lifelong use, side effects – especially occlusal and orthodontic changes – must be anticipated. Longitudinal studies show progressive reductions in overjet and overbite (up to 3.5 mm over 10 years), and minor molar positional changes [16, 17]. Beyond objective measures such as AHI reduction, oxygen desaturation or arousal index, several studies have highlighted the importance of patient-reported experience in guiding titration and ensuring long-term adherence. Recent work by Mezzofranco et al. [6] demonstrated that patients primarily value the reduction of snoring and improvement in sleep quality, while considering minor dental side effects of lesser importance. Similarly, Sutherland et al. [7] showed that tolerance is a key determinant of whether patients continue MAD therapy over time. According to Van der Hoek et al. [18], adherence to MAD treatment is influenced by multiple factors including psychological and personality traits, perceived effectiveness, side effects, the use of custom-made devices, dental treatments performed during MAD therapy, the initial experience with the MAD, and the communication skills of the practitioner. Taken together, these findings emphasize that systematic integration of patient-reported outcomes during titration is essential to optimize compliance and sustain therapeutic efficacy.

An 8-year observational study [9] confirmed that semi-rigid thermoplastic device (e.g. Somnodent™ Flex), induces minimal dental and skeletal changes, with mild incisor inclination modifications and negligible skeletal repositioning.

Various bibloc MADs, including semi-rigid devices with micrometric titration and soft liners, have shown comparable efficacy in reducing AHI and improving symptoms. Device selection should therefore rely on patient-specific factors (dental anatomy, tolerance, oral health status) rather than brand differences.

Finally, as with all therapeutic devices, clinical outcomes rely not only on device design, but also on practitioner expertise – particularly in impression quality, jaw registration, and adjustment protocols. The clinician’s familiarity with a specific MAD model plays a critical role in successful implementation. The evolution of digital workflows, including intraoral scanning and CAD/CAM manufacturing, is gradually gaining ground – even among non-dental practitioners – due to its potential to improve the precision and reproducibility of MADs. However, digital techniques do not eliminate the need for rigorous impression protocols. Proper tooth and soft tissue capture remains essential for device fit and efficacy. One major advantage of digital workflows is the ability to receive real-time feedback from the dental laboratory, enabling immediate validation or correction of the impression if needed. Moreover, digital files eliminate the logistical constraints of physical transport and processing delays, which can be particularly beneficial for practitioners working remotely from fabrication centers.

In addition, recent advances in telemedicine may further complement conventional follow-up of MAD therapy. Remote monitoring of patient-reported outcomes (snoring, sleep quality, daytime sleepiness) via digital questionnaires or mobile applications can provide valuable information between visits, while embedded microsensors (e.g., DentiTrac®, Theramon®, Thermocron iButton®) allow objective recording of device use [11]. At present, most of these tools have been developed for CPAP therapy rather than oral appliances, but with the increasing generalization of MAD indications, similar applications are expected to emerge to support patient monitoring and adherence. Teleconsultations may also help to address tolerance issues or adapt titration strategies in real time, particularly for patients living far from specialized centers. However, such tools should not replace in-person evaluation, since patients may not perceive progressive occlusal changes, tooth mobility, or early TMJ alterations. Clinical dental and medical follow-up remain essential, with telemedicine best considered as a complementary approach to strengthen, rather than substitute, standard care.

Conclusion and future directions

MADs are a proven, non-invasive alternative for the treatment of OSAHS, particularly in patients who cannot tolerate CPAP. Their success relies on precise diagnosis, appropriate patient selection, and rigorous clinical execution. Among available devices, titratable bibloc appliances combines titratability, comfort, and biomechanical safety, making it a valuable option for long-term management.

Digital workflows now enhance the precision and reproducibility of clinical steps and improve communication with dental laboratories. However, their success still depends on the operator’s technical skill and strict adherence to established protocols, along with long-term follow-up. While polysomnography remains the gold standard to confirm treatment efficacy, symptom resolution during titration often guides clinical decision-making in routine practice.

Looking ahead, wider access to MAD therapy will depend on the establishment of standardized clinical pathways and on strengthening interdisciplinary training and collaboration between dental and sleep medicine professionals.
