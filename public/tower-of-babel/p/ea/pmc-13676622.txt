A DFT-based Raman spectral atlas and vibrational assignment of aqueous glucose structures and derivatives
Authors: Kang Du, Meng Zhao, Tingting Wang, Guannan Qu, Hongxing Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus affects approximately 589 million adults worldwide, underscoring the need for accurate and noninvasive glucose monitoring to reduce complications. Although Raman spectroscopy shows promise for noninvasive blood glucose detection due to its molecular specificity and minimal interference, its application has been limited by the oversimplification of glucose as an open-chain structure in solution and unresolved assignments of characteristic bands—particularly the widely used 1125 cm⁻¹ peak. In this study, we combine density functional theory simulations with experimental Raman spectroscopy to elucidate the vibrational origins of glucose bands across all physiologically relevant tautomers and derivatives, including α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose, α-D-Glucofuranose and β-D-Glucofuranose, the open-chain form, D-Glucose-monohydrate, and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. We present the first comprehensive, assignment-validated Raman spectral atlas and vibrational assignment of accurate aqueous glucose structures, achieving unprecedented agreement between theory and experiment. Highly specific spectral markers are identified to distinguish pyranose and furanose rings, anomeric configurations, hydration states, and the bicyclic features of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. These findings resolve decades-long controversies in glucose vibrational spectroscopy and provide essential reference standards to advance Raman-based noninvasive monitoring of short-term glycemic control and early glycation risk in diabetic patients.

INTRODUCTION

Glucose, the most abundant monosaccharide in nature, serves as a critical nutrient and energy source in human physiology1. Diabetes mellitus currently affects more than 530 million adults worldwide, highlighting the urgent need for accurate and noninvasive glucose monitoring technologies2,3.

Conventional glucose detection methods, including enzymatic assays, infrared (IR) spectroscopy, and microwave-based optical techniques, are limited by operational complexity, high labor intensity, inability to differentiate sugar types, low absorption coefficients with significant overlap from water bands, high power consumption, elevated costs, instability due to oxygen dependence and biofouling, and sensitivity to environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and density4–14. Traditional invasive blood glucose monitoring further carries risks of pain and infection.

In contrast, Raman spectroscopy provides superior molecular specificity with minimal interference and high precision through detection of narrow vibrational bands characteristic of glucose15–18. It is insensitive to pH and temperature variations, requires no sample pretreatment or destruction, and exhibits a linear relationship between signal intensity and analyte concentration under controlled conditions, making it ideally suited for quantitative and noninvasive analysis19–22. Coupled with chemometric methods, Raman spectroscopy enables continuous monitoring and label-free concentration prediction, establishing it as one of the most promising core technologies for noninvasive blood glucose detection15–21.

However, despite substantial experimental progress since 2007, much of the literature has oversimplified glucose as existing predominantly in its open-chain form, largely overlooking the influence of its dynamic tautomeric equilibrium in aqueous solution on Raman spectra23–31. Many studies have relied on indirect evidence, such as statistical correlations with reference concentrations, and have routinely employed the band at 1125 cm⁻¹ for quantification—as observed in works by Wu24, Lin27, and Kang30—without rigorous vibrational assignment or justification. This lack of definitive evidence for glucose-specific Raman features has fueled long-standing skepticism regarding the technique’s specificity and reliability20,21.

Although early studies suffered from the limitation of neglecting the contribution of anharmonic effects to Raman intensities32,33, the hybrid protocol combining density functional theory (DFT) with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (QM/MM with explicit solvent) has emerged as a prominent direction in modern computational spectroscopy. This approach has been extended to the chiral variant of Raman spectroscopy—Raman optical activity (ROA). ROA is a sophisticated technique that measures the differential Raman scattering intensity of right- and left-circularly polarized light, thereby capturing three-dimensional chiral conformational information of molecules34–40. ROA is particularly powerful for probing the fine vibrational modes of carbohydrates in aqueous solution and is highly sensitive to hydrogen-bonding networks, ring puckering, and anomeric equilibria. MD simulations provide dynamic conformational sampling and a realistic hydration environment34, while DFT accurately computes polarizability derivatives and ROA tensors. The resulting averaged spectra show excellent agreement with experimental observations35–38. This methodology has achieved atomic-level structural elucidation for monosaccharides such as methyl-β-D-Glucopyranoside. However, challenges remain, including high computational cost, low efficiency, and the need for continuous refinement of accurate force fields through extensive experimental–computational complementarity39–43. Originating and developed primarily in the United Kingdom39–43, ROA is a valuable extension of Raman spectroscopy. Compared with conventional Raman instruments, ROA systems are significantly more expensive and impose stricter operational and environmental requirements particularly for fluorescence suppression. Consequently, its adoption remains limited. Nevertheless, ROA represents an important frontier in structural biology and spectroscopy: the high flexibility, conformational isomerism, and strong solvent dependence of carbohydrates—properties that are difficult to address with traditional methods—can be precisely quantified within the ROA + DFT/MD (QM/MM) framework. This level of accuracy opens new avenues for monosaccharide research40–47.

It is noteworthy that the static DFT Raman results obtained in this study can serve as a fundamental reference, providing a solid foundation for future dynamic investigations based on Raman/ROA combined with DFT/MD simulations. This perspective helps position the present work within the broader context of computational vibrational spectroscopy of carbohydrates.

As early as 1929, Haworth and coworkers established that glucose in solution predominantly exists as cyclic hemiacetals, forming six-membered pyranose or five-membered furanose rings rather than the open-chain aldehyde48–50. At 22 °C, the aqueous equilibrium consists of approximately 37% α-D-Glucopyranose, 62% β-D-Glucopyranose, <1% combined α-D-Glucofuranose and β-D-Glucofuranose, and only 0.002% open-chain form51–55. These cyclic structures possess lower free energy and exhibit weakened ring-breathing modes compared with the linear form, resulting in markedly different Raman signatures between solution and solid states53–57. In aqueous systems, glucose exists in a dynamic equilibrium involving open-chain and cyclic interconversion, anomeric isomerism, and conformational transformations. Consequently, slightly different distributions of glucose isomeric forms have been reported in previous studies refs. 51–55.

Critically, the minor open-chain and furanose forms—rather than the dominant pyranose species—are primarily responsible for protein glycation and polyol pathway activation in diabetic complications due to their reactive aldehyde functionality58,59. Even small increases in furanose content (<1%) disproportionately enhance glycation flux60. Traditional chromatographic and NMR methods lack the sensitivity and temporal resolution to monitor these trace tautomers effectively. Commercially available glucose powder typically exists in the form of α-D-Glucopyranose monohydrate, whose stability is maintained by an extensive hydrogen-bonding network involving water molecules. 1,5-Anhydroglucitol is a monocyclic derivative formed by intramolecular dehydration of β-D-Glucopyranose and is the only biomarker approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) of Japan for assessing short-term glycemic control3. The enzymatic colorimetric method currently used for its detection is costly and unsuitable for point-of-care or high-throughput testing scenarios61.

Existing studies41–46,62–66 lack a complete and rigorously calibrated set of Raman reference spectra for all physiologically relevant glucose tautomers and derivatives. This deficiency has resulted in inconsistencies and incompleteness in the historical band assignments within the carbohydrate fingerprint region (700–1500 cm⁻¹), significantly hindering the clinical translation of related technologies. In the present study, high-accuracy DFT simulations were combined with experimental Raman spectroscopy. With reference to the published literature24–30,41–46,62–65, the molecular vibrational origins of the characteristic bands for all relevant glucose tautomers and derivatives were systematically elucidated. A detailed analysis of the Raman spectra was performed, focusing on the three most critical regions for monosaccharides: the anomeric region (750–1000 cm⁻¹), the C–C/C–O fingerprint region (1000–1200 cm⁻¹), and the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region (1400–1500 cm⁻¹).

This work extends our team’s previous study published in 202566 by including the previously overlooked but important furanose forms, α-D-Glucofuranose and β-D-Glucofuranose, as well as the key derivative 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. Crystalline Raman spectra were simulated for both α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose. Furthermore, the molecular model of D-Glucose-monohydrate was refined based on the complementary nature of experiment and theory, leading to significantly improved agreement between the simulated and experimental spectra.

To the best of our knowledge, this study presents the first comprehensive and vibrationally assigned Raman spectral library for the accurate aqueous glucose structures, encompassing α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose, α-D-Glucofuranose, β-D-Glucofuranose, D-Glucose-monohydrate, the open-chain form, and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. Good consistency was achieved between theoretical calculations and experimental results.

The study also identified highly specific spectral markers capable of effectively distinguishing pyranose versus furanose rings, anomeric configurations, hydration states, and the bicyclic signature of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. These findings resolve the long-standing controversy regarding the assignment of the 1125 cm⁻¹ band. The results provide a critical reference standard and theoretical foundation for advancing Raman spectroscopy-based noninvasive short-term blood glucose monitoring and early glycation risk assessment in diabetic patients.

To validate the accuracy of DFT calculations employed in this study, we compared the computed Raman spectrum of α-D-Glucopyranose in its crystalline form with the corresponding experimental spectrum recorded under identical conditions. Both spectra are presented with the same intensity scaling and are overlaid for direct comparison.

Overall, the DFT-simulated spectrum with crystal lattice included via periodic boundary conditions reproduces the experimental Raman profile with excellent agreement throughout the entire fingerprint region (300–1500 cm⁻¹). Key spectral features, including band positions, relative intensities, and fine structure, are faithfully captured, demonstrating the reliability of the chosen computational protocol for predicting vibrational signatures of carbohydrate crystals.

In the low-wavenumber region (300–700 cm⁻¹), which is dominated by collective skeletal deformations and lattice modes, the simulated spectrum accurately reproduces the complex multiplet patterns observed experimentally. The intense doublet centered near 542 cm⁻¹, arising primarily from δ(C2–C1–O1) bending motion coupled with ring deformation, is particularly well resolved in both traces.

In the anomeric region, the crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose exhibits three characteristic Raman bands at approximately 762.9 cm⁻¹, 838.7 cm⁻¹, and 914.1 cm⁻¹, which are assigned to the ν(C–O) stretching vibration of the anomeric C1–O5 bond, coupled with CH₂ rocking vibrations. Notably, as early as 1942, Spedding65 reported a unique peak for crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose at 842 cm⁻¹. Considering possible experimental variations in methodology and instrumentation, this peak is confirmed to correspond to the characteristic band observed at 838.7 cm⁻¹ in both the experimental spectrum reported by Dujardin67 and the simulated spectrum in the present study. This consistency further validates the reliability of the computational simulation.

Minor deviations (<4 cm⁻¹) are observed for some weaker bands in this region, well within the typical accuracy of contemporary DFT methods for solid-state systems.

The C-C/C-O fingerprint region (1000–1200 cm⁻¹) exhibits excellent correspondence. The intense band at 1031.6 cm⁻¹, associated with coupled C–O and C–C stretching vibrations involving the anomeric carbon, appears at nearly identical wavenumber and intensity in the simulated spectrum. Adjacent features at approximately 1070 and 1115 cm⁻¹ are also accurately predicted, confirming that the theoretical model correctly describes the subtle vibrational differences introduced by the α-configuration at the anomeric center.

In the higher-wavenumber portion (1200–1500 cm⁻¹), which includes the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region (1400–1500 cm⁻¹), dominated by CH₂ and CH deformation modes, the agreement remains remarkably good. The characteristic multiplet structure and relative intensities are preserved, with root-mean-square deviation of scaled frequencies below 6 cm⁻¹ across the entire spectral range.

These results establish that the periodic DFT approach employed here provides spectroscopic accuracy comparable to high-resolution experimental measurements for crystalline carbohydrates. The close match validates its subsequent use for spectral assignments, conformational analysis, and interpretation of anomeric effects in both crystalline and amorphous glucose systems investigated in this work.

The experimental Raman spectrum is overlaid with the fully periodic DFT-simulated spectrum calculated at the same theoretical level as used for the α-anomer.

The level of agreement between theory and experiment for β-D-Glucopyranose is equally impressive and, in several regions, even superior to that observed for the α-form. The simulated spectrum accurately captures virtually all major bands in terms of position, intensity, and band shape across the entire fingerprint region, underscoring the high fidelity of the computational protocol for solid-state carbohydrate systems irrespective of anomeric configuration.

In the low-frequency region, the intense doublet near 527 cm⁻¹, previously assigned to δ(β)(C2–C1–O1) bending coupled with ring puckering, is precisely replicated in position and splitting pattern. The complex skeletal deformation manifold below 600 cm⁻¹, highly sensitive to crystal packing and hydrogen-bonding networks, shows near-quantitative agreement, with all major experimental features faithfully mirrored.

Particularly diagnostic differences between α- and β-anomers are reproduced with remarkable precision in the anomeric region. The most prominent marker band for the β-anomer, a strong and sharp peak at 896.1 cm⁻¹ assigned predominantly to the symmetric stretching of the anomeric C1–O5 and C1–O1 bonds ν(β)(C2–C1–O1) breathing mode), is predicted at 896.4 cm⁻¹ with nearly perfect intensity match.

Immediately adjacent, the C-C/C-O Fingerprint region the intense experimental band at 1051.2 cm⁻¹—characteristic of β-configured glucopyranose and absent in the α-form—is accurately reproduced both in wavenumber (1050.8 cm⁻¹) and relative intensity, providing unequivocal computational confirmation of its origin in coupled C–O/C–C stretching motions involving the equatorial anomeric hydroxyl group.

The higher-wavenumber portion includes the -CH₂OH region. also exhibit excellent correspondence, with band positions deviating by less than 7 cm⁻¹ on average and relative intensities preserved throughout. The overall root-mean-square deviation of scaled vibrational frequencies for the β-crystal is 5.4 cm⁻¹, slightly better than the 5.8 cm⁻¹ obtained for the α-polymorph.

Summary of Experimental and Simulated Raman Spectra of Crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose

Direct comparison of experimental and DFT-simulated Raman spectra of crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose highlighting anomer-specific vibrational signatures in the 300–1500 cm⁻¹ region.

Superposition of the experimental and simulated Raman spectra of the two pure anomers reveals a series of highly diagnostic, configuration-dependent differences that are reproduced with extraordinary fidelity by the periodic DFT calculations (Figs. 3 (c) and 5 (c)).

In the ring-breathing region from 500 to 550 cm⁻¹, the α-anomer exhibits an intense doublet with its maximum at 542 cm⁻¹, while the β-anomer displays a slightly lower-frequency, sharper doublet centered at 527 cm⁻¹. This approximately 15 cm⁻¹ downshift in the β-form stems from differential coupling of the axial versus equatorial anomeric OH group with ring skeletal motions.

The anomeric region at 750–1000 cm⁻¹ is the most diagnostically significant interval, with the largest number of distinguishable spectral features. Crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose exhibits three characteristic Raman bands at approximately 762.9 cm⁻¹, 838.7 cm⁻¹, and 914.1 cm⁻¹, whereas crystalline β-D-Glucopyranose shows only one prominent characteristic Raman band at 896.5 cm⁻¹.This indicates that, although both anomers display strong absorption peaks in the C–O stretching vibration region of the anomeric carbon, there are substantial differences in both the peak positions and the number of characteristic bands. In the crystalline state, the stretching vibration of the C1–O5 bond in the pyranose ring is highly sensitive to the anomeric configuration. Moreover, significant differences are observed in peak intensities and band shapes between the two anomers, enabling reliable differentiation of the configurations. The simulated Raman spectra are in good agreement with the experimental literature data for both anomers65,67.

In the C-C/C-O fingerprint region, a highly specific marker for the β-anomer appears as a prominent, isolated peak at 1051 cm⁻¹, which is completely absent in the α-anomer spectrum. This band, accurately predicted at 1050.8 cm⁻¹, arises from symmetric stretching involving the equatorial C1–OH and adjacent C–O bonds and is widely recognized as the most reliable Raman marker for β-configured glucopyranose units in solids and solutions. Conversely, the α-anomer exhibits stronger intensity near 1115–1125 cm⁻¹ and a distinct shoulder around 1032 cm⁻¹, features that are significantly attenuated in the β-form. These variations reflect altered C–C and C–O coupling patterns induced by the axial anomeric substituent.

More subtle yet reproducible differences occur in the CH₂ and CH deformation region from 1200 to 1400 cm⁻¹. The β-anomer consistently shows higher relative intensity in the 1350–1380 cm⁻¹ window, associated with CH₂ wagging and HO–C–H bending.

In the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region slightly reduced intensity near 1460 cm⁻¹ compared with the α-form, consistent with minor differences in exocyclic CH₂OH rotamer populations imposed by crystal packing.

All these anomer-specific differences are quantitatively reproduced by the DFT simulations, with frequency deviations typically below 6 cm⁻¹ and relative intensity errors under 10%. These results not only validate the vibrational assignments but also demonstrate that periodic DFT calculations at this level effectively capture the subtle electronic and steric effects underlying anomeric differentiation in vibrational spectra.

The confirmed anomer-specific Raman signatures—particularly the number and position of peaks in the anomeric region, the presence or absence of the 1051 cm⁻¹ β-marker and the distinct frequency and intensity patterns around 520–550 cm⁻¹—provide a robust, non-destructive spectroscopic tool for unambiguous identification and quantification of α- versus β-content in crystalline, amorphous, and solution-state glucose samples, as applied in subsequent sections of this study.

Taken together, the outstanding agreement observed for both α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose crystals unequivocally validates the accuracy of the periodic DFT approach for predicting Raman spectroscopic signatures of crystalline carbohydrates. These results provide a solid foundation for confidently using the same methodology to assign vibrational bands, interpret anomeric and exocyclic rotamer effects, and deconvolute spectra of amorphous and solution-state glucose samples presented in subsequent figures.

Comparative Analysis of Raman Spectral Differences between Furanose and Pyranose Forms of D-Glucose

In this study, a systematic comparative analysis was conducted on the Raman spectra of furanose forms (α-D-Glucofuranose and β-D-Glucofuranose) and pyranose forms (α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose). A series of distinct and diagnostically valuable spectral markers were identified, which can directly reflect the structural differences between five-membered and six-membered ring systems.

In the anomeric region: the characteristic peaks of α-D-Glucofuranose appear at 778 cm⁻¹ and 998 cm⁻¹, primarily attributed to coupled C–O stretching and ring breathing vibrations of the five-membered ring. Additional bands at 826 and 860 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–C stretching coupled with CH twisting vibrations. For β-D-Glucofuranose, the unique characteristic peak at 764 cm⁻¹ is assigned to terminal OH twisting vibration, while the bands at 732, 957, and 978 cm⁻¹ arise from five-membered ring C–O stretching coupled with ring breathing vibrations. In contrast, α-D-Glucopyranose exhibits characteristic peaks at 821 and 906 cm⁻¹, while β-D-Glucopyranose shows strong characteristic peaks at 816, 873, and 936 cm⁻¹. Both anomers share strong common peaks at 966 and 984 cm⁻¹. Overall, the peaks of pyranose forms are located at higher wavenumbers (particularly in the 966–984 cm⁻¹ range). The ring breathing vibrations of the six-membered ring occur at lower frequencies.

Furanose (five-membered ring) exhibits higher frequencies for ring breathing and C–O stretching vibrations, with sharper and more concentrated peaks. Pyranose (six-membered ring) displays lower frequencies and is dominated by -CH, -OH, and -CH₂OH deformation modes, resulting in richer but more dispersed intensity distributions. Both forms show coupling between C–C/C–O vibrations and the ring skeleton; however, the α/β anomeric configurations in furanose produce more pronounced differences. α-D-Glucofuranose tends to favor intra-ring C–O coupling, whereas β-D-Glucofuranose enhances terminal OH twisting, making the anomeric region more sensitive to the stereochemistry at the C1 position.

In the C–C/C–O fingerprint region: α-D-Glucofuranose displays characteristic peaks at 1028, 1100, 1139, and 1201 cm⁻¹, among which the band at 1100 cm⁻¹ is unique to α-D-Glucofuranose and corresponds to main-chain C–C stretching coupled with CH₂OH twisting vibration. β-D-Glucofuranose exhibits prominent characteristic peaks at 1028, 1072, 1157, and 1188 cm⁻¹ with significant intensities. The five-membered ring leads to tighter C–C/C–O coupling. For α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose, consistent peaks with similar intensities are observed at 1040/1042, 1085, and 1125 cm⁻¹, where the 1085 cm⁻¹ band is a characteristic -CCO breathing/bending mode.

The fingerprint region of furanose shows richer peaks with higher intensities, particularly for β-D-Glucofuranose, owing to the tighter C–C/C–O torsional coupling induced by the five-membered ring. In contrast, pyranose forms are characterized by the strong common peak at 1125 cm⁻¹ (C–C stretching on the pyranose ring backbone), reflecting a more stable six-membered ring skeleton, with a prominent -CCO breathing/bending peak. The band at 1028 cm⁻¹, assigned to main-chain CH–OH stretching coupled with ring C–O twisting, appears as a strong common peak in furanose, whereas 1125 cm⁻¹ serves as the universal skeletal peak in pyranose. This indicates that ring skeleton C–C/C–O stretching is the core vibrational feature shared by both ring types. The anomeric configuration indirectly influences coupling strength through the C1–O orientation, with the β-form enhancing the intensity of certain peaks in both ring systems.

In the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region:

α-D-Glucofuranose shows characteristic peaks at 1412 and 1472 cm⁻¹. β-D-Glucofuranose exhibits additional peaks with higher intensities at 1459, 1475, 1501, and 1532 cm⁻¹, indicating stronger CH–OH torsional coupling. For α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose, the characteristic peaks appear at 1374, 1420, and 1505 cm⁻¹ for the α-anomer, and at 1415 and 1530 cm⁻¹ for the β-anomer, respectively, with overall lower wavenumbers.

Furanose exhibits more peaks with higher intensities in this region—particularly β-D-Glucofuranose—due to stronger coupling between the terminal CH₂OH group and the ring moieties, induced by the five-membered ring structure. In contrast, pyranose peaks are consistently shifted to lower wavenumbers because of the greater conformational stability of the six-membered ring and the effects of solid-state hydrogen bonding, which enhance the -CH deformation modes.The common vibrational modes involving main-chain and ring C–C stretching, along with CH–OH twisting, indicate that local CH₂OH vibrations are less influenced by ring size and are primarily modulated by the anomeric configuration. The β-form enhances torsional intensities in both ring structures.

Significant differences exist between the Raman spectra of the five-membered furanose and six-membered pyranose forms of glucose across all three spectral regions. The furanose form exhibits higher frequencies for ring breathing and C–O/C–C coupling vibrations, stronger peak intensities, and a greater variety of vibrational modes—particularly in the β-anomer—reflecting a more direct influence of the anomeric configuration. In contrast, the pyranose spectra display generally lower wavenumbers and are dominated by the stable skeletal C–C stretching mode at 1125 cm⁻¹, along with CH and OH deformation modes. The correlation between the two ring forms is evident in the shared ring skeletal C–C and C–O stretching modes, as well as the CH₂OH twisting vibrations, reflecting the fundamental vibrational characteristics of glucose derivatives.

In summary, Raman spectroscopy enables precise differentiation between the furanose and pyranose configurations of glucose based on four robust and reliable criteria: (1) a strong absorption peak in the 730–735 cm⁻¹ region serves as a diagnostic marker for furanose; (2) a strong peak in the 1040–1060 cm⁻¹ region corresponds to pyranose; (3) an intense characteristic peak at 1125 cm⁻¹ signifies the chair conformation of pyranose, while its absence or extremely weak intensity indicates furanose; and (4) multiple rich absorption peaks in the 950–1000 cm⁻¹ region provide supporting evidence for furanose. These spectral features offer a rapid, non-destructive method for monitoring sugar ring conformational equilibrium in solutions, amorphous materials, and biological systems, presenting significant potential for applications in studies of mutarotation and the quantitative analysis of anomeric isomers.

Biological and clinical significance of furanose forms in diabetes research

Although D-Glucose predominantly exists in the pyranose form (>99%) in solid crystals and aqueous solutions at room temperature51–55, the furanose forms play a critical role as the truly reactive species in diabetes-related physiological, pathological, and pharmacological contexts. Protein non-enzymatic glycation requires exposure of the aldehyde or hemiacetal group in the open-chain or furanose configurations68–70. In pyranose rings, the C1 position is protected by the internal ether linkage and is largely unreactive toward glycation. In contrast, the higher ring strain in furanose structures facilitates easier opening of the C1–O5 bond, resulting in open-chain aldehyde concentrations approximately 20–50 times higher than in pure pyranose forms71,72. Consequently, even minor elevations in furanose proportion during chronic hyperglycemia in diabetic patients can markedly accelerate key glycation processes in hemoglobin, lens proteins, albumin, and other targets, ultimately leading to irreversible advanced glycation end products (AGEs)73. The distinctive Raman signature of furanose breathing at 730–735 cm⁻¹ offers a highly specific early signal of true glycation activity, detectable far earlier than elevations in HbA1c.In diabetic microvascular complications, local accumulation of furanose forms occurs at sites of pathology. Overactivation of the aldose reductase pathway in these regions converts substantial amounts of glucose to sorbitol while concurrently promoting buildup of furanose intermediates. In vitro experiments have shown significantly stronger furanose NMR and Raman signals in high-glucose-cultured endothelial cells compared with normal controls62–64,74–76. These observations suggest that furanose species may act as key toxic isomers driving oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades. As a potential Raman probe for next-generation noninvasive glucose monitoring, the furanose form exhibits an exceptionally strong and isolated peak at 730–735 cm⁻¹ with virtually no overlap from other biomolecules. Its levels correlate positively with instantaneous blood glucose, making it theoretically superior to the weaker pyranose signal at 1125 cm⁻¹, which suffers severe interference from starch and glycogen. Establishing a quantitative relationship between furanose peak intensity and glycation risk could enable development of novel diagnostic indicators that reflect true glycemic toxicity burden rather than mere concentration.

In conclusion, despite their extremely low equilibrium abundance, furanose forms represent the genuine active minority responsible for glycation, polyol pathway activation, drug responsiveness, and microvascular toxicity in diabetic states. The precise furanose-specific Raman peaks identified in this work—particularly the 730–735 cm⁻¹ breathing mode and the enriched region from 950 to 1000 cm⁻¹—provide a reliable spectral benchmark for capturing this critical isomer in vivo in tissues, dried serum spots, or noninvasive skin measurements, offering substantial potential for advancing diabetes mechanistic research and clinical translation.

D-Glucose-monohydrate

The detailed simulated Raman spectrum of D-Glucose-monohydrate in the fingerprint region has been described in our previous work66. However, upon comparison with the experimental Raman spectrum of D-Glucose-monohydrate, noticeable discrepancies were observed. Following the principle advocated by Mutter et al.41–46. regarding the complementary nature of experiment and theory, the comparison between simulated and experimental spectra can provide valuable structural insights and reveal information about molecular conformations, and vice versa. In fact, experimental data serve as the gold standard for guiding computational modeling and simulation.

After careful analysis, it was found that the D-Glucose-monohydrate molecular structure used in the previous work66, obtained from PubChem77, was the only bimolecular complex employed in the calculations. In this initial structure, the distance between the geometric centers of the water molecule and α-D-Glucopyranose was 6.5011 Å, while the centroid distance was 6.2980 Å. The relatively large difference between these two distances likely contributed to the instability of the overall molecular system. In accordance with relevant literature10, the structure of D-Glucose-monohydrate was therefore re-optimized. Using the geometry optimization function implemented in Gaussian 09 software, the structure was optimized to its minimum energy configuration. After optimization, the geometric center distance between water and α-D-Glucopyranose decreased to 4.3288 Å, and the centroid distance became 4.3452 Å. Raman spectrum calculations were subsequently performed on this optimized structure, yielding results that showed significantly improved agreement with the experimental spectrum.

Both α- and β-anomers show closely related bands near 1420/1415 cm⁻¹ (assigned to -CH deformation on -CH₂OH); however, these peaks are systematically lower in wavenumber than those in the solid state. This suggests that the CH₂OH vibrations in isolated molecules lack the hydrogen-bonding constraints imposed by neighboring water molecules and adjacent glucose units in the crystal lattice, resulting in systematic shifts in both position and intensity. In the crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose, both experimental and simulated spectra display only the H-symmetric vibration on -CH₂OH, with the -CH deformation mode being suppressed, likely due to the restrictive influence of the extended lattice hydrogen-bond network and crystal packing.

The substantial differences among the spectra can be attributed to the following factors:

(1) Glucose powder (D-Glucose-monohydrate) samples are typically in the crystalline form of α-D-Glucopyranose, occasionally containing minor amounts of the anhydrous α-form or trace β-form. During sample preparation, slight solid-state mutarotation or the presence of amorphous components may occur, leading to additional peaks. As a highly ordered three-dimensional hydrogen-bonded system, crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose is strongly influenced by the lattice hydrogen-bond network, crystal packing, and water molecules. These factors significantly affect vibrational modes in the anomeric region, including C–H deformations, pyranose ring C–O–C breathing vibrations, and modes sensitive to anomeric configuration.

(2) The Gaussian 09 calculations for isolated α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose molecules were performed at the B3LYP/6-311G level in the gas phase. This isolated-molecule model does not account for the periodic crystalline environment or the additional hydrogen bonds formed between crystalline water molecules and hydroxyl groups, resulting in relatively poor agreement with experimental solid-state spectra. In contrast, the D-Glucose-monohydrate model consists of a binary system comprising one α-D-glucopyranose molecule and one water molecule. By optimizing the centroid and geometric distances according to relevant literature, the simulation achieves significantly improved agreement with the experimental solid-state spectrum.

(3) Although the periodic density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) calculations using CASTEP 8.0 better incorporate the crystalline environment, they are still limited by the approximations inherent in the GGA-PBE functional, particularly in describing weak hydrogen-bond interactions and anharmonic effects. Additional constraints arise from computational parameters such as k-point sampling and dispersion corrections, which prevent complete reproduction of collective vibrations and temperature effects under experimental conditions. Furthermore, variations in experimental parameters—including laser wavelength and power, sample particle size, and humidity—can also subtly influence the solid-state Raman spectra37,65,67.

Overall, the experimental Raman spectra more closely reflect the true solid-state structure of D-Glucose-monohydrate, while the simulated spectra serve as valuable references for assigning individual conformational contributions. Better agreement is achieved when crystalline models are employed. The calculated spectrum of D-Glucose-monohydrate shows reasonable consistency with the experimental trend in terms of peak envelope and skeletal vibrations, indicating that the computational model provides useful insight into CH/OH deformation modes. The observed differences between experiment and simulation are physically reasonable and consistent with the distinct hydrogen-bonding networks present in α-D-Glucopyranose crystals versus the monohydrate. Theoretical calculations can effectively assist in the assignment of vibrational modes; however, the anomeric region of solid-state Raman spectra is highly sensitive to the crystalline environment. Further improvement in the agreement between experiment and theory will require higher-accuracy hybrid functionals or simulations that explicitly incorporate temperature effects.

The selection and assignment of characteristic peaks of D-Glucose

Theoretical simulations have demonstrated that the stability of the C-C skeleton structure in the precise configuration of glucose solution results in conformational changes occurring solely at the terminal ends. This phenomenon imparts consistent specificity to the characteristic peak at 1125 cm⁻¹. Consequently, this provides a crucial theoretical foundation for addressing the ongoing debate regarding the effectiveness of glucose characteristic peaks in Raman spectroscopy at the molecular structure level. The simulation and experimental results for the two configurations of glucopyranose and D-Glucose-monohydrate, as illustrated in Fig. 10, reveal peak values in the C-C/C-O fingerprint region at 1040 cm⁻¹, 1080 cm⁻¹, 1125 cm⁻¹, and in the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region at 1420 cm⁻¹. These peaks are indicative of the selected glucose compound.

High-precision Raman spectra of glucose solutions at various concentrations were acquired using WinSpec32 2.5 software. Baseline correction was performed via seventh-order polynomial fitting, followed by normalization using the water bending peak at 1640 cm⁻¹ as an internal standard. This procedure successfully yielded the Raman spectra of standard glucose solutions. The detailed experimental spectra have been described in our previous work66.

Water signal subtraction in glucose solutions

In the present study, particular attention was paid to the subtraction of the water signal in carbohydrate solutions. A recent computational study by Karafyllia76 demonstrated that polar or charged solutes can non-additively enhance the Raman scattering of surrounding water molecules, particularly in the OH stretching region. This effect renders perfect solvent subtraction often unattainable. The influence of anions and polar groups was found to be significantly stronger than that of cations, with the enhancement being even more pronounced under resonance conditions.

However, in the carbohydrate systems investigated in this work, with glucose as the representative molecule, although the extent of this effect differs markedly from that observed in charged or resonantly enhanced systems reported in the literature, the possibility of non-additive interference cannot be entirely ruled out. The specific reasons are as follows:

First, glucose is a neutral, non-absorbing, and transparent molecule that contains a large number of polar OH groups but carries no net charge. It exhibits no significant absorption at common Raman excitation wavelengths, e.g., 532 nm and thus does not undergo resonance enhancement. Second, once mutarotation equilibrium is reached in aqueous solution, the Raman spectrum of glucose becomes significantly broadened, exhibiting solvent-induced line broadening. In the carbohydrate fingerprint region (700–1500 cm⁻¹), the large number of C–C and C–O bonds with similar chemical environments results in extensive delocalization of numerous normal modes. This leads to highly overlapped frequencies, forming broad and complex envelope peaks rather than sharp, well-resolved characteristic bands. Such delocalized characteristics render the carbohydrate spectra insensitive to minor conformational or hydrogen-bonding perturbations, thereby increasing the difficulty of precise spectral interpretation.

More importantly, water, as the solvent, produces an inherently strong Raman signal. Its characteristic bands (approximately 200, 320–1020, 1650, 2167, and 3440 cm⁻¹) overlap severely with the vibrational modes of glucose, particularly in the OH stretching region (3100–3700 cm⁻¹) and the bending region 1640 cm⁻¹. The abundant OH groups of carbohydrates form dynamic hydrogen-bonding networks with water molecules, which can induce local perturbations and intensity redistribution in the Raman signal of water. Consequently, direct background subtraction using the spectrum of pure water is not always reliable and frequently results in residual OH stretching peaks or baseline distortions. Even with software-based subtraction, complete elimination of non-additive effects remains challenging.

Furthermore, as emphasized in this study, glucose in aqueous solution exists in multiple interconverting precise structural equilibrium states through various interactions with water molecules. This significantly increases the complexity of the solution spectra24–30,37,65,80. Both simulation and experimental results indicate that these interactions exert distinct effects on glucose conformation and its Raman spectrum. Moreover, according to previous MD studies on glucose34,37,39–46, accurate spectral simulation requires explicit inclusion of water molecules at distances ranging from 3 Å to 12 Å1,3,8–10. The influence on different wavenumber regions varies with the solvation radius, which does not align with the nearest few layers of the hydrogen-bonding network (<4–5 Å) emphasized in Karafyllia’s study76. While studies on polar systems in the literature have demonstrated that using NaCl solution rather than pure water as the background can significantly improve subtraction quality, this approach is not applicable to carbohydrate aqueous solutions represented by glucose. Therefore, the feasibility of pure-water baseline subtraction is lower for carbohydrates than for charged or resonantly enhanced complexes. Direct subtraction using the pure water spectrum is not always reliable and often leads to spectral distortion due to residual water signals, particularly in the OH stretching region.

In view of the above considerations, the present study, as well as our previous work66, did not adopt the simple method of directly subtracting the pure water spectrum. Instead, all Raman spectra of glucose solutions at different concentrations and the pure water spectrum underwent seventh-order polynomial baseline fitting to effectively remove fluorescence background and cosmic ray interference. Additionally, the pure water spectrum and the spectra of glucose solutions at various concentrations are presented together. Normalization was performed using the water bending peak at 1640 cm⁻¹ and the characteristic glucose peak at 1125 cm⁻¹ to clearly illustrate the relationship between glucose peak intensity and concentration, thereby minimizing the influence of solvent signals on data interpretation as much as possible.

Nevertheless, we fully recognize that, owing to the hydrogen-bonding interactions between the polar OH groups and water, the “non-additive enhancement” effect described by Karafyllia still exists to a certain extent and is difficult to completely eliminate. This represents a common challenge in the Raman spectroscopy of carbohydrate aqueous solutions.

Detailed comparison of the Raman spectra of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol and β-D-Glucopyranose

A detailed comparison of the Raman spectra of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol and β-D-Glucopyranose underscores their close structural relationship—the former being the 1,5-Anhydroglucitol derivative of the parent pyranose form—while highlighting characteristic differences arising from the absence of the anomeric hydroxyl group and the formation of an additional tetrahydrofuran-like ring in the anhydro compound. In the anomeric region, the diagnostic -CH and -OH deformation mode occurs at 796 cm⁻¹ in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol and at 816 cm⁻¹ in β-D-Glucopyranose. The lower wavenumber observed in the anhydro form is attributable to reduced hydrogen-bonding constraints at the anomeric position. Both compounds share several vibrational features that reflect the preserved pyranose chair conformation and the -CH₂OH side chain. For instance, the -CH₂O deformation vibration appears at nearly identical positions around 872 cm⁻¹. Multiple -CH deformation modes are present in both spectra; however, 1,5-Anhydroglucitol shows three distinct peaks at 925 and 954 cm⁻¹, compared to three peaks in β-D-Glucopyranose, consistent with slightly higher symmetry in the bicyclic system. In the C-C/C-O fingerprint region, the intense C-C stretching within the six-membered ring remains close to 1016 cm⁻¹. The characteristic hexagonal annular breathing mode shifts slightly from 1042 cm⁻¹ in β-D-Glucopyranose to 1059 cm⁻¹ in the anhydro derivative due to the fused bicyclic framework. Most notably, the diagnostically important pyranose C-C stretching band blue-shifts from 1125 cm⁻¹ in β-D-Glucopyranose to 1160 cm⁻¹ in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol, a shift of approximately 35 cm⁻¹ that reflects increased ring strain from the additional ether linkage. Higher-wavenumber -CH deformation modes appear near 1200–1400 cm⁻¹ in both compounds, but 1,5-Anhydroglucitol exhibits a more complex cluster arising from coupled -CH₂ and C-O stretching motions influenced by the bicyclic structure. In the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region, the symmetric H vibration on -CH₂O is sharper and occurs at a lower wavenumber of 1474 cm⁻¹ in the anhydro form, compared to a broad envelope between 1415 and 1530 cm⁻¹ in β-D-Glucopyranose, again indicating reduced intermolecular hydrogen bonding at C1. Overall, the spectral pattern of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol closely resembles that of β-D-Glucopyranose in the fingerprint region dominated by the pyranose conformation but exhibits systematic blue shifts in ring skeletal modes, sharper and better-resolved bands, and dramatic transformations in the anomeric region—from 816 to 796 cm⁻¹. Additionally, the intraring C–C stretching of the six-membered ring shifts from 1125 cm⁻¹ to 1160 cm⁻¹. These pronounced differences provide reliable spectroscopic markers for distinguishing 1,5-Anhydroglucitol from its parent hexose in biological, pharmaceutical, and synthetic samples62,81,82. A direct overlay of the simulated Raman spectra shown in Fig. 13(e) further emphasizes the unambiguous spectral changes induced by intramolecular 1,5 dehydration. These changes enable the detection of trace amounts of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol through the simultaneous appearance of the 796/1160 cm⁻¹ doublet and depletion of native glucose bands, offering a robust, non-destructive probe for monitoring dehydration in glucose-containing materials and for quantifying this validated plasma biomarker of short-term glycemic control83–85.

This work establishes a definitive Raman spectral reference library for 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. Due to the scarcity of reported Raman spectra for this compound in the literature, the accurately assigned characteristic bands—including 796 cm⁻¹ (–CH/–OH deformation), 872 cm⁻¹ (–CH₂O deformation), 1016 cm⁻¹ (C–C stretching), 1059 cm⁻¹ (ring breathing), and 1160 cm⁻¹ (ring skeletal C–C stretching)—address a critical gap and serve as a direct reference for the rapid identification of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol in subsequent analyses of biofluids, serum, tissues, or noninvasive Raman measurements.

The study identifies highly specific marker peaks suitable for rapid clinical detection. The significant ~35 cm⁻¹ blue shift of the C–C stretching band—from 1125 cm⁻¹ in the parent β-D-Glucopyranose to 1160 cm⁻¹ in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol—along with the ring breathing shift from 1042 cm⁻¹ to 1059 cm⁻¹, directly reflects the loss of the C1 hydroxyl group and the formation of an additional ether linkage, resulting in a rigid bicyclic system. These intense peaks exhibit minimal overlap with other carbohydrates, maintaining high specificity even in complex serum backgrounds, and thus represent ideal analytical targets for Raman-based quantification of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol.

The findings establish a new spectral probe foundation for noninvasive or minimally invasive monitoring of glycemic fluctuations. Current noninvasive glucose meters primarily rely on near-infrared absorption, where glucose signals are inherently weak and subject to significant interference. In contrast, 1,5-Anhydroglucitol concentrations vary over a much larger dynamic range than glucose itself and exhibit sharper Raman features. The precise peak positions and assignments reported here can be directly utilized to develop quantitative Raman models for real-time, dynamic assessment of short-term glycemic control86–88.

Furthermore, this methodology provides a transferable template for the rapid identification of carbohydrate drug metabolites and structural analogs. Since 1,5-Anhydroglucitol plasma levels increase significantly following treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, the integrated experimental, computational, structural, and spectral correlation workflow established here can be extended to Raman analysis of other deoxy sugars, sugar alcohols, and glycosylated drug metabolites89–91.

In summary, this Raman spectroscopic simulation and systematic comparison not only provide 1,5-Anhydroglucitol with a reliable spectral identity but also establish a solid theoretical foundation and characteristic peak reference for its clinical application as a short-term glycemic control biomarker in rapid Raman detection. This work offers significant academic value and promising prospects for point-of-care diagnostic applications62.

Comparative Raman Vibrational Analysis of D-Glucose Forms and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol

The tabulated Raman spectral data offer a comparative overview of characteristic vibrational bands in the fingerprint region for D-Glucose-monohydrate experimental and simulated, α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose, and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. These assignments emphasize structural sensitivities in carbohydrate vibrational spectra, particularly regarding hydration state, anomeric configuration, and ring modifications.

In the anomeric region, bands primarily arise from coupled –CH and –OH deformation modes. Features near 810–821 cm⁻¹ and 862–873 cm⁻¹ are consistently observed across most forms, with minor shifts reflecting differences in hydrogen bonding between the monohydrate and anhydrous states. A –CH₂O deformation mode at approximately 880 cm⁻¹ is prominent in hydrated glucose forms but absent in the α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose, and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. Additional –CH deformation vibrations appear at 906–936 cm⁻¹ and 960–984 cm⁻¹, with the higher-frequency band more pronounced in α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose as well as in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol.

In the C–C/C–O fingerprint region, the spectrum is dominated by skeletal C–C and C–O stretching modes characteristic of the pyranose ring. A conserved C–C stretching band appears near 1014–1042 cm⁻¹ across all compounds. Ring-specific C–C–O bending of the hexagonal pyranose framework is evident at 1074–1085 cm⁻¹ in the α- and β-anomers but is absent in the D-Glucose-monohydrate experimental and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol, consistent with differing conformational or hydration influences. Another strong C–C stretching mode is consistently observed near 1125 cm⁻¹, extending to 1160 cm⁻¹ in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol.

The higher wavenumber region predominantly involves –CH deformation and C–O stretching modes. –CH deformation bands appear at 1230–1252 cm⁻¹, followed by C–O stretching near 1316–1332 cm⁻¹. Overlapping –CH₂/–CH₃ stretching vibrations are observed around 1340–1363 cm⁻¹. A broad –CH deformation mode is present at 1374–1389 cm⁻¹ in most forms, except for β-D-Glucopyranose.

In the CH₂OH hydroxymethyl region, symmetric hydrogen vibrations of –CH₂O exhibit pronounced anomer-dependent shifts (1420/1505 cm⁻¹ in α-D-Glucopyranose versus 1415/1530 cm⁻¹ in β-D-Glucopyranose), extending to 1478 cm⁻¹ in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. A distinctive C–O vibration associated with –CH₂O is observed exclusively at 1580 cm⁻¹ in the hydrated forms of glucose.

Notable trends include a close agreement between the experimental D(+)-Glucose and simulated monohydrate spectra, supporting the reliability of computational assignments. Anomeric differences in α- and β-D-Glucopyranose are evident through positional shifts in ring breathing, C–O stretching, and CH₂O-related vibrational modes, attributable to variations in axial versus equatorial hydroxyl orientation and intramolecular hydrogen bonding. The spectrum of 1,5-Anhydroglucitol exhibits unique or intensified bands—for example, at 1059, 1221, 1239, and 1478 cm⁻¹—consistent with the loss of the anomeric hydroxyl group and the formation of an internal ether linkage. These comparative data underscore the high specificity of Raman spectroscopy for probing subtle structural variations in glucose polymorphs, anomers, and derivatives, providing valuable fingerprints for identification and conformational analysis in carbohydrate chemistry.

The present study represents the first systematic Raman investigation to simultaneously combine:

(1) High-precision quantum-chemical simulations of all physiologically and chemically relevant glucose forms: D(+)-Glucose, D-Glucose-monohydrate, α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose, α-D-Glucofuranose, β-D-Glucofuranose, 1,5-Anhydroglucitol, and D-Glucose-monohydrate experimental results.

This integrated approach holds profound scientific and clinical significance.

For the first time, a complete, fully assigned, and theoretically reproduced Raman fingerprint spectrum has been established for each major precise glucose structure. Notably, the simulated spectra of D-Glucose monohydrate, α-D-Glucopyranose, and β-D-Glucopyranose crystals exhibit excellent agreement with experimental results, with average deviations of less than 8 cm⁻¹. This spectral library resolves long-standing ambiguities in band assignments, particularly in the skeletal region, and provides rigorous vibrational mode identifications across the entire fingerprint region. It serves as a definitive benchmark for spectral interpretation in any glucose-containing sample—whether crystalline, amorphous solid, solution, biofluid, or tissue—thereby facilitating accurate reference assignments in future studies.

Direct superposition of simulated crystal spectra with experimental crystal spectra reveals that intermolecular hydrogen bonding within the lattice induces only minor shifts (typically less than 15 cm⁻¹) in most vibrational modes. Importantly, the critical ring-breathing marker peaks—essential for characteristic band assignments—remain essentially unaffected. This observation holds true for key structure-specific bands identified in the molecular simulations, including the furanose mode at 730–735 cm⁻¹, pyranose ring breathing at 1040–1060 cm⁻¹, and the pyranose chair C–C stretching at 1125 cm⁻¹. These findings demonstrate that such diagnostic spectral features are intrinsically linked to molecular structure and can be reliably extrapolated from crystalline standards to disordered solid or solution environments, underscoring their robustness for spectral interpretation across different physical states.

The approximately 35 cm⁻¹ blue shift of the C–C stretching on the pyranose ring backbone—from 1125 cm⁻¹ in β-D-Glucopyranose to 1160 cm⁻¹ in 1,5-Anhydroglucitol—along with the distinct furanose breathing mode at 730–735 cm⁻¹, has now been rigorously validated through both computational and experimental datasets. These two marker peaks exhibit high intensity, exceptional sharpness, and minimal overlap with proteins, lipids, or other carbohydrates, making them ideal candidates for detecting the short-term glycemic control biomarker 1,5-Anhydroglucitol and the true glycating species (furanose forms) in serum, tissues, or even via noninvasive transcutaneous measurements. This spectral specificity establishes a robust foundation for future Raman-based diagnostic strategies in diabetes management, enabling precise monitoring of glycemic fluctuations and early glycation risk beyond conventional glucose concentration measurements alone.

In summary, this study integrates advanced quantum chemical predictions with experimental data from crystals, solutions, and noninvasive measurements to provide a comprehensive Raman spectral atlas encompassing all relevant precise structural forms of glucose. This work not only resolves long-standing controversies in band assignments but also directly supports the development of rapid, noninvasive, spectroscopy-based tools for glycemic control monitoring, early glycation risk assessment, and glucose structural differentiation.

CONCLUSION

This study establishes a comprehensive Raman fingerprint library encompassing all accurate aqueous glucose structures within a unified, high-level density functional theory framework. Accurate aqueous glucose structures Raman spectra were simulated and fully vibrationally assigned for seven glucose-related species: D(+)-Glucose, D-Glucose-monohydrate, α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose, α-D-Glucofuranose, β-D-Glucofuranose, and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol. No prior single study has achieved such exhaustive coverage of these tautomers and derivatives. Experimental Raman spectra were acquired for common glucose powder (D-Glucose-monohydrate) as well as crystalline α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose—and directly compared with the simulations.

Through combined experimental and theoretical analysis, an isolated characteristic peak in the 730–735 cm⁻¹ region was identified and confirmed for furanose forms. This intense band was unambiguously assigned to the collective breathing vibration of the five-membered ring, establishing it as an unequivocal specificity marker for glucofuranose structures and addressing a long-standing gap in spectral assignments. This feature provides a reliable benchmark for detecting trace ( < 1%) furanose species in vivo or in solution.

Furthermore, systematic blue shifts were observed and quantified for the first time in the blood glucose biomarker 1,5-Anhydroglucitol relative to its parent compound, β-D-glucopyranose. Strong peaks at 1125 cm⁻¹ and 1042 cm⁻¹ shifted to 1160 cm⁻¹ and 1059 cm⁻¹, respectively. These shifts directly reflect increased rigidity due to the bicyclic ether linkage, providing highly specific spectral signatures for identifying 1,5-Anhydroglucitol in complex clinical matrices. Remarkably, simulated spectra of D-Glucose-monohydrate, α-D-Glucopyranose and β-D-Glucopyranose crystals achieved unprecedented agreement with experimental results, exhibiting an average absolute deviation of only 5.6 cm⁻¹. This exceptional accuracy confirms that core skeletal vibrations—specifically ring breathing and C–C stretching—are predominantly governed by intramolecular structure, even in the presence of extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding within the lattice. These findings provide a robust theoretical foundation for extrapolating crystalline reference spectra to disordered biological environments such as serum or tissue.

Building on these insights, a simple yet robust three-peak rule was proposed and validated for the rapid structural determination of glucose forms: a strong peak at 730–735 cm⁻¹ indicates furanose, a prominent feature at 1040–1060 cm⁻¹ denotes pyranose, and a peak at 1125 cm⁻¹ confirms the characteristic glucose signature. The latter appears in both glucose solutions and transcutaneous measurements, although it is partially obscured by skin components, further affirming 1125 cm⁻¹ as the optimal glucose marker and effectively resolving longstanding controversies regarding peak efficacy.

Finally, a direct link was established between furanose Raman features and the true glycation activity relevant to diabetic pathophysiology. The intensity at 730–735 cm⁻¹ emerges as a potential biomarker capable of reflecting glycation risk earlier and more directly than glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), marking a paradigm shift from mere concentration monitoring to targeted assessment of toxic isomers. In conclusion, this study not only addresses longstanding gaps in Raman spectral coverage for glucose and its derivatives but also constructs a comprehensive translational pathway—from quantum chemical computations to potential clinical applications in diabetes management—advancing carbohydrate molecular spectroscopy and photonic diagnostics for diabetes.

METHODS

Computational method

All ab initio calculations for the accurate aqueous glucose structures and its derivative 1,5-Anhydroglucitol were performed using Gaussian 0992. The Raman spectra of the crystalline forms were simulated with the CASTEP 8.0 package93,94. The plotting of molecular simulated Raman spectra and the visualization analysis of Raman vibrational modes were carried out using Multiwfn 3.895 and GaussView 6.096, with reference to the previously reported literature24–30,41–46,62–66.

Molecular Raman spectroscopy simulation

In this study, DFT62 calculations were performed using the Gaussian 0992 software package. Geometry optimizations and gas-phase Raman spectra calculations for the precise structures of glucose in solution were carried out at the B3LYP97,98 level of theory with the 6-311 G basis set. The molecular structures used included D(+)-Glucose, D-Glucose-monohydrate, α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose, α-D-Glucofuranose, β-D-Glucofuranose, and the β-D-Glucopyranose derivative 1,5-Anhydroglucitol, all of which were obtained from the PubChem database77.

Raman spectra of D(+)-Glucose, D-Glucose-monohydrate, α-D-Glucopyranose, β-D-Glucopyranose have been partially reported in our previous work. In the present study, the simulation system was expanded by including the previously overlooked structures of α-D-Glucofuranose, β-D-Glucofuranose, and 1,5-Anhydroglucitol, thereby completing the comprehensive set of precise glucose structures in solution. Additionally, the molecular model of D-Glucose-monohydrate was refined based on the complementary relationship between experiment and theory39–43,46, resulting in significantly improved agreement between the simulated and experimental spectra.

Compared with the early Hartree–Fock (HF) method, density DFT significantly improves the accuracy of calculated harmonic vibrational frequencies and better accounts for electron correlation effects. It has become the standard approach for computational Raman and ROA studies39–46.

Numerous benchmark studies have demonstrated that hybrid functionals generally outperform pure functionals. The B3LYP hybrid functional exhibits excellent performance in reproducing experimental Raman/ROA band shapes, relative intensities, and signs. It provides the best or near-optimal results compared with other functionals such as BLYP, B3PW91, CAM-B3LYP, M06L, or WB97X, while maintaining a favorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency39–46,62,99–103.

The choice of basis set is particularly critical for Raman and ROA calculations. Small basis sets such as 6-31 G or rDPS offer low computational cost but are insufficient for accurately reproducing spectral details. Although large basis sets such as aug-cc-pVTZ provide higher accuracy, they incur significantly increased computational expense with only marginal improvements. Medium-sized basis sets such as 6–311 G can deliver performance comparable to more expensive basis sets at a reasonable computational cost and are therefore recommended for most carbohydrate Raman/ROA simulations39–46,68,69. Overall, the DFT/B3LYP/6-311 G level of theory has become widely accepted for Raman and ROA simulations of carbohydrates, particularly monosaccharides. It has been proven in numerous studies to reliably reproduce experimental spectra, especially in the context of monosaccharide simulations39–46,99–105.

Crystal Raman spectroscopy simulation

The representative crystal structures of α-D-Glucopyranose (COD: 1518432)106 and β-D-Glucopyranose (COD: 2101292)107 were obtained from the Crystallography Open Database (COD)108–116. These structures served as the initial geometries for crystal geometry optimization and Raman spectrum calculations using the CASTEP 8.0 (Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package)93,94 software package within the framework of DFT with the plane-wave pseudopotential method. Geometry optimizations were performed using the Wu–Cohen (WC) generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with norm-conserving pseudopotentials. The plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 600 eV. Brillouin zone integration was carried out using the Monkhorst–Pack scheme with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional117. The k-point separation was set to 0.08 Å⁻¹, resulting in a k-point sampling grid of (1 × 1 × 1). This grid was fully equivalent in the reciprocal a, b, and c directions with no directional bias. Phonon modes were calculated using linear-response density functional perturbation theory (DFPT)118–122. The self-consistent field (SCF) convergence threshold was set to 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ eV per atom. Both geometry optimization and energy calculations were performed using the generalized gradient approximation with the PBE functional (GGA-PBE)117.

Molecular simulation Raman spectrum analysis

Quantum chemical programs directly calculate the Raman activity of each vibrational mode, with the unit typically being Å⁴/amu. Raman activity is an intrinsic characteristic of each vibrational mode, independent of the incident light frequency and temperature. Experimental measurements

Glucose powder (D-Glucose-monohydrate) analytical grade was purchased from Tianjin Hengxing Chemical Reagent Manufacturing Co., Ltd. and used directly without further purification. A 1600 mg/dL glucose standard solution was obtained from Guangzhou Weihe Medical Technology Co., Ltd. Serial dilutions were performed using ultrapure water at a 1:2 ratio for each step. After each dilution, the solution was mixed thoroughly using a vortex mixer for 10 min and then allowed to stand for 10 min to ensure mutarotation equilibrium, in accordance with the requirements of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia125 and relevant literature126,127. This process was repeated to obtain glucose solutions with final concentrations of 800, 400, 200, 100, 50, 25, and 12.5 mg/dL. Detailed sample preparation procedures followed those described in our previous work66. Raman spectra were measured in a dark room at a constant temperature of 22 °C using a Princeton Instruments (PI) TriVista CRS555 triple-stage micro-confocal laser Raman spectrometer in Fig. 14. The experimental conditions were as follows: excitation laser wavelength of 532 nm30,39–46,66, laser power at the sample of 400 mW; spectral resolution of 0.6 cm⁻¹, and an integration time of 5 s per acquisition. This integration process was repeated 10 times to minimize the influence of cosmic rays and random errors. For each concentration, at least three independent measurements were recorded via point-by-point scanning, from which average spectra and corresponding standard deviations were calculated. In the in vivo transcutaneous Raman measurements, the volunteer’s finger was placed on the sample stage and irradiated using a defocused laser illumination configuration without precise objective focusing. The laser power was set to 50 mW, generating a homogeneous circular spot with a diameter of approximately 6 mm on the skin surface. The spot area was calculated from the experimentally measured beam diameter rather than estimated using the diffraction-limited spot formula. Under these irradiation conditions, the laser power density was 0.177 W/cm², which remained below the maximum permissible skin exposure limit (<0.200 W/cm²) specified in the Occupational Exposure Limits for Hazardous Agents in the Workplace—Part 2: Physical Agents (GBZ 2.2-2007), Safety of Laser Products—Part 1: Equipment Classification and Requirements (IEC 60825-1:2014), and American National Standard for Safe Use of Lasers (ANSI Z136.1-2022)128–130. These parameters therefore satisfied established laser safety standards for skin exposure and were considered suitable for in vivo transcutaneous Raman spectroscopic measurements.

In vivo transcutaneous Raman spectra were subsequently collected under different blood glucose concentrations to obtain the raw spectral datasets. The Raman signal integration time was set to 3 s to ensure sufficient photon collection while avoiding detector saturation. Each acquisition was repeated ten times to minimize the influence of cosmic ray interference and random experimental noise. For each glucose concentration level, at least three independent spectral measurements were recorded through sequential point-by-point measurements, and the averaged spectra were used for subsequent analysis.

Blood glucose concentrations were determined using a medically certified YUWELL580 glucometer following completion of the in vivo transcutaneous Raman measurements. The volunteer’s blood glucose level was measured every 30 min after meals, resulting in four different blood glucose concentration levels.

Throughout the experiments, both the operators and volunteers wore laser-protective eyewear to prevent potential laser-induced ocular damage.
