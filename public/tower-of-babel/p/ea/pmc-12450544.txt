Carrier-free nanoparticles based on natural products trigger dual "synergy and attenuation" for enhanced phototherapy of liver cancer
Authors: Xiongqin Wang, Kunhui Sun, Junlin Dong, Yanli Ge, Haifan Liu, Xingyue Jin, Xiao Pei, Ping Wang, Jianbing Jiang, Tiejie Wang, Xuelei Hu, Shuguang Yuan, Bing Wang, Xie-an Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarrier-free nanoparticles have attracted significant interest owing to their exceptional drug-loading efficiency, simple preparation, and good biosafety. Inspired by the synergism and attenuation strategy of natural products, carrier-free nanoparticles were constructed from a compatibility perspective, aiming to carry natural products to tumor sites as nanoparticles to achieve precise anti-cancer effects. Herein, the carrier-free nanoparticles based on gambogic acid and glycyrrhizic acid (GG NPs) were successfully formed via self-assembly. The internally generated "synergism and attenuation" GG NPs could enhance the anti-tumor effect, reduce toxicity to normal cells, and exhibit tumor-targeting capabilities. Further, to explore the role of GG NPs in enhancing phototherapy, the photosensitizer ZnPc 2 was self-assembled with GG NPs to form GGZ NPs. In vitro studies demonstrated that the GGZ NPs significantly improved phototherapecutic efficacy. Compared with ZnPc 2, the photothermal conversion efficiency was increased to 80.8 %, along with a 1.8-fold increase in tumor growth inhibition, and the singlet oxygen yield of GGZ NPs increased by 2-fold. In vivo experiments demonstrated that GGZ NPs exhibit significant liver-targeting ability and could reduce the liver toxicity induced by gambogic acid, as well as decrease the blood biochemical levels of AST and ALT, achieving externally assisted "synergism and attenuation". Hence, carrier-free nanoparticles based on natural products would bring a new treatment modality to the field of precision liver tumor therapy.Keywords: Carrier-free nanoparticles, Natural products, Synergistic and attenuation, Phototherapy, Liver cancer

Introduction

Carrier-free nanoparticles, as an innovative form of drug delivery, are constructed through molecular self-assembly of active pharmaceutical ingredients (prodrugs, pure drugs, or amphiphilic conjugates) without inert excipients [[1], [2], [3]]. In recent years, carrier-free nanoparticles have attracted considerable research interest owing to simplified preparation, ultrahigh drug loading, and multifunctional therapeutic capabilities [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Despite the inherent limitations of carrier-free nanomedicines, such as the lack of active targeting functionality, which can lead to non-specific accumulation at non-target sites and raise safety concerns, affecting delivery and release efficiency in vivo, natural products offered significant potential for constructing carrier-free nanomedicines due to their intrinsic targeting properties [1,9,10].

Natural product-derived carrier-free nanotherapeutics have emerged as a promising strategy for oncological applications [11]. Researchers have incorporated natural products into nanomedicines to leverage their therapeutic potential and enhance drug stability through nanotechnology, achieving precise release and targeted delivery [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Natural products demonstrate broad-spectrum bioactivity, encompassing antitumor, free radical-scavenging, anti-inflammation, and immune-regulating effects [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, some natural products and chemotherapeutic drugs face challenges in clinical application due to their low bioavailability and systemic toxicity [21,22]. Recent advances in carrier-free nanotherapeutics demonstrate improved therapeutic outcomes through molecular self-assembly strategies [23,24]. Tian et al. developed celastrol/erianthin nanoparticles that simultaneously enhanced bioavailability and reduced toxicity in breast cancer treatment [25]. Similarly, Guo et al. engineered ICG@UA/PTX NPs via π-π stacking and hydrophobic/electrostatic interactions, achieving potent anticancer activity with improved safety profiles [26]. Therefore, formulating these natural products into carrier-free nanoparticles through self-assembly could leverage the advantages of nanotechnology to address issues such as bioavailability and systemic toxicity.

Herein, we formulated carrier-free nanoparticles (GG NPs) through the combination of natural products gambogic acid (GA) and glycyrrhizic acid (GL), utilizing hydrophobic interactions. GA, a potent natural antitumor compound, demonstrates broad-spectrum cytotoxicity against multiple malignancies including hepatocellular carcinoma, breast adenocarcinoma, and gastric neoplasms [[27], [28], [29]]. Previous studies indicate that GA can perturb cellular redox homeostasis by elevating reactive oxygen species (ROS) while reducing antioxidant glutathione (GSH), primarily through inhibition of thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) activity, leading to ROS accumulation [[30], [31], [32]]. Furthermore, studies have identified GA as a naturally occurring HSP90 inhibitor [33]. Nevertheless, its theraputic potential is constrained by poor bioavailability and dose-limiting systemic toxicity [34,35]. GL, a pentacyclic triterpenoid derived from Glycyrrhiza species, which exhibits hepatoprotective effects on the liver [36,37]. This compound selectively targets overexpressed GL receptors on hepatocellular carcinoma cells, enabling discrimination between malignant and normal hepatocytes. This receptor-specific recognition facilitates tumor-selective drug delivery [[38], [39], [40]]. And it protects liver cells from damage through the mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic pathway [41]. Therefore, we constructed GG NPs, which could effectively enhance the anti-tumor effect, reduce toxicity to normal cells, and realize the internally generated "synergism and attenuation" strategy of carrier-free nanoparticles. This innovative strategy not only demonstrates the great potential of modernization research of natural products but also provides new insights and strategic approaches for advancing tumor-targeted nanomedicine development.

Phototherapy offers distinct advantages in oncology by enabling precise, light-triggered activation that enhances tumor-specific drug release while overcoming critical limitations of natural products, including low bioavailability and systemic toxicity [42,43]. Conversely, natural products can potentiate phototherapy by sensitizing cancer cells to light-induced damage and mitigating off-target effects. Further, we constructed carrier-free nanoparticles with synergism and attenuation, combined them with photosensitizers, and implemented a combination strategy of carrier-free nanoparticles and phototherapy for treating hepatocellular carcinoma [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48]]. Using the photosensitizer ZnPc 2 as an example, we co-assemble it with GG NPs to form GGZ NPs (Scheme 1). ZnPc 2 exhibited an enhanced photothermal effect due to assembly process, which increased the photothermal conversion efficiency of GGZ NPs to 80.8 %. GGZ NPs demonstrated dual tumor-targeting mechanisms, combining passive accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention effect (EPR) with active targeting mediated by GL ligands. This synergistic targeting approach significantly enhanced tumor-specific drug deposition. The acidic tumor microenvironment further enhanced the photodynamic effect of GGZ NPs, specifically manifested as a twofold increase in singlet oxygen production. In addition, GGZ NPs could enhance the production of ROS to disrupt intracellular redox homeostasis by consuming glutathione and inhibiting thioredoxin, and they could also inhibit HSP90 to reduce cellular thermotolerance, thereby enhancing the overall phototherapy effect. Consequently, the natural products-based construction of carrier-free nanoparticles demonstrated excellent anti-tumor efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. This approach minimized off-target toxicity while enabling externally assisted "synergism and attenuation" with reduced side effects.

Materials and methods

Materials and instruments

Gambogic acid and Glycyrrhizic acid were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin (China). Phthalocyanine (ZnPc 2) was made in-house. 1,3-Diphenylisobenzofuran was purchased from Shanghai Macklin (China). MEM medium and RPMI-1640 medium were purchased from Wuhan Pricella (China). Cell Counting Kit-8, Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (JC-1), and GSH and GSSG Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime (China). Albumin transaminase (ALT/GPT) test kit and glutamine transaminase (AST/GOT) test kit were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng (China). CM-H2DCFDA, Hoechst 33342, MitoTracker Green, LysoTracker Red, LIVE/DEADTM viability/cytotoxicity kit (Green/Deep Red) were purchased from Thermo Fisher (USA). Antibodies were purchased from ABclonal (China).

The optical absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-2450 spectrophotometer (Japan). Particle size distribution and zeta potential were analyzed with a Malvern ZS90 nanoparticle analyzer (UK). Sample morphology and structure were examined by TEM (JEM-F200, Japan). Cytotoxicity against HepG2 cells was assessed using a Thermo Fisher microplate reader. Fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss Axio Observer 1, Germany) captured cell fluorescence (FL) images. Real-time thermal imaging and temperature monitoring were conducted with FLIR E8 infrared camera. Photoacoustic (PA) imaging was performed on a Vevo LAZR-X system (FujiFilm VisualSonics, Canada), while FL data were acquired using an IVIS Lumina III imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA). The drug release was measured using a triple quadrupole liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system (TripleQuad4500, SCIEX).

Fabrication of GA NPs, GG NPs, and GGZ NPs

GA, GL, and ZnPc 2 were dissolved in methanol to prepare stock solutions with final concentrations of 2 mM, 2 mM, and 2 mg/mL, respectively. The nanoparticle formulation was achieved through an optimized nanoprecipitation method: the mother liquor was gradually introduced into ultrapure water using a pipetting gun (10 μL/min) under continuous sonication (500W ultrasonic processor) in an ice bath maintained at 4 ± 1 °C. The resulting suspension was then subjected to methanol evaporation under controlled nitrogen purging (0.5 L/min, 2 h duration) to obtain the final nanoparticles.

In vitro drug release assay

GGZ NPs solution (2 mL) were placed into the dialysis bags, and the dialysis bags were tied at both ends. The dialysis bags were placed into pH 7 PBS buffer (40 mL). On the other, the dialysis bags were placed into pH 3 PBS buffer (40 mL). The release medium (1 mL) was taken out at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 16, 24, 36, 48 h respectively, and 1 mL PBS buffer were supplemented to the release circumstance. Subsequently, GA and GL in the NPs were determined and calculated by the LC-MS.

Photothermal effect

GGZ NPs were tested at five ZnPc 2 concentrations (50–150 μg/mL in 25 μg/mL increments) with appropriate controls (GG NPs, ZnPc 2, water). For photothermal experiments, GGZ NP solutions (100 μg/mL, 2 mL aliquots) were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation (10 min duration) at varying power densities (0.8–1.6 W/cm2 in 0.2 W/cm2 increments). To test stability, the GGZ NPs (100 μg/mL, 2 mL) underwent repeated laser exposure cycles (808 nm, 1.6 W/cm2), with real-time temperature measurements taken every 30s using IR camera. The photothermal conversion efficiency was determined according to the established methodology reported in reference. [49].

Detection of ROS

DPBF, a singlet oxygen (1O2) probe, was employed to evaluate ROS generation. GGZ NPs (acid-treated and untreated), GG NPs, and DPBF (80 μM) were dissolved in DMSO. The samples were exposed to 690 nm laser irradiation (power density: 0.2 W/cm2) for 120 s, during which UV–Vis absorption spectra (300–900 nm range) were recorded at 15-s intervals to track the decomposition kinetics of DPBF.

PA performance

The photoacoustic properties were evaluated using a multimodal imaging system. Imaging tubes containing reference samples (water, GG NPs, ZnPc 2) and GGZ NPs (200 μg/mL) were arranged in an ultrasound coupling gel phantom and scanned from 680 to 970 nm. Following initial characterization, a concentration-dependent study was conducted with GGZ NPs (0–200 μg/mL) using the same imaging parameters.

Cell culture

HepG2 and L02 cells were maintained at 37 °C/5 % CO2 in MEM and RPMI-1640 media, respectively, each supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % Antibiotic-Antimycotic.

AST and ALT assays

Following 24-h cell attachment, L02 hepatocytes were treated for overnight with either GA, GA NPs, or GG NPs, while untreated cells served as negative controls. Hepatotoxicity evaluation was performed by quantifying serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels according to the standardized protocols provided with the commercial diagnostic kits.

Cell viability assay

HepG2 and L02 cell were plated in 96-well culture plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. To investigate GA-GL combinatorial effects, cultures were exposed to GA, GA NPs, or GG NPs for 24 h. For phototherapy assessment, HepG2 cells were incubated with GGZ NPs (24 h) followed by light exposure: (1) 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2, 1 min), (2) 690 nm laser (0.2 W/cm2, 5 min), or (3) dual-wavelength irradiation. 24 h following therapeutic intervention, relative cell viability was determined through CCK-8 assay kits.

Intracellular GSH consumption

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plate (about 2 × 105) overnight. Following 24 h treatment with GG NPs, ZnPc 2, or GGZ NPs, selected groups (ZnPc 2 and GGZ NPs) were either irradiated with 690 nm laser light (0.2 W/cm2, 5 min) or maintained without light exposure. Cells were subsequently trypsinized, and intracellular GSH and GSSG levels were quantified using a commercial assay kit following the manufacturer's standardized protocol.

Intracellular ROS generation

HepG2 cells (approximately 1 × 104 cells) were plated in confocal overnight. Following PBS washing, cellular ROS generation was assessed using CM-H2DCFDA probe (2.5 μL, 20 min incubation), followed by PBS and medium washes. ZnPc 2 and GGZ NP-treated groups received 690 nm photoirradiation (0.2 W/cm2, 5 min). Finally, cells were imaged using fluorescence microscope.

Western-blot

HepG2 and L02 cells were plated in culture dishes overnight. For first experiment, culture medium was replaced with PBS, GA, GA NPs, or GG NPs, followed by 24 h incubation under standard culture conditions. For second experiment, HepG2 cells were pre-cultured similarly, then treated with PBS, ZnPc 2, GG NPs, or GGZ NPs, and incubated for 18 h under dark conditions. The dishes treated with ZnPc 2 and GGZ NPs were exposed to 808 nm laser, followed by 6 h of additional culture. After incubation, cells from both experiments were washed with PBS, collected by centrifugation, and subjected to Western blot protein assay.

In vitro apoptosis detection

HepG2 and L02 cells (1 × 104/well) were seeded in 96-well plates overnight. For the synergistic effects evaluation, cells wereincubated with PBS, GA, GA NPs, or GG NPs for 24 h. For phototherapy evaluation, HepG2 cells were treated with PBS, GA, ZnPc 2, or GGZ NPs for 18 h (dark), followed by 808/690 nm laser irradiation where indicated. After PBS washing, cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD reagent (20 min) and imaged using fluorescence microscope for viability assessment.

Animal model

Male BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. All experiments complied with the institutional animal care guidelines approved by Shenzhen Institute for Drug Control's Animal Ethics Committee (SZIDC-YL-20231228). HepG2-luc cells (1 × 106) were surgically implanted into liver lobes, with tumor engraftment and progression monitored via bioluminescence imaging. Treatment studies commenced when tumors reached predetermined sizes.

In vivo imaging and biodistribution

Orthotopic HCC nude mice were randomly allocated into two treatment groups receiving intravenous administration of either GGZ NPs or ZnPc 2 (1 mg/kg ZnPc 2 equivalent). At set intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h), mice were imaged using a small animal optical imaging system. Following terminal imaging at 24 h, animals were humanely sacrificed and major organs were harvested for ex vivo analysis.

In vivo PA imaging

HCC model mice were randomized to two groups and injected intravenously with GGZ NPs or ZnPc 2 (1 mg/kg ZnPc 2 equivalent). PA imaging was conducted at 1–8 h intervals using a small animal ultrasound/PA imaging system.

In vivo photothermal imaging

Post-injection of test agents (GGZ NPs, ZnPc 2, or saline), HCC tumors were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation while monitoring temperature changes via infrared thermography.

In vivo antitumor activity

Orthotopic HCC nude mice were randomized into five groups: control, GA NPs, ZnPc 2 + L, GG NPs, and GGZ NPs + L. Body weight was recorded daily, and drugs were administered intravenously every three days. Scheduled photothermal and photodynamic therapy was performed. Tumor progression was monitored via bioluminescence imaging. On day 12, mice were euthanized, and blood/organs were collected for serum biochemistry and H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

All the data were expressed by the mean ± SD of the independent experiment, and the experiment was carried out in three times. The difference was significant by T-test. Origin software was used to analyze the p-value of the statistical differences among the groups. The mean value of p < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Internally generated “synergism and attenuation” of GG NPs

GA and GL were selected to self-assemble through hydrophobic interaction to achieve a synergistic and attenuated strategy. To optimize the ratio of GA to GL, GA was mixed with different proportions of GL to explore the effect on the toxicity to normal liver cells was relatively low. The 1:0.5 GA/GL molar ratio showed minimal hepatotoxicity relative to other combinations (Fig. S1), indicating preserved safety in normal liver cells. The nanoparticles were synthesized using the volatile organic solvent ultrasonic method. UV–Vis spectra revealed that when the ultrasonic time was 0.5 h, GA NPs appeared to blue shift and the peak intensity was the highest, so the optimal synthesis time of GA NPs was 0.5 h (Fig. S2). The particle size showed that the synthesis ratio did not significantly affect GG NPs (Fig. S3). Therefore, a synthesis ratio of 1:0.5 was adopted for GG NPs, which are comparatively less toxic to normal cells. The particle size of GG NPs increased with prolonged synthesis time, so the optimal synthesis time was 1h (Fig. S4). Characterization data revealed that both GA and GG NPs exhibited uniform spherical morphology, as demonstrated by TEM (Fig. 1A–B). DLS measurements showed average hydrodynamic diameters of 147.6 ± 1.5 nm for GA NPs and 197.8 ± 2.5 nm for GG NPs. Surface charge analysis indicated zeta potentials of −27.0 ± 1.18 mV and −37.46 ± 4.34 mV for GA NPs and GG NPs, respectively (Fig. 1C). UV–Vis spectra of GG NP showed characteristic peaks of GA and GL and extensive absorption between 400 and 800 nm, indicating successful synthesis of GG NPs (Fig. S5).

The cytotoxic effects of GA, GA-NPs, and GG-NPs on HepG2 cells L02 cells were evaluated through CCK-8 assays to investigate the potential synergistic therapeutic effects and reduced toxicity of GG NP formulations. The cytotoxic effects of GA, GA NPs, and GG NPs on HepG2 cells following incubation are presented in Fig. 1D, S6, and S7. At the same concentration of GA, GA NPs induced a higher level of cell death than GA-treated HepG2 cells, suggesting that GA could enhance the anti-tumor effect to some extent after self-assembly (Fig. S8). The 1.26-fold lower half-inhibitory concentration (IC50) of GG NPs compared to GA NPs (Table S1) points to the internally generated "synergism and attenuation". GG NPs exhibited a decreased cell survival rate relative to GA and GA NPs, further confirming their enhanced anti-tumor efficacy. GA, GA NPs, and GG NPs were incubated with L02 cells. At equivalent concentrations, HepG2 and L02 cells treated with GG NPs exhibited significantly greater viability compared to those exposed to free GA or its nanoassemblies, demonstrating that the GG NPs effectively mitigates the cytotoxic effects associated with unmodified GA. The levels of AST and ALT in L02 cells were determined using assay kits after treatment with GA, GA NPs, or GG NPs. Fig. 1E and F shows that both GA and GA NPs increased AST and ALT levels, while GG NPs reduced them. These results further demonstrated the synergistic attenuation and hepatoprotective effects of GG NPs at the biochemical level.

To further characterize the synergistic and attenuated effects of GG NPs, live and dead cells of HepG2 and L02 were visualized using LIVE/DEAD staining. As depicted in Fig. 1G, the red fluorescence intensity enhanced under GA, GA NPs, and GG NPs treatment, consistent with the results of the above experiment. As shown in Fig. 1H, GA and GA NPs exhibited stronger red fluorescence compared to GG NPs, indicating that the addition of GL reduced the toxicity of GA to normal hepatocytes. Semi-quantitative data for fluorescence imaging were shown in Figs. S9–10. GG NPs showed the lowest death value for HepG2 cells and the highest viability value for L02 cells. Meanwhile, the expression of PARP1 and C-PARP1 in HepG2 and L02 cells under various treatments was examined using Western blot analysis (Fig. 1I and J), with β-actin as a loading control. Semi-quantitative data for apoptotic proteins were shown in Fig. S11. C-PARP1 expression was higher in GG NPs treated HepG2 cells but lower in GG NPs treated L02 cells, indicating synergistic and attenuated strategy for GG NPs. Based on the CCK-8 assays, biochemical indicators, LIVE/DEAD staining results, and western blot analyses, it has been confirmed that GG NPs can mitigate the toxicity of GA to a certain extent while exhibiting higher potent inhibitory effect on tumor cell activity, thereby achieving synergistic and attenuated effects.

Preparation and characterization of GGZ NPs

The synthesis and characterization of ZnPc 2 are provided in the Supporting Information, including complete 1H NMR and mass spectrometry data (Figs. S12–S17). Geometric optimizations for ZnPc 2 were initially performed using Gaussian 16 software with the BHandHLYP/6-31G (d,p) basis set [50]. As depicted in Fig. 2B–D, the electron density is delocalized over the entire conjugated π-system, indicating successful geometric relaxation and significant π-π overlap across the model compound's backbone. The HOMO energy level of the model compound was calculated to be −1.0068 eV, while the LUMO energy level was determined to be 1.3061 eV. Consequently, the calculated HOMO-LUMO gap of the model compound was 2.3130 eV, indicating that the absorption spectrum of ZnPc 2 is prone to redshift [51]. UV–Visible spectroscopic analysis of GGZ NPs revealed absorption bands corresponding to both GA and GL constituents, in addition to a newly formed spectral feature at 800–850 nm (Fig. 2E), confirming successful nanoparticles formation. In Fig. 2F, DLS results revealed that the size of GGZ NPs was approximately 233.2 ± 3.4 nm. TEM images confirmed that GGZ NPs had a spherical structure. Zeta potential measurements of GGZ NPs indicated negative surface charges of −50.73 ± 10.3 mV (Fig. S18). Such a large negative zeta potential suggested that GGZ NPs could be well dissolved and dispersed in aqueous solution without significant aggregation. The GGZ NPs formulation demonstrated excellent aqueous stability, maintaining consistent hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index (PDI) over 7 days of storage (Fig. 2G). To investigate the drug release profile of GGZ NPs, the release rates of GA and GL under different conditions were examined. As shown in Figs. S19–S21, in PBS at pH 7, the release of GA and GL from GGZ NPs was less than 10 %, indicating minimal drug leakage under extracellular matrix conditions. In contrast, at pH 3, approximately 40 % of GA and GL was released from GGZ NPs, demonstrating a significantly faster release rate compared to that in pH 7 buffer. This suggests that the acidic environment promotes drug release from the nanoparticles. The above results suggest that when GGZ NPs reach the tumor target, due to the acidic environment in the tumor, the nanoparticles will hydrolyze and trigger the release of the natural products GA and GL.

Photophysical properties of GGZ NPs

To assess photothermal performance, solution temperatures were monitored in real time during laser irradiation (Fig. 3A). As expected, water and GG NPs did not exhibit any significant temperature change, whereas ZnPc 2 increased the temperature by nearly 20 °C within 10 min. Interestingly, GGZ NPs achieved a higher temperature rise (30 °C increase for 10 min) than ZnPc 2, indicating an enhanced photothermal effect. These results demonstrate that the nanoprecipitation method can significantly enhance the photothermal performance. In addition, solution temperature increased rapidly upon laser irradiation, with the highest temperature rise depending on the GGZ NPs concentration and laser power density (Fig. 3B). Fig. 3C shows the thermal imaging of GGZ NPs at varying concentrations under 808 nm laser irradiation. Notably, GGZ NPs maintained comparable photothermal efficiency to ZnPc 2 under acidic conditions in Fig. S22. GGZ NPs exhibited remarkable photothermal stability, maintaining consistent performance over five successive laser on-off cycles (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis yielded a photothermal conversion efficiency of 80.8 %, calculated according to established methodologies (Fig. S23–S24).

The photoacoustic (PA) property of GGZ NPs was investigated within the wavelength range of 680–900 nm. While water and GG NPs exhibited no PA signals, ZnPc 2 demonstrated peak absorption at 700–750 nm. In contrast, GGZ NPs displayed broad absorption across the 680–900 nm range, with a distinct peak near 800 nm, suggesting an enhanced PA signal from GGZ NPs (Fig. 3E). The PA signal intensity of GGZ NPs exhibited a concentration-dependent increase across the tested range (0–200 μg/mL) (Fig. 3F–S25-S26). These results indicate that GGZ NPs exhibit a concentration-dependent PA signal, which could be utilized to monitor the peak time of drug distribution at the tumor site through photoacoustic imaging.

To evaluate the photodynamic potential of GGZ NPs, 1O2 generation was quantitatively monitored via DPBF oxidation under light irradiation. As shown in Figs. S27 and S28, the spectral change of DPBF after laser irradiation was negligible in the presence of GG NPs. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 3G and H, the spectral change of DPBF after laser irradiation was measured to be 0.19 for GGZ NPs. When GGZ NPs were present in an acidic solution, a corresponding spectral change of 0.38 was observed for DPBF after laser irradiation. Compared to GGZ NPs alone, the ability of GGZ NPs to produce 1O2 in an acidic environment increased by 2-fold. This suggests that the acidic environment promoted the photodynamic capabilities of GGZ NPs. Fig. 2I visually confirms the enhanced DPBF degradation kinetics of GGZ NPs under acidic conditions, as evidenced by the more pronounced absorbance decay at 415 nm. Fig. S29 showed that only the aqueous solution of GGZ NPs with acid became significantly lighter after laser irradiation.

Antitumor efficacy of GGZ NPs in vitro

Due to the high ROS generation and photothermal conversion efficiency, GGZ NPs were evaluated for cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells using CCK-8 assays, demonstrating the potential as phototherapeutic nanoparticles (Fig. 4A). Compared to the IC50 value of ZnPc 2 for HepG2 cells, the IC50 of ZnPc 2 significantly decreased from 63 μg/mL to 10 μg/mL when it was assembled with natural products to form GGZ NPs (Fig. S30). The pronounced reduction in required dosage demonstrates that the ZnPc 2-natural product assembly strategy significantly enhanced photosensitizer efficiency, enabling equivalent therapeutic effects at substantially lower drug concentrations, suggesting the externally assisted "synergism and attenuation" (Table S2). Additionally, GGZ NPs exhibited a significant therapeutic effect on HepG2 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Dual-wavelength irradiation (808 nm and 690 nm) of GGZ NPs (10 μg/mL) induced significantly greater cytotoxicity (46.1 %) compared to single-wavelength treatments (808 nm: 56.9 %; 690 nm: 57.8 %) or dark conditions (76.7 %), confirming synergistic photothermal-photodynamic action. This combinatorial effect establishes GGZ NPs as an efficient therapeutic nanocarrier for hepatocellular carcinoma. In parallel, cellular safety evaluations revealed that GGZ NPs at the chosen concentration showed minimal cytotoxicity in normal hepatocytes (Fig. S31).

Cellular uptake and subcellular distribution

The phototherapeutic efficacy of GGZ NPs was contingent upon their cellular uptake dynamics. Fluorescence microscopy revealed progressive accumulation in HepG2 cells, reaching optimal intracellular distribution within 1 h (Fig. S32). Colocalization studies using organelle-specific trackers demonstrated predominant lysosomal sequestration, as evidenced by spectral overlap (Fig. S33), suggesting endolysosomal trafficking as the primary internalization route.

GSH depletion, ROS generation, and Trx inhibition in vitro

To explore how GGZ NPs could enhance the PDT effect by modulating the intracellular redox state, we first examined the changes in intracellular GSH/GSSG levels. GSH is an important antioxidant in cells, effectively removing ROS and maintaining redox balance. The experimental results showed that GG NPs significantly reduced intracellular GSH levels compared with the control and ZnPc 2 groups (Fig. 4B). This result was consistent with literature reports, indicating that GG NPs can effectively consumption GSH, thereby weakening the antioxidant capacity of tumor cells [52]. As a key component of GG NPs, GA may reduce GSH levels by reducing oxidative stress (such as glutathione and glutathione peroxidase) factors. GSH depletion further disrupts the intracellular redox balance, creating conditions for ROS accumulation.

To verify the promoting effect of GGZ NPs on ROS generation during PDT, we examined intracellular ROS accumulation using CM-H2DCFDA as a fluorescent probe for ROS. The results showed that ZnPc 2 produced almost no fluorescence without laser irradiation, but only trace fluorescence under laser irradiation, indicating its limited ability to generate ROS. In contrast, GGZ NPs produced significant fluorescence with or without laser irradiation, and the fluorescence intensity was significantly enhanced under laser irradiation (Fig. 4C). This indicates that GG NPs can significantly enhance ZnPc 2-mediated ROS generation during PDT. In addition, GG NPs alone also induced moderate ROS accumulation in HepG2 cells, a phenomenon consistent with literature reports [30]. The possible mechanism is that GA disrupts the electron transport chain, leading to an increase in superoxide production, and it has a synergistic effect with GG NPs in inducing mitochondrial damage. This further disrupts the intracellular redox balance and aggravates oxidative stress, thereby causing the accumulation of ROS [32,53].

To further elucidate the inhibitory effect of GGZ NPs on the intracellular antioxidant system, we examined the expression levels of key antioxidant regulators TXN and TXNRD1 in HepG2 cells by Western blot. TXN and TXNRD1 are important components of the antioxidant system in cells, which can maintain intracellular redox balance by reducing oxidized proteins. Mechanism studies have shown that GA exerts a dual inhibitory effect on the Trx system through the following two mechanisms: It directly covalently modifies the cysteine residues (Cys32/Cys35) with redox activity in the catalytic center of thioredoxin, thereby weakening their electron transfer ability [31]. It inhibits the Nrf2 signaling pathway, resulting in the downregulation of the transcriptional expression of TXN and TXNRD1 [54]. Protein semi-quantitative data for TXN and TXRD1 were shown in Fig. S34. The experimental results showed that both GG NPs and GGZ NPs significantly reduced the expression levels of TXN and TXNRD1 compared with the control and ZnPc 2 groups (Fig. 4D). This result indicates that GGZ NPs not only reduce GSH levels but also inhibit the activity of the Trx system, thereby further weakening the antioxidant capacity of tumor cells. GGZ NPs synergistically amplify the anti-tumor effect of PDT through a dual mechanism (GSH reduction and Trx inhibition).

Intracellular HSP90 inhibition

To elucidate GA's thermotolerance-modulating mechanism, we performed Western blot analysis of HSP90 expression in HepG2 cells across treatment conditions. HSP90 is an important molecular chaperone protein that is commonly overexpressed in tumor cells and can help cells cope with heat stress and other environmental stresses, thereby maintaining tumor cell survival and proliferation. Studies have shown that GA can specifically act on the N-terminal ATP binding domain of HSP90 and competitively inhibit the activity of its partner proteins by directly occupying the nucleotide-binding groove [55]. Protein semi-quantitative data for HSP90 were shown in Fig. S34. The experimental results showed that ZnPc 2 + L significantly increased HSP90 expression compared with the control (Fig. 4E), suggesting that tumor cells resist heat stress and oxidative damage by upregulating HSP90 expression during PTT. However, GGZ NPs + L significantly reduced HSP90 expression levels, and the lowest HSP90 expression was observed when GG NPs were treated alone. The experimental data demonstrate that GA integration significantly suppressed HSP90 levels, confirming its therapeutic potential as a molecular inhibitor. GGZ NPs impaired the ability of tumor cells to cope with heat stress by reducing the expression of HSP90, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effect of PTT and providing a new strategy for tumor treatment.

In vitro apoptosis induced by GGZ NPs

Due to the multiple mechanisms of cytotoxicity, including GSH depletion, Trx inhibition, and HSP90 down-regulation, we further verified the externally assisted "synergism and attenuation" of the anti-tumor effects of GGZ NPs by drug efficacy experiments. To evaluate nanoparticle induced mitochondrial dysfunction, we monitored changes in membrane potential using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. A shift from red (polarized, healthy mitochondria) to green (depolarized, apoptotic) fluorescence signifies mitochondrial depolarization. As depicted in Fig. S35, HepG2 cells treated with ZnPc 2, ZnPc 2 + L, and GG NPs exhibited overlapping red-green fluorescence (appearing orange), accompanied by a marked reduction in red fluorescence intensity compared to untreated controls, indicating pronounced mitochondrial damage. This suggests that these treatment conditions caused some interference with mitochondrial function but did not completely disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential. In contrast, GGZ NPs + L significantly enhanced green fluorescence, indicating significant disruption of mitochondrial function, ultimately leading to cell death. In addition, to visually observe the apoptosis, the control and ZnPc 2 (with or without laser irradiation) showed only green fluorescence, indicating that these treatment conditions had a limited effect on tumor cells in Fig. 4F. GG NPs showed partial green fluorescence and partial red fluorescence, indicating internally generated “synergism and attenuation” of GG NPs. However, the cells incubated with GGZ NPs showed different degrees of cytotoxicity, and the red fluorescence signal was significantly enhanced, especially in the case of GGZ NPs + L, where the red fluorescence reached its strongest intensity. This indicated that GGZ NPs were able to significantly induce cell death under light conditions, further highlighting the key role of phototherapy in enhancing the cytotoxicity of GGZ NPs.

In vivo multimodal imaging of GGZ NPs

Building on promising in vitro results against HepG2 cells, we evaluated GGZ NPs in vivo performance using orthotopic HCC models. Fluorescence imaging revealed rapid tumor accumulation (detectable at 0.5 h post-injection), peaking at 2 h (Fig. 5A). Comparative analysis demonstrated significantly enhanced tumor targeting of GGZ NPs versus free ZnPc 2. Ex vivo organ imaging confirmed superior hepatic accumulation of the nanoformulation. In summary, these data suggested that GGZ NPs have good tumor-targeting ability, which will be beneficial for tumor therapy in vivo. GA NPs and GG NPs labeled with Cy5 were injected intravenously into the orthotopic HCC-bearing nude mice to further determine the hepatic targeting ability of GL. As shown in Fig. S36, GG@Cy5 NPs aggregated at the tumor site of liver tissues and peaked at 2h. Compared with GG@Cy5 NPs, GA@Cy5 NPs showed a systemic distribution in nude mice without obvious aggregation. The isolated fluorescence also showed that GG@Cy5 NPs were more distributed in liver tumor sites than GA@Cy5 NPs.

GGZ NPs exhibited a concentration-dependent PA signal. Therefore, the concentration distribution of GGZ NPs and ZnPc 2 in liver tissues was observed by intravenous injection of nanoparticles into the orthotopic HCC-bearing nude mice. PA imaging was based on the principle that it provides information about light absorption within the tissue, thereby reflecting the distribution and concentration of the drug. In the images, blue represents the hypoxic signal indicating the tumor site, while red represents the drug distribution. As shown in Fig. 5B, the red PA signal representing GGZ NPs permeated into the deepest tissue at 2 h and gradually weakened over time. This demonstrated that GGZ NPs accumulated in liver tissue and were gradually metabolized over time. ZnPc 2 also reached the peak of drug concentration at 2 h, but the penetration of nanoparticles was shallower, primarily limited to the epidermis region.

Collective analysis of FL and PA imaging data revealed peak tumor accumulation of nanoparticles at 2 h post-administration. This optimal timepoint was consequently selected for subsequent infrared thermographic evaluation of GGZ NPs. GGZ NPs treated tumors reached 43 °C within 5 min of irradiation, significantly higher than control groups (PBS: 37 °C, ZnPc 2: 39 °C) (Fig. 5C and S37). These results suggested that GGZ NPs had concentrated photothermally active in the vicinity of liver tumors, which established an optimal window for subsequent therapy. Overall, these data suggested that GGZ NPs have good tumor-targeting ability. This enabled GGZ NPs to target and deliver natural products to the tumor site, exerting their therapeutic effects and thereby reducing the toxic effects caused by off-targeting.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of GGZ NPs

To assess the therapeutic potential of GGZ NPs, we established an orthotopic liver cancer model using HepG2-Luc cells for longitudinal monitoring. In this study, the successfully established orthotopic HCC nude mouse models were randomly assigned to five groups: control, ZnPc 2 + L, GA NPs, GG NPs, and GGZ NPs + L. Fig. 6A illustrates the comprehensive timeline, detailing the progression from modeling the nude mice through the treatment phase to the dissection stage. By the 12-day timepoint, both ZnPc 2 + L and GA NPs exhibited suppression of tumor relative to untreated controls (Fig. 6B–C), demonstrating their therapeutic efficacy. However, the inhibition was relatively modest, potentially constrained by the inherent lack of targeting efficiency in both ZnPc 2 and GA NPs, as well as the challenge of effectively eliminating tumors solely with GA NPs. In contrast, GG NPs and GGZ NPs + L demonstrated notable advantages in inhibiting tumor growth, which was mainly attributed to the internally generated "synergism and attenuation" of GG NPs, the enhanced anti-tumor effect through natural product self-assembly and the liver-targeting ability of GL. The tumor inhibition effect observed with GGZ NPs + L was even more remarkable, further verifying the application of natural product carrier-free nanoparticles in phototherapy through the precise targeted delivery to liver tumors and dual adjuvant phototherapy to achieve externally assisted “synergistic and attenuated” effects.

Fig. 6D showed that the body weights of HCC-bearing nude mice during the treatment had no apparent differences among the groups. Finally, as observed through the isolated organs in Fig. S38, the liver tumors in GGZ NPs + L were almost unobservable to the naked eye. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of the tumor tissues, depicted in Fig. 6E, revealed that both ZnPc 2 + L and GA NPs exhibited a certain degree of tumor treatment efficacy, as evidenced by the presence of local necrotic zones and the relative shrinkage of the tumor area. The GG NPs not only showed an enlargement of the calcified area in the tumor site of the hepatocellular carcinoma mice but were also accompanied by extensive necrosis of the tumor cells, indicating that the tumors at that site were treated more effectively. Most notably, GGZ NPs + L showed a large number of necrotic areas as well as absorbed calcified foci after treatment, which demonstrates that this group had the best treatment effect. The outcomes of H&E staining of tumor tissues were consistent with the treatment results.

Biosafety and systemic toxicity evaluation

Systemic toxicity remains a major limitation for clinical translation of drug therapies [56]. To assess potential toxicity, we performed biosafety evaluations including H&E staining, blood routine analysis, and blood biochemical analysis to monitor tissue, hematologic, and metabolic effects across treatment groups. No significant abnormalities were observed in the blood routine analysis of any orthotopic HCC-bearing nude mouse group during the treatment (Fig. S39). Meanwhile, Histopathological analysis revealed GA NPs caused hepatocellular hypertrophy, characterized by increased hepatocyte volume and a decreased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, whereas no abnormalities or lesions were detected in the major organs of the other treatment groups (Fig. 7A). Biochemical analysis revealed GA NP-induced hepatotoxicity, as evidenced by elevated AST/ALT levels (Fig. 7B–G). In contrast, all other treatment groups maintained normal hepatic and renal function markers. In summary, these results underscore the capacity of GG NPs to mitigate GA-induced toxicity by harnessing the synergistic interaction between GL and GA. Similarly, GGZ NPs demonstrated a comparable and substantial reduction in toxicity, indicating that the self-assembled natural product nanoparticles exhibit satisfactory biological safety.

Conclusions

This study innovatively introduces carrier-free nanoparticles, GG NPs, which enhance the anti-tumor effect through the self-assembly of natural products and leverage the inherent tumor-targeting ability of natural products to achieve internally generated "synergism and attenuation". Additionally, to apply natural product carrier-free nanoparticles in phototherapy, we prepared GGZ NPs. GGZ NPs significantly improved accumulation at tumor sites by exploiting the active targeting ability of natural products. Furthermore, GGZ NPs possess dual sensitizing capabilities: first, they depleted GSH and Trx, and second, they inhibited HSP90. These combined effects contribute to elevated intracellular ROS levels, enhanced PDT efficacy, and effectively counteract PTT thermoresistance. As a result, GGZ NPs demonstrated enhanced therapeutic effects both in vitro and in vivo, which achieved externally assisted "synergism and attenuation". Moreover, the biological safety assessment of GGZ NPs in the body is satisfactory. Compared with the treatment using single chemotherapy drugs, it has fewer side effects and overcomes the inherent defects of natural products, such as low bioavailability and systemic toxicity. In conclusion, the synergistic and attenuation strategy adopted by carrier-free nanoparticles based on natural products is expected to pave a new way for synergistic anti-tumor therapy with chemotherapy and phototherapy.
