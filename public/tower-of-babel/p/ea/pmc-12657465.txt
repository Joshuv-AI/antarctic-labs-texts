Comparative analysis of scaffold-free tissue-engineered cartilage derived from different seed cells: in vitro and in vivo studies
Authors: Mengxin Xue, Kaiwen Zheng, Jiewei Chen, Che Zheng, Xiaofeng Liu, Changqing Zhang, Dajiang Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe development of cartilage tissue engineering offers a promising strategy for effective cartilage regeneration. This study aims to synthesize scaffold-free tissue-engineered cartilage (TEC) using articular chondrocytes (ACs), costal chondrocytes (CCs), bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), and synovial-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (SDSCs) as seed cells, comparing their cartilage matrix production in vitro and repair capabilities in vivo to guide seed cell selection.MethodsACs, CCs, BMSCs, and SDSCs were seeded at high density (4.0 × 105 cells/cm2) in 24-well plates with 0.2 mM Ascorbate 2-phosphate and cultured to form scaffold-free TEC. In vitro, we performed real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), biological analyses, and histochemical staining to assess structural characteristics and cartilage-related gene expression. In vivo, a rat model of knee cartilage defect was used to evaluate tissue repair capabilities, followed by histological assessments and statistical analysis.Results In vitro, ACs and CCs showed significantly higher GAG/DNA ratios, with CCs exhibiting the highest chondrogenic gene expression. Histological analysis revealed AC and CC TECs were positive for Safranin-O, Alcian blue, and Col2 staining. In vivo, CC-derived TEC maintained a uniform distribution of Col2, while BMSCs and SDSCs primarily showed Col1 distribution, demonstrating better integration with cartilage and in situ differentiation by 12 weeks.ConclusionACs and CCs excelled in cartilage matrix synthesis; however, AC TEC exhibited phenotypic instability. CC TEC sustained a hyaline chondrocyte phenotype but had later connectivity issues, while BMSCs and SDSCs integrated better but primarily produced fibrous tissue.

Articular cartilage injuries remain a major clinical challenge due to the tissue’s limited ability to heal itself. This study demonstrates that scaffold-free cartilage engineered from costal chondrocytes can effectively regenerate hyaline-like cartilage without relying on external scaffolds or growth factors. By comparing different cell sources, we highlight the unique advantages of costal chondrocyte-derived constructs in maintaining stable cartilage characteristics and promoting natural integration. These findings advance the understanding of optimal seed cell selection for cartilage tissue engineering and present a clinically translatable, simplified approach for restoring joint function in patients with cartilage defects.

Introduction

Because of its avascular and low cellularity nature, cartilage has a limited self-repair and regeneration capacity, making the treatment of articular cartilage defects still a significant clinical challenge.1,2 Current clinical options, such as microfracture, autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI), and osteochondral transplantation, have demonstrated varying degrees of success; however, they all present certain limitations, including limited donor sources and inadequacy for large and irregular defects.3 These challenges have fostered the emergence of tissue engineering, a field that aims to enhance cartilage repair and regeneration through the development of innovative strategies, ultimately offering fresh hope for patients suffering from cartilage defects.4

Despite traditional tissue engineering methods that combine cells, signals and scaffolds have made significant progress in solving clinical problems, the introduction of scaffolds inevitably raises concerns regarding decreased cell viability, heterogeneous cell distribution and insufficient integration with native tissue.5 In light of these challenges, scaffold-free approaches have emerged as promising alternatives, eliminating many of the complications associated with scaffolding materials while still facilitating the development of functional cartilage tissue. These methods prioritize the use of cell-based systems that foster natural tissue organization and integration, potentially overcoming some of the limitations inherent in scaffold-based strategies.6,7 The scaffold-free tissue-engineered cartilage (TEC) approach that relied on high cell density and the presence of ascorbic acid has been identified multiple advantages in previous experiments. First, TEC can transition from a monolayer structure to a 3-dimensional structure through self-contraction and can be synthesized in one step, thus, avoiding the nonuniformity at the boundaries and internal uneven distribution caused by secondary integration of micro-pellets.8,9 In addition, this method does not require special equipment or growth factors, particularly ­simplifying the process and reducing costs compared to ACI technology.10 Moreover, previous studies have found that TEC exhibits adhesive properties to host tissues without any fixation, which effectively promote the translational potential of TEC in clinical applications.11

A critical factor in cartilage tissue engineering is the choice of seed cells. Seed cells, as the biological prerequisite for cartilage regeneration, determine the quality of the regenerated cartilage, given that the long-term success of regeneration relies on the cells’ ability to deposit and remodel new matrix.12 Currently, in articular cartilage regeneration strategies, seed cell sources are primarily categorized into two groups: hyaline chondrocytes and mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs). Articular cartilage is the most classic and widely used type of hyaline cartilage. ACI strategies based on articular chondrocytes have been clinically applied and has undergone several generations of development.13,14 Due to the limited availability of ACs and their insufficient in vitro redifferentiation ability, recent research has shifted its focus to extra-articular hyaline cartilage. Costal chondrocytes (CCs) have emerged as a promising alternative source because of their abundant availability, high initial cell yield, strong proliferation capacity, and robust redifferentiation ability.15-17 Clinical trials related to costal cartilage or chondrocytes transplantation for the repair of articular cartilage have been gradually conducted and have reported satisfactory therapeutic outcomes. These studies indicate that costal cartilage can effectively replace articular cartilage for the repair of damaged joints. In many cases, patients have experienced significant improvements in joint function, relief from pain symptoms, and an overall enhancement in quality of life.18,19 In addition to hyaline chondrocytes, MSCs also play an important role in cartilage regeneration. Due to their diverse sources, extensive proliferative capacity, and multipotent differentiation potential, MSCs offer significant advantages in the field of cartilage regeneration.20 MSCs include bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and synovial-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (SDSCs). BMSCs are widely used in cartilage regeneration due to their potential to regenerate mature and stable cartilage. Additionally, the inflammatory immunomodulatory capabilities of BMSCs contribute to the effective repair of cartilage defects caused by degenerative diseases.21 In recent years, SDSCs have also emerged as promising seed cells for cartilage regeneration, significantly promoting articular cartilage through cell injection.22

Cell types suitable for articular cartilage regeneration should be capable of producing abundant extracellular matrix of cartilage and effectively integrating with the surrounding natural cartilage tissue.23 It is particularly necessary to compare different cell sources derived scaffold-free TEC to provide a basis for seed cell selection. Therefore, in this study, ACs, CCs, BMSCs, and SDSCs were seeded separately to form scaffold-free TEC (Graphic abstract). In vitro, GAG/DNA was measured to compare the secretion of glycosaminoglycans among TECs. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) were performed to compare gene expression related to cartilage matrix secretion. TEC samples were sectioned and histologically stained to evaluate the synthesis of the cartilage matrix and different types of collagen in the extracellular matrix. In vivo, TECs from different groups were implanted into a rat knee joint cartilage defect model, and the distal femur samples were collected at weeks 6 and 12 for histological staining and scoring to evaluate the degree of cartilage regeneration. By comparing their abilities to produce cartilage matrix in vitro and repair cartilage defects in vivo, this study aims to elucidate the characteristics of different cell types and provide a basis for the selection of appropriate seed cells.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of ACs, CCs, BMSCs, and SDSCs

ACs, CCs, BMSCs and SDSCs were obtained from 10- to 12-week old SD rats that weighed roughly 250 g. Briefly, after intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium, the knee joint cartilage, the cartilaginous portion of the 6th to 10th ribs and the knee joint synovial tissue were harvested and minced into 1 mm3 pieces and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 3 times. Then, the articular cartilage fragments and the costal cartilage fragments were initially digested with 1.5 mg/mL type II collagenase in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) for 0.5 h and 2 h, respectively, followed by digestion in 0.75 mg/mL type II collagenase in DMEM overnight. The knee joint synovial tissue was digested in 1.0 mg/mL type II collagenase in DMEM overnight. After digestion, the freshly isolated cells were filtered through 70 µm cell strainers, centrifuged, resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS) and seeded onto a culture dish. The culture dishs were kept sterile at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 3 days. For BMSCs, after separating femurs and tibiae under aseptic conditions, bone marrow was flushed with PBS using a sterile syringe. Bone marrow samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 1000× g and after centrifugation, the fragments were resuspended in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS. After 48 h, the medium was changed for the first time, and then replaced every 3 days afterward. Cells reaching 80%-90% confluence were detached using 0.25% trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution and then re-seeded onto new culture dishes at a standardized density. Passage 3 cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Characterization of BMSCs and SDSCs

For surface marker identification, BMSCs and SDSCs of passage 3 were selected for flow cytometry analysis. The cells were resuspended in PBS, and then a 100 μL sample was treated with rabbit anti-rat polyclonal antibodies for incubation. Surface markers CD29, CD44, CD45, and CD90 were analyzed. Data were obtained from over 10 000 events per analysis.

For determining the multi-lineage differentiation potential including osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation, BMSCs and SDSCs of passage 3 were placed in 6-well plates at the density of 5.0 × 104 cells/well. Osteogenic differentiation medium, adipogenic differentiation medium and chondrogenic differentiation medium were added for trilineage differentiation, respectively.24 The specific reagents and concentrations of the trilineage differentiation media preparation was described in the supplementary file (Tables S1-S3). The osteogenic differentiation medium and the chondrogenic differentiation medium were changed every 3 days. The adipogenic differentiation medium was replaced alternately with liquid A every 3 days and liquid B every 1 days. At day 14-21, after the tissues fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, the osteogenic differentiation, the adipogenic differentiation and the chondrogenic differentiation were confirmed by using 2% Alizarin Red S staining, 0.5% Oil Red O staining and 0.1% Safranin-O staining, respectively.

Development and characterization of TEC

The development of TEC follows previously established protocols.25,26 ACs, CCs, BMSCs, and SDSCs were first digested, counted, and seeded at a density of 4.0 × 105 cells/cm2 on a 24-well plate in growth medium containing 0.2 mM Ascorbate 2-phosphate and cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The medium was changed every 2 days. Within a day, the cells began to fuse. After an additional 14 days of culture, the cultured cells and cell-synthesized ECM complexes could be separated from the plate by applying shear stress and gentle pipetting. After resuspending in growth medium for another day, the separated complexes formed 3-dimensional structures through active tissue contraction. Cell morphology of TEC at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after seeding were captured by phase contrast microscope. Cell viability was assessed at 7 and 14 days using a Calcein/PI Live/Dead Viability Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; Lot No. C2015S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following medium removal, TEC samples were washed with sterile PBS and incubated with the staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min in darkness. Fluorescence microscopy images were acquired using excitation/emission wavelengths of 494/517 nm for Calcein AM (green fluorescence) and 535/617 nm for PI (red fluorescence). The gross morphology of the detached complexes and the TECs were captured and accurately measured using Image J software. The contraction ratio was calculated to compare the contraction capabilities of four different seed cells when they formed 3-dimensional structures. Contraction ratio = Detached size (mm)/After contraction size (mm).

Nanoindentation tests

To evaluate the mechanical properties of TECs, we employed a Piuma nanoindenter (Optics11, Netherlands) with a calibrated borosilicate probe (spring constant: 2.8 N/m, tip radius: 25 μm), following the manufacturer’s protocol. TEC samples were secured on a magnetic stage and tested under ambient conditions (23 °C, 45% RH). To prevent sample dehydration, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to the testing area to maintain hydration throughout the entire duration of the tests. Indentations were performed in a 3 × 3 grid pattern with 20 μm spacing, applying a maximum load of Vmax = 100 μN, a maximum indentation time of t = 2.5 s, and a holding time of 2 s. The Effective Young’s Modulus (Eeff) were derived from the load-displacement curves using the Oliver–Pharr method. The average value of these measurements was considered as the representative result for each sample (n = 6).

Biochemical analysis

To analyze glycosaminoglycan (GAG) synthesis, we measured the total GAG and DNA content following a previously established method.27 In brief, TEC from each group was digested in a papain buffer (5 mM L-cysteine, 200 µg/ml papain, 0.1 M sodium acetate) at 65 °C for 18 h, followed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant after centrifugation was then collected for subsequent GAG and DNA quantification. GAG content was quantified using the dimethylmethylene blue (DMMB) assay following previous protocol28 and monitored at 525 nm using a Thermo Varioscan LUX spectrophotometer and normalized to the pre-prepared standard curve of chondroitin sulfate. DNA from TEC of each group was quantified using the Helixyte™ Green Fluorimetric dsDNA Quantitation Kit (Lot No.17645, AAT Bioquest Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and fluorescence intensity was measured using the Thermo Varioscan LUX at an excitation/emission wavelength of 480/520 nm and normalized to a dsDNA standard. The total GAG amount was normalized to the total DNA amount in the same sample.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

At day 14 post-seeding, the total RNA was extracted from the TEC in each group using the Tissue RNA Purification Kit PLUS (EZBioscience, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Then complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was carried out with the 4×EZscript Reverse Transcription Mix II (EZBioscience, USA). RT-qPCR were performed in a 10 μL volume, with cDNA being amplified using specific primers and SYBR Green Master Mix on the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The amplification was performed under certain conditions: an initial activation step at 95 °C for 5 min, then, 40 cycles with a denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 60 s. RT-qPCR was conducted under standard conditions, with each experiment being repeated 3 times. The relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2−(ΔΔCT) method, with glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the reference gene. The primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., China, and sequences are listed in the ­Supplementary File (Table S4).

Animal experiments

Male SD rats weighing between 250 and 300 g were used for this study after obtaining approval from Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s hospital (No.DWSY2022-0173). Before the experiment, the rats were given a week to get used to the lab environment. A total of 30 twelve-week-old male SD rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 6 knees per group at each time point) including the blank group, the ACs derived TEC implanted group, the CCs derived TEC implanted group, the BMSCs derived TEC implanted group and the SDSCs derived TEC implanted group. The knee joint was exposed by a longitudinal midline incision following anesthesia and sterilization, enabling a cautious dissection. In the tracheal groove, an osteochondral defect measuring 1.5 mm in diameter and 1 mm in depth was made using a microdrill. To keep tissue hydrated and avoid heat generation during drilling, saline irrigation was used. Rats in the blank group were not given any treatment after osteochondral defects were induced. Rats in four additional groups underwent implantation of the TECs, which were carefully placed onto the rats’ knee joint defects. Afterwards, the skin and joint capsule were closed. The rats were allowed to move freely in the cage after the operation. Rats were put to appropriate death with an overdose of intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at week 6 and 12 following surgery (3 rats per group at each time point). The distal portions of their femurs were collected for further analysis. The rat femur samples were imaged and evaluated by Leica S8 APO stereoscope. The International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) overall macroscopic score29 was utilized to macroscopically evaluate the defect sites (Table S5).

Histological and immunohistochemical evaluation

TEC samples from each group were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a series of graded sucrose solutions, embedded in OCT compound, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then, sectioned into 10-μm thick slices that were adhered to adhesive slides. Similarly, each set of femoral samples was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with 10% EDTA, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 6-μm thick slices, and mounted onto adhesive slides. The TEC sample slices were stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (H-E), Safranin-O/Fast green, and Alcian blue. The femoral slices were stained with Safranin-O/Fast green and further evaluated using the International Cartilage Repair Society Visual Histological Assessment Scale (ICRS-VHAS) and the O'Driscoll score (Tables S6 and S7).30,31 Immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess the histological production of type I and type II collagen. Briefly, after the steps of deparaffinization (only for femoral slices), rehydration, and Tris-EDTA antigen retrieval, the slices were blocked with a PBS solution containing 0.1% Triton-X 100 and 3% BSA and then incubated with rabbit anti-type I or type II collagen antibodies (1:100, Affinity Biosciences, China) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated with HRP (1:200, Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd) for 2 h at room temperature. Chromogen 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Beyotime, China) was used to visualize the immunocomplex’s area for 3 min. The images were captured by a Leica DM6 digital microscope (Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Three blinded observers evaluated all histological scores independently. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the significant differences among the groups. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad). P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of BMSCs and SDSCs

Flow cytometry analysis showed surface markers CD29, CD44, and CD90 were positive, while CD45 was negative (Figure S1A). Cultured BMSCs and SDSCs easily adhered to the plastic surface of culture plates and demonstrated typical spindle shape and a vortex distribution (Figure S1B). The multilineage differentiation potential of BMSCs and SDSCs was confirmed by positive staining of the extracellular calcium matrix using Alizarin Red staining in osteogenic media, the deposition of red-stained lipid droplets upon Oil Red staining in adipogenic media and the production of glycosaminoglycans in the extracellular matrix highlighted by Safranin-O staining (Figure S1C). These results met the minimal criteria of MSCs established in the ISCT position statement.32

Morphological characterization of TECs

Within 24 h after seeding at 4 × 105 cells/cm2, four different types of seed cells adhered to the surface and arranged tightly. ACs presented as irregular strips on day 1 and day 3, transitioning to a mixed distribution of irregular strips and polygons on day 7 and day 14. CCs exhibited a gradual morphological change from irregular polygons to elongated fusiform from day 1 to day 14. Both BMSCs and SDSCs maintained a spindle-shaped morphology until day 14 (Figure 1A). Within 14 days, the viability of the four different cells did not decrease significantly. As observed through live-dead cell staining, despite the high cell density, most cells remained high viability with only a few scattered dead cells (Figure 1D). Following detachment, the four different seed cells derived TECs appeared as nearly circular, translucent membrane-like structures (Figure 1B). The diameters after detached were similar among ACs, CCs, and BMSCs derived TECs, while SDSCs derived TEC had a significantly smaller diameter compared to the other three groups (all P < 0.001; Table 1 and Figure 2A). After resuspension, TECs underwent spontaneous contraction (Figure 1B). The contracted diameter of ACs derived TEC was significantly larger than that of CCs, BMSCs, and SDSCs derived TEC (all P < 0.001). The contraction ratio of SDSCs and BMSCs derived TECs were similar and significantly higher than that of ACs and CCs derived TECs (all P < 0.05). Notably, TECs derived from CCs exhibited a significantly higher contraction ratio than those from ACs (P < 0.001; Table 1 and Figure 2A). After removing surface moisture, TECs displayed a dense, opaque, smooth disk-like morphology with a certain thickness (Figure 1C). From the top view, the diameter of ACs derived TEC was significantly larger than that of CCs, BMSCs, and SDSCs derived TECs (all P < 0.001). The diameter of CCs derived TEC was significantly larger than that of BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, respectively). Furthermore, the diameter of BMSCs derived TEC was significantly larger than that of SDSCs derived TEC (P < 0.001; Table 1 and Figure 2A).

Analysis of the effective Young’s modulus of nanoindentation tests

To assess the mechanical properties of TEC from different cell sources, we conducted nanoindentation testing. The TEC from all four cell sources showed substantial mechanical strength, sufficient to endure subsequent in vivo transplantation. Among them, ACs derived TEC (2.37 ± 0.79 kPa) had a significantly lower Effective Young’s modulus than the other three groups (all P < 0.001), while SDSCs derived TEC (9.1 ± 1.61 kPa) exhibited the highest modulus (all P < 0.001). The mechanical properties of CCs (4.92 ± 1.46 kPa) and BMSCs derived TEC (4.72 ± 0.93 kPa) were intermediate between these two groups, with no statistically significant difference observed between them (Figure 2B).

Measurement of glycosaminoglycan content in the matrix

To compare the level of cartilage matrix synthesis in different groups, GAG contents were quantified (Figure 2C). The results indicated that the GAG content was significantly higher in ACs and CCs derived TECs compared to BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs (all P < 0.001), while there were no significant differences between ACs and CCs derived TECs or between BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs (P = 0.29 and 0.40, respectively). The DNA content was significantly higher in ACs, CCs, and BMSCs derived TECs compared to the SDSCs derived TEC (all P < 0.001), with no significant differences among the other groups. As for normalized GAG production, the GAG/DNA ratio in ACs derived TEC was significantly higher than that in other groups (all P < 0.05). The CCs derived TEC had a significantly higher GAG/DNA ratio compared to BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs (all P < 0.001), while there was no significant difference between BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs.

RT-qPCR results for related gene expression

RT-qPCR was performed to investigate the chondrogenesis-related genes (Sox9, Col2, Acan) and osteogenesis-related gene (Col1) expression (Figure 2D). CCs derived TEC demonstrated the highest expression level of chondrogenic genes. The expression levels of Acan and Sox9 in CCs derived TEC were significantly higher than that in ACs, BMSCs, and SDSCs derived TECs (P < 0.001 and P < 0.01, respectively). The expression level of Col2 in CCs derived TEC was significantly higher than that in BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs (P < 0.05, respectively), but there was no significant difference with ACs derived TEC (P = 0.09). There was no difference in the expression levels of chondrogenic genes among ACs, BMSCs, and SDSCs derived TECs. The Col1 expression levels in BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs were similar and significantly higher compared to ACs and CCs derived TECs (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively). There was no difference in Col1 expression levels between ACs and CCs derived TECs (P = 0.99).

Histological analysis of TECs

To compare the structure, GAG deposition and distribution, as well as the synthesis of cartilage matrix of TECs, we performed Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E), Safranin-O/Fast-green, and Alcian blue staining. All four groups of TECs exhibited high cell density (Figure 3A). ACs and CCs derived TECs demonstrated significantly more staining of cartilage matrix, as evidenced by positive Safranin-O staining and Alcian blue staining. BMSCs derived TEC showed partial positivity for Safranin-O staining and negativity for Alcian blue staining. Meanwhile, SDSCs derived TEC exhibited partial positivity for both Safranin-O staining and Alcian blue staining (Figure 3B and C). Immunohistochemical staining indicated that ACs and CCs derived TECs displayed a homogeneous and widespread positivity for collagen type II and a low positivity for collagen type I. BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs showed widespread positivity for collagen type I staining. Specifically, BMSCs derived TEC were negative for collagen type II staining, while SDSCs derived TEC showed partial positivity for collagen type II staining (Figure 3D and E).

Macroscopic evaluation of the defect repair

Until the end of the observation period, no animals developed infection or died. Samples of distal part of femurs were collected and then imaged for macroscopic analysis. The blank groups showed obvious defects at 6 and 12 weeks (Figure 4B and C). All TEC-implanted groups maintained good restoration in the defect sites which were filled with white, shiny, opaque tissue (Figure 4B and C). ICRS overall macroscopic scores were evaluated from aspects of macroscopic appearance, integration to the border zone and degree of defect repair. The ICRS scores indicated that all TEC-implanted groups had significantly higher scores than the blank group at both 6 weeks and 12 weeks (all P < 0.001). However, except for the CC derived TEC implanted group which showed similar results to the positive control at 12 weeks, all other scores remained significantly lower than those of the positive control groups (Figure 4D and E). At 6 weeks after surgery, the scores of BMSCs and CCs derived TEC implanted groups were significantly higher than those of ACs derived TEC implanted group (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, respectively) due to the irregular surface and the fissures between the surrounding native cartilage of the AC group. No differences were observed among the other TEC implanted groups (Figure 4D). At 12 weeks, CCs derived TEC implanted group scored significantly higher than SDSCs derived TEC implanted group (P < 0.05) because of the slightly poorer filling in the SDSC group, while no differences were found among the other TEC implanted groups (Figure 4E).

Histological evaluation of animal samples

At 6 weeks, the defect site in the blank group was partially filled with discontinuous and irregularly arranged fibrous-like tissue, showing Safranin-O negative staining. The defect sites in all implanted groups were filled with TECs and demonstrated positive Safranin-O staining, with CCs derived TEC implanted group showing strong positivity and the other groups relatively weaker. The repaired tissues in ACs and CCs derived TEC implanted groups exhibited a cluster-like distribution of cartilage tissue-like lacunar structures similar to the intact group (Figure 5A). ACs, CCs, and BMSCs derived TEC implanted groups exhibited smooth and complete filling, while SDSCs derived TEC implanted group had an irregular surface slightly below the normal cartilage level. At this stage, the graft–host boundary in the subchondral bone area appeared less distinct in the CC group compared to others, suggesting ongoing subchondral bone remodeling (Figure 5A). Immunohistochemical staining revealed the blank group displayed strong positive staining for type I collagen and negative staining for type II collagen, indicating that the repaired tissue formed fibrous tissue rather than cartilage tissue. CCs derived TEC implanted group showed hyaline cartilage-like tissue, uniformly positive for type II collagen staining and negative for type I collagen similar to the intact group. ACs derived TEC implanted group showed distribution of both type II and type I collagen. BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC implanted groups exhibited widespread and uniform type I collagen but was deficient in type II collagen (Figure 5B). The ICRS VHAS score showed that all implanted groups were significantly higher than the blank group (all P < 0.001). However, except for the CCs derived TEC implanted group, all other groups performed significantly worse than the intact group. The CCs derived TEC group also showed significantly higher outcomes than the SDSCs derived TEC implanted group (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed among the remaining groups (Figure 5C). Similarly, the O'Driscoll score indicated that all implanted groups were significantly higher than the blank group (all P < 0.001), but fell short of the intact group. Among the implanted groups, CCs derived TEC scored significantly higher than the other three groups (all P < 0.001), while ACs derived TEC outperformed both BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC (P < 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively). No significant difference emerged between BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC groups (Figure 5D).

At 12 weeks, the defect site in the blank group was almost at the same level as the normal tissue but remained negative for Safranin-O staining. All TEC implanted groups exhibited improved integration with adjacent cartilage, except for the CCs derived TEC group, which displayed disrupted graft–host interface continuity at 12 weeks. Immunohistochemical staining revealed widespread type I collagen positivity and type II collagen negativity in the blank group at 12 weeks, indicating spontaneous repair tissue as fibrous tissue rather than cartilage. CCs derived TEC implanted group maintained the characteristics of hyaline cartilage, exhibiting a uniform distribution of type II collagen and negative for type I collagen resembling the intact group. ACs derived TEC implanted group still showed both type I and type II collagen distribution. Compared to the 6-week, BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC implanted groups exhibited type II collagen distribution at the junction between the graft and the subchondral bone, suggesting in situ differentiation (Figure 6B). The ICRS VHAS scores revealed significantly higher values in all implanted groups compared to the blank group (all P < 0.01). Only the SDSC derived TEC implanted group showed significantly lower scores than the intact group, while no significant differences were observed among the other groups (Figure 6C). The O’Driscoll scores were significantly higher in all implanted groups than in the blank group (all P < 0.001), but only the CCs derived TEC implanted group approached the intact group’s score, while the other groups remained lower values. Notably, ACs and CCs derived TEC implanted groups were significantly higher than BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC implanted groups (all P < 0.05) and there was no difference between BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC implanted groups (Figure 6D).

Discussion

Cartilage defects affect millions of patients worldwide, and it is challenging to repair these defects through conventional treatments.3 Tissue engineering technology offers promising strategies for the structural regeneration and functional reconstruction of cartilage, showing promise for efficient cartilage regeneration.4 The scaffold-free TEC approach has initially demonstrated its potential for cartilage repair and translational potential for clinical applications in both in vitro and in vivo experiments.25,26 Seed cells play a crucial role in tissue repair and have a significant impact on the long-term success of regenerated cartilage. Currently, the seed cells used for articular cartilage regeneration primarily include hyaline chondrocytes and mesenchymal stem cells. Hyaline chondrocytes can produce chondrogenic growth factors and cartilage-specific ECM components, such as collagen II and glycosaminoglycan, thereby creating a chondrogenic microenvironment to ultimately achieve cartilage regeneration.33,34 However, issues such as aging, dedifferentiation of hyaline chondrocytes, and insufficient cell numbers limit their application in cartilage generation.35 Mesenchymal stem cells, due to their capacity for chondrogenic differentiation and their immune and inflammatory regulatory properties, are widely used in the field of articular cartilage regeneration.36 Nevertheless, the spontaneous chondrogenesis of these stem cells is insufficient, requiring a chondrogenic induction system to achieve cartilage regeneration, which increases the uncertainty of cartilage repair.37 Therefore, it is particularly important to identify suitable seed cells to achieve effective regeneration of cartilage.

In this study, cells in the TECs of all groups maintained high cell viability up to day 14 (Figure 1D). This was attributed to the TEC approach, which addresses the issue of tissue size, circumventing the central necrosis commonly observed in traditional methods due to insufficient nutrient and oxygen diffusion. Additionally, it facilitates the formation of a 3-dimensional structure with a uniform distribution of cells, rendering it more suitable for clinical applications.26 During the in vitro formation of scaffold-free 3-dimensional structures of TECs, the cells exhibited distinct morphological characteristics across different groups. Despite having the same initial cell number and seeding area, the tissue-engineered constructs (TECs) formed after 14 days of ex vivo culture exhibited spontaneous contraction, both on the matrix and after detachment, resulting in a noticeable reduction in size compared to the initial seeding area. Moreover, there were significant differences in the TEC diameter and contraction ratio among the different cell groups, with both showing a similar trend across the groups (Figure 1B and C). These morphological changes in TEC may be related to the actin contraction-driven changes in cell morphology that are dependent on rho-associated kinase (ROCK), as it regulates how cells integrate mechanical and molecular information from the microenvironment.38 When TEC detach from the matrix, they are released from the constraints imposed by substrate-cell interactions, leading to rapid actomyosin contraction and cytoskeletal rearrangement, which results in contraction.39 Notably, SDSCs and BMSCs derived TECs exhibited a significantly higher contraction ratio within 24 h compared to ACs and CCs derived TECs, indicating that undifferentiated MSCs exhibit stronger contractile ability, which may be attributed to their active cytoskeleton and intense interaction with the external environment (Figure 2A). Firstly, MSCs possess robust migratory capacity and a high degree of mechanical sensing ability, enabling them to respond to external mechanical stimuli.40,41 Secondly, the cytoskeleton of MSCs is rich in actin fibers, which form a network within the cells, facilitating cell contraction and extension. MSCs participate in tissue repair, migration, and interaction with the external environment through tension regulation and the contraction of actin fibers.42 In contrast, chondrocytes, as mature cells, are functionally more focused on the synthesis and maintenance of cartilage matrix, lacking strong contractile and migratory requirements43; therefore, their ability to construct 3-dimensional structures is relatively weak.

Under the same in vitro culture conditions, different groups of cells also exhibited varying trends in differentiation. In vitro experiments showed that ACs derived TEC had the highest GAG/DNA ratio, followed by CCs derived TEC, which also exhibited significantly higher levels of chondrogenic genes (Sox9, Col2, Acan) and pronounced cartilage matrix secretion, as indicated by Safranin-O, Alcian blue, and Col2 staining (Figures 2B and 4). CCs derived TEC produce abundant hyaline cartilage matrix, maintaining a good structural morphology and matrix content for a long time after transplantation, consistent with our previous research findings.44 In this study, the cartilage repair effect of CCs derived TEC was found to be superior to that of ACs derived TEC. Previous studies investigating the potential of CCs as an alternative source of seed cells to ACs have demonstrated that CCs exhibit superior capabilities in terms of in vitro proliferation, redifferentiation, and secretion of cartilage-related ECM compared to ACs.15,16 Furthermore, CCs can maintain a hyaline cartilage phenotype for an extended period post-transplantation.45 In contrast, BMSCs and SDSCs derived TECs demonstrated significantly higher Col1 expression, with notable Col1 secretion (Figure 2C), while CCs and ACs derived TECs exhibited widespread positivity for the cartilage matrix stains (Figure 3). These results are consistent with previous reports, indicating that chondrocytes express cartilage-related genes and produce matrix during the process of TEC formation, while MSCs maintain lower expression levels.25,26 This disparity can be attributed to two main factors. First, MSCs exhibit varying gene expression profiles, which means that undifferentiated MSCs require specific exogenous growth factors and conditions to effectively initiate differentiation into chondrogenesis.46,47 Second, their subcellular structures might not be as well adapted to meet the demands of matrix synthesis as those of chondrocytes. The well-developed rough endoplasmic reticulum in chondrocytes enables efficient synthesis and secretion of large amounts of cartilage matrix components.48 MSCs, conversely, lack such specialized organelles and secretory mechanisms.

During the formation of TECs in this study, there was no pre-induction of chondrogenic differentiation; instead, the inherent cartilage matrix secretion capability of chondrocytes was utilized while preserving the differentiation potential of MSCs. This method not only simplifies in vitro culture but also avoids issues related to pre-differentiation with exogenous growth factors, such as the typical terminal differentiation of hypertrophic chondrocytes expressing type X collagen during MSCs chondrogenesis and the tendency of CCs to hypertrophy.44,45,49,50 It is worth noting that MSCs derived TEC exhibited in situ differentiation after implantation at the defect site. As it is shown that staining of 6-week MSCs derived TEC samples revealed almost no Col2 production; however, after 12 weeks of implantation, Col2 was significantly present at the graft–subchondral bone interface in both BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC implanted groups, showing consistent outcomes (Figures 3E, 5B, and 6B). The in situ differentiation of MSCs derived TECs is essential because the formation of MSCs derived TEC is primarily composed of Col1, which is a mechanically inferior tissue type with poor durability and resistance to high tensile forces, making it prone to further damage.51 However, it has been reported that the repair of MSCs derived TEC is not perfect, as the regenerated tissue still contains some fibrous tissue, primarily located at the surface or superficial area, which may result in some impaired mechanical properties.52 This suggests that if these TECs are used for clinical repair of articular cartilage injuries, it is advisable to extend the time the affected limb is kept non-weight-bearing. In contrast, CCs derived TEC does not have these concerns, as they consistently maintain high levels of Col2 and cartilage matrix distribution, allowing for a faster recovery of cartilage structure and function. In addition, ACs derived TEC displayed a significant expression of both Col1 and Col2 following transplantation into the osteochondral defect site, indicating a potential degeneration of the cartilage tissue. This phenomenon may be attributed to the limited capacity of ACs to regenerate the cartilage extracellular matrix in vitro. Conversely, CCs derived TEC did not exhibit elevated levels of Col1 expression, highlighting the reliability and advantages of CCs-derived TEC in the context of articular cartilage repair strategies (Figure 6B).

The advantage of MSCs-derived TEC lies in their ability to integrate with the surrounding host tissues. Histological results from in vivo experiments showed that CCs derived TEC implanted group exhibited a disruption of continuity at the graft–host interface at 12 weeks, while BMSCs and SDSCs derived TEC implanted groups demonstrated satisfactory integration (Figure 6A). In our previous experiments, we discovered that chondrocytes were less robust than stem cells in terms of graft–host integration. Furthermore, we found that the addition of stem cells could enhance the integration capability.45 This suggests that MSCs possess superior integration capabilities with the surrounding cartilage, which is particularly ­crucial for the successful repair of articular cartilage. The integration between grafts and host tissues forms an essential interface; however, this connection can become a weak link if not well integrated, as it may not withstand normal mechanical loads, ultimately leading to repair failure.53 Chondrocytes in mature cartilage are known to have a limited ability to migrate to defect sites due to their confinement within the pericellular matrix. Additionally, mature chondrocytes lack N-cadherin, a protein that forms a cadherin adhesion complex, bridging neighboring cells and linking them to the actin-myosin cytoskeleton. This complex is crucial for mechanical coupling between cells, which drives various morphogenetic events and tissue repair.54 As a result, this deficiency may lead to a significant interfacial difference between repaired cartilage and native cartilage.55 In contrast, studies have shown that during the chondrogenesis of MSCs, N-cadherin plays a vital role in mediating cell–cell adhesion through its extracellular domain via homotypic protein-protein interactions, ultimately promoting better integration with the surrounding cartilage.56 To address this issue, more technical means may be required to improve the biological characteristics of chondrocytes, thereby enhancing their ability to integrate with surrounding cartilage.

This experiment conducted both in vitro and in vivo investigations of TEC; however, there are still several aspects that require further exploration in future research. For example, the specific mechanisms underlying the effective graft–host integration of MSCs derived TEC and strategies to improve the integration capacity of chondrocytes derived TEC remain to be investigated in future studies. Additionally, although SDSCs derived TEC has been applied in clinical treatment, TECs from other cell sources must undergo in vivo testing in large animal models before they can be prepared for clinical translation.

Conclusion

This experiment conducted a comparative analysis of the characteristics of TECs from different cell sources and validated the feasibility of TEC-forming repairs for articular cartilage injuries. The results indicated that ACs and CCs exhibited superior cartilage matrix secretion in vitro. While CCs derived TEC showed later connectivity issues in vivo, they achieved better in vivo repair effects, making it a promising alternative cell source to ACs which exhibited phenotypic instability in vivo. Although MSCs derived TECs showed relatively insufficient cartilage matrix production and cartilage repair capacity, their integration ability surpassed that of chondrocytes derived TECs. Future research can leverage this characteristic to optimize chondrocytes derived TECs.
