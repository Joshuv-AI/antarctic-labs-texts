Physical activity and sedentary behavior patterns in cognitive impairment: findings from a cross-sectional study in Punjab, North India
Authors: Neha Yadav, Vineet Chaudhary, Kallur Nava Saraswathy, Naorem Kiranmala Devi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical activity (PA) and sedentary behavior (SB) are recognized as potential factors affecting cognitive function; however, there is limited evidence of their association with cognitive impairment (CI) in Indian populations. The present study aimed to investigate the role of PA and SB in CI among adults living in rural communities in the Mansa district of Punjab, India.MethodsThe study included 1206 adults of both sexes, living in rural Punjab. Sociodemographic and lifestyle data were collected using modified interview schedules. The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) assessed PA and SB, and cognitive function was evaluated using RUDAS.ResultsLow PA was associated with an increased risk of CI only among older adults (≥ 60 years) [OR: 1.68 (0.98–2.88), p-value = 0.061], but not in the middle and younger age groups. Conversely, high SB was significantly associated with an increased risk of CI in both the older and middle age groups (40–59 years) [OR: 2.17 (1.06–4.30), p-value = 0.026; OR: 1.83 (1.03–3.27), p-value = 0.040, respectively]. Further, when examining the composite effect of PA and SB, the group with low PA and high SB had significantly increased odds of CI [OR: 2.15 (1.19–3.88), p-value = 0.011] compared to the reference category of high PA and low SB in the overall sample.ConclusionsAdhering to recommended PA levels and maintaining low SB (< 7 h/day) can be beneficial in reducing the burden of CI. Additionally, reducing sedentary time may independently contribute to reducing the burden of CI among middle-aged individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26940-1.

Background

One in every six individuals is projected to be an older adult (≥ 60 years) by the year 2030 globally [1]. Additionally, the population within this age bracket is expected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050, doubling from 1 billion in 2019. While the increase in life expectancy is a significant accomplishment, the aging of populations poses significant challenges for healthcare and social systems worldwide. This is due to the fact that aging is often associated with a decline in both physical and mental capabilities [2–5].

Although age-related cognitive decline is commonly observed, in some instances, it can develop into pathological conditions such as cognitive impairment (CI) and dementia. This progression may ultimately hamper the motor skills and independent living abilities among older adults [6]. As a result, the pathological decline in cognition places a significant strain on healthcare systems [7]. For instance, in 2019, the global number of people living with dementia exceeded 57.4 million, with associated economic costs amounting to $2.8 trillion and projected to reach $16.9 trillion by 2050 [6, 7, 8]. A growing body of evidence suggests that optimum modifications in behavioral risk factors may delay the onset of cognitive decline [9, 10].

Lack of physical activity (PA) has been identified as one of the most important modifiable risk factors for dementia [10]. Numerous cross-sectional and observational studies have highlighted the beneficial role of regular high-level PA on cognitive decline among middle-aged and older adults [11, 12]. Further, recent genetic epidemiological evidence, utilizing a Mendelian randomization approach, has supported the so observed relationship in the previous studies reflecting a direct, beneficial link between the amount of time spent engaging in moderate to vigorous PA and cognitive functions [13].

In recent years, sedentary behavior (SB) has emerged as a possible lifestyle factor having a direct effect on health and appears to be independent of PA [14]. In other words, although PA and SB are related, they are not entirely dependent on each other, as an individual can be both highly physically active and sedentary at the same time. For instance, those groups meeting the WHO’s recommended PA guidelines (≥ 600 min per week) while remaining sedentary for the rest of the day. However, the relationship between SB and cognitive function remains unclear and lacks consensus among studies, particularly in otherwise physically active population groups, such as farmers and laborers [15–17].

Most studies examining the relationship between CI, PA and BS have been conducted in Western countries. There is a lack of research on the impact of PA and SB on cognitive functions in low-resource settings, such as rural areas, where access to healthcare, especially for mental and neurological health issues, remain limited. In such settings, patterns of PA and SB may differ significantly from those in high-income countries. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the role of PA and SB in CI among adults living in rural communities in the Mansa district of Punjab, India.

Methods

Study design and area

This population-based, cross-sectional study is part of a larger study funded by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), Government of India, that has been conducted among rural community-dwelling adults in 14 villages of Mansa district, Punjab.

Recruitment of participants

A total of 1206 individuals of both sexes were recruited for the present study from nine randomly selected villages in Mansa district, Punjab between January 2023 and December 2023. Individuals aged 18–75 years, who consented to participate in the study were recruited by block-wise, door-to-door visits in each village. The median age of the study participants was 50 years with an IQR range of 39–60 years.

Participants were recruited from rural communities in the Mansa district, Punjab through door-to-door visits, following prior approvals and meetings with local leaders and health professionals to ensure community engagement and trust (Fig. 1). Of the individuals approached, 1,206 met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and provided consent to participate in the study. Data on all variables included in this study were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted in a single visit. For village-wise distribution of recruited participants, refer to Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants included in the study were adults aged 18–75 years residing in rural communities within the Mansa district, Punjab who provided informed consent to participate. Individuals with a history of cardiovascular diseases or cancer, or those currently on extended medication regimens were excluded, as these conditions could potentially impact cognitive health. Additionally, pregnant and breastfeeding females were not recruited for the study to avoid potential confounding factors related to their physiological status. However, common health conditions and cardiovascular disease risk factors, such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes, were not exclusion criteria, nor were individuals on medication for these conditions.

Ethical clearance

The study protocol was approved by Ethics Committee, Department of Anthropology, University of Delhi, Delhi-110007 (Reference No. Anth/2022-23/823). Informed consent, typed in Punjabi, was obtained from each participant prior to data collection.

Tools and techniques administered for data collection

Sociodemographic variables

Data related to sociodemographic variables like age, sex, literacy status, marital status, family members, current employment, per capita annual family income, diet type, and current smoking & alcohol status were collected using pretested and modified interview schedules.

Estimation of PA and SB

The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) was used to collect data on PA. GPAQ is one of the extensively used tools in various epidemiology research. It consists of 16 items and 6 domains PA i.e., vigorous intensity work, moderate intensity work, walking or cycling, vigorous-intensity sports, moderate-intensity sports, and sitting time in a typical day. This questionnaire was developed by the WHO step-wise approach for surveillance of risk factors for chronic disease (STEPS) [18, 19]. As per WHO guidelines of GPAQ, individuals who had total PA in a typical week ≥ 600 min were considered to have a high PA level and those individuals who had < 600 min per week were considered to have a low PA level [20].

SB was assessed using a single item in a GPAQ scale “How much time do you usually spend sitting or reclining on a typical day?” As there is no standard cutoff to classify sitting/reclining time assessed by GPAQ, 7 h/day was used as a cutoff point for classifying individuals in high and low SB categories (sitting time ≥ 7 h/day: high SB and < 7 h/day: low SB) as used in meta-analysis by Chau et al. [21], and Ku et al. [22].

Screening for Cognitive Impairment (CI)

Screening for CI was done by using the Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS) [23]. RUDAS is one of the most widely used and validated tools used to screen for CI in culturally and linguistically diverse (CLAD) population groups in various epidemiology research worldwide [24]. RUDAS is a 30-point scale and consists of 6 domains of cognitive skills namely visuospatial orientation, praxis, visuo-constructional drawing, judgment, memory recall, and language. Participants who scored > 22 points were considered as cognitively normal and participants who scored ≤ 22 points were considered to have impaired cognition.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses for this study were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 22. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of the data distribution. To evaluate potential multicollinearity between PA and SB, we conducted a Pearson correlation analysis. The results indicated a moderate negative correlation between the two variables (r = − 0.413, p < 0.001), suggesting that while related, they do not overlap to a degree that would distort regression estimates. Therefore, PA and SB were treated as independent exposure variables and included in the same logistic regression models.

Frequency and percentage distributions were calculated for categorical variables. The bivariate relationship between CI and PA/SB, as well as other sociodemographic and lifestyle variables, was evaluated using cross-tabulations and the Chi-square tests. Sociodemographic and lifestyle variables that demonstrated significant differences in relation to CI prevalence were identified as potential confounders (age, sex, literacy status, marital status, and employment status) (Supplementary Table 3). To assess the multivariable association between CI and PA/SB in the overall sample, binary logistic regression models were employed, adjusting for age, sex, literacy status, marital status, and employment status. To explore the effect of PA and SB on CI within specific age groups, participants were categorized into three groups: younger (< 40 years), middle-aged (40–59 years), and older (60 years and above). Binary logistic regression models, adjusted for sex, literacy status, marital status, and employment status, were used to investigate the relationship between CI and PA/SB within these age categories. Additionally, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed with PA, SB, age, and other selected confounders as independent variables, and CI as the dependent variable.

Furthermore, to examine the combined effect of PA and SB on CI, participants were categorized into four groups based on high and low levels of PA and SB: High PA + Low SB, High PA + High SB, Low PA + Low SB, and Low PA + High SB. Differences in CI prevalence among these four groups were assessed using the Chi-square test. Additionally, binary logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age, sex, literacy status, marital status, and employment status, was conducted to calculate the odds of CI across these groups, with the High PA + Low SB group serving as the reference. Missing data was handled by pair-wise deletion. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

General overview of the study sample

The study included a diverse sample of 1,206 participants, with a fairly balanced representation across age groups and family structures. A higher proportion of participants were middle-aged, and more than half were literate, employed, and belonged to smaller family units. The majority were married, and over two-thirds followed a vegetarian diet. Notably, smoking and alcohol consumption were relatively uncommon in the sample. Gender-wise, significant differences emerged across multiple sociodemographic and lifestyle variables, including age distribution, literacy levels, dietary habits, and substance use patterns (Table 1).

Overall and age-stratified proportion of CI cases across PA and SB categories

CI appeared more common among individuals with lower PA, particularly in the overall sample and among older adults; however, these associations did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, SB demonstrated a robust and statistically significant relationship with CI. Participants with higher SB levels (≥ 7 h/day) consistently exhibited greater CI prevalence across all age groups, especially among individuals aged 40–59 years and older adults. These findings suggest that prolonged sedentary time may be a more sensitive indicator of cognitive risk than physical inactivity in this population (Table 2). Association of CI with PA and SB

After adjusting for confounders (age, sex, literacy status, marital status, and employment status), PA showed no significant association with CI in the overall sample or among younger and middle-aged adults (Fig. 2). However, among older adults, those with low PA levels demonstrated a trend toward increased CI risk, approaching statistical significance. In contrast, SB emerged as a significant predictor of CI. High SB was associated with a markedly increased risk of CI in the overall sample, with the strongest associations observed among middle-aged adults, where the risk more than doubled, and among older adults, who also exhibited a substantially elevated risk. These findings highlight SB as a more consistent and significant risk factor for CI than PA across age groups (Fig. 2).

When PA and SB were included together in the same adjusted model along with all the selected confounders, the results remained consistent. High SB continued to show a significant association with increased risk of CI (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.14–2.67), while low PA did not demonstrate a significant effect (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.76–1.58) (Supplementary Table 4).

The composite effect of PA and SB on cognitive status

Analysis of combined PA and SB categories revealed distinct patterns in CI prevalence (Table 3). Individuals who engaged in high PA and maintained low SB had the most favourable cognitive outcomes, with the lowest proportion of CI. In contrast, those with low PA and high SB exhibited the highest prevalence of CI, indicating a compounded negative effect of physical inactivity and prolonged sedentary time.

Adjusted analyses further confirmed this pattern: participants in the low PA and high SB group faced more than double the risk of CI compared to those with high PA and low SB. Although individuals with high PA but high SB also showed an elevated risk, this was not statistically significant. Notably, low PA combined with low SB did not appear to increase CI risk, suggesting that SB may have a stronger independent effect than low PA (Table 3).

Discussion

The study found that low PA was associated with a higher risk of CI among older adults (≥ 60 years), but not in younger or middle-aged groups. High SB was significantly linked to increased CI risk in both middle-aged (40–59 years) and older adults. Notably, individuals with both low PA and high SB had over twice the odds of CI compared to those with high PA and low SB, indicating a compounded negative effect when both risk factors are present.

The observed age-specific association between low PA and CI partially aligns with previous research, which has highlighted low PA as a potential contributor to cognitive decline and dementia [25, 26]. Also, high PA has been shown to offer protective benefits. Blondell et al. [25] emphasized that PA could mitigate cognitive deterioration and support cognitive health. Furthermore, Jia et al. [26] conducted a meta-analysis that reported higher PA levels to be associated with a reduced risk of CI and dementia across diverse populations. This analysis also found that even moderate PA levels can yield significant cognitive benefits.

From a public health point of view, specific forms of PA are reported to offer unique advantages. For instance, a randomized controlled trial found that older adults engaging in a combination of aerobic and resistance training exhibited greater improvements in cognitive function compared to those participating in aerobic exercise alone [27]. This suggests that a varied exercise regimen may provide more comprehensive benefits, potentially through the composite effects on cardiovascular health, muscle strength, and metabolic regulation.

However, contrary to the findings of the present, previous studies indicate that the benefits of high PA on cognitive health are not limited to older adults [28]. Emerging evidence suggests that PA during midlife can offer long-term advantages for cognitive function in later years. For instance, Sabia et al. [28], conducted a longitudinal study following participants over several decades and found that those who engaged in regular PA during midlife had a reduced risk of cognitive impairment in older age. This underscores the importance of maintaining an active lifestyle throughout one’s life as a strategy for preserving cognitive health and preventing cognitive decline. The discrepancy in the present study’s findings may be due to the fact that the sample predominantly consisted of farmers, who may have different patterns of physical activity and lifestyle factors compared to other populations studied in previous research.

Additionally, other studies suggest that social and environmental factors may influence the relationship between PA and CI. Engaging in group exercises or community-based PA can offer social stimulation and support, which are independently linked to better cognitive outcomes [29]. Further, access to safe and supportive environments for PA, such as parks and recreational facilities, is vital for promoting an active lifestyle, particularly in underserved communities.

One of the possible physiological mechanisms by which PA may protect cognitive function is through its impact on neuroplasticity. PA, particularly aerobic exercise, has been shown to increase the production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which supports the growth and survival of neurons, and enhances synaptic plasticity, critical for learning and memory processes [30]. Additionally, regular physical activity improves cerebral blood flow and reduces neuroinflammation, contributing to a healthier brain environment conducive to cognitive resilience [31–34]. While evidence of cognitive benefits from PA is robust, especially among older adults, variability in study designs and measures, along with a need for more research on optimal PA types and intensities, present challenges.

Further, the present study indicates high SB to be a significant risk factor for CI in middle-aged and older adults. This observation supports the growing concerns about sedentary lifestyles and their impact on cognitive health. SB is increasingly being recognized as a major public health issue, with recent research highlighting its detrimental effects on cognitive function, particularly in midlife and older age [35–37]. Prolonged sedentary time has been associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia, underscoring the need for effective interventions to address this escalating concern [35–37].

For instance, a meta-analysis by Falck et al. [35] revealed a significant association between higher levels of SB and an increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia. Ku et al. [38] found that high SB in older adults was associated with a 30% increased risk of developing dementia over a ten-year follow-up period. Ishikawa et al. [39] reported that each additional hour of daily sedentary time was linked to a 1.3-fold increase in the risk of cognitive decline in middle-aged and older adults. Furthermore, the LIFE study by Sink et al. [40] demonstrated that structured PA programs significantly reduced the incidence of mild CI in older adults. Interventional studies targeting SB, such as breaking up sitting time with light activity, have shown promise in improving cognitive performance [41]. Additionally, midlife has been identified as a crucial period for interventions aimed at preventing CI and early detection and intervention for CI during this stage can be vital [10].

Middle age presents an opportunity to adopt healthier behaviours that positively affect cognitive health. Modifying lifestyle habits by reducing sedentary time and increasing PA, particularly during middle age, may particularly be crucial. Research suggests that poor diet and inadequate sleep, often accompanying SB, can exacerbate cognitive decline [37]. Interventions during this period can provide long-lasting benefits, potentially delay cognitive decline and reduce the risk of related conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes.

The underlying physiological mechanisms linking SB to cognitive decline are multifactorial. SB has been associated with impaired neuroplasticity, whereas regular PA can enhance neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity [42]. Additionally, impaired glucose and lipid metabolism, which are also associated with high SB, are potential risk factors negatively impacting cognitive function [43–45]. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, linked to high SB and indicated by elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and nterleukin-6 (IL-6), may further adversely affect brain health [46]. Additionally, SB has been found to be associated with vascular dysfunction, which impairs cerebral blood flow and contributes to vascular dementia [47].

Regarding the composite effect of PA and SB, individuals with low PA and high SB had the highest risk of CI (OR = 2.15), with the high PA and low SB group as the reference. This suggests that while high PA is beneficial, it may not fully counteract the negative effects of high SB on cognitive health. Engaging in PA may not be sufficient to protect against cognitive decline if SB is not also reduced. These observations indicate that SB might contribute to CI independently of PA levels.

However, contrasting findings regarding SB and cognitive decline have been noted [47]. These discrepancies may result from differences in how SB and cognitive function are measured. For example, while this study defined SB as total sitting or reclining time, other research may focus on specific activities such as television watching or smartphone use [48]. Further, this study assessed the overall association between CI and SB without distinguishing between types of sedentary activities, while some of the previous studies have differentiated between mentally stimulating sedentary activities, such as puzzles, and more passive activities, like watching television [49–50].

To optimize cognitive health, it may be beneficial to focus on reducing SB in conjunction with promoting PA. Specifically, targeting reductions in SB among middle-aged individuals appears to be crucial. Individuals who are physically active but still have significant SB might be more at risk than expected. Thus, differentiating between physically active individuals based on their levels of SB could be important in accurately assessing and addressing cognitive health risks.

Strength and limitations

The present study has several strengths, including a large sample size of 1,206 individuals, which enhances the statistical power and reliability of the findings. It involves comprehensive data collection on sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviours, and cognitive function, allowing for detailed analysis of associations between PA/SB and CI in the studied community.

However, there are some limitations that should be acknowledged. The observed associations between PA/SB, and CI must also be interpreted in light of the possibility of reverse causality. It is plausible that cognitive decline itself may lead to reduced PA levels and increased sedentary time, rather than these behaviours being the cause of CI. This is particularly relevant in aging populations, where early cognitive changes might already influence daily activity patterns. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, we are unable to determine the directionality of these associations, which remains a key limitation. Longitudinal research is needed to establish whether PA and SB contribute to cognitive decline or are consequences of it.

Additionally, PA and SB are conceptually related. Although we assessed multicollinearity between them and found only a moderate inverse correlation (r = − 0.413, p < 0.001), indicating that both variables could be included as independent predictors in the regression models without risk of distortion, complete independence cannot be asserted. Further, other limitations include the use of self-reported sedentary time, which did not capture the nature of the sedentary activities, and reliance on RUDAS for cognitive screening, which, while culturally appropriate, may not capture the full clinical spectrum of CI.

Conclusion

The present study reveals the significant impact of PA and SB on cognitive health. While low PA increased the risk of CI in individuals aged 60 and older, high SB was associated with a higher CI risk in both older and middle-aged groups. Additionally, low PA combined with high SB was linked to a significantly higher CI risk compared to those with high PA and low SB. These findings underscore the importance of both increasing PA and reducing SB to optimize cognitive health. Engaging in the optimal levels of PA, along with limiting SB, can significantly reduce CI risk.
