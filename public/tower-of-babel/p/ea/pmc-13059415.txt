Efficacy and safety of immunotherapy in elderly patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer: a real-world study stratified by age
Authors: Yi Dong, Mei-cen Liu, Yun-ye Mao, Yi-heng Lu, An Wang, Xin Zhou, Yin-min Ji, Shu-yan Xiao, Ya-hui Lv, Tao Li, Xiao-ran Cui, Yi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy in elderly patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), stratified by age.MethodsA retrospective analysis included elderly patients (≥ 65 years) diagnosed with stage III–IV NSCLC who received immunotherapy at the PLA General Hospital between January 2016 and October 2024. Participants were stratified into two age groups: <75 years and ≥ 75 years. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline characteristics, including age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score, and treatment line. Primary endpoints were objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and drug safety.ResultsAmong 535 enrolled patients, the median age was 70 years. The ORR was 54.40% in the group aged < 75 years and 41.75% in the group aged ≥ 75 years. Median overall survival (mOS) was 29.23 months for the < 75 years group and 27.93 months for the ≥ 75 years group. No significant differences were observed between groups in OS (HR = 1.274, P = 0.068) or PFS (HR = 0.907, P = 0.421). The overall incidence of adverse events (AEs) was 90.28%, with no significant differences in AE rates across age groups.ConclusionImmunotherapy demonstrated feasibility and efficacy in elderly patients with advanced NSCLC. Age did not significantly affect survival outcomes between subgroups (< 75 vs. ≥75 years), and safety was manageable. These findings suggest that the 75-year threshold alone should not preclude treatment; instead, clinical decisions should be guided by a comprehensive geriatric assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07248-6.Keywords: Elderly patients, Advanced non-small cell lung cancer, Immunotherapy, Adverse events

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and representing a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. The incidence of NSCLC varies considerably by region, with higher rates observed in areas where tobacco use—the primary risk factor—is widespread [3]. Clinically, NSCLC often presents with persistent cough, chest pain, and dyspnea. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, leading to a poor prognosis [4]. Advances in molecular subtyping have revealed numerous oncogenic driver genes in NSCLC, facilitating the development of targeted therapies that substantially improve patient outcomes [5, 6]. Despite these advancements, the five-year survival rate for NSCLC remains low, highlighting the urgent need for continued research into early detection and innovative treatment strategies [1].

Elderly patients with NSCLC face unique challenges in immunotherapy due to age-related physiological changes and a higher prevalence of comorbidities [7]. Studies indicate that the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is often limited in elderly NSCLC patients, who tend to have shorter overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared to their younger counterparts [8, 9]. Furthermore, immune senescence, characterized by age-related decline in immune function, adversely impacts treatment outcomes in this population [10]. However, emerging evidence suggests that ICIs may still confer survival benefits to elderly patients who maintain a good performance status [11]. Consequently, it is crucial to develop personalized treatment strategies that comprehensively account for the unique physiological and clinical characteristics of elderly NSCLC patients in order to optimize therapeutic outcomes [12].

Immunotherapy has emerged as a cornerstone treatment for advanced NSCLC, substantially transforming its therapeutic landscape [13]. Recent evidence indicates that immune checkpoint inhibitors, including programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, significantly improve survival outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC, particularly when administered in combination with chemotherapy [14, 15]. Nevertheless, the efficacy of immunotherapy demonstrates considerable interindividual variability, influenced by factors such as PD-L1 expression level and tumor mutational burden [16]. Despite these advancements, immunotherapy continues to present substantial challenges, particularly in managing immune-related adverse events (AEs) and identifying reliable biomarkers predictive of treatment response [17]. Consequently, ongoing research is essential to further refine immunotherapy strategies for advanced NSCLC and enhance clinical outcomes [18].

This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy in elderly patients with advanced NSCLC across different age cohorts, thereby providing evidence to optimize treatment strategies. Previous research indicates that elderly patients frequently present with comorbidities and declining organ function, which compromises their tolerance to standard treatments [12, 19]. Immunotherapy, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, has demonstrated potential efficacy and an acceptable toxicity profile in this population. However, as most clinical trials exclude patients aged over 75 years, robust evidence remains limited [20, 21]. Furthermore, factors including Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status and number of metastatic sites have been identified as potential predictors of PFS in this patient group [20]. This study seeks to address critical gaps in the current literature by providing comprehensive real-world evidence on immunotherapy outcomes in elderly NSCLC patients, thus informing clinical decision-making and potentially improving patient outcomes [22].

Methods

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from elderly patients with NSCLC who attended the First Medical Centre of the PLA General Hospital between January 2016 and October 2024. Age ≥ 65 years was defined as elderly according to World Health Organisation (WHO) criteria. Patients were further stratified into two age groups: <75 years and ≥ 75 years. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) pathological diagnosis of NSCLC; (2) clinical stage III–IV according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system for lung cancer; (3) age ≥ 65 years; (4) receipt of immunotherapy during first-line or subsequent treatment; (5) ≥ 2 cycles of immunotherapy; (6) Complete medical records and follow-up data; (7) At least one measurable lesion according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST 1.1). Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Multiple primary tumours; (2) Severe cardiac, cerebral, renal disease, or psychiatric disorders; (3) Incomplete clinical data; (4) Fewer than two cycles of immunotherapy. Ultimately, 535 patients were enrolled. All enrolled patients in Phase III (n = 188) received palliative systemic immunotherapy. They did not undergo curative concurrent chemoradiotherapy, primarily due to advanced age, comorbidities (e.g., severe pulmonary impairment), excessive tumor burden, or patient preference. Through medical record review, we excluded patients who received standard curative chemoradiotherapy and consolidation immunotherapy to ensure treatment intention homogeneity in the cohort. All efficacy and safety analyses were conducted based on outcomes starting from the initiation of the index line of immunotherapy that qualified the patient for study inclusion. For patients who had received immunotherapy in a prior treatment line, this index line represents a new, subsequent course of therapy.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy in elderly patients with advanced NSCLC across different age groups, providing evidence for optimising treatment strategies in this population. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Ethics No.: S2021-256-02), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Treatment and efficacy assessment

Efficacy was assessed according to RECIST 1.1 criteria by two experienced radiologists, with a third senior radiologist serving as arbiter in cases of disagreement. Tumor imaging (primarily CT) was performed at baseline and subsequently every 2–3 treatment cycles, categorised as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from initiation of immunotherapy to death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included PFS, defined as the time from immunotherapy initiation to the first imaging-confirmed disease progression, or death; Objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR. Disease control rate (DCR) was calculated as the percentage of evaluable cases achieving response (PR + CR) or stable disease (SD). Data for endpoints not reached by 30 April 2025 were handled as censored. AEs were assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis summarised patient clinical characteristics and disease status. To balance baseline data, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed at a 1:2 ratio, with a clamp value of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the PS score. Survival analysis employed Kaplan-Meier methods for survival curve plotting, with intergroup comparisons using the Log-rank test. Normally distributed quantitative data were analysed using t-tests, while non-normally distributed data used rank-sum tests; Categorical data were analysed using χ² tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were analysed using in both univariate and multivariate analyses to assess the associations of different age groups with PFS and OS. Effect sizes were expressed as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), with significance set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R 4.3.3, SPSS 26.0, or OriginPro 2025.

Results

Patient Baseline Characteristics

A total of 535 eligible patients aged ≥ 65 years with locally unresectable or advanced NSCLC who received immunotherapy were included in this study (Fig. 1). The median age of this cohort was 70.00 years, and 447 patients(83.55%) were male. ECOG-PS scores of 0–1 were present in 87.85% of patients, indicating generally favourable performance status. The cohort consisted of 432 patients aged < 75 years and 103 aged ≥ 75 years. Clinical stage distribution was stage III in 188 cases (35.14%) and stage IV in 347 cases (64.86%); T4 stage was present in 270 cases (50.47%), lymph node metastasis in 476 cases (88.97%), and distant metastasis in 346 cases (64.67%). Histologically, 276 cases (51.59%) were squamous cell carcinoma, and 229 cases (42.80%) were adenocarcinoma. Non-smokers constituted 25.79% of the cohort, while 68.79% had no family history of cancer. Comorbidities included hypertension in 41.87%, diabetes in 18.50%, and coronary heart disease in 17.38%. PD-L1 expression levels were low, intermediate, and high in 13.83%, 29.53%, and 19.44% of cases, respectively. Metastasis rates to bone, liver, Central Nervous System, intrapulmonary sites, and pleural cavity were 24.49%, 9.16%, 10.84%, 30.28%, and 14.39%, respectively. Previous treatments included surgery (12.90%), radiotherapy (12.90%), and ICIs (14.02%). Immunotherapy was administered as first-line treatment in 390 patients and as second-line or later treatment in 145 patients. Regarding the initial systemic treatment regimen, 431 patients (80.56%) received immunotherapy concurrently with platinum-based chemotherapy, while the remaining 104 patients (19.44%) received immunotherapy monotherapy. Following PSM matching at a 1:2 ratio, no significant differences were observed between groups in baseline characteristics, including gender, ECOG PS score, and number of treatment lines (P > 0.05, SMD < 0.2). The matched cohort comprised 170 patients aged < 75 years and 92 aged ≥ 75 years. Detailed clinical characteristics before and after matching are presented in Table 1.

Efficacy Outcomes

By the end of follow-up, 241 (55.79%) and 74 (71.84%) patients in the < 75 years and ≥ 75 years groups, respectively, experienced an overall survival event. Regarding efficacy, the < 75 years group achieved CR in 3 (0.69%), PR in 232 (53.70%), SD in 156 (36.11%), and PD in 41 (9.50%). In the ≥ 75 years group, there was 1 CR (0.97%), 42 PR (40.78%), 53 SD (51.46%), and 7 PD (6.80%). ORR in the two groups were 54.40% and 41.75%, respectively, while DCR were 90.51% and 93.20%, respectively (Table 2).

Evaluation of efficacy

Following PSM matching, the < 75 years and ≥ 75 years cohorts comprised 170 and 92 patients respectively. By the end of follow-up, 98 (57.65%) and 67 (72.83%) patients had experienced an overall survival event. Regarding tumor response, the < 75 years cohort achieved 1 complete response (CR; 0.59%), 99 PR (58.24%), 60 SD (35.29%), and 10 PD (5.88%); the ≥ 75 years group had 1 CR (1.09%), 35 PR (38.04%), 50 SD (54.35%), and 6 PD (6.52%). ORR in the two groups were 58.82% and 39.13%, respectively, while DCR were 94.12% and 93.48%, respectively (Fig. 2; Table 2).

The efficacy stratified by age

Survival Outcomes

Survival analysis revealed a median overall survival (mOS) of 27.93 months (95% CI: 19.87–34.93) and median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 10.93 months (95% CI: 8.87–20.13) in the ≥ 75 years group; in the < 75 years group, mOS was 29.23 months (95% CI: 26.03–35.07) and mPFS was 11.37 months (95% CI: 10.07–12.90). Compared with the < 75 years group, aged ≥ 75 years did not significantly affect OS (HR = 1.274, 95% CI: 0.982–1.654, P = 0.068) or PFS (HR = 0.907, 95% CI: 0.715–1.151, P = 0.421) (Fig. 3A and B). The median follow-up time for the overall cohort was 21.0 months (range: 1.0–88.5 months). Notably, within the analytic cohort, 66 patients (12.3%) experienced disease progression within the first 3 months of immunotherapy initiation, confirming that the spectrum of treatment responses, including rapid progression, is represented in this real-world study.

Kaplan–Meier curves showing (A) OS of the patients before PSM, B PFS of the patients before PSM, C OS of the patients after PSM, D PFS of the patients after PSM. OS: overall survival; PFS: progression-free survival

Following PSM matching, The mOS in the ≥ 75 years group was 23.23 months (95% CI: 17.20–32.60), with a mPFS of 10.93 months (95% CI: 8.87–20.13); in the < 75 years group, mOS was 26.40 months (95% CI: 20.03–35.97) and mPFS was 10.77 months (95% CI: 9.03–13.90). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of OS (HR = 1.298, 95% CI: 0.951–1.771, P = 0.099) or PFS (HR = 0.874, 95% CI: 0.662–1.154, P = 0.343) (Fig. 3C and D).

Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses and subgroup analyses

In the PSM-adjusted data, univariate analysis revealed that clinical stage IV (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.11–2.19, P = 0.010), presence of bone metastases (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.01–2.00, P = 0.045), pleural metastases (HR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.29–2.75, P = 0.001), and receipt of second-line or higher immunotherapy (HR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.17–2.49, P = 0.006) were associated with increased mortality risk. Multivariate analysis further indicated that pleural metastases (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.01–2.34, P = 0.043) constituted an independent prognostic factor for OS (Table 3). Subgroup analysis revealed that in patients aged ≥ 75 years, ECOG performance status ≥ 2 (HR = 2.61, 95% CI: 1.14–5.93, P = 0.023) and concomitant hypertension (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.01–2.44, P = 0.047) were associated with increased mortality risk; while absence of bone metastases (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.09–2.24, P = 0.016) and no prior radiotherapy (HR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.04–2.00, P = 0.027) were also associated with higher mortality risk (Table 4).

In the univariate analysis of PFS, pleural metastasis (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.18–2.32, P = 0.004) and receipt of second-line or later immunotherapy (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.11–2.18, P = 0.010) were associated with increased risk of disease progression, whereas concomitant coronary heart disease (HR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.51–0.98, P = 0.039) was associated with reduced progression risk. Multivariate analysis revealed that pleural metastases (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.02–2.06, P = 0.036) and immunotherapy at second-line or later (HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.06–2.12, P = 0.021) remained independent risk factors for PFS (Table 5). In subgroup analyses, prior radiotherapy (HR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.09–0.97, P = 0.045) was significantly associated with reduced disease progression risk, whilst other factors showed no apparent effect. Subgroup analysis results are presented in Table 6.

Adverse events

Prior to matching, adverse events occurred in 483 patients (90.28%). There were no significant differences between groups in the incidence of any-grade AEs (P = 0.269) or ≥Grade 3 AEs (P = 0.090) (Table 7). By organ system, the highest grade of adverse events following immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy predominantly occurred within the first three months. The most common were haematological toxicities (54.69%), primarily manifesting as bone marrow suppression, including leukocytopenia, anaemia, and neutropenia. The second most frequent were gastrointestinal events (22.14%), encompassing gastrointestinal reactions and abnormal liver function. followed by the endocrine system (7.29%), the respiratory system (5.73%), the skin (3.91%), and the cardiovascular system (2.08%) (Fig. 4). Among laboratory abnormalities, anaemia was most prevalent, followed by leukocytopenia and thrombocytopenia; elevated blood glucose and alkaline phosphatase levels were also observed (Table S1). Regarding clinical manifestations, bone marrow suppression had the highest incidence (66.20% vs. 58.25%), with other common events including abnormal liver function, pneumonia, and gastrointestinal reactions (Table S2). The distribution of Grade 1–2 and Grade 3–4 AEs across both groups is illustrated in Fig. 5.

Following matching, AEs occurred in 237 patients (90.46%). There was no significant difference between groups in the incidence of AEs of any grade (P = 0.063) or grade 1–2 AEs (P = 0.051). However, the incidence of grade 3 or higher AEs was significantly lower in the ≥ 75 years group (P = 0.011) (Table 7). Organ system distribution remained similar to pre-matching patterns, with haematological (56.78%) and gastrointestinal (20.10%) systems predominating during the first three months, followed by endocrine (8.04%), respiratory (5.03%) systems and skin(3.02%) (Fig. 4). Laboratory abnormalities were most frequently anaemia, followed by leukocytopenia and thrombocytopenia (Table S1 and Table S2). Among clinical manifestations, bone marrow suppression had the highest incidence (72.35% vs. 58.70%); specific grading details are shown in Fig. 5.

We compared the safety profiles of patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy (n = 431) versus immunotherapy monotherapy (n = 104). Results are detailed in Table S3. Patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy had a higher incidence of any‑grade AEs (93.74% vs. 75.96%) and grade 3–4 AEs (30.86% vs. 22.12%). This increased toxicity burden was driven predominantly by chemotherapy‑associated events. Myelosuppression (representing hematological toxicity) was substantially more frequent in the chemoimmunotherapy group (any grade: 72.39% vs. 32.69%; grade 3–4: 20.88% vs. 7.69%). Gastrointestinal reactions were also more common with combination therapy (any grade: 22.51% vs. 11.54%).

The incidence of key immune-related adverse events (irAEs) was comparable between patients aged < 75 years and those aged ≥ 75 years (Table S4). The most common any-grade irAEs were gastrointestinal reactions, rash, and hypothyroidism, with incidence rates closely matched between age groups. Grade ≥ 3 events were infrequent, and pneumonitis was the most common severe irAE.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that among elderly patients with advanced NSCLC receiving immunotherapy, survival outcomes and treatment efficacy did not significantly differ between those aged ≥ 75 years and those < 75 years. Specifically, the median overall survival in the ≥ 75 age group was comparable to that in the < 75 age group, indicating that chronological age alone should not be a decisive factor in treatment selection [12, 20]. Furthermore, in line with previous studies, our findings confirm that elderly patients can generally tolerate immunotherapy with manageable safety profiles [19, 23]. These results suggest that clinicians should consider age as a contextual factor rather than a contraindication in treatment decisions, thereby facilitating equitable access to potentially life-prolonging therapies [24]. Therefore, within the elderly population (≥ 65 years), our data support a treatment approach that does not disproportionately withhold immunotherapy based solely on crossing the 75-year threshold. Collectively, these findings advocate for a more nuanced understanding of the role of age in NSCLC treatment response and emphasize the importance of developing individualized patient care strategies.

The comparative safety analysis revealed that the addition of chemotherapy to immunotherapy significantly increased the risk of adverse events, especially myelosuppression and gastrointestinal reactions. This finding directly accounts for the lower incidence of high‑grade AEs observed in patients aged ≥ 75 years after PSM, as this group received chemoimmunotherapy less frequently. The comparable rates of irAEs between treatment groups further suggest that the safety profile of immune checkpoint inhibitors is not substantially affected by concomitant chemotherapy in this elderly population.

The principal innovation of this study resides in its systematic evaluation of immunotherapy efficacy and safety in elderly NSCLC patients, thereby addressing a significant evidence gap in this demographic. Previous clinical trials often excluded older individuals, resulting in limited data regarding their responses to immunotherapy, particularly within the context of immunosenescence [25, 26]. Our results demonstrate that elderly patients (≥ 65 years), including those aged ≥ 75, can derive meaningful clinical benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, with no statistically significant difference in overall survival observed between the two stratified age groups (≥ 75 vs. <75 years). Comparative analysis between the two elderly cohorts revealed no significant disparity in PFS. Notably, following propensity score matching, disease progression occurred later in the ≥ 75 years group than in the < 75 years cohort, possibly reflecting optimized treatment regimens and care approaches tailored to older patients. This finding not only reinforces the tolerability of immunotherapy in elderly populations but also highlights the necessity of developing personalized therapeutic strategies that account for age-related physiological alterations [27, 28]. Ultimately, this research provides a foundation for promoting more inclusive clinical trial designs and individualized treatment approaches for elderly NSCLC patients, potentially informing therapeutic decisions for this growing demographic and improving clinical outcomes.

This study demonstrates substantial clinical significance by establishing the feasibility of immunotherapy for elderly patients with advanced NSCLC. The findings indicate that chronological age does not significantly influence survival outcomes in this population, suggesting that personalized treatment strategies should be prioritized to optimize therapeutic efficacy and maintain quality of life [29, 30]. Moreover, the robustness of this finding is supported by a sensitivity analysis confirming no significant interaction between age and prior immunotherapy exposure (P-interaction = 0.446 for OS and P-interaction = 0.591 for PFS). Additionally, this research highlights the critical importance of tailoring treatment regimens to the specific physiological and clinical characteristics of elderly patients, as their immunological responses to immunotherapy may substantially differ from those of younger individuals [31, 32].

This study confirms that chronological age itself is not an independent predictor of efficacy or safety in elderly NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy, indicating that this population can derive significant clinical benefit with generally favorable safety profiles [33, 34]. However, advanced age is frequently associated with multiple comorbidities, including cardiovascular diseases and gastrointestinal dysfunction, which can substantially influence treatment decisions and medication tolerability [35]. Notably, elderly patients undergoing immunotherapy show increased susceptibility to immune-related interstitial pneumonia, which is often complicated by concurrent infections and requires complex clinical management [36]. These complications may necessitate treatment interruption or dose adjustment. Furthermore, immunotherapy is commonly administered in combination with chemotherapy, targeted agents, or anti-angiogenic drugs. The cumulative toxicity of multiple therapeutic modalities may elevate both the incidence and severity of adverse reactions, posing substantial challenges for clinical management [37]. Consequently, particular caution is advised when developing immunotherapy regimens for elderly patients. A comprehensive assessment of their systemic status, comorbid conditions, and potential risks associated with combination therapy is essential. Rigorous monitoring of relevant clinical parameters throughout treatment, coupled with proactive prevention and management of adverse events, is crucial for optimizing therapeutic efficacy while ensuring patient safety.

This study aims to provide granular, practice-oriented evidence targeting a specific gap in the current literature. Its novelty lies not in being the first to report on immunotherapy in the elderly, but in its refined approach: First, by implementing an intra-elderly stratification at the 75-year threshold, it moves beyond the common broad definition to address the clinically relevant question of differential outcomes between “younger-old” and “older-old” patients. Second, it deliberately focuses on the under-represented cohort aged ≥ 75 years, generating much-needed real-world data for this trial-excluded population. Finally, the comprehensive, organ system-based safety profiling offers nuanced insights for managing the very elderly. Thus, our work provides incremental but vital evidence to inform personalized strategies in geriatric oncology.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, which may compromise the generalizability of the findings [38]. Future research should prioritize prospective studies to further investigate the long-term benefits of immunotherapy in this population and identify predictive biomarkers of treatment response to optimize therapeutic efficacy [39].

The principal limitation of this study stems from its retrospective design, which carries an inherent risk of selection bias and limits the control over data completeness and standardization, specifically the systematic assessment of PD-L1 expression [40]. Additionally, the limited sample size constrains the generalizability of the findings to the broader patient population [41] and, particularly in the ≥ 75-year subgroup, may be insufficient to detect effects within rare subgroups or of modest magnitude. Moreover, as a single-center investigation, the applicability of these findings to other institutions with divergent patient demographics and treatment protocols may be limited [42]. The relatively short follow-up duration further impedes comprehensive evaluation of long-term outcomes and safety profiles [43]. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter clinical trials with extended follow-up periods to strengthen the robustness and external validity of the results [44]. Furthermore, the observed association between the absence of prior radiotherapy and poorer survival likely reflects unmeasured confounding, such as more aggressive disease biology or patient frailty at baseline, rather than a direct causal effect of omitting radiotherapy [45, 46]. This underscores the challenge of interpreting treatment history variables in retrospective analyses.

Therefore, incorporating direct comparisons with younger patient cohorts (< 65 years) is essential to better elucidate the specific impact of biological age on immunotherapy outcomes. Together, such efforts will facilitate a more thorough investigation of optimal, age-informed treatment strategies across the entire patient spectrum.

Conclusion

This real-world study provides stratified evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy for elderly patients (≥ 65 years) with advanced NSCLC.

Key findings indicate that chronological age, even at the 75-year threshold, was not a significant determinant of survival outcomes or treatment safety. Patients aged ≥ 75 years derived clinical benefit comparable to those aged < 75 years, with a manageable toxicity profile supporting the feasibility of immunotherapy in the very old.

Therefore, comprehensive geriatric assessment and performance status, rather than chronological age alone, should guide personalized treatment decisions for elderly patients with advanced NSCLC.
