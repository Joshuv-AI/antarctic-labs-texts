NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 antagonistically regulate NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8 mediating diverse GA biosyntheses and tiny-plant in Narcissus tazetta
Authors: Zhuoyu Wu, Xi Wu, Zheng Gu, Jingwen Yang, Yajue Chen, Yun Xu, Ruizhe Feng, Yu Zhang, Yujun Ren, Ying Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMiniaturization and multiple branching are characteristic features of horticultural plants. Gibberellins participate in regulating the size and branching of plant architecture. Gibberellin 3β hydroxylase (GA3ox) and gibberellin 2β hydroxylase (GA2ox) are a pair of key enzymes for the homeostasis of active GA. However, the transcriptional regulation of GA3ox and GA2ox and the synthesis of active GAs remain unknown. Here the roles of narcissus NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 in regulating the biosynthesis of diverse active GAs were addressed. Overexpression of NtMYB1 in Arabidopsis and Narcissus shows a tiny-type phenotype. The NtMYB1 co-expression network reveals that NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 is closely related to NtKS1, NtGA20ox, NtGA2ox, and NtGA3ox in the GA biosynthetic pathway of narcissus. NtMYB1 can interact with NtbZIP53. Overexpression of NtMYB1 and NtMYB1/NtbZIP53 in transgenic narcissus upregulated the expression of upstream genes NtKS1 and NtGA20ox in the GA biosynthesis pathway; however, NtMYB1 and/or NtbZIP53 directly targeted the promoters of NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8 and antagonistically controlled NtGA3ox2 and NtGA3ox8 transcription, respectively, and both decreased GA1 accumulation. NtMYB1 recruiting NtbZIP53 enhanced the repression of NtGA3ox2 and the activation of NtGA2ox8, reducing the GA1/GA3 ratio and plant size in narcissus. This provides a conceptual basis for controlling the architecture of Chinese narcissus.

Introduction

Horticultural plant architecture holds significant ecological and economic value. Miniaturization and multiple branching are characteristic features. Narcissus tazetta var. chinensis cv. ‘Jinzhanyintai’ belongs to the Amaryllidaceae family and is a perennial bulbous plant widely cultivated in East Asia and China. It is one of the 10 most renowned traditional flowers in China, renowned for its beautiful and highly ornamental flowers with a rich fragrance. However, its simplistic plant architecture limits its commercial appeal.

Gibberellins (GAs) are a class of tetracyclic diterpenoid phytohormones that regulate multiple aspects of plant development. Although large numbers of different GA species have been identified, only a few, such as GA1, GA3, GA4, and GA7, possess biological activity [1, 2]. The other forms of GA present in plants are either precursors of the bioactive forms or deactivated metabolites. GA1 and GA4 are the two major active GAs in most plant species, although GA3 has also been detected in plants. The bioactivity of GA4 is significantly higher than that of GA1 in both Arabidopsis and rice, which is presumably attributable to their differences in binding affinity to the GA receptor [3–6]. Different plant species have diverse ratios of GA1, GA4, and GA3 in various tissues. The most prominent functions of GAs include promoting stem elongation, leaf expansion, flowering, fruit development, and seed germination, and influencing plant architecture [7, 8]. Plants deficient in GA biosynthesis or the GA signaling pathway display characteristic phenotypes, such as dwarfism, small dark-green leaves, prolonged germination, root growth retardation, suppression of flowering, reduced seed production, and male sterility [9, 10].

The biosynthesis of GAs originates from a branch of the diterpenoid pathway. It can be divided into three major stages based on the localization and nature of the enzymes involved [11, 12]. The formation of GAs begins with the cyclization of geranyldiphosphate in the plastid. Subsequently, GA12-aldehyde is formed by the action of cytochrome P450-dependent mono-oxygenases on the exterior of the endoplasmic reticulum. GA12-aldehyde is the first GA produced and serves as the precursor of all other GA species. They are formed in the cytosol in a complex metabolic network that varies among species and developmental stages, and in a tissue-specific manner. The later steps of the pathway are catalysed by soluble 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases. This group comprises enzymes either involved in the formation of the bioactive GA1 and/or GA4 by GA20-oxidases and GA3-oxidases or involved in their deactivation by GA2-oxidases. Both GA3-oxidase and GA2-oxidase have been regarded as important regulators of the biosynthetic pathway in biochemical and molecular studies [11–13]. GA3ox can oxidize non-bioactive GA12 and GA53 to the bioactive GA3 and GA1. In contrast, GA2ox can oxidize the bioactive GA4 to non-bioactive GA8 in rice [2, 7].

It has been reported that the transcription of GA biosynthesis genes GA3ox1/2 and GA20ox2 and the GA metabolic gene GA2ox is affected by DELLA proteins, which are members of the GRAS family in Arabidopsis [14], and accumulate in the nucleus [15, 16]. DELLA proteins activate the expression of AtGA20ox2 and AtGA3ox1 genes, but there has been no direct evidence showing that DELLA proteins bind to the AtGA20ox2 and AtGA3ox1 promoter. Overexpression of YAB1 resulted in a semi-dwarf phenotype with decreased OsGA3ox2 transcript levels, whereas co-suppression of YAB1 in transgenic plants induced expression of OsGA3ox2. YAB1 binds to the GA-responsive element (GARE) located in the GA3ox2 promoter, indicating that YAB1 regulates OsGA3ox2 directly [17]. The GARE sequence does not exist in the OsGA20ox2 promoter. Later, an RSG binding sequence (rbe) in the promoter region of NtGA20ox1 was identified. RSG, a bZIP transcription factor, binds to this sequence of the NtGA20ox1 promoter in vivo in response to a decrease in GA levels, and the binding is abolished by exogenous GA treatments [18, 19]. Therefore, RSG not only controls the expression of NtKS [20], but also the feedback regulation of NtGA20ox1 [19]. Further studies in transgenic tobacco plants indicated that SbMYB2 and SbMYB7 might regulate secondary biosynthesis through the GA pathway [21]. In previous research, based on comparative transcriptomic and metabolite analysis during narcissus development, the co-expression network of transcription factor and structural genes showed that NtMYB1, NtbZIP, and NtGRAS co-expressed with DXR, NCED, and PDS genes during narcissus development [52, 53]. Overexpressing NtMYB1 increased the contents of ABA, zeatin,□β-carotene, and zeaxanthin, but reduced the GA1 content and increased the GA3 content, resulting in an early petal senescence phenotype in narcissus and a mini-type plant in Arabidopsis overexpressing NtMYB1 [52]. However, how the mechanisms of MYB1, bZIP, and GRAS affect the biosynthesis of the diverse GAs in various plants is unclear, particularly in narcissus plants.

In this study, we identified both NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 genes from Narcissus tazetta. We further generated oeNtMYB1, oeNtbZIP53, and both oeNtMYB1 and oeNtbZIP53 in narcissus and transgenic Arabidopsis plants. Meanwhile, based on weighted gene co-expression network (WGCNA) analysis, several candidate targeted genes were selected, the promoter sequences of NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8 were isolated by using a genome walking assay, and then we biochemically investigated the mechanism of NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 in regulating the production of diverse GAs in narcissus. This provides a fundamental basis for controlling narcissus ‘mini’ architecture breeding.

Results

Overexpressing NtMYB1 in Arabidopsis and narcissus produced a GA-deficient-like dwarfism plant phenotype

In a previous report, the overexpression of NtMYB1 in Arabidopsis and narcissus manifested an accelerating leaf and petal senescence phenotype [52]. Additionally, a tiny plant type including a short root, hypocotyl, petiole, and rosette area and a late-flowering plant phenotype as well as higher chlorophyll b content were observed in Arabidopsis plants overexpressing NtMYB1 (Supplementary Material Fig. S1). Further systematic phenotyping in narcissus revealed a tiny-bulb phenotype in oeNtMYB1-GFP transgenic narcissus plants in the T1 generation compared with the wild-type (WT) and oeGFP transgenic narcissus plant (Fig. 1A). Considering that the tiny phenotype of oeNtMYB1 transgenic lines might stem from reduced GA biosynthesis, an exogenous GA treatment would rescue the tiny phenotype induced by NtMYB1 overexpression. To test this hypothesis, we administered exogenous GA3 to transgenic Arabidopsis and narcissus bulbs, which promptly increased the endogenous GA1 and GA3 contents of treated leaves compared with the mock (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Material Fig. S2). The expression level of the GA receptor gene NtGID1 was promoted in the WT and oeGFP alone narcissus bulb after GA3 treatment; however, overexpressing NtMYB1 reduced the transcript level of NtGID1 to the same level, and even exogenous application of GA3 did not alter the NtGID1 expression level (Fig. 1C). The tiny-plant phenotype of the oeNtMYB1 narcissus bulb and Arabidopsis partially recovered after 4 weeks of growth (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Material Fig. S2), indicating that overexpression of NtMYB1 reduced the GA1 level, resulting in a mini-bulb, GA-deficient like dwarfism plant phenotype, which can be partially rescued by exogenous GA3 application.

Overexpression of NtMYB1 alters diverse gibberellin contents in narcissus

The influence of NtMYB1 overexpression on the phenotype was hypothesized that NtMYB1 might represses the GA biosynthesis pathway. To test this, we measured the contents of diverse GAs, including GA12, GA53, GA20, GA5, GA1, GA3, and GA4, in the control and transgenic lines. The GA1 content of the transgenic lines was significantly lower than that of control plants (Fig. 2), with the maximum repression in plant size observed in transgenic narcissus and Arabidopsis (Fig. 1; Supplementary Material Fig. S2). As expected, the degree of reduction in GA1 content in narcissus followed the degree of overexpression of NtMYB1 (Fig. 2A). However, the content of GA3 in different transgenic lines was significantly increased, even though the level of GA3 was much lower than the level of GA1 in narcissus leaves, while GA4 was undetectable in leaves (Fig. 2B and C). Similarly, the contents of GA12 and GA53 were also augmented in the transgenic narcissus lines overexpressing NtMYB1 (Fig. 2B and C). Thus, it seems that the tiny phenotype caused by overexpressing NtMYB1 is attributable to the decline in the GA1/GA3 ratio in narcissus leaves (Fig. 2D).

NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 co-expressed with NtGA3ox and NtGA2ox in Narcissus tazetta

Referencing the GA biosynthesis pathway in Arabidopsis and rice, GA biosynthetic pathway-related enzyme genes, including CPS, KS1, KO, KAO, GA3ox, GA20ox, and GA2ox, were selected from Arabidopsis (Fig. 3A; KEGG pathway). The previous transcriptome dataset (NBCI, PRJNA750844, https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov) of N. tazetta during flower development was employed for alignment analysis [22, 23] and produced a series of GA biosynthetic pathway-related enzyme genes (195 genes) in narcissus (Supplementary Material Dataset S1), and a putative GA biosynthesis pathway in narcissus was established (Fig. 3B). Then, the co-expression network of the transcription factors with GA enzyme genes from the narcissus transcriptomic dataset was analysed by WGCNA, a common method for constructing gene co-expression networks [23, 24]. According to the thresholds (|GS| > =0.2;|MM| ≥ 0.8, P-values <0.05), six modules of transcription factors were produced during narcissus development (Supplementary Material Fig. S3). We analysed the co-expression networks of all transcription factors from these six modules and all the selected 195 genes related to GA metabolism by WGCNA. Several transcription factors, including GRAS, C2H2, bZIP, WRKY, MYB1, ARF, TUB, HB, and GARP-G2, are involved in the GA metabolism pathway at different development stages of N. tazetta (Fig. 3C). Among them, NtbZIP (c145326) is 52.08% homologous to AtbZIP53, which is designated as NtbZIP53 (Supplementary Material Fig. S4). NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 were strongly related to NtGA20ox (c110461), NtGA3ox2(c62538), and NtGA2ox8 (c109460) enzymes. The relative intensity is 0.79 (Supplementary Material Fig. S5), where GA20ox and GA3ox are key restrictive enzymes for non-active GA12 or GA53 transferring to active species GA1, GA4, and GA3 in Arabidopsis and rice, while GA2ox is a key enzyme for the deactivation of active GAs [7]. Thus, both NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 might be implicated in the regulation of NtGA20ox1, NtGA3ox1/2, and NtGA2ox8 expression in narcissus.

NtMYB1 interacts with NtbZIP53 in the nucleus

To test the relationship among the three transcription factors NtMYB1, NtbZIP53, and NtGRASthat belong to the same family as DELLA proteins, the interaction among three candidate proteins, NtbZIP53, NtGRAS, and NtMYB1, was examined by yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. The Y2H results indicated that yeast strain AH109 co-transformed with plasmids AD-NtbZIP53 and BD-NtMYB1 grew well in medium SD minus amino acids Leu, Ade, Trp, and His, while colonies co-transformed with an AD-NtMYB1 with BD-NtGRAS and AD vector with BD-NtMYB1 did not grow infour amino acids deficient medium (SD minus amino acids Leu, Ade, Trp, and His), suggesting that NtMYB1 can interact with NtbZIP53 in yeast cells (Fig. 4A); however NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 did not interact with NtGRAS (Supplementary Material Fig. S6). The localization of NtbZIP53 was further detected in the nucleus of protoplasts of narcissus leaf (Fig. 4B). Next, we confirmed the interaction of NtbZIP53 and NtMYB1 in epidermal cells of tobacco leaf by a BiFC assay. When plasmids NtMYB1-YFPc and NtbZIP53-YFPn were co-injected into epidermal cells of tobacco leaves, a YFP fluorescence signal emerged in the nucleus (Fig. 4C). However, when NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 was co-transformed with empty negative control, the YFP fluorescence signal did not appear in the cell (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, pulldown of co-transformed tobacco leaf with oeNtMYB1-HA, oeNtbZIP-GFP plasmids using GFP antibody was immunodetected with antibody against HA. This result showed that NtMYB1-HA can be detected in the immunoprecipitate (IP) (Fig. 4D). This demonstrated that NtMYB1 can physically interact with NtbZIP53 (Fig. 4D).

NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 antagonistically regulated NtGA3ox1/2 and NtGA2ox8 expression

Next, to explore the regulatory roles of both NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 in the GA biosynthetic pathway, initially we generated and identified overexpression of NtbZIP53 and overexpression of both NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 in narcissus lines and Arabidopsis atbzip53 mutant lines (Supplementary Material Figs S7 and S8). Based on the intensity of the relation rate (relative rate >0.79) of the co-expression network, we selected NtKS1, NtGA3ox1, NtGA3ox2, NtGA20ox, NtGA2ox2, and NtGID1 genes of the GA biosynthetic pathway in narcissus tissue (Fig. 3C) as candidates to examine their expression levels in transgenic narcissus overexpressing NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53. Quantitative RT–PCR analysis revealed significant alterations in the expression levels of NtKS1, NtGA3ox1/2, NtGA2ox2, and NtGID1 with various fold changes. The expression of NtKS1 increased by 1.5- to 2-fold in the oeNtMYB1 and oeNtbZIP53 narcissus and by 3-fold in oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 narcissus compared with WT (Fig. 5A); the expression of NtGA20ox increased by 20-fold in oeNtMYB1 and oeNtbZIP53 and by 25-fold in oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 narcissus relative to WT (Fig. 5B); the expression of NtGA3ox2, NtGA2ox2, and NtGID1 was down-regulated by 4-fold in oeNtMYB1 and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53, but was upregulated by 3-fold in the oeNtbZIP53, in comparison with the WT. In contrast, the expression of NtGA3ox1 increased by 20-fold in oeNtMYB1 and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53, as well as in atbzip53 mutant, yet remained unaltered in oeNtbZIP53, compared with the WT (Fig. 5C–F). These results suggested that overexpression of both NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 in narcissus has the potential to modify the expression of genes such as NtKS1- and NtGA20ox1 involved upstream in the GA biosynthesis pathway; however, NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 antagonistically affected the expression of NtGA3ox1 and NtGA3ox2, respectively, as well as NtGA2ox2. NtMYB1 predominantly repressed the expression of NtGA3ox2 but activated that of NtGA2ox2 and NtGA30x1, while oeNtbZIP53 had no effect on that of NtGA3ox1. In addition, the overexpression of NtbZIP53 in Arabidopsis knockout line atbzip53 could rescue the phenotype of atbzip53 (large size of plant) so that it was like WT plants (Fig. 5G). This suggests that NtbZIP53 has conserved function with respect to AtbZIP53, being involved in the regulation of plant size.

Next, we functionally characterized the transcriptional regulatory activity of NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 on the targeted genes in narcissus. For this purpose, we conducted a genome walking assay to clone the promoters of NtGA2ox2, NtGA2ox8, NtGA3ox1, and NtGA3ox2 (downstream genes of NtMYB1); however, the promoter sequence of NtGA3ox1 and NtGA2ox2 could not successfully be cloned; a short promoter of NtGA2ox2 was cloned and it has no function. Alternatively, NtGA2ox8 is also co-expressed with NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 (Fig. 5C). The promoter sequences were submitted to the NCBI database and analysed using the PlantCARE program (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [25]. The analysis identified three MYB binding elements located −905, −1161 and − 1310 bp upstream of the NtGA2ox8 coding sequence and two MYB binding elements −489 and − 291 bp upstream of NtGA3ox2 (Supplementary Material Fig. S9). To determine whether NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 or both directly bind to the promoters of NtGA3ox2 or NtGA2ox8 and regulate their transcription, we carried out a dual-luciferase reporter assay to measure the binding activity of NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 or both with the promoters of NtGA3ox2 or NtGA2ox8 in tobacco leaf cells injected with the region containing many MYB motifs (CAACAG or GTTGTC) and the G-box (Fig. 6A and D). As illustrated in Fig. 6B and E, NtMYB1 repressed luciferase gene expression driven by NtGA3ox2 or AtGA2ox8 promoter; in contrast, NtbZIP53 activated luciferase gene expression driven by NtGA3ox2 or AtGA2ox8 promoter in fluorescence imaging in tobacco leaf. Furthermore, luciferase activity was assayed in vivo in the narcissus leaf protoplast system. Remarkably, the luciferase activity in narcissus protoplasts co-transfected with oeNtMYB1 and NtGA3ox2 promoter was 4-fold lower than that in cells transfected with oeNtMYB1 and empty vector (negative control), while the luciferase activity in cells co-transfected with oeNtMYB1 and the NtGA2ox8 promoter was 3-fold lower than that in cells transfected with oeNtMYB1 and empty vector (pGreen, T7pro:LUC), used as negative control (Fig. 6C and F). In contrast, the luciferase activity in narcissus protoplasts co-transfected with oeNtbZIP53 and NtGA3ox2 promoter was 2-fold higher than that in cells transfected with oeNtbZIP53 and empty vector, while the luciferase activity in cells co-transfected with oeNtbZIP53 and the NtGA2ox8 promoter was 8-fold higher than that in cells transfected with oeNtbZIP53 and empty vector (Fig. 6E). Surprisingly, the luciferase activity in narcissus protoplast cells co-transfected with oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 and NtGA2ox8 promoter was 7-fold higher than that in cells transfected with oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 and empty vector. However, the luciferase activity in cells co-transfected with oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 and the NtGA3ox2 promoter was 2-fold lower than that in cells transfected with oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 and empty vector (Fig. 6C and F). These data demonstrated that NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 or both could specifically bind to the promoters of NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8, and that NtMYB1 repressed their expression and NtbZIP53 activated their expression, respectively. NtMYB1 interacting with NtbZIP53 enhanced the activation of NtbZIP53 on NtGA2ox8 and the repression of NtMYB1 on NtGA3ox2.

Next, the recombinant NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 proteins expressed in Escherichia coli were extracted and purifed with GST beads (Supplementary Material Fig. S10). The results of the EMSA assay showed that complex bands appeared when the reconbinant NtMYB1, NtbZIP53, or NtMYB1 plus NtBZIP53 (1:1) proteins incubated with the probes for the regions containing the binding sequence (probe 1: ACGTGT; probe 2:TAACGT), the reverse sequence (1r and 2r), and the mutation sequence (1m or 2m) of NtGA3ox2 or NtGA2ox8 promoters were labeled with Cy5; the intensity of complex bands (laddering bands) in the lane with NtMYB1 plus NtBZIP53 proteins was stronger than with NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 alone, while the intensity of the complex bands was decreased when 30-fold non-labeled probes (cold competitor) were added before incubation, and when mutant sequence probes were used. This indicates that NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 proteins specifically bound to the region of promoters of NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8, and the interaction of NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 enhanced their binding affinity to the target promoters.

NtMYB1 interacting with NtbZIP53 promotes GA3 accumulation and reduces GA1 content in narcissus

The soluble 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases (GA3ox) have been reported to be involved in the formation of the bioactive GA1, GA3, and/or GA4. Both GA3-oxidase and GA2-oxidase have been considered as important regulators of the biosynthetic pathway in Arabidopsis [11–13]. Next, transgenic narcissus plants with oeNtbZIP53 and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 were generated (Supplementary Material Fig. S8). Overexpression of NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 or oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 in narcissus led to a tiny-bulb and less branching bulb phenotype compared with WT (Fig. 7A and B). The phenotypes of Arabidopsis overexpressing NtMYB1, NtbZIP53, and NtMYB1/NtbZIP53 showed a similar tiny plant, but with a more branching phenotype (Supplementary Material Fig. S11). Furthermore, in the oeNtMYB1, oeNtbZIP53, and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 transgenic narcissus and Arabidopsis, the contents of various GA species, including GA1, GA3, GA20, and GA5, were measured, and the results showed that the content of GA1 was significantly reduced in both oeNtMYB1 and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 transgenic narcissus tissue, while the content of GA3 was significantly increased in both oeNtMYB1 and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 transgenic narcissus tissue and Arabidopsis leaves (Supplementary Material Fig. S11). The amounts of GA20 and GA5 in narcissus were increased in both oeNtbZIP53 and oeNtMYB1/bZIP53 narcissus tissue (Fig. 7C and D). Thus, it is supposed that NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 or both can specifically bind to the promoters of NtGA2ox8 and NtGA3ox2, that NtMYB1 repressed their expression and NtbZIP53 activated their expression; oeNtMYB1 recruiting NtbZIP53 enhanced the activation of NtbZIP53 on NtGA2ox8, resulting in conversion of GA1 to the non-active species GA8 and enhancing the repression of NtMYB1 on NtGA3ox2, resulting in GA20 accumulation, then conversion of GA5 to GA3.

Discussion

The GA catabolic gene GA2ox1 and the GA biosynthetic gene OsGA3ox2 were expressed with tissue and cell-specific expression patterns of biosynthesis or deactivating enzymes, which can be used for the fine tuning of plant growth [26]. Therefore, as changes in the contents of diverse GAs cause a mix of positive and negative effects on traits of agronomic importance (e.g. reduced plant size—a mini architecture, reduced seed yield, promoting flowering), it is of utmost importance to determine how one GA effect could be uncoupled from another [7]. In this study, we identified an NtMYB1 and an NtbZIP53 gene in narcissus. Overexpression of NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 in transgenic lines of Arabidopsis and narcissus led to upregulation of the gene expression of upstream biosynthetic enzymes of the GA biosynthesis pathway, while oeNtMYB1 or oeNtbZIP53 antagonistically controlled GA3ox2 and GA2ox8 transcription, and NtMYB1 recruiting NtbZIP53 enhanced the repression of GA3ox2 by NtMYB1 and the activation of GA2ox8 by NtbZIP53, resulting in reduced active GA1 content and increased GA3 content, leading to a tiny plant type. This study suggested the potential role of NtMYB1 as a repressor of GA3ox2 to inhibit GA1 biosynthesis and of NtbZIP53 as an activator of GA2ox8 for conversion of active GA1 to inactive GA8. NtMYB1 interaction with NtbZIP53 significantly reduced GA1 biosynthesis and promoted GA3 biosynthesis in narcissus and Arabidopsis (Fig. 7E).

NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 co-suppress the GA1/GA3 ratio, leading to a tiny plant type in both Arabidopsis and Narcissus

The soluble 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, GA3oxs, have been reported to be involved in the formation of bioactive GA1, GA3, and/or GA4. Both GA3-oxidase and GA2-oxidase have been considered to be important regulators of the biosynthetic pathway in biochemical and molecular studies in Arabidopsis [11–13]. In transgenic oeNtMYB1 Arabidopsis and narcissus plants, the ratio of GA1 to GA3 was reduced, while with NtMYB1 recruitment of NtbZIP53 the ratio of GA1 to GA3 was significantly reduced. Although the amount of GA3 in leaves is much lower than that of GA1, overexpressing oeNtMYB1 and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 increased the amount of GA3, and the tiny phenotype occurred in oeNtMYB1, oeNtbZIP53, and oeNtMYB1/NtbZIP53 Arabidopsis and narcissus. It seems that the GA1/GA3 ratio is essential for controlling plant rosette size. It has reported that in Arabidopsis GA4 and GA1 accumulate in growing tissue, flower buds, flowers, and the early developing silique, and in mid-seed development [27–29]. In this study, GA4 was undetectable in Arabidopsis and narcissus leaves, but could be detected in the roots of narcissus, but the GA4 level did not alter in various overexpression lines, suggesting that NtGA3ox2 might not function from GA9 to GA4 (Fig. 4B); exogenous application of GA3 did not recover the short root phenotype of oeNtMYB1 Arabidopsis; and GA3 favors stem elongation through cell growth regulation and in response to environmental stress and accumulates in the growing stem. GA3 application promotes the transcription of xyloglucan endotransglycosylase (XET), α- and β-expansins [30] and reduces the expression of PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) under a short-day photoperiod [51]. In blueberry, the inflorescence number and vegetative growth increased with GA3 treatment [31]. However, in Matthiola incana, GA induced stem elongation but no effect on flowering was observed [32]. These results highlight diverse aspects of GAs species, as the species-specific responses and the variability found in some cases can discourage their application. In both Arabidopsis and narcissus, NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 seem to have conserved function for plant size, though not for branching. NtMYB1 seems to dominantly inhibit GA1 biosynthesis via NtGA3ox2 compared with NtbZIP53, and activated GA3 biosynthesis via redirection of accumulated precursors towards GA3 or NtGA3ox1, indirectly affected by NtbZIP53. The results also do not exclude NtbZIP53 activating NtGA2ox8, resulting in feedback regulation, which needs further biochemical evidence.

NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 control expression of various targeted genes such as NtGA3ox or NtGA2ox or NtNCED1/2 at the transcriptional level in a developmental-dependent manner

A link between pigment metabolites and hormones can be established due to their biochemical origin and similar biological roles. Three branches of the methylerythritol phosphate pathway involved in biosynthesis of carotenoids (also ABA), biosynthesis of the phytol side chain of chlorophylls, and diterpenoid biosynthesis have been identified in many plants [33–37]. In this case, Geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) is not only used for carotenoid biosynthesis, but also serves as an immediate substrate for biosynthesis of the phytol side chain of chlorophylls and of diterpenoids such as gibberellins. Allocation of GGPP among different downstream metabolic branches largely determines the biosynthetic capability of each branch [33]. This can explain why, in Arabidopsis overexpressing NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53, high levels of carotenoids and chlorophyll, as well as a low content of GA1, were detected (Figs 1 and 7) in the seedling stage, suggesting the presence of competition of carotenoids or the phytol side chain of chlorophylls and terpene backbone biosynthetic branches for GGPP [23]. In a previous study, we showed that NtMYB1 directly targets the MYB motif region of NtNCED1 and NtNCED2 promoters and activates their transcription [52]. NCED protein is a key rate-limiting enzyme for the conversion of violaxanthin to xanthoxin, which is responsible for abscisic acid biosynthesis [38, 39]. Collectively, an NtMYB1–NtNCED1/2 module plays a crucial role in controlling ABA biosynthesis and the accumulation of β-carotene and ABA. Strikingly, NtMYB1 overexpression led to a decrease in the level of GA1 and an increase in chlorophyll in 3-week-old seedlings (Supplementary Material Fig. S1), while causing tepal senescence and leaf yellowing in both narcissus and Arabidopsis after the bolting stage (8 weeks old) [52]. This may be because NtMYB1 or NtbZIP53 targets and represses or activates antagonistically the transcription of NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8, respectively, at an early developmental stage. High or low transcript levels of NtGA2ox8 or NtGA3ox2 are shown in a transcriptome dataset (Supplementary Material Fig. S12). A second possibility is that currently a small amount of evidence has shown that the promoter regions of NCED and GA3ox or GA2ox are modified by epigenetic marks during leaf senescence, resulting in alteration of the metabolic pathway, in a developmental-dependent manner. For example, the histone acetylation of the NCED gene by HDA19 contributes to positive regulation of ABA synthesis in Arabidopsis and by HDA15 in rose petal senescence [40–42]. Several GA2ox enzymes orchestrate the catabolism of GAs, in which GA2 oxidases (GA2oxs) were notably regulated epigenetically by HDACs in Arabidopsis root [43, 44]. Furthermore, NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 gene bodies contain the EAR motif and ZIP motif, respectively. The EAR motif has the potential to be involved in epigenetic (histone acetylation) regulation of transcriptional repression. bZIP has a consensus binding sequence of 5′-TTG-CG-CAA-3′, with a central CpG dinucleotide flanked by two CpA sites. Methylation of the Cp(G/A) sites produces a DNA motif, with every pyrimidine having a methyl group at the ring carbon C5 position of thymine or 5mC [45, 46]. These scientific insights into whether the epigenetic modification/regulation of key enzymes regulates the metabolic flux direction of the ABA biosynthetic pathway and the GA biosynthetic pathway in narcissus to regulate the production of various GA species will be challenged in future studies.

Conclusions

Overexpression of NtMYB1/NtbZIP53 transgenic lines in Arabidopsis and narcissus upregulated the gene expression of upstream biosynthetic enzymes of the GA biosynthesis pathway, while oeNtMYB1 or oeNtbZIP53 antagonistically controlled GA3ox2 and GA2ox8 transcription. NtMYB1 recruitment of NtbZIP53 enhanced the repression of GA3ox2 by NtMYB1 and the activation of GA2ox8 by NtbZIP53, resulting in reduced active GA1 content and increased GA3 content, leading to a tiny plant type. This provides a conceptual basis for controlling the architecture of Chinese narcissus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Narcissus tazetta cv. ‘Jinzhanyintai’ was purchased from a local farmer (Zhangzhou, Fujian, China). After the old bulb scales and roots were removed, cleaned bulbs were sand-cultured in a greenhouse set at 23°C with constant illumination (16 h light/8 h dark) [22]. Narcissus tepals (combined organ of petal and calyx) and corona were first collected at the fifth day (samples T1 and C1) when sheathed leaves emerged from the bulbs, and then collected at three other stages (samples T2–T4 and C2–C4) as the umbel in the spathe developed. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. This material was used to produce the transcriptome sequencing data of Yang et al. [23] and the NtMYB1 and NtbZIP53 expression profile.

Transformation and treatment of narcissus plants

Transgenic bulbs of N. tazetta were generated with a stable genetic transformation system for Narcissus callus (patent CN202210162166.8). In short, Narcissus ovaries in the S0–S1 period were used as explants to induce callus after disinfection, and Agrobacterium EHA105 carrying the NtMYB1or NtbZIP53 overexpression vector (35Spro:NtMYB1-GFP-GUS or 35Spro:NtbZIP53-GFP-GUS or both) was used to transform narcissus callus following 3 d of co-cultivation. After 27 d of screening in a medium containing 25 μg ml−1 kanamycin, the transformed callus was used for material identification. The positive callus was continually induced to generate transgenic plants. The T1 generation plants were used to collect bulb and root material.

Arabidopsis (Columbia) was transformed with an NtbZIP53-overexpressing plasmid (35Spro:NtbZIP53-GFP-GUS) by using a floral dip assay. Six-week-old oeNtMYB1 Arabidopsis plants and T1 transformed narcissus bulbs were treated with 100 μM GA3 (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for further assays and T1 transformed narcissus bulbs were treated by adding 100 μM GA3 to the medium.

Tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) plants were cultivated in a long-day climate chamber. The long-day cultivation conditions were set as follows: a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark, a constant temperature of 23°C, and a humidity of 60%.

Genome walking assay

Genomic DNA was isolated from leaves of N. tazetta at the S2 stage. The 5′ upstream sequences of the NtGA3ox2 and NtGA2ox8 coding sequences were cloned using Takara’s Genome Walking Kit (cat. no. 6108), and the primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Material Table S1.

Plasmid construction

The oeNtMYB1 plasmid was provided by previous work and the oeNtbZIP53 plasmid was constructed in the same way as described [52]. Briefly, pK7FWG2 vector was used as the backbone to construct the plasmids 35Spro:NtMYB1-GFP-GUS and 35Spro:NtbZIP53-GFP-GUS. Plasmid pCBIM-GFP-GUS (35Spro:GFP) was used as a positive control in narcissus protoplast transformation experiments. pK7FWG2 (35Spro:GFP) or modified pCAMBIA3301 maize polyubiquitin promoter (ZmUbipro:GFP) instead of Arabidopsis ACT2 promoter and referred to as ACT2pro:GFP was used to construct the overexpression vectors in a dual-luciferase assay in tobacco leaf. The dual luciferase vector under the control of the T7 promoter was constructed using pGreen (T7pro:LUC). In the dual-luciferase reporter system, the Renilla luciferase gene (REN) under the control of the NOS promoter was used as an internal control.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay

The YFPc-NtMYB1, YFPn-NtbZIP53, YFPn-NtGRAS, YFPn-NtMYB1, YFPc-NtbZIP53, and YFPc-NtGRAS plasmids were extracted using the EndoFree Plasmid Midi Kit (CW2105), and were then co-transformed into epidermal cells of tobacco leaf mesophyll and fluorescence was observed under a laser confocal microscope (Leica SP8) after 16 h. Co-transfection of YFPc and YFPn empty vector was used as the negative control.

Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting assays

The combination of constructs expressing the corresponding MYB1-HA-tagged proteins and NtbZIP53-GFP tagged proteins were co-transformed into narcissus protoplasts and co-injected into tobacco leaf. After incubation for 16 h, the transfected protoplasts were harvested for co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunoblotting assays as described previously [47]. The HA-tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated with anti-GFP antibody-coupled beads (TransGen, HT801, 1:1000 dilution). The immunoprecipitated proteins were then separated by 13.5% SDS–PAGE, blotted and analysed by immunoblotting with anti-HA (anti-HA affinity matrix, Roche) and anti-HA-peroxidase (Roche, 3F10, 1:500 dilution) antibodies.

Measurement of various gibberellins

Leaves from Arabidopsis or bulb and root from T1 plants of N. tazetta, transformed bulbs, and T1 transgenic Arabidopsis plants were collected, and the contents of pigments and hormones were measured by HPLC [53]. Briefly, ~100 mg of freeze-dried bulb and roots of narcissus or Arabidopsis leaves were homogenized with 1 ml of a methanol–dimethyl sulfoxide mix (1:1, v/v) at 40°C for 30 min with agitation (190 rpm) in darkness. A final volume of 10 μl of homogenate was used for injection into the HPLC instrument. The following reagents were purchased: standards GA12, GA53, GA20, GA5, GA3, GA4, and GA1 from Sigma– Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA). All standards were prepared with methanol–dimethyl sulfoxide (1:1, v/v) and stored at −20°C before use.

Dual-luciferase assay

The dual-luciferase experiment followed the method of Rodriguez-Concepción et al. [48], with slight changes. The constructed ACT2pro:NtMYB1-GFP and ACT2pro:NtbZIP53-mCherry overexpression vector (as effector), empty: LUC, NtGA3ox2pro:LUC, NtGA2ox8pro:LUC vector (as reporter), GFP-tagged empty vector (as negative control for effector) and pGreen (35S:LUC) vector (as positive reporter) were transferred into Agrobacterium GV3101 (pSoupp19) competent cells, and the positive clones were screened and identified, and the Agrobacterium resuspended solution with various combinations of effector and reporter was mixed in an equal ratio of 1:1 and used for tobacco injection and transforming the protoplasts from narcissus leaves. The experimental group (e.g. ACT2pro:NtMYB1-GFP + NtGA3ox2pro:LUC; ACT2pro:NtMYB1-GFP/NtbZIP53-mCherry + NtGA3ox2pro:LUC; ACT2pro:NtbZIP53-mCherry + NtGA3ox2pro:LUC), the negative control (ACT2pro:NtMYB1-GFP + empty LUC report vector), and the positive control group (pGreen) were injected into the same leaf. The injected tobacco plants were cultured in the dark for 12–24 h. After 2–3 d, the GFP and mCherry fluorescence signals were observed under a fluorescence microscope or a laser confocal microscope (Leica SP8) to illustrate the expression of effector protein.

The luciferase activity of each sample was qualitatively analysed using an in vivo plant imaging system (Immunization Center of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University). Subsequently, the relative luciferase activity was quantified with a microplate reader [49]. The ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase activity (LUC/REN) was calculated as the relative luciferase activity.

Reverse transcription–quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from bulbs of N. tazetta following the protocol of Ren et al. [22]. Total RNA was extracted from leaves using TRIzol extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The primer sequences used for expression analysis of the candidate genes are shown in Supplementary Material Table S1. The endogenous AtGAPC2 and NtACTIN were used as the reference genes. The data for NtACTIN are shown in the figures. Relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method of Schmittgen and Livak [50]. In this experiment three biological replicates were tested, and each biological replicate contained leaves or tepals from three independent plants.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

WGCNA is a common method for constructing gene co-expression networks [24]. WGCNA analysis of GA metabolism enzyme and related transcription factors was according to the description by Yang et al. [23].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using at least three biological replicates, and each measurement was repeated three times, as indicated in the legends. Duncan’s new multiple range test in one-way ANOVA was used to determine the significance of differences between treatments.
