From free speech advocates to critics: The Trump administration’s new rhetoric promotes support for censorship among Trump voters
Authors: Matthew E. K. Hall, B. Tyler Leigh, Brittany C. Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most Americans claim to support freedom of speech, including President Donald Trump who has portrayed himself as an ardent defender of this constitutional right. However, in the wake of Charlie Kirk’s assassination, Trump and members of his administration began to explicitly call for speech restrictions. This largely unprecedented rhetoric from a sitting president and his allies provides an opportunity to examine competing theories of public opinion: the influence of partisan elites versus the public’s purported commitment to free speech. In a preregistered survey experiment, we found that exposure to such unusually explicit antispeech rhetoric increased Trump voters’ support for government censorship of outparty media and individuals but prompted backlash among non-Trump voters, increasing their support for protecting outparty speech. While prior research has established that partisans favor censorship of outparty members, we demonstrate that partisans differentially shifted their censorship attitudes in response to the Trump administration’s new rhetoric, whether or not the source was attributed to Trump and his allies. These findings highlight the influence of elite rhetoric on public support for even the most foundational—and popular—democratic norm, raising concerns about the resilience of American democracy in the face of explicitly illiberal elites.

US President Donald Trump has portrayed himself as a champion of free speech and, in 2025, claimed he would “bring back free speech to America” (1). However, following the assassination of the conservative activist Charlie Kirk, Trump and his administration suddenly reversed this messaging, arguing that speech is not free “[w]hen 97 percent of the stories are bad about a person” (2) or “when you come out and you say ‘it’s ok what happened to Charlie [Kirk]’” (1). When answering questions about flag burning, Trump was unambiguous: “We took the freedom of speech away” (3). Trump had promoted arguably antispeech policies before (4). However, this rhetorical shift marked the first time in modern history that a US President explicitly advocated for restricting a constitutional liberty that 98% of Americans describe as important, even in today’s highly polarized environment (5). How did this radical shift in messaging affect popular attitudes toward free speech—especially among Trump voters, who are avid proponents of speech rights and voted for a self-proclaimed free speech champion (1)?

Elite commitment to core democratic norms, like free speech, is crucial to long-term democratic resilience (6) because elites can exert powerful influence over public opinion—particularly in highly polarized political contexts (7). Even well-informed citizens often mechanically adopt elite partisan cues rather than reason for themselves (8). Accordingly, antidemocratic elite rhetoric can undermine popular support for democratic norms, including trust in elections (9) and support for inclusiveness in political processes (10). Support for free speech is especially susceptible to elite framing (11), potentially endangering this constitutional right. Indeed, while Americans profess strong support for free speech (5, 12, 13), that support is conditional and declines when considering disfavored speakers and ideas (12–15). Given such fragility, Trump’s new anti–free speech rhetoric may lead his supporters to become more supportive of censorship.

However, the influence of elite rhetoric may be limited when partisan leaders suddenly shift their position on important issues (7, 16; cf. 17). Research has found that, although Trump’s norm-violating rhetoric—unrelated to speech—undermines public support for some democratic norms, it has negligible effects on free speech attitudes (10). Given the overwhelming popularity of free speech in the United States—at least in abstract (5), and especially among Republicans (15)—Trump’s rhetorical inflection on this fundamental right may have minimal influence on his supporters’ censorship attitudes. Moreover, Trump’s supporters may recall his past antispeech positions (4) and dismiss any shifts in Trump’s rhetoric as consistent with his prior views.

Accordingly, Trump’s shift in free speech rhetoric presents a novel opportunity to test competing theories of public opinion: the influence of partisan elites versus purported popular commitment to free speech. Explicit calls for censorship from mainstream elites are rare in modern American politics, especially at the presidential level. Thus, until now, researchers could not assess the effects of explicit and ecologically valid calls from such elites to restrict free speech. Therefore, we ask whether a sudden change in elite rhetoric affects public opinion when the rhetoric challenges longstanding support for a core American value and democratic norm.

Specifically, we tested the effect of exposure to real quotations from Donald Trump and members of his administration (SI Appendix) on Trump voters’ support for government censorship of outparty media and outparty individuals compared to a no-treatment control (H1a-b) and researcher-generated, prospeech rhetoric designed to directly mirror the antispeech messages (H1c-d; see SI Appendix). Additionally, we examined whether identifying the rhetoric’s source (i.e., attributing it to Trump or specific Trump allies vs. a generic prominent politician) enhanced these effects (H2a-d) to determine whether the rhetoric’s influence depends on an explicit partisan cue or the rhetoric itself conveys an implicit partisan signal (e.g., due to its content, style, or familiarity to participants). Finally, we explored the effects of antispeech and prospeech rhetoric on support for censoring outparty media and individuals among non-Trump voters, including Kamala Harris voters, third-party voters, and nonvoters, and whether identifying the rhetoric’s source moderated those effects.

Results

Fig. 1 presents treatment effects of exposure to antispeech and prospeech rhetoric for Trump voters and non-Trump voters. As expected, antispeech rhetoric increased Trump voters’ support for censoring outparty media (compared to the control, H1a: b = 0.35, SE = 0.11, P = 0.002, 95% CI [0.13, 0.58], and the prospeech treatment, H1c: b = 0.25, SE = 0.12, P = 0.032, 95% CI [0.02, 0.49]) and support for censoring outparty individuals (compared to the control, H1b: b = 0.34, SE = 0.11, P = 0.002, 95% CI [0.13, 0.56], and the prospeech treatment, H1d: b = 0.29, SE = 0.11, P = 0.011, 95% CI [0.07, 0.51]). The magnitude of these effects was considerable: A shift of roughly one-third of a point is comparable to one in three Trump voters moving from a neutral response to somewhat agreeing with censorship or from somewhat disagreeing with censorship to a neutral response—which recent research has shown is an overlooked threat to democracy (18). In contrast, antispeech rhetoric had a backlash effect among non-Trump voters, decreasing support for censoring outparty media (b = -0.25, SE = 0.10, P = 0.015, 95% CI [-0.44, -0.05]) and outparty individuals (b = -0.19, SE = 0.09, P = 0.039, 95% CI [-0.38, -0.01]) compared to the no-treatment control. Thus, Trump’s new, explicit anti–free speech messaging effectively persuades his voters to express greater support for censorship of outparty media and individuals but prompts his opponents to express greater support for protecting outparty speech. The pro–free speech rhetoric had nonsignificant effects on support for censorship among both Trump voters (both P’s > 0.400) and non-Trump voters (both P’s > 0.350); see the SI Appendix.

Marginal effects of the treatments on (A) support for censoring outparty media and (B) support for censoring outparty individuals. Capped bars represent 95% CI.

We also tested whether identifying the source of the rhetoric moderated these effects. We found no statistically significant interactions between source identification and the antispeech or prospeech treatments when predicting support for censoring outparty media or individuals, among Trump voters (H2a-d: all P’s > 0.450) or non-Trump voters (all P’s > 0.150); see the SI Appendix. These null effects suggest the rhetoric itself may convey implicit partisan signals.

Discussion

The Trump administration’s abrupt shift in free speech rhetoric poses a novel and difficult test of elite influence. Research has long indicated that political elites can influence public opinion through their rhetoric, particularly among their supporters. Yet there may be meaningful limits on that influence (10, 16, 17): Can a sudden reversal by prominent elites on long-standing commitments to a popular and cherished democratic value influence their supporters’ willingness to uphold that value? Our findings suggest the answer is yes.

We found that the Trump administration’s new rhetoric—explicitly calling to restrict speech—increased his supporters’ endorsement of censorship while producing backlash among non-Trump voters. These reactions did not depend on identifying the speaker as Trump or a member of his administration, suggesting the rhetoric’s content, style, or familiarity prompts these divergent responses, irrespective of explicit source identification (including naming Trump specifically). The durability of these effects is unclear. Given the centrality of free speech in American democracy, censorship attitudes may revert to a baseline over time—possibly depending on whether elites reinforce and normalize their antispeech rhetoric. But even temporary increases in support for censorship could undermine democratic norms in practice, as evidenced by Jimmy Kimmel’s suspension and the many American employees who faced disciplinary action (including termination) for criticizing Charlie Kirk in the wake of his assassination (3).

Overall, new elite rhetoric unambiguously criticizing free speech does substantially affect partisan attitudes toward censorship—at least in the short term. Beyond simply favoring censorship of outparty (vs. inparty) messaging, we show that, in response to elite rhetoric, partisans on both sides shift their censorship attitudes but differentially so. Amid increasing concern about American democracy, this work highlights the importance of elite commitments to democratic norms (6) and the potential for elite rhetoric to promote illiberal attitudes.

Methods

Participant Sample.

Our preregistered experiment was fielded from October 15 to October 20, 2025 (https://osf.io/9wj5m). We recruited adults (N = 1,305) through Bovitz, quota-matched to the US population regarding age, gender, education, race/ethnicity, and region, balanced between participants who voted for Donald Trump in 2024 (N = 653) and those who did not (including Harris voters, third-party voters, and nonvoters; N = 652). Respondents’ whose reported vote in the survey did not match the vote they reported to Bovitz were excluded (N = 48), as were those who failed either of two pretreatment attention checks (N = 438).

Survey Design.

Participants first reported demographics and their 2024 presidential vote. They were then randomly assigned to one of three conditions: anti–free speech, pro–free speech, or no-treatment control. Participants in the antispeech and prospeech conditions were also randomized to an identified source or generic source condition. The antispeech rhetoric was based on four quotes (slightly adapted or implied) from US President Donald Trump, Pamela Bondi (US Attorney General), and Brendan Carr (Chairman of the Federal Communications Commission). The four prospeech quotes simply reversed the messages in the antispeech quotes (e.g., modified “we need to regulate speech to prevent this from happening again” to “regulating speech isn‘t going to prevent this from happening again”). See the SI Appendix for treatment details, survey items, and manipulation checks. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Notre Dame (protocol #25-10-9627). Participants provided informed consent before beginning the study.

Measures.

We used two three-item indices to assess support for censoring outparty media, adapted from Holliday et al. (19) [e.g., “The government should be able to censor media sources that spend more time attacking (inparty members) than (outparty members)”] and support for censoring outparty individuals, adapted from the General Social Survey’s tolerance items [e.g., “The government should be able to censor a person giving a speech in my community who spends more time attacking (inparty members) than (outparty members)”]. The inparty/outparty piping was based on participants’ self-reported party affiliation at the beginning of the survey.

Analyses.

To test H1a-d, we regressed the outcome variables on indicators for assignment to the antispeech and prospeech treatments, controlling for identified source, age, gender (male; 0/1), race (White; 0/1), and education (college educated; 0/1), in a subsample of Trump voters (N = 653). To test H2a-d, we ran the same regression models, including interaction terms between an indicator for identified source and the indicators for the antispeech and prospeech treatments. We explored the same effects in a subsample of non-Trump voters (N = 652). See the SI Appendix for additional preregistered, exploratory analyses.
