Challenges in Implementation of National Leprosy Eradication Program: Cross-Sectional, Qualitative Study in Three High Endemic Districts of West Bengal, India
Authors: Soumalya Ray, Nivedita Das, Kajari Bandyopadhyay, Diptakanti Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Leprosy, although eliminated at the national level in 2005, remained a public health problem in a few districts of India. This study aims to explore the perception and experience of healthcare providers and programme managers in delivering the services to the community members under National Leprosy Eradication Program in West Bengal.Methods:In this descriptive, cross-sectional study with a qualitative survey approach, data were collected during the period of January–May, 2022 from the service providers and programme managers through In-depth Interviews (IDIs) and Focused Group Discussions (FGDs) in three high endemic districts of West Bengal, namely, Purulia, Birbhum, and Uttar Dinajpur. Data were analysed through inductive coding, and emergent themes were identified. Pile sorting exercise was done for improving neutrality in theme identification in R with Anthrotools.Results:Twelve IDIs and Six FGDs were conducted before reaching data saturation. Triangulation of findings led to four themes encompassing various challenges – delay in recognising the problem by infected individuals, perceived stigma, delay in accessing healthcare by the affected individuals, and delay in treatment after approaching the health care system. Mass media campaigns through new age social media (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, etc.) with emphasis on audio-visual mode and financial incentives for improving compliance were suggested by study participants as solution.Conclusions:Improving health communication, frequent case detection camps, and capacity building with the help of various professional organisations is the need of the hour. Concerted effort of the policymakers involving various stakeholders in this will help in eliminating the disease from these high-endemic districts.

Introduction

Leprosy, commonly known as ‘Kustha’ in ancient Indian scriptures, is considered as a public health problem in India for long.[1] For combating this, the Government of India launched National Leprosy Control Programme in 1954–55 which was rechristened as National Leprosy Eradication Program (NLEP) later.[2] Although India has eliminated the disease at the national level in 2005, the disease is still high endemic; that is, more than one new case are detected per 10,000 population in a year in several districts of India.[23] Unfortunately, those few districts were responsible for almost two third of the new leprosy cases diagnosed globally.[4] The complexities related to control and elimination of leprosy may not be understood by mere number of leprosy cases alone. Elimination of the disease requires better implementation of the programme with a comprehensive approach.[5] Previous literatures have identified challenges in delivering the services such as lack of awareness, maldiagnosis, stigma, limited access to health care institutes due to time and distance, inability to track individuals approaching private practitioners, and so on.[456] Implementation challenges are heterogeneous in nature, varying considerably with location and demographics of the beneficiaries.[78]

Healthcare providers play a crucial role in implementing any healthcare programme, and they are the brand ambassador of the programme to the general population.[8] Hence, to achieve sustainable elimination of leprosy in endemic areas, it is imperative to explore the perception and experience of healthcare providers and programme managers.

In this context, this study was undertaken with the objective to explore the challenges faced by the service providers and programme managers in delivering the sevices to the community members under the program in West Bengal. West Bengal was chosen purposively as it consistently reported higher proportion of multibacillary cases among the newly detected cases. Despite this importance, peer-reviewed literature studies on the same are scarce in this part of India. This study aimed to fulfil this gap in knowledge.

Materials and Methods

Study type and design

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional study with a qualitative survey approach. It was conducted during the period of January to May, 2022 in three high endemic districts of West Bengal, namely, Purulia, Birbhum, and Uttar Dinajpur.

Study participants

Services related to screening of cases, confirmation of diagnosis, and provision of multi-drug therapy (MDT) are provided by Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and Para medical Workers (PMWs) for Leprosy and Medical Officers. For this study, they were considered as service providers. Block Medical Officer of Health (BMOH), Zonal/District Leprosy Officers (ZLO/DLO), and Chief Medical Officer of Health (CMOH) were considered as Programme managers. The study population consists of both service providers and programme managers of the above-mentioned three districts.

Data collection

Data collection was done through In-depth Interviews (IDIs) and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with the help of interview and discussion guides, respectively, in purposively selected two blocks in each of these three districts. All the IDIs and FGDs were conducted with the respondents in a pre-determined place and time of mutual convenience. Their responses were recorded in an audio recorder with permission, and field notes were taken. Probes and prompts were applied as necessary.

IDIs were conducted with programme managers. The number of FGDs was not decided in advance. It was planned to conduct at least one FGD in each block until data saturation is reached. Participants were asked to sit in a semi-circular manner. A series of open-ended questions in logical sequence was asked to stimulate discussion among the participants. Complete proceedings of the FGD including level of participation and sociogram were noted.

All investigators were trained in qualitative research methods. However, 2 days’ workshop was arranged prior to data collection for improving the quality of data collection and analysis, where briefing of the data collection tools was done. The first author and last author were present in 10% of the interviews.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were done simultaneously. After each interview and FGD with service providers, data including all field notes and recorded audio were transcribed and translated from local language (Bengali) into English, close to verbatim on the day of data collection. Two researchers coded each transcript separately, and any discrepancies in the coding were sorted out following exhaustive discussion. These coded notes were thematically analysed through inductive coding, and emerging themes were identified with illustrative quotations. Thematic analysis was done in Taguette, an open source, free software.[9] At the end of each of the focus group discussions, the participants were debriefed about the salient features of discussion with the help of field notes. Necessary corrections and clarifications were noted.

For improving consistency and neutrality in theme identification, it was decided to conduct a pile sorting exercise with a panel of public health experts, having at least 5 years experience in Community Medicine and/or Leprosy. For this purpose, cards were prepared with the codes identified from thematic analysis with a unique number on the back. Inclusion of items for pile sorting was based on the Smith’s salience score, where the elbow of the scree plot was taken as cut-off. The experts were requested to group them according to their perceived similarity and provide their rationale for the grouping. Finally, during debriefing of the preliminary results, the member checking was done and based on the clarifications, the results were refined and finalised. Their responses imported in R, and an aggregate priority matrix for all the experts was created with AnthroTools package.[10] This was represented graphically through a concept map depicting the relationship between the identified codes.

Human subject protection

The study obtained ethical clearance from Institutional Ethics Committee of the institute. Written informed consents were obtained from each participant after explaining all requisite information and their rights.

Results

In this study, a total of 12 IDIs were conducted. The mean duration of the IDI sessions was 43 (±25) minutes. After conducting six FGDs with ASHAs, data saturation was reached. Hence, further FGDs were not conducted. Each session had 7–10 participants. From these discussions, several challenges in delivering the programme had been identified. These challenges were grouped in to four themes – delay in recognising the problem by infected individuals, perceived stigma, delay in approaching the healthcare system, delay in treatment, and other factors. Delay in recognising the problem by infected individuals

According to different study participants, symptomatic individuals often fail to recognise the depigmented skin lesions as a serious health problem and thus do not seek care immediately. Information about this disease and its long-term health hazard is unknown to them. Language barrier among the service providers and beneficiaries probably aggravate this problem further. A ZLO mentioned -

When you visit [a particular place], you will find that they speak in a different language. It is completely different from Hindi, Bengali etc. As a result, it sometimes become difficult to understand the complaint they are presenting with. Our health workers also could not explain them properly. (One ZLO, West Bengal)

Delay in accessing healthcare by the affected individuals

During the FGDs, ASHAs reported that the cost associated with the detection and management of the leprosy might act as a barrier in early detection of the cases. If the suspected persons miss the camp for diagnosis, (s) he has to visit the nearest hospital for confirmation of the diagnosis by the medical officer. This is associated with direct cost (e.g., transport cost to the nearest hospital) and indirect cost (e.g., loss of wage for daily wage earners). One of the ASHAs mentioned that -

I had a suspected case. I asked him to visit the nearest hospital for confirmation of the diagnosis. He told me that if he did not attend his work, he’d have nothing to eat in the night. OPD is already closed when he returns from his work. So, there was delay in confirmation of the diagnosis in this case. Similar incidents happened in other cases as well. What could we do in these cases?

Another problem in delayed diagnosis was related to socio-economic status as perceived by the service providers. Individuals belonging to the relatively well-to-do family do not want to be screened by the government health system functionaries. This might be related to the perceived stigma.

When we visit well to do families, they often do not allow us to enter inside their house. When enquired about the symptoms related to Leprosy, they refused to answer. There were incidences where we know that one of the family members had Leprosy, but they replied in negative. Sometimes, the individual might have not disclosed the fact to the family members. However, most of the times they tell us that they do not suffer these diseases of poor people. (ASHA)

Delay in treatment

Supervision of the treatment was another challenge, particularly for those who were not available in their permanent location. There is a provision in the programme to transfer out the patients to the healthcare facility nearest to their location. However, the infected individuals usually do not want to share the correct location, might be due to perceived stigma. This poses challenge in completing the treatment within the stipulated timeframe. One of the PMWs mentioned that

A married lady was diagnosed with Leprosy. She was at her paternal house at that time. After few days, she was to return at her in-laws house. So, I told her that she could receive the medication from the ASHA of her in-laws village. But, she asked me not to transfer out. She told me that she will collect the medicines from this locality. It seems she was afraid that this information [infected with Leprosy] will affect her marital life.

Another problem was lack of skilled manpower. Suspecting/diagnosing a leprosy case based on clinical parameters at PB stage requires much more skills compared to making a diagnosis at MB stage, indirectly contributing to increase the burden of infective cases, which is lacking in considerable proportion of healthcare workers including doctors.

I am working with NLEP as a medical officer for a long duration. In most of the districts, they do not allow much time for Leprosy in monthly meetings. In our district, I show different images of Leprosy and discuss the case with medical officers, ANMs, sometimes even with ASHAs. Most of them participates in the sessions wholeheartedly. This type of capacity building is required in all the high endemic districts. Support from the higher authority is needed in this regard. (One medical officer associated with NLEP)

Networking with private practitioners was another challenge in implementing the programme. For various reasons, people tend to approach private practitioners; some of them are rural practitioners. They often misdiagnose leprosy cases as other skin disorders and are often reluctant to refer the cases to the government health system.

One of my cases had skin lesions 3–4 years prior to diagnosis. Initially, it was only one, around the forearm. The numbers increased gradually. But he thought it was case of fungal infection (‘daad’). He consulted local rural practitioner and received treatment on and off for three years. But he did not mention about these skin lesions during ACDRS survey as it was not affecting his day to day activities. He was a plumber by profession. After marriage, his mother-in-law brought the case to my attention. The numbers of skin lesions at the time of diagnosis were twelve! (One PMW, West Bengal)

Other factors

Lack of data entry operators at the district level

All national programmes are now data-driven. However, unlike other national programmes, there is no post for data entry operators in the district. This creates problem for timely submission of reports to higher authorities.

Other national programmes have a designated data entry operator under the programme. However, there are no such opportunities in NLEP. Most of us are not very tech-savvy and it creates problems for us in timely submission of reports. (One ZLO)

Discussion

The present study had focussed on perceptions of service providers and programme managers for exploring the challenges associated with management of leprosy cases under NLEP in West Bengal. It was evident through triangulation that the problem is multi-factorial. Lack of perceived seriousness about the disease, lack of knowledge, perceived stigma, loss of daily wages, and lack of trained manpower were some of the major challenges identified by service providers and programme managers associated with NLEP.

The disability and disfigurement in leprosy develop at a later stage, years after the initiation of disease.[1112] In the initial period, a few depigmented skin lesions are unlikely to disrupt the day-to-day activity of the affected individuals and may not be overtly visible. As a result, individuals do not feel urgency to attend the health care providers initially.[4613] The participants of the present study perceived that lack of information about the disease among the population is a major contributor in this. Another issue was the language barrier. Some of the community members are not able to read either Bengali or Hindi. As a result, IEC materials which are usually published in Bengali, Hindi, or English and are displayed in different places (hospital, transport hubs, etc.) may not have the desired effect in propagating the information about leprosy and importance of early detection in the same. Earlier studies also corroborated with this finding.[141516] Mass media educations are thus important to consider for dealing with this lack of knowledge. Due to the language barriers, these education materials might be provided through audio-visual mode. Different participants suggested that it could be provided through different social media like Facebook and Instagram.

Since ancient times, leprosy was associated with stigma.[1561517] Despite the availability of effective multi-drug therapy, this deep-rooted stigma has remained a serious challenge in the management of leprosy.[1517] Even in this study, it was reported that people were hesitant to approach the health care delivery system, even to inform their disease status to their family members. There are earlier researches corroborating this effect of perceived stigma on delayed diagnosis of leprosy.[1561517] Concerted efforts from different sectors of the community might be helpful on managing this dreaded menace and improving family and social support to fight with leprosy.

Although the diagnosis and treatment under NLEP are free, the direct cost of travel to healthcare facility and the indirect costs such as missing daily wage were found to be another important challenge. This was corroborated by other researchers.[561819] In some national programmes, the Government provides cash incentive to persons with disease for treatment completion.[20] A similar approach might be considered in NLEP to improve the treatment compliance.

Lack of trained manpower is another challenge to deliver NLEP intervention effectively. Lack of skill in detecting and managing leprosy cases among private practitioners was added problem. This was identified by all involved stakeholders – ASHAs to CMOH. This finding is corroborated with earlier literature including an Indian study conducted in Maharashtra in 2017.[4] Due to declining number of cases, the clinical training on leprosy is hampered during the medical course. Continuous medical education might be helpful in this regard. Various medical associations (IMA, IPHA, IAPSM) might be roped in for this purpose.

The study did not include the patient perspective in this exploration. This may be considered as one of the limitations of the study. However, the objective of the study was to explore challenges faced by service providers and programme managers and agreements of our study finding with other local and global studies provide credence to our study findings. Another limitation is that the samples were chosen purposively rather than the probability sampling method. Generalisability of our finding is thus not possible. However, we have included different levels of stakeholders who are directly associated with provision of services under NLEP from three high endemic districts of West Bengal. This is likely to increase the richness of our data. This may be considered as one of the strengths of this study.

Conclusion

The present study highlights several challenges as perceived by the service providers and programme managers while implementing NLEP in different districts of West Bengal. Health professionals, policymakers, and programme implementers should be aware of such factors and initiate sustained health education campaigns directed towards patients, healthcare providers, patients’ close contacts, traditional health practitioners, and the community for increasing awareness about leprosy and availability of free, effective multi-drug therapy for the same. Capacity building of health professionals with the help of different professional associations (e.g., IMA, IPHA, IAPSM) is the need of the hour.
