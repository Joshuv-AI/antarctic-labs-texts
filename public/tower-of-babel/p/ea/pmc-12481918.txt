Comparative analysis of cell-specific promoters in AAV9-mediated gene therapy targeting the central nervous system
Authors: Sergiy Chornyy, Jessica A Herstine, Caleb Holaway, Abigail Biddle, Tatyana A Vetter, Nettie K Pyne, Allison M Bradbury
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a comprehensive toolkit of ubiquitous and cell-specific promoters for potential use in adeno-associated virus (AAV)9-mediated gene therapy for central nervous system (CNS) disorders, systematically evaluating biodistribution, cellular specificity, and peripheral targeting. While ubiquitous promoters, such as the cytomegalovirus early enhancer/chicken beta-actin (CAG) promoter, drive widespread expression, cell-specific promoters offer greater targeting precision. We introduce a novel, astrocyte-specific, truncated glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) promoter, named gfaABCD1405 (gfa1405), which enhances astrocyte specificity while reducing size, improving utility for gene therapies requiring larger transgenes. gfa1405 demonstrated broader CNS expression than gfaABC(1)D, addressing previous limitations in AAV-mediated applications. The methyl CpG binding protein 2 promoter (p546) effectively targeted neurons, with strong expression in the neocortex and hippocampus, making it a promising candidate for neuronal disorders. Oligodendrocyte-specific promoters exhibited distinct patterns, with the myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG) promoter driving expression in the corpus callosum, while the 2′,3′-cyclic nucleotide 3′-phosphodiesterase (CNP) promoter showed broader transduction, suggesting wider therapeutic applicability. This study establishes a promoter selection framework for AAV9-based gene therapy, enabling variable CNS cell-type targeting and optimizing therapeutic efficacy. By integrating comparative promoter analysis with gfa1405 development, we provide a practical collection for CNS-directed gene therapy, minimizing off-target effects and advancing treatment strategies for neurological disorders.Keywords: AAV9-mediated gene therapy, cell-specific promoters, astrocyte-specific promoters, oligodendrocyte-specific promoters, neuronal targeting, gfa1405 promoter, intracerebroventricular injection, viral vectors, neurodegenerative diseases, therapeutic gene delivery toolkit

Introduction

Neurological disorders are a leading cause of disability and rank among the primary causes of death globally. Rare neurodevelopmental disorders, predominantly affecting children, often result from inherited genetic mutations that disrupt normal brain function and CNS development.1,2 The majority of these disorders are monogenetic in nature, making gene therapy a promising solution to address genetic deficiencies.3 Due to a number of its favorable properties, adeno-associated virus (AAV) has emerged as the predominant viral delivery vehicle in gene therapy and has been safely used in over 300 clinical trials. In fact, there are now commercially available AAV-based drugs for neurological conditions including an AAV serotype 9 (AAV9) treatment for spinal muscular atrophy.3,4,5 AAV9 is known for its efficient CNS transduction and ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and is most commonly used for delivering therapeutic agents directly to the brain.4,6 Recombinant single-stranded AAV (ssAAV) genomes, up to approximately 4,700 nucleotides long, are engineered with inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) and an expression cassette containing a promoter, intron, transgene, and poly(A) signal to drive and stabilize gene expression. Using single-stranded AAV9 (ssAAV9), we selected from an assortment of promoters varying in length and expression profiles (Table 1) to evaluate stable expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) throughout the CNS.

Inherited genetic mutations that disrupt normal brain function and CNS development lead to abnormal cellular functions, structural changes, and degenerative mechanisms across different cell populations. Various neurological conditions arise depending on the specific cell types affected. For example, in leukodystrophies, disease typically manifests due to aberrant glial cell populations, which are crucial for maintaining homeostasis, forming myelin, providing support, and protecting neurons.4,6,7 Disruption of glial cell functions can cause widespread white matter damage and detrimental neurological deficits. Conversely, other neurological conditions are due to dysfunction of neuronal cells, which can, for example, lead to an imbalance in excitatory and inhibitory activity within the brain.15,16 This imbalance may contribute to the onset of neurodevelopmental disorders, such as epilepsy, autism, and neurodegenerative diseases.17 Despite advances in capsid engineering for precise cell and tissue targeting, few of these novel capsids have made it to the clinic for pediatric neurological indications to date. Concurrently, the exploration of optimal promoters to drive cell-specific expression of established capsids remains underexplored.18,19 The development of specialized ssAAV promoters is key for achieving targeted expression, thereby enabling safe and efficacious intervention to halt disease progression. Over half of AAVs in clinical trials use ubiquitous promoters like the cytomegalovirus (CMV) early enhancer and the chicken β-actin promoter (CBA) or CAG (hybrid CMV enhancer, CBA, and rabbit β-globin splice acceptor).20 However, these promoters range from 750 to 2,000 base pairs (bp) and often challenge AAV’s limited 4.7–4.8 kilobase pairs (kb) packaging capacity depending on the transgene utilized. Herein we assessed both ubiquitous and cell-specific promoters, including truncated versions, to enhance or retain cellular targeting and allow for larger transgenes or multiple regulatory elements within the same vector. Our full-length ubiquitous promoter CAG drives high constitutive expression across various cell types, which proves beneficial for multisystemic diseases. We also tested the efficiency of several cell-specific promoters including glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), which targets astrocytes in the CNS and promoters specific to oligodendrocytes—myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG), 2′,3′-cyclic nucleotide 3′-phosphodiesterase (CNP), and myelin basic protein (MBP). To maximize transgene size, we explored truncated promoters including the shortened version of eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha 1 (EF1A) known as EFS,11 tCAG (previously published as CAG7), p546 (a version of the methyl CpG binding protein 2 promoter9), and gfaABC(1)D (a version of GFAP8) to assess cellular targeting and efficiency (Table 1). Finally, we also detail for the first time the generation and assessment of a novel, rationally designed, truncated version of GFAP named gfaABCD1405 (gfa1405), which unlike previous truncated versions, retains the CNS expression profile of the endogenous full-length promoter. Our approach addresses the packaging limitations of AAV vectors, aiming to identify the minimal promoter elements necessary for effective and specific gene expression in target cells, ultimately enhancing the versatility and efficiency of gene therapy vectors for neurologic diseases.

Results

Design and development of novel astrocyte-specific promoter derived from GFAP

In pursuit of optimizing astrocyte-targeted gene therapies, we engineered a novel promoter derived from the foundational GFAP sequence, to achieve enhanced specificity and expression profiles within the CNS. Due to significant expression differences driven by the full-length GFAP promoter and its truncated version, gfaABC(1)D,7 we aimed to increase neuraxial targeting while maintaining astrocyte specificity and minimizing promoter size. To do this, we referenced the first publication that generated gfaABC(1)D,8 which identified a variable region within the endogenous promoter, C (Figure 1). The reported C region of the endogenous promoter was divided into 6 parts (C1–C6) of approximately 250 bp, and each was individually returned into the promoter containing all other required regulatory regions named A, B, and D. Through their promoter iterations driving LacZ in transgenic mice, they found that gfaABC(1)D had the strongest expression throughout the brain.8 Our approach included delivery of gfaABC(1)D or the full-length GFAP promoter in the context of AAV. Our results indicated less expression driven by gfaABC(1)D as compared to GFAP at both the 4-week and 8-week groups after AAV9-gfaABC(1)D-EGFP or AAV9-GFAP-EGFP intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration at post-natal day (P)0–P1.7 To increase expression throughout the CNS while trying to maximize transgene size within the confines of AAV, we purposefully withheld region C2 due to its inhibitory nature. We then added regions C3, C5, and C6 into gfaABC(1)D, thereby generating a novel intermediate promoter of 1,405 bp that we termed gfaABCD1405 (gfa1405) (Figure 1). Through our promoter engineering, we were able to maximize the accommodating transgene length to 2.3 kb, thereby allowing for larger transgenes to be packaged within ssAAV while also maintaining strong neuraxial and astrocyte targeting.

Examination of promoter-driven EGFP mRNA expression and protein distribution in CNS and peripheral tissues post-ssAAV9 delivery

Comparative analysis of ssAAV9 with different promoter-driven EGFP gene expression (mRNA) and protein distribution highlights the importance of selecting appropriate promoters based on the specific requirements of gene therapy studies. Our approach provides a quick and straightforward preliminary assessment of the distribution patterns, including both CNS and peripheral tissue mRNA expression, highlighting potential off-target effects and non-specificity that could influence therapeutic outcomes. Following our initial studies involving the GFAP, gfaABC(1)D, and tCAG promoters,7 we aimed to expand the selection of promoters for targeting the CNS and specific cell types using the i.c.v. delivery method in neonatal wild-type (WT) mice (Figure 2A). To assess promoter specificity and peripheral expression, multiple tissues, including the rostral (brain area 1 [BA1]), central (brain area 2 [BA2]), and caudal (brain area 3 [BA3]) parts of the brain; cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions of the spinal cord; liver; heart; and quadriceps muscle, were examined for EGFP mRNA expression by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) (Figure 2B). Additionally, we performed visualization of EGFP protein expression in whole tissues to detect EGFP signal (Figure 3). It is worthy of noting however that whole-organ imaging is impacted by several factors, including tissue density, thickness, color, quality of perfusion, etc., and therefore was used in our study solely as a qualitative readout.

The ubiquitous CAG promoter demonstrated the highest levels of EGFP mRNA expression across both CNS and peripheral tissues (Figure 2B), including the heart and quadriceps (Figure 2B). Whole-organ fluorescence revealed strong and consistent EGFP protein expression in CNS tissues (brain and spinal cord), heart, and quadriceps, while the liver showed only weak or negligible fluorescence (Figure 3). Protein analysis by western blot corroborated these findings, confirming abundant EGFP protein expression driven by CAG in BA1 and BA2, moderate expression in BA3, strong expression in the heart, and notably lower expression in the liver (Figure S1). Fluorescence imaging showed that the tCAG promoter drove lower EGFP protein signal intensity in the CNS compared with CAG (Figure 3). Although whole-tissue staining in peripheral tissues is weak, tCAG has previously been shown to drive EGFP mRNA expression in the liver and particularly high levels in the heart.7 Another ubiquitous promoter, EFS, also showed increased EGFP mRNA expression in the CNS, particularly in BA1 and BA2, along with moderate expression in the liver (Figure 2B). The p546 promoter emerged as one of the strongest candidates, driving high levels of EGFP mRNA expression in the brain, comparable to the CAG promoter, while exhibiting lower expression in peripheral tissues (Figure 2B). Fluorescence imaging demonstrated that the p546 promoter drove a strong EGFP signal predominantly in the brain, particularly in BA2, while showing significantly less expression in the spinal cord and peripheral tissues such as the heart, liver, and muscle compared to the CAG promoter (Figure 3).

For the astrocyte-specific promoters in the CNS, the novel gfa1405 promoter drove robust EGFP mRNA expression, comparable to the GFAP promoter,7 and even similar to or exceeding CAG and p546 in several CNS regions (Figure 2B). Although sensitive qPCR analysis revealed robust EGFP mRNA in the liver and only low-level transcripts in the heart (Figure 2B), whole-organ fluorescence showed minimal signal in either tissue (Figure 3); however, western blot quantification confirmed the significant protein expression in the liver (Figure S1). Our previous studies have analyzed the GFAP and gfaABC(1)D promoters, demonstrating their ability to drive EGFP mRNA expression in the CNS.7 In this study, we included whole-organ imaging from these promoter groups to facilitate a direct comparison between the full-length GFAP promoter and its truncated versions (Figure 3). Taken together, our previous and current data suggest that the gfa1405 promoter exhibits a similar effect on EGFP protein expression and tissue distribution as the full-length GFAP promoter but drives stronger EGFP protein distribution compared to gfaABC(1)D.

The oligodendrocyte CNP promoter proved highly effective, comparable to CAG, gfa1405, and p546, according to both qPCR (Figure 2B) and imaging data, particularly in the brain. In the spinal cord, EGFP protein expression was primarily concentrated in the cervical region (Figure 3B), and low levels of EGFP protein expression were detected in the heart and liver with the CNP promoter but not in muscle tissue (Figure 3E). The MAG promoter showed minimal EGFP mRNA expression in BA3 and modest expression in the liver (Figure 2B), while the rMBP (rat myelin basic protein) promoter drove extremely low EGFP mRNA expression in BA3, cervical spinal cord, and liver. Neither promoter demonstrated EGFP mRNA expression in the heart (Figure 2C), and their overall activity across other tissues remained negligible. Due to the lack of significant EGFP mRNA expression, these promoters were excluded from whole-organ imaging analysis.

To resolve discrepancies between transcript levels and observed protein signal, we complemented our mRNA and imaging analyses with quantitative western blotting of selected tissues (BA1, BA2, BA3, heart, and liver) from mice treated with the four top-performing promoters: CAG, p546, gfa1405, and CNP (Figure S1). Quantification indicates the gfa1405, CNP, and CAG promoters drive comparable EGFP protein levels in both BA1 and BA3, whereas p546 drives low total EGFP protein expression in these regions. In BA2, CAG has significantly higher total EGFP protein than CNP and p546. Despite the strong EGFP fluorescence observed in the sagittal sections of the brain, p546-driven protein levels by western blot were lower than expected but corresponded to the lowest mRNA expression among the tested promoters. In the periphery, CAG and gfa1405 drove the greatest EGFP protein expression in the heart and liver, respectively. Overall, the western blot results corroborate our RT-qPCR data (Figure 2B), with CAG driving the most EGFP protein in BA1, BA2, and the heart; CNP driving the most EGFP protein in BA3; and, finally, gfa1405 driving the most EGFP protein in the liver (Figure S1).

This comprehensive evaluation allowed us to assess the overall efficiency of each promoter in driving EGFP mRNA and protein expression and characterize protein distribution in the CNS while noting some degree of peripheral mistargeting. Based on our findings, the CAG, p546, gfa1405, and CNP promoters demonstrated the highest potential for CNS-targeted gene therapy, with robust EGFP mRNA and protein expression levels and favorable protein distribution profiles.

Comparative fluorescent analysis of EGFP protein expression and distribution in the mouse brain post-ssAAV9 i.c.v. injection

To assess biodistribution and cellular targeting of the 10 recombinant ssAAV9 vectors, we analyzed the distribution of EGFP-positive cells across the neuroaxis and identified their morphology and specific brain regions with abundant EGFP expression. Additionally, we used mean EGFP intensity (relative fluorescence units, RFUs) as a proxy for assessing promoter-driven gene expression across different brain regions (Figure 4). The data presented in Figure 4 illustrate the relative EGFP expression across all promoters, with statistical comparisons primarily benchmarked against the CAG promoter. A detailed comparative analysis of EGFP distribution and intensity among all tested promoters is available in Table S1, providing additional insights into the relative efficiencies of different promoters. The CAG promoter demonstrated widespread EGFP protein expression, covering up to 42% of the total brain area, with strong signals observed in the neocortex, hippocampus, olfactory areas, and thalamus, among other regions (Figures 4A and 4B). Microscopy confirmed robust EGFP signals, showing both neuronal and glial-like morphology, with visible projections, supporting CAG’s ubiquitous nature. Mean EGFP intensity for the CAG promoter reached 474.8 RFUs, the highest among all promoters tested (Figure 4C). In contrast, the tCAG promoter exhibited much lower levels of EGFP protein expression, with a maximum distribution of only 5.8% of the total brain area, predominantly localized in the hippocampus and neocortex (Figures 4B and 4C). The EGFP-positive cells displayed neuron- and glia-like characteristics, though at much lower intensity compared to CAG, with a mean EGFP intensity of 8.443 RFUs (Figure 4C). The EFS promoter displayed lower EGFP distribution compared to CAG, covering 5%–9% of the brain area, with some sections showing up to 16% (Figures 4B and 4C). Though expression was visible in the neocortex, hippocampus, and corpus callosum, it lacked the strong, ubiquitous pattern seen with CAG. The mean EGFP intensity was 2.678 RFUs, indicating weak overall protein expression (Figure 4C). The p546 promoter demonstrated widespread EGFP protein expression in regions associated with Mecp2 activity, including the neocortex and hippocampus. Notably, while the p546 promoter’s total distribution area was comparable to CAG (41% of the total brain area), the protein expression pattern was distinct, with lower intensity in projections compared to soma-restricted expression. Upon closer examination, EGFP-positive cells displayed neuronal-like morphology, characterized by prominent cell bodies and projections, with extensive presence in the neuropil (Figures 4A and 4B). Mean EGFP intensity for the p546 promoter was moderate, at 154.0 RFUs (Figure 4C).

Among the astrocyte-specific promoters, gfa1405 demonstrated the broadest EGFP distribution, covering 35% of the brain area. EGFP protein expression was strong in the neocortex, hippocampus, and olfactory areas, with additional expression in regions close to the fiber tracts and ventricular system (Figure 4B). High-magnification images confirmed astrocytic morphology, with star-shaped cells and processes radiating from the soma (Figures 4A and 4B). The mean EGFP intensity of 412.1 RFUs indicated robust promoter activity (Figure 4C). The full-length GFAP promoter exhibited a similar but slightly more restricted distribution, covering ∼24% of the brain area, with a mean EGFP intensity of 387.7 RFUs (Figure 4C). Both GFAP and gfa1405 showed strong astrocytic targeting, although gfa1405 achieved broader coverage across the neuroaxis. In contrast, the truncated gfaABC(1)D promoter drove significantly lower EGFP protein expression, covering only 9% of the brain area, with reduced intensity (91.54 RFUs) (Figures 4B and 4C). While astrocyte-specific targeting was observed, the overall efficiency was weaker compared to the full-length GFAP and gfa1405 promoters.

For the oligodendrocyte promoters, the CNP promoter drove widespread EGFP protein expression, covering 37% of the total brain area, including significant EGFP protein expression in the neocortex, hippocampus, olfactory areas, thalamus, and hypothalamus (Figure 4B). Higher-magnification images revealed diverse cell types exhibiting glial and neuron-like morphology, with strong oligodendrocyte targeting in the corpus callosum. The mean EGFP intensity was 45.51 RFUs, indicating moderate but widespread expression (Figure 4C). In comparison, the MAG promoter exhibited a much more limited EGFP distribution, covering only 5.5% of the brain area, primarily localized to white matter regions, particularly within the corpus callosum (Figure 4B). The mean EGFP intensity was 9.548 RFUs, indicating weak EGFP protein levels (Figure 4C). The rMBP promoter exhibited very low EGFP protein abundance, covering only 2%–4% of the brain area, with a mean EGFP intensity of 3.258 RFUs.

This comprehensive analysis highlights the varying efficiencies of different promoters in driving EGFP protein expression across the CNS. Ubiquitous promoters like CAG demonstrated the highest distribution and intensity, while neuron-specific promoters such as p546 showed more targeted but widespread EGFP signal in neuronal projections. Astrocyte-specific promoters, particularly gfa1405, exhibited robust and widespread EGFP protein levels, while oligodendrocyte-specific promoter CNP displayed widespread but lower-intensity EGFP signal across both glial and neuronal populations. Promoters with low expression, such as rMBP and EFS, were excluded from further cell-type-specific colocalization analysis due to their minimal EGFP signal. Overall, the CAG (ubiquitous), gfa1405 (novel astrocyte), GFAP (full-length astrocyte), p546 (neuronal), and CNP (oligodendrocyte) promoters demonstrated the most promising results for driving widespread EGFP protein expression in the brain.

Colocalization analysis of EGFP protein expression with cell-specific biomarkers in the brain

As we proceeded to the next step in our investigation to identify the cell types with the best transduction levels in the brain, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) to evaluate the expression patterns of EGFP with neuronal and astrocyte markers. We selected the hippocampus for high-resolution confocal imaging due to its high cellular density and strong GFAP signal, which enhances the detection and visualization of astrocytes. Additionally, the hippocampus is a critical region for neurogenesis and neural connectivity, making it highly relevant for examining promoter-driven EGFP protein expression in different cell types.23,24 Using brains from 1-month-old mice that were injected with ssAAV9 vectors within 48 h after birth, we tested the strongest ubiquitous promoter (CAG), a neuron-specific promoter (p546), and astrocyte-specific promoters (GFAP, gfa1405, and gfaABC(1)D). We captured high-resolution images of EGFP signal alongside NeuN (for neurons) and GFAP (for astrocytes) in the hippocampus using confocal microscopy (Figures 5A and S2).

The CAG promoter shows overlap with both GFAP and NeuN, indicated by white and yellow regions, suggesting EGFP protein expression in both astrocytes and neurons, with a stronger neuronal presence. The p546 promoter displays prominent yellow regions, indicating significant EGFP overlap with NeuN, suggesting predominant EGFP protein expression in neurons and minimal EGFP levels in astrocytes. EGFP driven by the GFAP promoter strongly overlaps with the astrocytic marker GFAP, indicating predominant EGFP levels in astrocytes and minimal colocalization with NeuN, suggesting limited neuronal EGFP protein expression. Similarly, the gfa1405 promoter shows significant overlap with GFAP, while the gfaABC(1)D promoter also retains astrocyte specificity, as evidenced by extensive white regions in the images.

Additionally, we conducted a regular fluorescence microscopy colocalization study of EGFP with S100B and NeuN in the cortex, hippocampus, and olfactory areas, which were identified as the most abundant regions from the EGFP distribution analysis. S100B, known for its wider distribution compared to GFAP in the mouse brain, provided a complementary marker for astrocytes.25,26 We employed the NIS-Elements software for automatic detection and quantification of colocalization across a wide range of cells, ensuring accurate and reproducible results (Figure 5B). For astrocytes, the EGFP signal levels were found to be quite varied: CAG, 36%; p546, 1.9%; GFAP, 44%; gfaABC(1)D, 58%; and gfa1405, 37.7% colocalization with astrocytes. On the other hand, neuronal colocalization presented a different pattern: 27% with CAG, 72% with p546, 13% with GFAP, 19% with gfaABC(1)D, and 11.9% with gfa1405 (Figure 5B). Consistent with previous EGFP/GFAP colocalization data, the CAG promoter exhibited notable colocalization with both S100B and NeuN, confirming its ubiquitous nature and ability to target both astrocytes and neurons effectively. The p546 promoter demonstrates a pronounced preference for neurons, indicated by the substantially highest colocalization with NeuN, contrasting sharply with its minimal EGFP signal in astrocytes. gfaABC(1)D demonstrated the greatest affinity for astrocytes among all astrocyte-specific promoters, although it drove lower overall EGFP protein expression compared to both GFAP and gfa1405, which also effectively targeted astrocytes.

Following examination of astrocytic and neuronal populations for the aforementioned promoters, we conducted a detailed fluorescence microscopy colocalization study of EGFP with Olig2 and NeuN for the MAG and CNP promoters. Due to specific expression in oligodendrocytes, the CNP and MAG promoters can be effectively used with the Olig2 biomarker to identify and study these myelin-producing cells in the CNS. This part of the analysis focused on the MAG promoter using a 20× objective to capture 5 fields of view (FOVs) per sample (n = 9), specifically along the corpus callosum region, effectively analyzing oligodendrocyte and neuronal colocalization patterns (Figures 5C and S3). Similarly, for the CNP promoter, we obtained images from 4 to 7 FOVs using the same magnification (n = 5) and location. In the CNP group, all EGFP-positive cells had up to 16% of oligodendrocytes and up to 21%–29% of neurons that potentially left ∼50% unidentified cells, and, according to the data from EGFP protein expression and distribution in the mouse brain, they potentially could be astrocytes that were abundant all over the brain sections. In the MAG group, we found less overall distribution but much more oligodendrocyte-specific results with up to 65% of EGFP-positive oligodendrocytes and only 13.5% of neurons. Therefore, the CNP promoter has more widespread distribution but is also leaky and less specific to oligodendrocytes than initially expected.

Based on our data showing robust promoter activity at 4 weeks, we extended our analysis to assess the stability of the gfa1405 (astrocyte-specific) and p546 (neuron-specific) promoters at 8 weeks, focusing on EGFP distribution, intensity, and colocalization with cell-specific markers (Figure S4). The results demonstrated consistent expression patterns for both promoters, with gfa1405 maintaining stable colocalization with the astrocyte marker S100B and p546 exhibiting steady colocalization with the neuronal marker NeuN, indicating sustained EGFP protein expression and promoter specificity over time in the CNS.

Discussion

Our study underscores the importance of selecting the appropriate promoter in the design of gene therapy vectors, particularly for targeting specific cell populations within the CNS. We employed i.c.v. injections in neonatal mice, a widely used model in preclinical gene therapy studies targeting early-onset CNS disorders, where early intervention is critical. This approach enabled delivery of vectors directly into the cerebrospinal fluid, facilitating widespread and uniform distribution across the brain and spinal cord, which is particularly beneficial for treating diffuse CNS disorders.27 Intracerebroventricular delivery has been shown to provide a more uniform distribution across the CNS compared to other routes28 and to significantly improve motor function and muscle health in models of spinal muscular atrophy compared to intravenous delivery,29 supporting its use for achieving broad transduction in diffuse neurological conditions. We also employed a very narrow treatment timing window treating all mice within 48 h after birth to ensure similar development stages were being compared. The results from our comparative analysis of EGFP reporter expression revealed significant differences in both distribution and expression intensity, contingent upon the promoter used, further emphasizing the importance of optimizing delivery methods for therapeutic outcomes.

Ubiquitous promoters, such as CAG and EFS, are known for their widespread gene and protein expression but lack cell-type specificity. As a result, they can drive transgene expression not only in the CNS but also in peripheral tissues, such as the heart and liver, where unintended expression poses risks. Our study found that the full-length CAG promoter (1,700 bp) exhibited the strongest distribution and protein expression in neurons and astrocytes within the CNS following i.c.v. injection in neonatal mice. This enhanced activity may be due to additional regulatory elements that boost transcriptional efficiency. In contrast, truncated promoters like tCAG (581 bp) and EFS (240 bp) showed significantly weaker EGFP intensity, likely because they lack key structural components, such as the hybrid CBA intron and the rabbit beta-globin splice acceptor site in the case of tCAG, which may influence mRNA processing and stability, thereby impacting transgene expression. Promoter structure differences, especially between truncated and full-length variants, can substantially affect gene expression outcomes. For example, a shorter 212 bp EFS promoter version reported in the literature30 demonstrated a ∼2.5-fold increase in EGFP distribution across the brain after neonatal i.c.v. injection, compared to the 240 bp variant used in our study. This difference could be attributed to an optimized sequence or shorter length enhancing transcriptional activity. The study by Hughes30 also showed that a 643 bp CAG promoter variant drove robust in vivo gene expression, achieving near-complete EGFP intensity and CNS distribution. This underscores the necessity of precise promoter engineering and optimization for therapeutic applications, especially when comparing ubiquitous promoters with cell-specific options. However, the lack of specificity in ubiquitous promoters increases the risk of transgene expression in non-target tissues, which may lead to toxicity or immune-related adverse events. In clinical settings, high-dose AAV vector administration has been linked to such complications, as demonstrated by fatalities after administration of Zolgensma for SMA and in a myotubular myopathy trial, where excessive transgene expression in the liver was identified as a contributing factor.31,32 These outcomes emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate promoters to minimize off-target effects and ensure safety, particularly in gene therapy applications targeting sensitive tissues like the CNS.

It is known that the MecP2 promoter includes key regulatory elements like CpG islands, which are crucial for the binding of transcription factors that modulate gene expression, essential for its role in neuron-specific expression as they contribute to the regulation of MecP2 gene transcription in response to neuronal activity and developmental cues.9 In our study, the p546 promoter, a truncated version of the MecP2 promoter, demonstrated robust and extensive EGFP fluorescent signal across multiple brain regions, encompassing up to 41% of the total brain area according to imaging analysis. This widespread distribution was predominantly evident in neuronal populations within the hippocampus and neocortex, areas traditionally associated with MeCP2 activity, suggesting its utility for studies targeting neuronal network involvement and Rett syndrome research.9 The use of confocal microscopy confirmed that the p546 promoter drives EGFP protein expression mainly in neurons, with minimal astrocytic activity. This highlighted its specificity and potential in gene therapy applications focused on neuronal pathology, showing expression in neurons approximately two times higher compared to the CAG promoter and similar to another well-known neuronal promoter—hSyn1 (human synapsin 1, ∼495 bp)19,33—in terms of distribution and the number of EGFP-positive neurons. Both CAG and p546 promoters show high specificity to the neocortex region and hippocampus, with visible EGFP signal in soma and neurites, though there is a lower EGFP signal in the cerebellum region. Notably, we were unable to corroborate our p546 IHC data with western blot, likely due to the nature in which our brains were divided and processed (as described)7 for protein quantification (Figure 2A). With p546’s neuronal specificity and location-specific fluorescent expression patterns, we are most likely diluting EGFP signal by contribution of other non-expressing regions. Western blot data follows qPCR trends, however, with p546 expression being the lowest in all three brain regions. Overall, this promoter’s specific neuronal targeting makes it a valuable tool for comprehensive studies aiming to manipulate neuronal gene expression across brain regions.

All astrocyte-specific promoters (GFAP, gfa1405, and gfaABC(1)D) showed robust gene and protein expression in astrocyte-rich areas, such as the neocortex, hippocampus, olfactory regions, and corpus callosum. Notably, the newly developed gfa1405 promoter was designed to balance efficiency with size constraints, offering broader distribution than the full-length GFAP promoter while saving 803 bp of space in the AAV vector, and thus can be used for larger therapeutic genes, critical for conditions like leukoencephalopathy with vanishing white matter. This enhanced distribution is likely due to the inclusion of regulatory regions C3, C5, and C6, which may promote stronger interaction with transcription factors, thus increasing expression efficiency. In contrast, the gfaABC(1)D promoter, which also lacks the inhibitory C2 region,8 displayed lower overall distribution, suggesting that the specific regulatory elements present in gfa1405 extend its transcriptional reach across the CNS. Truncated promoters are often preferred in gene therapy because their smaller size allows for a larger transgene payload while retaining key regulatory features that improve specificity and efficiency and reducing risks such as insertional mutagenesis.34 In previous studies with transgenic models, gfaABC(1)D showed broad distribution similar to full-length GFAP,8 but, in our AAV-mediated approach, we observed a more limited pattern. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including differences in transduction efficiency due to the use of ssAAV vectors, which rely on episomal expression and are more susceptible to local cellular environments or differences in the visualization techniques used (LacZ enzymatic staining vs. EGFP fluorescence). Developmental stages may also play a role, as the transcriptional activity of the gfaABC(1)D promoter could vary with timing. For instance, AAV injections at P0–P1 might miss critical periods of heightened promoter activity observed in the transgenic model. Finally, we acknowledge that sole testing in neonatal animals may not provide a complete picture of promoter activity or expression profiles. While outside the scope of this publication, we aim to fully characterize the novel gfa1405 promoter by delivering the same EGFP reporter construct to adult mice in a future study.

The truncated gfa1405 and p546 promoters demonstrated strong cell-type specificity, with gfa1405 effectively targeting astrocytes and p546 driving widespread neuronal expression, particularly in the hippocampus and neocortex. Both promoters maintained stable expression over time, with no significant changes in distribution or intensity at 4 and 8 weeks.

To effectively address leukodystrophies caused by oligodendrocyte dysfunction, such as globoid cell leukodystrophy (Krabbe disease),6 developing new AAV-based gene therapies with promoters that specifically target oligodendrocytes is crucial. Our study complements previous research by comparing the design, function, and effectiveness of the CNP, MAG, and rMBP promoters in driving selective transgene expression in the CNS. The CNP promoter exhibited strong EGFP protein expression in oligodendrocytes and displayed a broader distribution than MAG, with significant EGFP protein expression observed not only in the corpus callosum but also in other brain regions, including the neocortex, hippocampus, and olfactory areas. This extensive reach highlights CNP’s potential for targeting multiple regions within the CNS and suggests its utility in therapies requiring both oligodendrocyte and neuronal transduction. Notably, the highest oligodendrocyte-specific EGFP protein expression with CNP was still observed in the corpus callosum, consistent with its active role in myelination during early development. In contrast, the truncated 1.5 kb MAG promoter, previously shown to achieve over 90% specificity for oligodendrocytes and sustained EGFP signal for at least 8 months following intraparenchymal AAV delivery in neonatal or adult mice,13 demonstrated a more localized EGFP protein expression pattern in our study. Following i.c.v. injection in neonatal mice, EGFP protein expression driven by the MAG promoter was primarily concentrated in the corpus callosum. This targeted specificity may be due to active myelination processes in the neonatal brain and the cerebrospinal fluid dynamics favoring vector distribution to midline structures. Compared to the broader reach of CNP, MAG’s specificity suggests that it may be advantageous for therapies focusing on oligodendrocyte populations, particularly in regions undergoing significant myelination. The rMBP promoter, while sharing significant sequence homology with both mouse and human MBP promoters particularly in its core cis-regulatory regions,14 displayed weaker in vivo reporter gene expression 1 month after injection. This discrepancy between its strong in vitro efficacy and limited in vivo performance, also observed in prior studies, may indicate that rMBP’s efficiency could improve with later developmental stage administration, as seen when AAV vectors are injected post-natally at day 10 (P10).35 Overall, CNP’s broad distribution and capacity for targeting both neurons and oligodendrocytes across multiple brain regions make it promising for widespread CNS applications, while MAG and rMBP may serve more specialized roles with MAG showing high corpus callosum specificity and rMBP warranting further optimization for in vivo use.

Overall, CAG, gfa1405, and CNP each produced strong gene-to-protein correspondence across the examined brain regions, whereas p546, despite its broad fluorescent footprint, yielded lower total protein. This distinction underscores that promoters driving protein predominantly into fine neuronal projections may appear under-represented when measured in bulk tissue. In the liver, however, a different pattern emerged: CAG drove modest, but highly variable, transcript levels but only minimal protein accumulation. Such uncoupling is common in hepatocytes and likely reflects post-transcriptional controls that limit translation or accelerate protein turnover.36 Importantly, this tempering effect reduces concern about excessive peripheral protein burden when CAG is used for CNS applications and serves as a reminder that transcript data alone can overestimate off-target impact. Whole-organ fluorescent imaging provided a rapid view of spatial distribution, yet its qualitative nature, sensitive to tissue density, pigment, and perfusion, necessitates interpretation alongside quantitative measurements. Therefore, we believe that it is particularly valuable to evaluate tissues as done herein by a multitude of assays including immunofluorescent staining to demonstrate distribution throughout the neural axis as well as cell specificity, qPCR to determine transcript levels of expression, and western blot to quantify protein levels of expression. These latter two experiments rely on tissue homogenization of a particular region and thus lose the context of expression within regions and cell populations, rendering the immunofluorescent staining all the more critical. Further, we took serial sections of the brain to perform various assays. The brain sections taken for qPCR were rostral to the sections collected for western blot analysis. The hindbrain (BA3) was likely particularly vulnerable to variability between the given assays due to the positioning, size, and anatomical nature (i.e., the western blot analysis included more brainstem and caudal cerebellum while the qPCR contained more central white matter structures). While each of these methodologies has inherent deficiencies, we identified CAG, gfa1405, and CNP as the most reliable high-output promoters, while p546 remains preferable where neuronal selectivity outweighs absolute protein yield.

In conclusion, our comprehensive analysis of cell-specific promoters demonstrates the critical role of promoter selection in achieving precise, efficient, and targeted gene expression within the CNS. The success of promoters such as gfa1405, which combines enhanced specificity with reduced size, highlights the potential for optimizing gene therapies that are both effective and safe. The broad distribution and high gene and protein expression levels achieved by gfa1405, coupled with its reduced off-target effects, underline its promise for future therapies aimed at astrocyte-targeted interventions. Moreover, promoters like CNP, with its ability to target both oligodendrocytes and neurons, showcase the versatility required to address complex neurodegenerative diseases. While some promoters, such as rMBP, may require further optimization, this underscores the need for continued research to refine these tools.

The ability to fine-tune promoter activity will not only enhance gene therapy outcomes but also broaden the range of treatable CNS disorders. This work paves the way for the development of highly specialized, next-generation vectors that can safely and efficiently deliver therapeutic genes to specific cell types within the CNS. By continuing to explore and optimize cell-specific promoters, we can accelerate the development of targeted gene therapies that have the potential to revolutionize treatments for a variety of neurological conditions.

Materials and methods

Construct design and development

All constructs were designed to fit within the 4.8 kb packaging capacity of the ssAAV vector. All sequences were synthesized by GenScript with appropriate restriction sites added for cloning and cloned into a Kanamycin-resistant ssAAV backbone (Andelyn, USA) and modified in Dr. Bradbury lab (Nationwide Children’s Hospital, Columbus, USA) using a rapid DNA dephosphorylation and ligation kit (Roche, 50-100-3347). First, EGFP along with the simian virus 40 (SV40) intron and poly(A) was ligated into the AAV ITR containing backbone along with a multiple cloning site upstream for cloning in promoters. Most of the promoters tested have been previously published including GFAP and gfaABC(1)D,8 CNP,12 MAG,13 rMBP,14 EFS,11 and full-length CAG.10 The short tCAG promoter (581 bp) contains a shortened CMV enhancer and the CBA promoter but lacks the hybrid CBA intron and the rabbit β-globin splice acceptor site and was designed by our lab for use with longer transgenes.7 The gfa1405 promoter (1,405 bp) is a novel promoter designed by our lab and is a truncated version of the GFAP promoter, rationally designed to include regions of strong promotor activity, and exclude inhibitory regions based on the publication by Lee et al. (2008).8 After synthesis, promoters were ligated into the AAV-EGFP backbone. The ligated constructs were then transformed into NEB stable competent cells (New England Biolabs, C3040H) and were selected for using kanamycin-supplemented plates and media. All constructs were then sequence confirmed. Large-scale preparations of plasmid were made and isolated using a cesium chloride ultracentrifugation purification method. Finally, these large-scale preps were again sequence-confirmed using complete plasmid sequencing through Massachusetts General Hospital, Center for Computational and Integrative Biology sequencing core. Sequence-confirmed plasmids were then sent to Andelyn for packaging into AAV9.

AAV production

Recombinant AAV production was performed using a calcium phosphate-mediated triple transfection in adherent HEK293 cells. Harvested media were filtered, concentrated, and then purified by gradient ultracentrifugation followed by ion-exchange chromatography. Vectors are formulated in 20 mM Tris (pH8.0), 1 mM MgCl2, 200 mM NaCl, and 0.001% Pluronic F68 and sterile-filtered. A droplet digital polymerase chain reaction process specific to the AAV2 ITR sequence was used to determine the AAV titers. All recombinant AAV vectors were generated at Andelyn Biosciences.

Intracerebroventricular injections

All animal procedures were performed under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Nationwide Children's Hospital and conducted in accordance with institutional and federal guidelines. C57Bl/6 neonates (P0–P1) were cryo-anesthetized (∼2 min) before the i.c.v. injections. The i.c.v. injections were performed using a Hamilton syringe (Cal7635-01) with 33GA 30° beveled needles (Hamilton, 7803-05) into the left hemisphere, positioned at 2/5 of the distance from the lambda suture to the eye. C57Bl/6 neonates were injected with 5 μL volumes of ssAAV9 vectors encoding GFP under various promoters. The viral genome (vg) doses for each vector were as follows: 7.50E10 vg per animal for AAV9.GFAP.EGFP, AAV9.gfa1405.EGFP, AAV9.gfaABC(1)D.EGFP, AAV9.P546.EGFP, AAV9. CAG.EGFP, and AAV9.EFS.EGFP; 7.00E10 vg per animal for AAV9.tCAG.EGFP; 8.50E10 vg per animal for AAV9.CNP.EGFP and AAV9.rMBP.EGFP; and 8.00E10 vg per animal for AAV9.MAG.EGFP. After the injections, the mice were placed on a heating pad until they regained mobility and were then returned to their breeding cages.

Cohorts and necropsy

Our study cohorts consisted of 10 C57Bl/6 mice (5 male and 5 female) per promoter construct or control, along with 3 additional mice of either sex for whole-organ images. Some groups consisted of fewer replicates due to cannibalism or unexpected deaths (p546 and MAG groups, N = 9; rMBP and control group, N = 8). Four weeks after i.c.v. injection, mice were terminally anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine/acepromazine (100/10/2 mg/kg intraperitoneal) and transcardially perfused with heparinized PBS (25 mL at 7 mL/min). Tissues were dissected and fixed in 4% PFA in PBS for 12–24 h. Three mice per construct had whole brains, spinal cords, quadriceps, heart, and liver removed for whole-organ imaging, while the remaining 10 animals per construct were used for all other assays. All peripheral organs, spinal cord (sectioned into cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions), and the sectioned left hemisphere of the brain were flash-frozen for RNA isolation. The right hemisphere was cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4°C for 3 days for use in immunofluorescent imaging.

Microscopic examination of EGFP biodistribution and IHC

One group of mice (3 mice per construct) was used to visualize the EGFP-positive signal across whole organs. Large-scale imaging was conducted using an Olympus microscope and cellSens version 1.3 software (Olympus, Center Valley, PA). All other right brain hemisphere samples (5 male/5 female per construct) were embedded and frozen in OCT compound (Scigen Tissue-Plus, Fisher). Sagittal sections were cut at 30 μm thickness on a cryostat (1950 Leica, Germany). Free-floating sections were washed in PBS and incubated with DAPI solution in PBS for 1 min at room temperature (RT). To retrieve antigens from PFA-fixed tissues, slices were treated with 0.1% sodium borohydride in PBS for 15 min at RT. Part of the sections were used for EGFP biodistribution and expression analysis. To facilitate the visualization of brain areas and enhance the anatomical context, DAPI staining was employed, which labels cell nuclei and thus delineates the overall brain structure and cellular distribution. In addition to analyzing general distribution, we included 20× magnified images covering an area of 90,000 square micrometers to identify which cell types expressed EGFP based on their morphological features. For the morphological analysis and determination of brain regions, we utilized the Allen Brain Atlas as a primary reference. This resource aided in accurately identifying specific brain areas and was instrumental in constructing schematic sagittal sections for visual representation within this article.37 Sagittal sections were chosen as they offer a longitudinal perspective of the brain, encompassing various regions including the neocortex, hippocampus, striatum, ventricular system, midline structures such as the corpus callosum, midbrain, pons, and medulla, as well as the cerebellum, thalamus, and hypothalamus.

For EGFP colocalization with specific cellular markers, all the slices were blocked and permeabilized in 10% goat serum and 5% donkey serum (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS with 1% Triton (PBST) for 1 h at RT, followed by overnight incubation in antibody diluent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with chicken anti-GFAP (Abcam, 1:300) or mouse anti-S100B (MilliporeSigma, 1:400) or mouse Olig2 (MilliporeSigma, 1:50) and rabbit anti-NeuN (Cell Signaling, 1:500) at 4°C. Sections were washed in PBS and subsequently incubated with donkey anti-chicken Cy5 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:500), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500), donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500), and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) secondary antibodies in PBST with 10% normal donkey serum for 1 h at RT. Sections were then washed in PBS and mounted on slides in ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Images were acquired using a Nikon Ti2-E motorized fluorescence microscope with a Nikon DLED-I multiline LED engine, a Hamamatsu ORCA Fusion camera, and Nikon Plan Apochromat Lambda D objectives. A quad-pass filter cube (C-FLL LFOV D/F/T/Cy5) was used for excitation and emission, and an emission cleanup wheel was used with Semrock filters for each channel. Images of EGFP colocalization with S100B were captured as 20× objective, 18–22 z stacks at a resolution of 0.16 μm/pixel with a Z step size of 0.6 μm. A total of 13 fields of view were acquired from 5 different brain regions (visual cortex, somatosensory cortex, motor cortex, olfactory areas, and hippocampus) with three replicates totaling approximately 1,800 cells in each replicate. Additional representative high-resolution images of EGFP and GFAP colocalization were captured as z stacks on a Nikon AX R confocal in resonant mode with a 60× Plan Apochromat Lambda D oil immersion objective at a resolution of 0.09 μm/pixel and a Z step of 0.19 μm. The confocal z stacks were compressed to 2D images using the extended depth of focus module in NIS-Elements. Separate channels for EGFP, GFAP, NeuN, and DAPI or EGFP, Olig2, NeuN, and DAPI channels are shown in Figures S2 and S3.

Images were processed and analyzed using NIS-Elements software v.5.30, employing custom algorithms developed in the General Analysis 3 module (Nikon Instruments). Each image underwent automatic rolling ball background correction, using a large radius to maintain all features. The DAPI signal delineated the total brain area. An automatic threshold for EGFP, derived from the tissue’s background intensity, identified EGFP-positive pixels. Statistical analysis of EGFP expression, both in terms of area coverage and intensity across different groups, was performed with one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. The results were presented as boxplots showing the percentage of EGFP-positive area and the adjusted mean EGFP intensity (mean intensity in EGFP-positive areas minus median intensity in EGFP-negative areas) in RFUs.

Colocalization of EGFP with S100B or NeuN was analyzed using maximum intensity projections of z stacks representing 19 individual fields of view from each brain and approximately 7 fields of view for colocalization of EGFP with Olig2 or NeuN. Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes were identified using bright spot detection with automatically calculated minimum intensity thresholds in the red channel (AF568). EGFP-positive pixels were segmented using an automatically calculated threshold for EGFP signal in the green channel (GFP). The overlap between the centroid of each bright spot representing an astrocyte, oligodendrocyte, or neuron and EGFP-positive signal was then used to identify cells as EGFP positive or EGFP negative. Neurons were segmented in a similar way within the far-red channel (AF647). The total and EGFP-positive cell counts from all regions were added up to calculate the percentage of EGFP-positive cells by the type for each brain section. These results were then graphed, and statistical analyses such as ordinary one-way ANOVA were performed to determine significance for most of the promoters and experiments, except for colocalization analysis for CNP and MAG, where an unpaired t test was used to determine significance, using GraphPad Prism.

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR for EGFP distribution

Left hemispheres of the 4-week EGFP-injected C57Bl/6 mice (4–5 animals per construct, male and female) were cut into 6 sections and snap-frozen for molecular analysis. Peripheral tissues were also dissected from the same animals and divided and snap-frozen for molecular analysis. RNA was then extracted from 3 mouse brain areas, rostral (anterior region from bregma), central (posterior region from bregma), and caudal (posterior region from lambda); cervical spinal cord, thoracic spinal cord; lumbar spinal cord; heart; and liver using QIAGEN RNeasy Plus mini kit (QIAGEN, 74134), and RNA from quadriceps was isolated using the RNAeasy fibrous tissue mini kit from QIAGEN (QIAGEN, 74704) following the manufacturer’s protocol for snap-frozen tissue. Genomic DNA and AAV episomal DNA were then removed from RNA using DNA-free DNA removal kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM1906) following manufacturer’s rigorous protocol. After DNA removal, RNA purity and concentration were measured using NanoDrop One microvolume ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer. 200 ng of RNA was then loaded into cDNA reactions using RevertAid first strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1621). One sample each from the CNS and peripheral organs for each promoter was set up in a cDNA reaction without reverse-transcriptase enzyme to create “No RT” controls. qPCR reactions were prepared by adding 1.75 μL of cDNA reaction product to 18.25 μL of prepared qPCR master mix per well containing TaqMan universal PCR master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4305719), Ambion nuclease-free water (Invitrogen, AM9937), EGFP TaqMan FAM-MGB gene expression assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4331182, Assay ID Mr04329676_mr), and mouse ACTB (β-actin) TaqMan VIC-MGB gene expression assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4352341E). qPCR cycling conditions were adopted from TaqMan universal PCR master mix manufacturer protocol, omitting the optional UNG incubation at 50°C prior to polymerase activation at 95°C. All experiments were performed on QuantStudio 6 flex real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4485692). All samples were run in triplicate on a plate, and 2–3 plates were run per promoter, resulting in 6–9 wells per sample. Data analysis was performed using the delta CT method, calculating expression relative to corresponding endogenous gene expression within the sample (β-actin). The 2–3 plates were averaged together to create the final graphs.

Protein isolation and western blots for EGFP protein quantification

Total protein was extracted from mouse BA1, BA2, BA3, heart, and liver on 4-week-old EGFP-injected animal tissues following the manufacturer’s protocol using T-PER tissue protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78510) with Pierce protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32963).

Protein lysate was quantified using the DC protein assay kit II (Bio-Rad, 5000112). Western blot loading samples were prepared using NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NP0007) with NuPAGE sample reducing agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NP0004). Loading samples were then denatured at 70°C for 10 min, and 25 μg of protein was resolved on 4%–12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris Plus mini protein gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NP0336BOX) followed by transfer to nitrocellulose membrane using the semi-dry transfer system. After transfer, membranes were blocked for overnight at 4°C in Pierce protein-free blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 37572). Blocked membranes were incubated for 2 h at RT with the following antibodies: mouse monoclonal antibody to GAPDH (1:5,000; Synaptic Systems) and chicken polyclonal antibody to GFP (1:5,000; Abcam). Following incubation with primary antibodies, membranes were washed with phosphate-buffered saline with .1% Tween (0.1% PBST). Membranes were then incubated for 1 h at ambient temperature with the following secondary antibodies: goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 568 (1:1,000; Abcam) and donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1,000; Invitrogen). Membranes were then washed briefly in 0.1% PBST. Blots were subsequently imaged for fluorescence using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, 12003154). For accurate comparison between tissue types, all blots were imaged at the same exposure (1 s exposure for AF488; 101 s exposure for AF568).

Quantification of band intensity was performed using Bio-Rad Image Lab software. Data were then analyzed in GraphPad Prism, and statistical analyses were performed through a two-way ANOVA between all groups. All fluorescence images were inverted and converted to black and white. Due to differences in transfer efficiency, p546 and gfa1405 heart blots had minor contrast enhancement for GAPDH band visualization. All post-image processing was performed in Adobe Photoshop software.
