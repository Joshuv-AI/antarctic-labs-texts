Psychiatric Adverse Events Associated With Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blockers: A Pharmacovigilance Study Based on FAERS Database
Authors: Huafeng Jiang, Yimin Lin, Lidan Tu, Qingxia Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (DHPCCBs) are widely employed in managing cardiovascular diseases. Although some studies suggest a potential link to psychiatric adverse events (PAEs), a systematic investigation is lacking.Methods:Adverse event reports for seven DHPCCBs marketed in the United States were collected from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). PAEs were extracted from these reports and then performed using disproportionality analysis.Results:A total of 9164 PAEs associated with 7 DHPCCBs were identified, revealing 18 positive signal associations. For amlodipine, 18 positive signals were detected, with “completed suicide,” “suicide attempt,” and “mental status changes” being the most frequent. In contrast, nifedipine showed only 2 positive signals, which overlapped with amlodipine. No positive signals were found for the remaining five DHPCCBs. PAEs typically occur within the first 30 days of treatment. After adjusting for sex and age, 13 positive signals remained, 11 of which were related to suicidal behaviors.Conclusions:The disproportionality analysis identified signal associations between DHPCCBs and PAEs. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating signals requiring further validation through controlled studies. Clinicians may consider monitoring psychiatric symptoms in high-risk patients, particularly given the conflicting evidence from previous studies.

Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) play a crucial role in modulating cellular function by selectively inhibiting the influx of calcium ions, thereby altering intracellular calcium concentrations. Clinically, these agents are predominantly utilized in the management of hypertension, coronary heart disease, and chronic stable arrhythmias. Due to their efficacy, CCBs rank among the most frequently prescribed medications globally.1 Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) can be classified into subclasses based on their chemical structure, including dihydropyridines (DHPs), benzothiazines, and phenylalkylamines. DHPCCBs are recognized for their potent vasodilatory effects, with minimal impact on cardiac pacemaker activity and myocardial contractility. This makes them particularly suitable for elderly hypertensive patients who exhibit significant vascular rigidity and those with abnormal vascular regulation.2 There is a wide variety of DHPCCBs, the most common ones being, for example, nifedipine, amlodipine, nimodipine, etc. In addition to their approved indications, DHPCCBs have been employed off-label for the treatment of Raynaud’s phenomenon, high-altitude pulmonary edema, and migraine.3–6

However, given the widespread use of DHPCCBs, their adverse reactions have become increasingly recognized. Commonly reported adverse reactions include headache, dizziness, flushing, and dose-dependent peripheral edema, with an incidence rate ranging from ∼20% to 30%.7,8 Case reports suggest a potential link between DHPCCBs and PAEs.9,10 However, comprehensive and systematic data confirming this correlation remain lacking. Although PAEs are infrequent, they can profoundly affect patients’ quality of life. This study aims to extract adverse event report data from the FAERS database and employs various analytical methodologies, including the reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN), and Multi-Goal Particle Swarm Optimization (MGPS), to investigate signals associated with DHPCCBs and PAEs. The overarching objective is to provide preliminary signals for clinical monitoring and enhance vigilance in using these medications.

DATA AND METHODS

Data Source and Preprocessing

We obtained the original ASCII data packages from the FAERS database, encompassing a total of 81 quarters from Q1 2004 to Q1 2024 (http://fis.fda.gov/extensions/FPD-QDE-FAERS/FPD-QDE-FAERS.html). Subsequent data preprocessing was conducted using SAS 9.4 software. Following FDA recommendations, we implemented a deduplication strategy: for reports with identical CASEIDs, we retained the FDA_DT with the highest value; in instances where both CASEID and FDA_DT were identical, we preserved the PRIMARYID with the most significant value. A list of deleted reports is included in each quarterly packet starting from Q1 2019. Following the deduplication process, reports were excluded based on CASEIDs present in the list of deleted reports. Through this rigorous de-emphasis approach, we ultimately identified a total of 17,627,340 FAERS cases.

According to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system (http://atcddd.fhi.no/atc_ddd_index/), DHPCCBs are classified under ATC code C08CA and encompass a total of 17 distinct medications. Of these, eight are currently marketed in the United States, specifically amlodipine, nifedipine, nicardipine, felodipine, nimodipine, isradipine, nisoldipine, and levo-anlodipine. The first 7 DHPCCBs were all marketed before 2004, whereas levo-anlodipine was introduced to the US market in 2019. To ensure consistency and accuracy in our analysis, we utilized the World Health Organization Drug Dictionary (accessed in March 2024) to standardize and identify the eight drugs mentioned above in the FAERS database. Subsequently, we adopted these standardized names for the screening of target drugs.

In the FAERS database, each patient (each report) is associated with a unique Primary Suspect (PS) Drug. To define the target drug use population, only reports where the PS drug is one of the DHPCCBs are considered. Due to the absence of reported data for levo-anlodipine as a PS Drug in the database, the analysis was limited to data from the 7 DHPCCBs available in the United States. Patients were classified into the Target drug population if their PS Drug corresponded to the target drug under investigation in the analyzed background database. Conversely, patients whose PS drug did not align with the target drug were categorized into the Other drug population.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted for reports where DHPCCBs were listed as PS Drugs. This resulted in a total of 53,766 reports, encompassing 196,721 adverse events (AEs). The data were standardized using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 27.0). Adverse event names recorded in the FAERS database were mapped using the MedDRA Dictionary, and the most current PTs were identified. PTs were categorized according to System Organ Class (SOC), focusing on screening all PTs related to Psychiatric Disorders (SOC Code 10037175) associated with DHPCCBs. We extracted PAEs associated with DHPCCBs, resulting in 9164 reports and 11,995 AEs. The selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Data Analysis

The disproportionality analysis is a crucial analytical tool in pharmacovigilance, facilitating a comparative study of the incidence rates of drug-specific adverse events. This approach contrasts the frequency of AEs associated with the drug under investigation against those observed for all other pharmaceuticals, thereby offering valuable insights into the relative safety profile of the study drug.11 Table 1 outlines the structure of the four tables employed in the disproportionality analysis. This study employed a multifaceted approach integrating 4 distinct methodologies to enhance the reliability of our analysis of drug-adverse event correlations. Signal detection was conducted using Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN), and Multi-item Gamma Poisson Shrinker (EBGM) to evaluate the association between the seven DHPCCBs and PAEs.12,13 The results of these calculations are presented in Table 2. Generally, higher values derived from the algorithm signify a stronger signal, indicating a more substantial correlation between the drug and the adverse event.

RESULTS

Descriptive Analyses

A total of 9164 reports of PAEs were associated with DHPCCBs. Amlodipine was the most frequently reported agent, with 8452 reports, followed by nifedipine with 599 reports. In contrast, felodipine, nisoldipine, nicardipine, irradipine, and nimodipine exhibited relatively fewer reported cases. Overall, a more significant number of female patients (4704, 51.33%) were reported compared with male patients (3610, 39.40%). The age group of 18 to 64 years comprised the largest proportion of patients (3406, 37.17%). Physicians were the predominant reporters, accounting for 3382 cases (36.91%), while over half of the reports originated from North America (4651, 50.75%). Results of Positive PAE Signals

The signals of PAEs associated with the 7 DHPCCBs were analyzed using disproportionality analysis. A total of 18 PAE signals were identified, demonstrating positive signals across all 4 methodologies. Further analysis of individual drugs revealed that positive signals were detected exclusively for amlodipine and nifedipine, while no positive signals were observed for the remaining 5 DHPCCBs. For amlodipine, a total of 18 positive signals were identified, with the 3 most frequently reported cases being “Completed suicide” (n=3132, RORL=12.68), “Suicide attempt” (n=1134, RORL=6.26), and “Mental status changes” (n=197, RORL=2.12). Notably, the strongest signal was observed for “Hallucination, gustatory” (n=4, RORL=14.04). In the nifedipine cohort, only two positive signals were identified, which mirrored those found for amlodipine: “Completed suicide” (n=292, RORL=11.74) and “Suspected suicide” (n=23, RORL=17.40). This article exclusively examines the drugs that exhibited positive signals, with detailed information regarding these signals presented in Table 4.

Time to the Occurrence of PAEs Following Drug Administration

The time to the occurrence of an adverse event was calculated using the following formula: Time to occurrence=Date of adverse event−Date of initiation of the target drug. Reports lacking information on the timing of adverse events or drug administration, as well as those containing data entry errors (eg, events recorded before the initiation date), were excluded from the analysis. This approach resulted in a total of 1269 reports that provided comprehensive information regarding the timing of potential adverse events associated with the 7 DHPCCBs. The majority of patients (n=920, 72.50%) experienced PAEs within 30 days of drug initiation. However, a subset of patients continued to report PAEs beyond 180 days, as illustrated in Figure 2.

Adjusting for Sex and Age

The analysis indicates that the amlodipine group yielded 18 positive signals, in contrast to only 2 signals recorded in the nifedipine group, as depicted in Figure 3A. To minimize the effect of confounders, ROR was recalculated for 18 PTs with adjustments for sex and age. Following the exclusion of these confounding effects, 2 PTs in the amlodipine group did not meet the threshold of a ≥3 and were therefore excluded from further analysis. Final adjustments were subsequently made for the remaining 16 PTs associated with amlodipine and 2 PTs associated with nifedipine, ensuring a robust evaluation of their safety profiles. Following adjustment, 13 PAE signals were identified as statistically significant in the amlodipine group. The most frequently reported signal was “Completed suicide” [n=3132, adjusted ROR (aROR)=13.73; 95% CI: 13.22-14.26], followed by “Suicide attempt” (n=1134, aROR=8.51; 95% CI: 7.97-9.08) and “Mental status change” (n=197, aROR=2.12; 95% CI: 1.82-2.48). the strongest signal remained “Hallucination, gustatory” (n=4, aROR=45.59; 95% CI: 15.64-132.86). In the nifedipine group, the 2 PTs remained consistent with those observed before adjustment, as depicted in Figure 3B.

DISCUSSION

DHPCCBs are frequently prescribed for the management of cardiovascular diseases, particularly hypertension. As their clinical applications broaden, several associated PAEs have emerged in the literature. Early case reports indicate that elderly patients taking nifedipine have experienced acute psychotic symptoms, including confusion, delusions, suspected Munchausen syndrome, and akathisia.9,10 Moreover, a case-control study conducted in Germany revealed a significant association between exposure to CCBs and an increased risk of depression in diabetic patients.14 A large retrospective cohort study from China corroborated these findings, showing that patients using CCBs had a slightly elevated risk of developing depression compared with those on angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs).15 In addition, a meta-analysis encompassing data up to September 2021 further substantiated the correlation between CCBs and the onset of depression.16

While some studies propose that DHPCCBs may induce mood swings and psychiatric adverse reactions through mechanisms affecting dopamine neurotransmitter release, neuro-microdynamics, and interactions with the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.17–20 These mechanisms remain relatively underexplored and outdated. Consequently, there is an urgent need for more authoritative and contemporary research to investigate these pathways in depth. Given these concerns, caution is warranted in the use of this class of medications, particularly among individuals predisposed to mental health disorders.

Our analysis reveals that among the 7 DHPCCBs, positive signals related to PAEs were identified exclusively for amlodipine and nifedipine. Notably, amlodipine was reported significantly more frequently than other DHPCCBs. The majority of positive signals associated with amlodipine pertained to suicide-related events, including completed suicide, suspected suicide attempts, and suicidal ideation. Considering the existing literature, we hypothesize that DHPCCBs might elevate suicide risk through alterations in psychiatric systems, such as mood swings. In the nifedipine group, 2 positive signals related to suicide were identified; however, the frequency of these reports was considerably lower compared with amlodipine. This disparity may be attributed to the market share of these drugs. Nifedipine, as a first-generation DHPCCB, has been available longer, whereas amlodipine, a third-generation DHPCCB, offers a longer half-life and improved patient tolerance and blood concentration stability.21 Consequently, amlodipine holds a larger market share. In addition, research by Colbourne and Harrison suggests that calcium channel blockers with better brain permeability could potentially offer protection against neuropsychiatric disorders. In contrast, amlodipine is noted for its relatively poor brain permeability, which may further complicate the association between this drug and PAEs.22 It is noteworthy that while our study identified more PAE signals for amlodipine, disproportionality analysis should only be considered as exploratory to generate signals; the signals should be assessed through a case-by-case analysis and contextualized with evidence from other data sources and methods.23,24 The observed differences in signal strength among different DHPCCBs may be influenced by reporting bias (including higher prescription rates and differential reporting behaviors), and therefore should not be interpreted as direct evidence for comparative safety profiles among this drug class.

Following adjustments for confounding variables, including sex and age, our analysis indicated that suicidal signals constituted a significant proportion of the total reported cases. Notably, a higher prevalence was observed among female patients compared with their male counterparts, with the age group of 18 to 64 years exhibiting the highest incidence. This finding aligns with relevant guidelines, indicating a higher prevalence of such events in individuals over 18 years old.25

According to the original drug manufacturer’s insert from Pfizer Pharmaceuticals, the reported incidence of PAEs associated with amlodipine ranges from 0.1% to 1.0%. These PAEs encompass a range of symptoms, including sexual dysfunction, insomnia, nervousness, depression, abnormal dreams, anxiety, and depersonalization. Similarly, the documentation provided by Bayer, the original manufacturer of nifedipine, specifies that the PAEs linked to this drug include anxiety and sleep disorders. In our study, we identified 16 adverse drug event (ADE) signals, with anxiety and personality disorders aligning with the descriptions provided in the manufacturer’s documentation. This consistency suggests that our findings from the FAERS database analysis possess a degree of validity. Furthermore, our study uncovered several novel ADE signals, which we anticipate will contribute to addressing existing gaps in drug safety data.

While our data analysis suggests signal associations between DHPCCBs and PAEs, it is crucial to acknowledge that some recent studies have not corroborated this finding. A systematic review conducted by Cipriani et al,26 evaluated the use of calcium channel blockers in the management of bipolar disorder, indicating that these agents may represent a viable therapeutic option for this condition.26 In addition, an analysis of data from the Norwegian HUNT3 study, which included up to 10 years of follow-up, revealed that monotherapy with calcium channel blockers was linked to a decreased risk of initiating antidepressant medication. This observation suggests a potential protective effect of calcium channel blockers against the onset of depression.27 Moreover, an earlier retrospective case-control study concluded that the mental health effects associated with calcium channel blocker use were manageable and found no evidence supporting an association between the use of these agents and an increased risk of suicide. These contrasting findings underscore the complexity of the relationship between DHPCCBs and psychiatric outcomes, warranting further investigation to elucidate these associations.28

Limitations

In addition, our study is subject to several limitations: (1) the use of disproportionality analysis allows the detection of potential safety signals, but there is no case-by-case analysis or contextualization, further limiting the validity and significance of the analysis.24 (2) As FAERS relies on spontaneous reporting, data may be subject to underreporting, misclassification, duplication, or missing information, potentially affecting signal reliability. (3) The data reported in the FAERS database predominantly originate from the United States and Europe, which may introduce some ethnic bias. (4) Although our study adjusted for confounding factors such as age and sex, certain limitations remain. For instance, we did not account for patients’ concomitant medications or underlying comorbidities, which may potentially introduce bias and lead to misleading conclusions in our findings. Current research indicates a significant interrelationship between hypertensive disorders and psychiatric conditions. Patients with poorly controlled blood pressure exhibit higher mean anxiety scores.29,30 Given limited clinical data, we could not determine whether psychiatric AEs resulted from medication effects or uncontrolled hypertension. Further studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study identified signal associations between DHPCCBs and PAEs. The signals identified warrant further pharmacoepidemiologic studies with active surveillance designs. Current evidence is insufficient to support practice changes, but highlights the need for incorporating neuropsychiatric outcomes in DHPCCB safety evaluations. Given that most prior studies of this drug class have predominantly focused on cardiovascular outcomes, it is recommended that future research incorporate neuropsychiatric outcome metrics to enhance the evidence base for clinical medication safety.
