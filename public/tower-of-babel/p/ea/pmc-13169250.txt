Improving Timeliness of Sweat Test for Cystic Fibrosis in All Infants with Out-of-range Screen
Authors: Jaclyn Davis, Rachel Gordon, Kristen Richard, Cecelia DeBlasi, Lena Joseph, Christine Thayer, Emily Barsky, Gregory S. Sawicki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction.Early diagnosis of cystic fibrosis (CF) leads to better outcomes. CF newborn screening occurs in the United States, and the ultimate diagnosis requires referral for sweat chloride testing (SCT), ideally within 28 days of life. At our center, 60% of CF newborn SCTs occurred outside the recommended window. Recognizing known differences in CF detection by newborn screen across race and ethnicity groups, we also identified a gap in understanding of potential inequities in SCT timeliness in our center.Methods.We used the Model for Improvement to understand key drivers for late SCT, focusing on optimizing internal processes and partnership with primary care providers and patient families. We implemented iterative Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles to effect change. Additionally, we developed a tracking tool to evaluate timeliness by self-reported race, ethnicity, and primary language.Results.We improved the rate of timely SCT from 40% to greater than 80% within the first project year, which was sustained over the subsequent year. We identified disparities in SCT timeliness by demographic characteristics, with those identified as Hispanic, Black, and multiracial having lower rates of timely SCT compared with those identified as White. There was a trend toward a lower rate of timely SCT in those speaking a non-English language as their primary language.Conclusions.This work provided a framework for CF center–based improvement of the SCT process after an out-of-range CF screen, recognizing the importance of partnership with referring providers and understanding the patient voice. After uncovering disparities within our CF center, further work is needed to understand the drivers of these inequities and to develop and study solutions.

INTRODUCTION

Problem Description

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a life-limiting, autosomal recessive disease caused by variants in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene. Early diagnosis is linked to improved clinical outcomes.1,2 Newborn screening (NBS) plays an integral role in timely CF diagnosis, and the screening method used varies by state across the United States. After an out-of-range CF NBS, guidelines recommend prompt referral and completion of a sweat chloride test (SCT) within 28 days of life (DOL).3 Examination of our CF center’s data from 2019 to 2021 showed that 60% of CF NBS SCTs occurred outside this window. In addition, considering known differences by race and ethnicity in CF detection by NBS,4–7 we recognized that we lacked center-level data on potential differences in SCT timing by key demographic factors. This work was designed to improve the timeliness of SCT completion at our center and identify existing disparities related to CF diagnosis.

Available Knowledge

The process of diagnosing CF through NBS is complex and spans multiple care settings. It starts with the newborn screen itself, which in Massachusetts is a 2-tier “immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT)/DNA” process. Blood spot IRT is measured, and if elevated, the sample undergoes CFTR DNA analysis to identify the presence of 39 causative variants.8 A CF NBS is out of range if the sample has an elevated IRT and either 1 or 2 CFTR gene panel variants.

All infants with an out-of-range CF NBS should undergo prompt SCT9 to identify and treat those with CF as soon as possible. Infants with CF identified through NBS most at risk for delayed or missed diagnosis are those with 1 variant identified, as the diagnosis from screening seems less clear. Screening gene panels are designed to detect the most common variants and people with CF from minoritized groups are more likely to carry uncommon variants not represented on these panels.6,10–13 Consequently, people with CF from minoritized groups are at a higher risk of delayed diagnosis.

Rationale and Specific Aim

Our global aim was to improve the timeliness and equity of SCT for a referral following NBS at our center. We developed a Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-based aim to increase the percentage of first-time SCTs occurring within 28 DOL to 80% or more during 2 years. Secondarily, we built systems to allow the evaluation of possible disparities in CF diagnosis at our center.

We used the Model for Improvement to understand the key drivers of our low rate of timely SCT. At the outset of our project, we had an outdated process map, lacked tracking measures to follow referrals through to diagnosis, and had anecdotal evidence of confusion from community primary care providers (PCPs) and patient families. We hypothesized that a multiprong approach focusing on our internal processes, stakeholder engagement, and a disparities assessment would improve timeliness and equity of SCT following NBS.

METHODS

Context

Site

Boston Children’s Hospital is a major academic medical center, and our CF center is the largest of 3 in Massachusetts. We care for more than 200 children with CF, perform approximately 120 SCTs for out-of-range CF NBS, and 500 total SCTs per year.

Sweat Chloride Testing

SCT is the gold standard for the diagnosis of CF and relies on pilocarpine iontophoresis. The test requires advanced scheduling with a trained technician, who conducts the test for 1–2 hours with specialized equipment. At our center, testing takes place only on weekdays. There are 4 possible outcomes of the test: 60 mmol/L or higher is consistent with a diagnosis of CF; between 30 and 59 mmol/L is indeterminate, indicating that further testing is needed; less than 30 mmol/L indicates CF is unlikely; and quantity not sufficient (QNS) means not enough sweat was collected to reveal a result. QNS results are followed up with a repeated SCT, requiring the family to present for another lengthy appointment. To minimize QNS rates, our center implemented practices to wait until babies are at least 42 weeks corrected gestational age (CGA) and 2.5 kg, despite more lenient requirements put forth by the CF Foundation of at least 10 DOL, 36 weeks CGA, and 2 kg.

Interventions

Screening Improvement Process Team

We assembled a dedicated QI team for this project and included a QI analyst (R.G.), CF Center Director (G.S.S.), and Associate Director (E.B.), CF physician (J.D.), CF fellow (K.R.), CF nurse (L.J.), pulmonary laboratory supervisor (C.T.), and technician (C.D.). We met at least monthly to develop our strategy within the Model for Improvement, first developing a key driver diagram (Fig. 1), using a prioritization matrix to rank interventions, and then completing iterative Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles to test change ideas.

CF Center Processes

Much of the work centered on refining our CF center processes. We redesigned our internal process map, with our final working version shown in Figure 2. (See figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the newborn screen for CF process map, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A704.) Significant changes included outlining the process from the time of the NBS result to clarify the steps taking place before referral to our center. The working process map was shared with all those in our division caring for patient families referred for NBS.

Process map designed to capture the steps from an out-of-range NBS result until diagnosis. BCH, Boston Children’s Hospital; EHR, electronic health record.

Separately, we developed and implemented a tracking system for referred infants, recording reasons for delayed SCT and capturing demographics to inform whether there were disparities in timeliness. This database was built in the Smartsheet platform, a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant resource available at our institution.

From this tracking database, we recognized the need to create processes around no-shows and those lost to follow-up. We made a no-show/lost to follow-up dashboard and added to our internal process points to contact a patient’s PCP regarding a canceled or missed test.

Stakeholder Engagement and Communication Improvement

With input from a community PCP affiliated with our institution, we designed a survey assessing confidence in and knowledge about the interpretation of CF NBS and the guideline-based recommended follow-up testing. Surveys were programmed into REDCap and disseminated electronically to local PCPs in 2 ways: (1) we directly emailed all providers who made a referral to our center in the past 2 years, and (2) we used hospital listservs reaching offices affiliated with our hospital network. Survey responses were collected from April to November 2023 and were anonymous. The total number of providers invited to complete the survey was unknown, though estimated at more than 500 providers. Our goal was for 100% of providers to answer all knowledge questions with 100% accuracy. We planned to develop resources for clinicians targeting knowledge or process gaps identified from the survey.

For patient families, we designed and disseminated electronic surveys to assess experience. We programmed surveys into REDCap, and an invitation to complete them was offered via a quick response code at the SCT appointment. Responses were anonymous and were collected from April 2023 to August 2024. We planned to develop educational information for patient families and consider changes to our process in response to findings from the survey.

Measures

The primary measure we studied was the percentage of first-time SCT for out-of-range CF NBS occurring within 28 DOL. This was chosen to understand our compliance with the CFF recommendation that all SCT for out-of-range CF NBS be completed by 28 DOL. We tracked reasons for late SCTs to help guide subsequent intervention. As a balancing measure, we tracked QNS rates.

We obtained stakeholder-related measures at a single time point through surveys. We measured PCP confidence in (3 questions) and knowledge of (4 questions) the CF NBS and asked 5 questions about process logistics at their office. We measured patient experience with a patient-facing survey that included 12 questions assessing scheduling logistics, preparedness, and visit conclusion. (See figure 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the survey instruments for families and providers, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A705.)

Analysis

Analysis of our primary outcome measure relied on a statistical process control p-chart, which we tracked monthly. Based on consensus and review of baseline data, we determined that a goal of 80% of first-time SCTs occurring within 28 DOL would be a meaningful improvement and allow for unmodifiable patient factors delaying SCT. Centerlines were shifted to reflect our data collection period, the initiation of Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles, and when we observed 8 points above or below the center line. We similarly tracked QNS rates using a statistical process control p-chart. We created control charts using SQCpack (PQ Systems, https://www.pqsystems.com). We visualized reasons for delayed SCT using a Pareto chart. We analyzed survey data from stakeholders descriptively.

Statistical testing was performed to understand differences in the frequency of timely SCT across race and ethnicity groups, as well as between those with English versus non-English as their primary language. Equity analysis was performed using the Boston Children’s Hospital Program for Patient Safety and Quality Health Equity Analysis Tool. The Fisher exact test was selected due to the small size, and a P value of less than 0.1 was used in this exploratory analysis.

Ethical Considerations

This study was reviewed and approved as QI by the Performance Excellence Group of the Boston Children’s Hospital Department of Pediatrics, which offers a thorough consultation process to assess whether a project qualifies as QI or research.

RESULTS

CF Center Processes

We successfully reached our goal of attaining more than 80% of first-time SCTs for out-of-range NBS within 28 DOL after the first project year (Fig. 3). We continued to meet this goal for the subsequent year.

The newly developed tracking system helped inform reasons for late-occurring sweat tests, the most common of which (Fig. 4) were challenges with scheduling from both the laboratory and families, waiting until 42 weeks CGA, and late referrals. In response to these findings, we changed staffing models to increase SCT availability, accommodating periodic influxes of referrals. This increased timely SCTs. We also modified the internal criteria from waiting until an infant was both 42 weeks or more gestational age and weighing 2.5 kg or more, to requiring an infant to be at least 10 DOL, 36 weeks CGA, and weighing 2 kg or more, consistent with CFF guidelines.

PCP Attitudes and Knowledge

Thirty-two representatives from PCP offices in Massachusetts responded to our survey invitation (<10% response rate): 78% physicians, 16% advanced practice practitioners. Fifty percent had more than 20 years in practice, whereas 19% had less than 5 years. Seventy-eight percent were in private practice. There was a varied experience with out-of-range CF NBS: 16% had 0, 28% had 1–2, 38% had 3–4, and 19% had 5 or more cases across their careers.

We found 16% were not confident in interpreting out-of-range CF NBS, and 25% were unable to answer most questions from caregivers. In assessing knowledge, we found 91% correctly answered that an out-of-range CF NBS does not confer a diagnosis of CF. Eighty-four percent identified the correct interpretation for an out-of-range CF NBS, with 1 variant identified, meaning the infant could be either a carrier or have CF. With regard to referral timing, 72% did not know the recommended timeframe for SCT completion was by 28 DOL, and 41% indicated they did not know whether the race or ethnicity of the infant should impact the timing of SCT referral. (See figure 3, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which displays the provider survey results, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A706.)

Regarding the NBS results receipt and referral process, only 34% of physicians indicated that they routinely answer calls from the New England Newborn Screening Program (NENSP) and 56% reported having a system in place to ensure that an SCT was completed. Based on our survey findings, we planned, designed, and published a hospital-hosted webpage for providers specifically addressing SCT following NBS, including easy-to-find logistical information, such as the direct phone numbers to our SCT laboratory and CF coordinator, and fax number for referral faxes (https://www.childrenshospital.org/treatments/sweat-testing).

Patient Family Experience

Forty-eight families (35% response rate) completed the experience survey following their NBS SCT. Four individuals had difficulty scheduling the appointment, citing reasons including not knowing how to contact the laboratory, difficulty reaching the laboratory, and challenges placing the referral. Forty percent used resources to understand what might happen before the test, including our website (26%), the CFF website (42%), Google (26%), and family (5%). Twenty-five percent desired more information, including what to expect (92%) and the reason for SCT (42%). These findings led to an update of our hospital-hosted patient-facing webpage with key facts regarding SCT, including indications for sweat test following NBS, referral logistics, what to expect during the visit, and a clear explanation of SCT results (https://www.childrenshospital.org/treatments/sweat-testing).

Equity Analysis

Over our project period (January 2023 to February 2025), we identified significant differences in timeliness of SCT completion across race and ethnicity categories, with 55% of those categorized as White completing SCT within the recommended window compared with 36% of those identified as Hispanic and 22% of those classified as Black (Fig. 5). There was a trend toward those speaking non-English languages as a primary language to have a lower rate of timely SCT, which was not significant (Fig. 5). DISCUSSION

We present our experience completing a successful QI initiative to improve the timeliness of SCT following out-of-range CF NBS for infants seen at a single center. Early and equitable diagnosis of CF is critical to optimize nutritional outcomes and ensure access to specialized CF care regardless of race, ethnicity, or primary language. We attribute our success in improving the timeliness of SCT to a multipronged approach that focused on internal processes, partnership with referring providers, and enhancement of the patient experience. The domain within which we could effect the most significant change was optimizing our internal processes, and we made multiple iterative adaptations to our CF center’s process, leading to more than 80% of first-time SCT following out-of-range CF NBS occurring within the first 28 DOL. Assembling a project-specific team was critical to successfully evaluating and refining our existing process. For example, by designing our scheduling process to flow through a single SCT technician who was a member of our project team, we learned that our center’s twice-weekly SCT slots for NBS were driving late SCTs. Trialing a more flexible scheduling approach had a significant impact on improving SCT timeliness. To address the need for rescheduling after no-shows and canceled tests, our tracker’s automated notifications allowed us to follow up with families promptly, and this intervention also improved timeliness. Our intervention to relax internal requirements around CGA and the weight of infants undergoing NBS to conform to CFF guidelines took place at a time when we had high rates of timely SCT. We proceeded with the intervention after learning this had been a driver of late tests in our Pareto chart analysis. Although we did not see an immediate effect of this change idea, we believe it helped maintain our high rates of timely SCTs over time. Importantly, we did not see an increase in QNS rates after this change. Changes to internal processes were essential in improving rates of timely SCT at our center following NBS.

Understanding the experience and knowledge of our referrers and patient families was crucial to the success of this initiative. Based on our findings, we think that referrer uncertainty and a lack of awareness of the recommended timeframe directly lead to delays in SCT referrals and ultimately CF diagnoses. Although the NENSP makes a great effort to connect with community PCP offices following out-of-range screens, only a minority of offices report that physicians discuss these cases directly with the NENSP. It is possible that some details, including the recommended timeframe for testing, may be lost. We expect that our hospital-hosted webpage may serve as a helpful state-specific resource for providers and patients seeking information following out-of-range CF NBS. We plan to assess the impact of our webpage intervention in future work, through follow-up communications targeting recent referrers.

We were struck that 41% of primary care respondents were either unsure or incorrect about the impact of race or ethnicity on the timing of SCT referral. Neither race nor ethnicity should factor into whether or when an infant should be referred for SCT. Historically, there is a misconception that CF only affects those of Northern European descent, and this myth must be debunked. A lack of knowledge in this area could lead to later referrals for testing in minoritized groups, fueling healthcare disparities.

In our single center, we found that certain non-White groups had higher rates of delayed SCT following out-of-range NBS, a discovery made possible with deliberate tracking of referrals. Our findings are consistent with work completed by McColley et al5 describing people with CF from minoritized groups had a later age of first event for CF evaluation compared with those categorized as non-Hispanic White. This delay was associated with worse nutritional outcomes at 1 year. Considering the well-described disparities associated with race and ethnicity in the CF population,14–18 it is plausible they may begin as early as the newborn period due to screening systems suboptimally designed to detect CF in babies with diverse ancestral backgrounds.

We do not know the drivers of the differences in SCT timeliness uncovered from the present work. However, our findings suggest that misinformation or bias among referrers may impact clinical judgment around referral for SCT. Further effort is needed in our center and nationally to understand factors related to late SCT completion in non-White groups, analyzing time to referral and reevaluating the referring provider’s perceptions and family experience. Once drivers are identified, we are called to implement solutions to ensure every infant screened receives a timely diagnosis and access to available treatments.

Limitations

We describe our single-center experience in a state with multiple CF programs; we cannot describe the full scope of SCT patterns across the state, including those who may have had an out-of-range screen but were never referred for testing. We had a limited response of 32 clinicians to our PCP survey, and findings may not be generalizable statewide or nationally. This work does not address potential healthcare disparities that may be reinforced by the nature of our NBS process, which relies on a limited gene panel.

CONCLUSIONS

This work provides a framework for CF center-based improvement of the SCT process following out-of-range CF NBS, recognizing the importance of partnership with referring providers and understanding the patient voice. Ensuring a clear process map and center alignment with the guidelines is essential. Importantly, we uncovered healthcare disparities within our CF center that we were previously unaware of. Next steps include understanding the drivers of these inequities and developing solutions. Ensuring timely and equitable SCT for all infants following out-of-range CF NBS remains a priority to improve care for people with CF in our region and globally.
