Predicting bilingual aphasia treatment outcomes using digital twins: a double-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Swathi Kiran, Erin Carpenter, Uli Grasemann, Michael Scimeca, Manuel J Marte, Marissa Russell-Meill, Claudia Peñaloza, Yorghos Tripodis, Risto Miikkulainen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBilingual aphasia rehabilitation faces the challenge of determining which language to target in therapy to maximize recovery across both languages. This double-blind randomized controlled trial (48 Spanish–English bilinguals with chronic aphasia; NCT02916524) evaluated whether the BiLex computational model could predict the optimal language for aphasia therapy. Participants received 40 h of semantic feature-based treatment in either the BiLex-recommended language or the opposite language. Both groups showed similar gains in treated-language naming, with no significant difference in proportion of maximal improvement (Difference (SE) = –0.03 (0.07); t = –0.46; p = 0.65). However, the model-opposite group showed significantly greater cross-language generalization (Difference (SE) = –0.16 (0.07); t = –2.38; p = 0.02), though with higher response variability. Further, when the participants were divided into subgroups according to performance, the model-assigned group had a significant advantage in all but the lowest performing subgroups. All these differences were consistent with BiLex model predictions.Subject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Aphasia, a neurogenic language disorder that often follows a stroke or brain injury, impairs a person’s communication, social participation, and quality of life. The challenge becomes more complex for bilinguals with aphasia (BWA), as impairment and recovery in both languages can vary. Today, bilinguals represent over half of the global population and a growing percentage of the U.S population. The US Census also projects that by 2030, the Hispanic population will increase to 74 million, contributing to this growth. Further, the largest increase in stroke prevalence is expected amongst the Hispanic population1, underscoring why effective rehabilitation for BWA is so important2,3. While Spanish-English bilinguals represent the largest bilingual population in the U.S., only 8.3% of speech-language pathologists (SLPs) are equipped to provide therapy in languages other than English4. This gap underscores the need for research that can develop more effective bilingual rehabilitation approaches, specifically tailored to the needs of Spanish-English bilingual individuals with aphasia (BWA).

A critical, unanswered question in bilingual aphasia rehabilitation is what language to target in therapy and how much cross-language generalization may be observed following treatment in one language5. Cross-language generalization, where treatment gains in one language transfer to the untreated language, has practical significance in bilingual aphasia by potentially doubling treatment efficiency through improvements in both languages while treating only one. It is also scientifically important because it validates evidence that both native (L1) and second-acquired (L2) languages are co-activated in a bilingual’s brain, supporting theoretical models like the Bilingual Interactive Activation models6, Revised Hierarchical Model7, and Multilink model8. Yet, two recent meta-analyses9,10 had mixed results on improvements and generalization in multilingual aphasia following language therapy. Both studies found moderate improvements for trained words within the treated language, with limited generalization to untrained items, especially those that were semantically unrelated. Cross-language generalization was modest, showing small effects for trained translations but no significant gains for other word categories. These outcomes may be influenced by individual differences in proficiency and severity of aphasia11,12, further compounded by the variability in the type and duration of language treatment13,14. The complexity and considerable variability in key factors determining treatment outcomes make it virtually impossible to design large-scale bilingual aphasia rehabilitation studies that would generate reliable predictions about individual outcomes and guide optimal language selection for treatment. As an innovative alternative, the BiLex computational modeling approach described in this paper allows for systematic investigation of individual factors affecting language recovery in BWA at a scale infeasible with human studies alone. The BiLex architecture comprises three interconnected self-organizing maps (SOMs)15–17: one representing a shared semantic map and two representing distinct phonetic maps, one for each language. Together, these components create a digital representation of the adult bilingual lexicon that can simulate naming performance in each language. BiLex was previously validated in neurotypical bilinguals, with simulated naming scores explaining 83% and 68% of variance in English and Spanish naming abilities, respectively18. The model also successfully simulated the degree of L1 and L2 impairments individually in post-stroke bilingual aphasia when artificial lesions were introduced into the semantic and phonetic maps, with simulated naming performance accounting for 95% of the variance in patients’ semantic impairments and 98% and 97% of the variance in patients’ English and Spanish naming deficits, respectively19. Furthermore, BiLex effectively simulates treatment response by retraining the lesioned model to mimic therapy. Our prior work has shown that simulated naming outcomes explained up to 82% and 60% of variance in naming recovery in the treated and untreated languages, respectively19.

This study used BiLex to create personalized digital twins for each bilingual patient by simulating therapy outcomes in both languages prior to patients receiving therapy, based on individual variables (i.e., L2 age of acquisition, L1/L2 language exposure/use, and degree of impairment). BiLex then predicted the optimal treatment language expected to yield the greatest naming improvement in both languages. The efficacy of BiLex’s predictions was tested based on the prediction that patients treated in the model-assigned language would show superior direct and cross-language treatment gains compared to those treated in the model-opposite language.

Interim discussion

Contrary to expectations, the double-blind RCT results showed that the model-assigned treatment group did not outperform the model-opposite group in proportional maximal gains in the treated language and performed slightly worse in the untreated language. However, this result does not indicate BiLex’s failure to predict treatment outcomes. The same lack of group differences appeared in BiLex’s simulated data, suggesting that the computational model accurately predicted treatment responses despite no observable differences between groups in either observed or simulated datasets. The extensive variability in outcomes within the model-opposite group may have obscured potential significant differences between the two treatment approaches. To that end, we conducted follow-up analyses on the time series data to evaluate if different rates of improvement would provide additional context to the pre-post treatment changes.

Follow-up analysis 1. Behavioral and simulated time series results

As the first follow-up analysis, we used generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a binomial logit link function to examine the effects of probe session (Time) and treatment group on the probability of providing a correct response (naming accuracy). Each model included Time (continuous, 10 probe sessions), Treatment Group (categorical, dummy coded with model-opposite as the reference level), and their interaction (Time × Treatment Group). Additionally, to account for individual variability, random intercepts for Participant and random slopes for Time by Participant were also included see Supplementary Note 1 for details).

For observed direct treatment gains, results showed significant effects of time (β = 0.336, p < 0.001) and Treatment Group (β = 1.511, p = 0.001), indicating improved naming accuracy over the 10 weeks and better overall naming accuracy in the model-assigned group, but no significant Time × Treatment Group interaction (p = 0.592), suggesting similar treatment response trajectories between groups. Observed cross-language generalization showed no significant effects for Time, Treatment Group, or their interaction, with substantial individual variability (random intercept σ² = 5.353). Interestingly, the BiLex simulated data exhibited similar patterns: significant Time effect (β = 0.252, p < 0.001) but no significant Treatment Group effect or interaction for treated language, and no significant effects for untreated language. These results suggest that while the BiLex model accurately predicted improvements in naming accuracy over the course of treatment, after randomization the two study groups did not differ in terms of the magnitude of these gains (see Supplementary Fig. 1).

Follow-up analysis 2. Cluster analysis and individual variability

To address this substantial between-subject variability influencing treatment outcomes, we devised a data-driven approach to identify meaningful subgroups among BWA enrolled in the trial. A community detection analysis (CDA20–24) was used, incorporating bilingual language experience metrics from the Language Use Questionnaire (LUQ) and pre-treatment assessment scores from the Western Aphasia Battery-Revised (WAB-R)25, Boston Naming Test (BNT)26, and Bilingual Aphasia Test (BAT)27. Using the Louvain algorithm as part of the CDA, the analysis identified five distinct clinical profile clusters: (1) slightly L1-dominant bilinguals (defined by late L2 AoA, and reported higher daily use, lifetime exposure, and lifetime confidence in L1 relative to L2) who showed severe impairment in both languages (N = 9); (2) slightly L1-dominant skewed bilinguals (defined by higher lifetime exposure and confidence in L1, but equal languages used in their daily lives during their lifetime) who showed mild-to-moderate impairments, performing better in L1 while using both languages fairly equally (N = 5); (3) Equally proficient bilinguals (defined by equal exposure to, confidence in, and daily use of L1 and L2 over their lifetime) who showed mild impairments and roughly equal proficiency in both languages (N = 9); (4) slightly L2-dominant skewed bilinguals (defined by early AoA L2 and higher L2 use more than L1 during their lifetime despite proficiency in both languages) who showed severe impairments but better L2 performance (N = 10); and (5) highly L1-dominant bilinguals (defined by late L2 AoA, and reported higher daily use, lifetime exposure, and lifetime confidence in L1 relative to L2) who showed mild impairments and significantly better L1 performance (N = 14)28(see Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to test the efficacy of computational modeling in predicting rehabilitation outcomes for Spanish-English bilingual adults with aphasia. Below, we discuss the main findings.

Contrary to our predictions, BWA receiving therapy in the language recommended by BiLex (i.e., model-assigned group) did not show greater naming outcomes in the treated or untreated language than patients receiving therapy in the opposite language to what the model recommended (model-opposite group). The practical implication of this outcome is that BWA improved in their trained language, irrespective of which language they received therapy and whether the model had recommended that language or not. At first pass, these results would suggest that despite being provided with detailed information about individual BWA’s L2 AoA, language proficiency, and post-stroke impairment, BiLex failed to identify the optimal language for treatment. However, the model did make individual recommendations for each patient, but when evaluated in aggregate within the RCT, neither the observed or simulated data showed significant group differences. Specifically, the model simulations showed the same null group differences observed in actual patients, suggesting the model accurately captured the individual variability that would obscure group-level treatment advantages.

As Fig. 1 shows, outcomes in the model-opposite were varied, indicating that treatment outcomes are highly individualized, an unsurprising interpretation29. The initial implication of these results was that individual differences in patients led to the observed results (where some patients made improvements no matter in which language they were treated, whereas others showed limited benefits). Notably, follow up analyses of the participants revealed five clusters that differed on their bilingual language use profiles and language impairment participants. The five clusters showed distinct bilingual exposure, use and proficiency patterns, including highly L1-dominant, slightly L1- or L2-skewed, and balanced bilinguals, with post-stroke impairment patterns largely reflecting pre-stroke proficiency and asymmetry between L1 and L2. Importantly, the results showed that clusters 2 (light blue), 3 (green), 4 (yellow), and 5 (dark blue) experienced notable direct treatment gains and cross-language generalization, both observed and accurately simulated by BiLex, while Cluster 1 (orange) showed minimal improvement over time (see Fig. 3).

Further, there was little cross-language generalization, with naming accuracy in the untreated language remaining largely unchanged across clusters (Fig. 3c, d), with both observed and simulated data exhibiting near-flat lines. Only clusters 3 and 5 (mild patients) showed some cross-language generalization, and notably, improvements were higher for the model-opposite group relative to the model-assigned group. The superior cross-language generalization in the model-opposite group suggests that therapy in the sub-optimal language may activate cross-linguistic processes that enhance recovery in the untreated language, since in this group, the BWA were trained in the language that BiLex estimated was not optimal for recovery. In other words, if BiLex estimated English to be the optimal language for treatment for BWA X, this person received treatment in Spanish instead, and showed more improvements in English through cross-language generalization. In this context, the cross-language generalization results are indirectly indicative of a strong language treatment effect where training the less optimal language may enhance access to the stronger language, an observation made in our early rehabilitation studies5,13. Notably, Bilex did not capture these subtle effects as the overall cross-language generalization gains were minimal.

As noted above, BWA with milder impairments (Clusters 3 and 5) responded similarly to treatment, regardless of the therapy language or which group they were randomized to. In contrast, participants with more severe impairments made limited gains (Cluster 1), regardless of the language of therapy or group assignment. For participants in the moderate-to-severe impairment clusters (Cluster 4), those who received treatment in the model-assigned language showed better naming improvements than those who received treatment in the model-opposite language. Importantly, these different outcomes were accurately captured by BiLex in its simulations of treatment outcomes (Fig. 3a, b). Thus, BiLex accurately simulated recovery patterns and predicted treatment outcomes in BWA, especially when accounting for the individual differences across patients (captured in the five 5 cluster subtypes). Thus, while the model predictions were not borne out as expected in the aggregate, they did so when the level of patient impairment was taken into account. This novel and important finding underscores the importance of personalized treatment matched to individual phenotypes in bilingual aphasia rehabilitation.

There are four main limitations and opportunities for future work in this study. First, the complexity of bilingual aphasia recovery requires further exploration of individual factors such as cognitive abilities, social environment, and treatment dosage, which were not yet fully accounted for in this study. Second, the limited cross-language generalization effects, while consistent with the extant literature9,10, warrant further investigation to understand the mechanisms that drive cross-linguistic recovery in BWA. Third, the sample sizes within the clusters were small. Therefore, although the model was more accurate when taking proficiency and severity into account, the overall conclusions are tentative. Fourth, computational models like BiLex should be refined to incorporate neurobiological, cognitive and social factors, which may play a significant role in determining treatment outcomes. Further, a consequential methodological choice was set at 10% as the difference in the simulation outcomes that was then chosen to decide the language for treatment. Future work could examine how changing this parameter may create different results, allowing future examinations of personalizing treatment outcomes for individual patients.

In conclusion, the study highlights the complexity of bilingual aphasia recovery and demonstrates the potential of computational models to guide rehabilitation strategies. As the field of computational modeling advances, these tools will become increasingly valuable for developing individualized therapy plans that account for the unique linguistic profiles of bilingual patients.

Methods

Study design

The study design involved a double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine whether BWA who received therapy recommended by the BiLex model (i.e., model-assigned group) would demonstrate greater overall treatment gains (i.e., greater naming improvements on both trained items (direct treatment gains) and translations of trained items (cross-language generalization) compared to BWA who received therapy in the language opposite of the model’s recommendation (i.e., model-opposite group). To determine the optimal language for therapy, an individuals’ bilingual language experiences and post-stroke impairment scores were used by BiLex to simulate their response to therapy (i.e., changes in naming accuracy) in both the treated and untreated language. The modeling approach then compared the simulations and predicted which language would yield the best treatment outcomes across languages, taking into consideration both direct treatment gains and cross-language improvements. To evaluate the model’s success in predicting treatment outcomes, BWA were randomly assigned to receive therapy in either the optimal language as determined by the BiLex model (i.e., model-assigned group) or the less optimal language (i.e., model-opposite group) and behavioral outcomes were compared. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02916524, posted 2016-09-16), and ethical approval was granted by the institutional review board (protocol #4492E) at Boston University30. All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Participants

Participants were 48 Spanish-English bilinguals diagnosed with aphasia (see Fig. 4 for flow chart of enrollment). Inclusion criteria were (i) age between 18 and 85 years, (ii) any years of education, (iii) chronic (>6 MPO) aphasia secondary to stroke or focal brain damage (e.g., tumor resection) confirmed by a neurologist or CT scan, (iv) pre-stroke proficiency in both Spanish and English as determined by a previously published language use questionnaire (LUQ31,32), (v) naming deficits as evidenced by an extensive naming assessment battery, and (vi) self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and audition. Exclusion criteria included (i) multilingual (i.e., proficiency in language(s) other than just English and Spanish), (ii) comorbid active medical or psychiatric conditions or other neurodegenerative disorder. Table 1 provides the summary participant demographics (Supplementary Table 3 provides the racial and ethnic characteristics of the randomly assigned groups in the study). All participants were diagnosed with aphasia based on the Western Aphasia Battery-Revised (WAB-R)25 and underwent an extensive battery of language assessments to evaluate their lexical-semantic abilities in English and Spanish. While a detailed description of the diagnostic and treatment protocols have been detailed elsewhere30, details of specific assessments pertinent to the computational modeling are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Of note, 40 individuals reported Spanish as their L1 (8 reported English), the average age of L2 acquisition was 13.2 (SD = 11) years.

Intervention

As noted, participants were randomly assigned to one of two study groups: the experimental model-assigned group and the control model-opposite group. All participants were randomly assigned to a therapy language based on computer model recommendations, except for one (P04), whose preference was accommodated to prevent study withdrawal. The statistical analyses exclude this individual’s data. Of note, the control treatment (i.e., model-opposite) was designed to be a true-placebo: it was identical to the experimental treatment in every regard except the selection of the “optimal” treated language by the computational simulation. To strengthen the rigor of the experimental design, it was intentionally designed to not reflect treated language by classic criteria in aphasia therapy, such as the patient’s language of choice, or the language with the highest post-stroke proficiency. Irrespective of the study group, all participants received 40 h of semantic feature-based treatment (SFT)13 in either English or Spanish. The treatment was administered over 10 weeks and consisted of two, 2-h sessions per week for a total of 20 treatment sessions. The treatment included six steps that utilized the semantic features of target words to facilitate word retrieval. Treatment items consisted of 15 concrete nouns that the participant could not name in either language during pre-treatment testing enabling us to capture naming improvements in both the treated language, to examine direct treatment gains, and the untreated language, to evaluate the degree of cross-language generalization. Treatment procedures have been detailed previously30,33,34. The only difference between the experimental (model-assigned) and the control (model-opposite) group was the language in which the therapy was provided, i.e, model-assigned group received therapy in the language recommended by BiLex and model-opposite group received therapy in the opposite language to what BiLex recommended. Responses to 30 naming probe items served as the primary dependent measure administered at three baseline sessions, 10 treatment probe sessions and three post-treatment probe sessions. Treatment was discontinued after completion of 20 treatment sessions (10 probe sessions).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the change in naming accuracy, quantified as the proportion of maximal gain (PMG) ((mean of post-treatment probes – mean of baseline probes)/(mean of baseline probes)) for treatment items in the treated language (Treated Set 1). Likewise, to examine cross-language generalization, PMG was computed for treatment items in the untreated language (Untreated Set 1). Secondary outcome measures included (i) ES calculated as: (mean of post-treatment probes—mean of baseline probes)/SD of baseline) for Treated set 1 and Untreated set 1, respectively and (ii) composite PMG of both the Treated Set 1 and Untreated Set 1. Additional outcome measures, including semantic knowledge, lexical-semantic processing, comprehension, single word and sentence production, and executive function obtained from standardized measures are reported elsewhere35.

Computational modeling

The overall architecture of the BiLex computational model is described in the Supplementary Note 2 and elsewhere19. Briefly, BiLex was used to generate individualized language predictions across three simulation stages: stage one simulated the premorbid language state, stage two simulated the post-injury impairment state, and stage three simulated language over the entire treatment duration. To achieve this, BiLex was provided with patient parameters including (i) age at testing, (ii) L2 AoA, (iii) lifetime exposure to and use of each language, (v) age at stroke onset, (vi) poststroke semantic (ie, Pyramid and Palm Trees Test, PAPT scores)36 and (vii) naming performance (i.e., scores on the Boston Naming Test26, a 60-item naming screener, and baseline naming probes) in each language. In the premorbid state simulation, we first created a digital simulation (“twin”) of each patient by training an individual BiLex neural network to mirror their unique bilingual history, which included (a) delaying the training of the L2 to match the patient’s reported L2 AoA, and (b) varying the proportion of English versus Spanish words trained over time to precisely match the patient’s reported language exposure and use throughout their lifetime. The training process continued until the model’s simulated age matched the patient’s age at brain injury onset. The result was a personalized BiLex model that simulated the patient’s premorbid L1 and L2 naming abilities. As described in Supplementary Note 2, BiLex neural-network training parameters (i.e., map sizes and learning rates for SOMs and associative connections) were optimized using an Evolutionary Algorithm (EA)18. Next, we simulated L1 and L2 impairment in BWA by lesioning each patients’ personalized BiLex model to simulate stroke effects on their bilingual processing system using two alternative methods: removing either a circular portion of map neurons or removing neurons in a two-dimensional Gaussian pattern19. The lesioning process first damaged the semantic map until it matched the patient’s post injury semantic performance (PAPT score), then additionally damaged English and Spanish phonetic maps until it matched the patient’s naming performance (English and Spanish BNT scores19). Next, treatment outcome simulation was accomplished by selecting treatment words matching a patient’s baseline naming probe performance and retraining those words to approximate naming therapy session outcomes, with specific parameters governing different aspects of the retraining process (Supplementary Note 2). To create final model prescriptions, an ensemble-based approach selected the 50 highest performing combinations of (a) premorbid state training parameters, (b) impairment simulation method, and (c) treatment outcome simulation parameters from 12 successful evolutionary algorithm runs, with selection based on how well models matched simulated treatment response across all patients, both languages, and all treatment sessions while maintaining diversity by limiting selections from the same evolutionary algorithm generation. To preserve diversity, no two parameter sets came from the same EA generation, and no more than 10 were selected from the same EA run19.

Finally, to create a prescription for every new patient in the RCT, their personalized BiLex model was trained for each of the 50 parameter sets, matching the patient’s background (using pre-stroke training parameters and the patient’s LUQ data). Each model was then damaged to fit post-stroke naming and semantic performance (obtained during baseline testing). Treatment simulations were then run in pairs, once using English as the treatment language and once using Spanish. Each treatment simulation was repeated ten times to ensure that variation in possible outcomes was included, resulting in 1000 individual treatment simulations (50 parameters × 10 repetitions × 2 treatment language conditions), or 500 English/Spanish pairs. To eliminate unnecessary sources of variance, each English-Spanish pair was identical in all ways except treatment language (i.e., they used the exact same lesioned BiLex model, and the same words were treated in the same order each session). Predicted treatment outcome for each BiLex model was calculated as PMG in both languages, i.e., the proportion of possible words (re-)acquired during treatment using the same formula as described above. Using the PMG outcomes for all 1000 BiLex simulations, the treatment prescription of the ensemble model was then determined using a simple voting scheme: For each model pair, if the PMG for treatment in English was greater than that for Spanish, a vote for treatment in English was counted; better PMG for Spanish led to a vote for Spanish (ties were discarded). The language with at least a 10% vote advantage was selected for treatment, if neither language reached this threshold, the median PMG across all simulation served as a tiebreaker. This way BiLex predicted the optimal language for treatment (i.e., the language with ≥10% of all 50 simulations showing superior outcomes when treatment is provided in one language over the other) for each individual patient. Once the optimal language prediction for an individual patient was generated by BiLex, it was communicated to the clinical trials team to provide the intervention as the next step.

Randomization and group allocation

Following a non-stratified randomization design with a 1:1 allocation ratio, each patient was randomly assigned to one of 24 slots in either the experimental model-assigned group or the control model-opposite group. Patients were blinded, treating clinicians (and thus assessors) were also blinded. Further, the treating clinicians, once informed which language to use for treatment, followed the treatment protocol described earlier. Of note, 28 patients received treatment in Spanish and 20 received treatment in English.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by an independent statistician, who was otherwise not involved in the study, to assess the differences between the two groups on direct treatment gains and cross-language generalization.
