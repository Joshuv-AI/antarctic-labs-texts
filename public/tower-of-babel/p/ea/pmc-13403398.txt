Application of a typology for sustainment of Public health interventions using real-world case studies
Authors: Karen Lee, Alix Hall, Adrian Bauman, Luke Wolfenden, Melanie Crane, Nicole Nathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe sustainment of effective, scaled-up public health interventions is key to ensuring that resources are not wasted and opportunities to improve population health maintained. An initial typology of practical approaches to sustain interventions were developed, however it is unknown how frequently these approaches are applied in the real world. This study aimed to explore how scaled-up obesity preventions interventions have addressed sustainment and whether their sustainment approaches could be classified into the sustainment typologies.MethodsBuilding on previously published study that identified 90 obesity prevention interventions scaled up to at least city-wide level, we extracted relevant information from each of the interventions pertinent to their development, funding, delivery, magnitude of scale and length of sustainment. Interventions were further coded into one of two sustainment typologies, that is self-sustainment and supported sustainment. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the characteristics of the intervention and their sustainment approach.ResultsFrom the dataset of 90 interventions, a quarter of the interventions were identified as using the ‘self-sustainment’ approach, with the remaining three quarters of interventions coded as ‘supported sustainment’. Self-sustaining interventions were mostly delivered and funded by private or community organisations while supported sustainment interventions were government organisations. Over a third of the supported sustainment interventions had been found to be scaled up without prior research.ConclusionsThis hypothesis generating study revealed the presence of self-sustained and externally supported approaches to sustainment with some differences. Self-sustaining interventions were found to employ a commercialisation strategy to facilitate their sustainment and were delivered at larger magnitudes of scale. In contrast, supported interventions were mostly developed and delivered by government organisations and delivered at smaller magnitudes of scale. Our results suggests that government developed and delivered interventions tend to remain dependent on ongoing government support, rather than evolving into self-sustaining models. It is also concerning that governments may be supporting the sustainment of interventions where their evidence base is questionable. Importantly, future research needs to explore the characteristics of programs that are sustained and identify the most effective strategies for supported sustainment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00961-8.Keywords: Sustainment, Obesity prevention, Scale-up, Self sustainment, Supported sustainment

Contributions to the literatureSustainment of interventions delivered at scale are critical for ongoing delivery to achieve population health outcomes. An initial typology of practical approaches to sustainment have been hypothesized but have not been validated to determine the extent to which they occur.Our analysis of 90 obesity interventions provides support for the hypothesised sustainment approaches where we found the majority of interventions using the ‘supported sustainment’ approach while the remaining quarter achieving ‘self sustainment’. We observed that many of the self-sustaining interventions were mostly delivered and/or funded by private or community organisations while supported sustainment interventions were government organisations. These observations have, increased our understanding of how strategies for sustainment have been employed for scaled-up interventions as well as identifying future areas for research.

Introduction

In an environment where resources are scarce, the importance of sustaining effective, scaled-up public health interventions has gained increased attention [1, 2]. When such interventions are not sustained, resources are wasted and opportunities to improve population health are lost [3]. Sustainment refers to ‘the sustained use or delivery of an intervention following external implementation support’ [4–7]. Despite often being considered the end goal in the translational pipeline, it is acknowledged that sustainment needs to be considered right from the outset of program planning [7, 8].

Several frameworks have sought to improve sustainment of health interventions by identifying factors influencing their success; such as, the Exploration, Adoption/Preparation, Implementation and Sustainment Framework (EPIS) [9] and the Dynamic Sustainability Framework (DSF) [10]. Additionally, tools like the Program Sustainability Assessment Tool (PSAT) were created to evaluate the potential sustainment of interventions [11]. Strategy taxonomies such as ERIC have also begun to consider sustainment, including recent work identifying sustainment-explicit strategies [12]. However, these approaches primarily focus on determinants, evaluation, or individual strategies, rather than specifying system-level approaches to sustaining delivery at scale. While these provide a range of factors to consider when planning for sustainment, few provide specific practical approaches that can be taken to address sustainment. Increasingly, the sustainment of interventions is being recognised as being a dynamic process, where it may evolve as programs mature and funding mechanisms and organisational processes are established [13, 14].

To improve conceptual clarity and support both research and practice, an initial typology of the practical approaches used by policy agencies to sustain the implementation of evidence based public health initiatives was developed [15]. The typology comprises three key approaches (Fig. 1). The first, termed ‘Self-Sustainment’, occurs where an organisation (e.g., hospital, clinic, school) independently maintains the intervention in the absence of external (agency) support such as monetary or non-monetary support. The other two typologies fall into the supported sustainment category, with the first being ‘static sustainment’ which involves ongoing pre-defined external support from an agency to assist the organisation to continue to deliver the initiative to a level sufficient to accrue benefit either through time limited support of staff, professional development or monetary assistance. The next approach in the supported sustainment category is the ‘dynamic sustainment’ approach, which requires continued external agency support, but this support is adaptive, evolving over time to meet changing needs of the intervention and its sustained delivery. This may include monetary or other non-monetary supports.

Summary of self-sustainment and supported sustainment approaches as described by Wolfenden et al. 2024 [15]

While these typologies help clarify various approaches to sustainment, there is limited evidence on how frequently these approaches are applied in practice. Furthermore, it is unknown whether specific or shared characteristics of interventions align with each sustainment approach outlined above. Such evidence is important to examine whether the typology captures approaches to sustainment that commonly occur in practice and better characterise these.

This paper aims to bridge this gap by using the typology of sustainment approaches developed by Wolfenden and colleagues [15] to;

(i)Explore how frequently the sustainment approaches are found in practice through a sample of scaled-up public health interventions aimed at preventing obesity.

(ii)Identify a range of intervention characteristics such as length of sustainment, scale-up magnitude, intervention development lead, funding agency and scale up pathway is associated with the sustainment approach taken.

Methods

In this secondary review, we build upon a previously published study that identified 90 obesity prevention interventions scaled up to at least city-wide level [16]. These interventions were originally identified through a structured literature search focusing on scaled-up initiatives addressing obesity prevention through physical activity and diet (PANO), which updated an earlier search conducted by two authors (KL, AB). Three databases were searched (SCOPUS, Ovid Medline and EMBASE) to include literature published between January 2015 and January 2020. Search strategies were guided by PRISMA and can be found in Supplementary Material 1 & 2. Interventions were included for consideration if they explicitly targeted PANO and delivered to at least ‘city/regional’ level to be considered as scaled-up [16]. The search revealed n = 158 potential interventions for inclusion and upon closer review, n = 68 interventions were excluded due to insufficient information (n = 10), not a primary prevention intervention (n = 11), not related to PANO (n = 6), not an intervention with service delivery (n = 6), still in research stage or not scaled-up (n = 17), a grant program or umbrella strategies with multiple interventions (n = 14). Ninety interventions were included for further analysis (See Supplementary Materials 2).

Additional detail on the search methods for identifying these initiatives are described elsewhere [6, 17].

Data collection

For this secondary analysis, we used the primary data collected in the original review, which documented the timeline of each intervention, including: a) development; b) scale-up and c) ongoing operation as of 2021. This allowed us to determine the length of sustainment, that is the period from initial development to its status as at the end of 2021.

We also extracted a subset of available data from the following variables including the country of intervention development, the health prevention focus, the length of sustainment, the funding agency, delivery organisation and the magnitude of scale-up (Table 1 below). Full descriptions of all variables collected from interventions can be found in Supplementary Materials.

Coding methodology

To determine the sustainment approach used by the 90 scaled up obesity interventions, we extracted key information on both the funding source (indicating the ‘external agency’ initially supporting the interventions’ delivery) and the ‘delivery organisation’ (indicating the agency responsible for ongoing implementation) (MC and KL). Three authors (KL, NN and AH) then independently coded each intervention into the sustainment typology categories and met to discuss the results. At this meeting, all of the 90 interventions were reviewed individually to confirm the sustainment typology even for the interventions where all authors were all in agreement. Where required, source materials (ie original peer reviewed papers) were used to verify information where disagreements arose and discussions between all three authors were conducted until consensus was reached.

Coding categories

The initial coding categories were based on the overarching sustainment approaches identified by Wolfenden et al. paper [15] ‘Self-sustainment’ – which involves no ongoing support from an external agency, and ‘Supported’ sustainment - which involves external agency support, including static support (i.e., lower, consistent level of support) or dynamic support that involves adaptive, evolving support over time.

As this study relied on publicly available data, detailed information on the nature, intensity, duration, and evolution of external support was often not reported. As a result, we were unable to reliably distinguish between the dynamic and supported sustainment across interventions. Rather than risk misclassification, we took a conservative approach and combined these into a single ‘supported sustainment’ category. Interventions were therefore categorised into either (a) self-sustainment or (b) supported sustainment.

Statistical analysis

As this is an exploratory study, descriptive statistics were used to describe the characteristics of the two categories of sustainment: self-sustainment and supported. Fisher’s Exact was used to assess any differences between the two typology categories for the following characteristics: country of development, health prevention scope, length of sustainment, funder, delivery organisation and scale-up magnitude.

In terms of sustainment approaches, a quarter of interventions were coded as ‘self-sustainment’ (n = 23), while the remaining three quarters of interventions were coded as ‘supported sustainment’ (n = 67). USA/Canada was the originating country of development of nearly 60% of the interventions that were coded as self-sustainment and within those interventions, they were sustained either through funding by commercial (privately run) organisations or by users, who had to pay a fee to receive the intervention. Supported sustainment interventions on the other hand, were observed to largely received funding support from Government and/or non-government organisations (n = 46) (additional detail can be found in Supplementary Materials).

None of the interventions that were coded as self-sustainment were developed or driven by government agencies, with just over half of them starting out as research projects developed by universities or researchers. In contrast, half of the supported sustainment interventions were either co-produced with or developed by government/policy-based organisations (n = 39).

The length of sustainment of the interventions was on average 14.6 years (sd. 6.6 years) with the range of sustainment between the shortest at 4 years and the longest at 24 years. Only a quarter of the supported sustainment interventions had (as at 2021) been sustained for 15 years or longer compared to nearly two thirds of self- sustainment interventions that had been sustained over the same period.

Nearly 85% of the interventions that were in the self- sustainment category were delivered by either community organisations (48%) or by private organisations (35%). Interventions that were considered self-sustaining were reported to be delivered at larger scale, where just over 90% of were delivered either at a national or international scale. This contrasts with only 30% of supported sustainment interventions that were delivered at a national or international scale. Just over half of the supported sustainment interventions were delivered at smaller magnitudes of scale, either at the State/province level or County/regional or city-wide levels. Finally, a third of our sample of obesity prevention interventions (n = 30) were scaled up without an evidence base, that is taking a Type IV scale up pathway and n = 26 of those interventions were coded under the supported sustainment category.

Examples of interventions under each of the sustainment typologies

The following case studies below provide examples of the types of interventions categorised under the sustainment typologies. These case studies illustrate the different intervention characteristics as well as their different approaches to intervention delivery sustainment.

Case Study 1 - Self Sustainment approach- No external support

Active Living Every DayThe Active Living Every Day (ALED) intervention is described as an evidenced based behaviour change program developed through a partnership between The Cooper Institute, researchers at Brown University, The Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation [18]. ALED is a facilitated group based intervention to assist adults with learning the skills they need to become and stay physically active. The program was extensively tested prior to being scaled-up and has also been tested in the older adult population and adaptable for specific cultural groups [19, 20]. Resources to participate in and to deliver the intervention is available for purchase on the Human Kinetics website [21]. There are a range of resources available for purchase including videos and books to train facilitators to deliver the intervention. As at 2021, this intervention was still available for purchase and has been operating since 1993.

Case study 2 Supported sustainment approach: Ongoing funding provided by external agency

Get Healthy Coaching Service (GHS)The GHS is a free telephone based six month coaching service to support adults to make sustained improvements in healthy eating, physical activity, and achieving or maintaining a healthy weight [22]. This service was delivered at scale across New South Wales, Australia in 2009 and was still operating as at 2021 [23]. Funding for this service is provided the State health body and the funding for service delivery is allocated as part of the annual budget. The intervention is delivered by a not-for profit community organisation. The intervention was named as part of the ten year ‘Healthy Eating & Active Living Strategy’ [24]. Over the years, the intervention has also been evaluated, modified and adapted to meet the ongoing and emerging needs of different target populations. Additions to the service have included specific modules to meet the needs of priority populations such as pregnant women [18], Aboriginal [19], Chinese speaking populations [20] as examples. Comprehensive evaluations of the service were also undertaken to monitor service impacts [21]. This service is reliant on the provision of funding from the external agency to the delivery organisation. This service is delivered at a State/provincial level and has been sustained as at 2021 for 12 years.

Discussion

Sustaining interventions continues to be a challenge, with research demonstrating that interventions are rarely sustained beyond two years after their initial implementation [25]. The sustainment of effective interventions is critical as significant resources are expended during implementation or scale-up and the cessation of such interventions may negatively impact the community. Furthermore, the abandonment of these interventions can erode community support or trust in research or health institutions [26].

This hypothesis generating study identified the presence of sustainment typologies as described by Wolfenden and colleagues [15] by evealing differences between self-sustained interventions and externally supported intervention (static or dynamic) approaches. These sustainment typologies focus primarily on the different potential strategies employed by interventions to sustain service delivery [15]. The typology applied in this study conceptualises sustainment from the perspective of the system or external agency responsible for supporting implementation and therefore focuses on continued delivery rather than the maintenance of health outcomes. As such, our findings relate to sustainment of delivery rather than sustainment of effectiveness.

Firstly, fewer (just over a quarter) of the ninety obesity interventions, had adopted the self-sustainment approach, without reliance on an external agency to provide ongoing funding to support delivery. Over half of these interventions originated in the USA and had been sustained for at least 15 years at the time of data collection. From this, it appears that the culture of program sustainment in the US has been to self-sustain interventions, particularly in the past decades [6, 7]. The dominant sustainment strategy used by these self-sustained interventions appeared to be through ‘commercialisation/ privatisation’, which often entailed a ‘fee for service’ payable by the users. ‘Commercialisation’ as a strategy is increasingly being recognised as a mechanism through which medical technologies and services delivery can be sustained for population health improvement [27]. We observed that many of the self-sustainment interventions commenced as research programs likely funded through research grants to develop the intervention and then progressed to self-sustainment through commercialisation. This approach may have been facilitated by specific grant schemes in the USA that offer funding for the sole purpose attracting the partners and investors to commercialise the subsequent health innovations [28] however, this approach to date is less common in jurisdictions like Australia. It may however become increasingly more frequent as some of Australia’s largest funding medical research grants are now suggesting that commercialised health research could lead to better health outcomes for the population [29].

Interventions developed by government or policy organisations on the other hand, typically remain in the ‘supported sustainment’ category. This may suggest that government developed and delivered interventions tend to remain dependent on ongoing government support, rather than evolving into self-sustaining models. It does then pose the question whether supported sustainment models can ever become truly ‘self-sustaining’ without adopting some aspect of commercialisation or fee for service strategy.

Interestingly, self-sustained approaches were found to operate at larger scales (i.e. national and/or international) while supported interventions were more likely delivered either by community or government organisations, typically at smaller scale (i.e. at the local community level). It is however unclear from this study whether it is the different funding models or the delivery scale that facilitates the intervention’s self-sustainment approach. While there appears to be some support for funding strategies that promote reliance on grant cycles for sustainment [30], it was also observed that many of the supported sustainment interventions were funded by governments, the level of scale tended to reflect the ‘level of government’ providing support. For example, local or provincial level government funding also then funded services to their local constituents only. This may be because local community based or government organisations are limited in their ability to effectively manage large-scale interventions, particularly, as interventions are scaled up to greater magnitudes, the complexities and costs of their delivery also increase.

Within supported sustainment interventions, we observed the presence of varying levels of support from external agencies. For example, at the lower end of the spectrum, some external agencies provided minimal support, such as maintaining websites with downloadable resources like manuals and/or guides for intervention delivery. On the other hand, higher levels of support provided by external agencies included direct services provided to the community or population such as face-to-face training or in-person service delivery. These findings suggest that the level of support provided by external agencies can vary considerably depending on the nature of the intervention and the type of support required for its continued delivery. Although the typology distinguishes between static and dynamic forms of supported sustainment, this distinction could not be reliably examined in the current study due to limited reporting on how support was provided or adapted over time. As such, our findings relate to the broader category of supported sustainment and further work is needed to test the presence of sub-types.

Finally, we noted that over a third of the supported sustainment interventions had been scaled up without a research pathway [16], implying a questionable evidence base. While the reasons for why some interventions scale-up without evidence of effectiveness are not always clear, evidence suggests that in some cases, the need to take advantage of certain ‘windows’ of opportunity triggers this pathway [31, 32]. Often this means that it is less about the features of the intervention that lends itself to scale-up, rather it is contextual factors such as availability of funding or strong political support that expedites its scale-up [31, 32]. This raises concerns that ‘external agencies’ may be providing support to sustain interventions that are ineffective or worse, may cause unintended negative consequences. Without a solid evidence base, there is a risk that these interventions may not deliver the expected health outcomes, potentially wasting resources or damaging public trust in health initiatives. Where situations like these occur, that is, if the research pathway is circumvented, we must have robust methods of evaluating and monitoring that the intervention is effective, is not causing harm and delivers sustained positive effects as it is scaled and maintained [33].

It is important that future research in this area explore the characteristics of programs that are sustained and identify the most effective strategies for supported sustainment. While there is a significant body of evidence on effectiveness of strategies for implementing evidenced based interventions [33] there is still limited understanding of which strategies are most effective for sustaining them long term. A recent systematic review by Riley-Gibson et al. in 2025 [34] found only three RCTs assessing strategies designed to support the sustainment of chronic disease prevention initiatives in clinical and community settings. Although the review identified a range of strategies, such as developing and distributing educational materials, preparing champions, and providing ongoing training, the evidence on their effectiveness was inconclusive. This highlights the challenge of determining how best to sustain interventions without placing an excessive burden on both the external agencies responsible for supporting their implementation (eg the government or NGO) and the internal agencies responsible for the direct delivery of the programs (e.g., schools or hospitals). Until stronger evidence emerges, sustainment strategy taxonomies or glossaries such as the sustainment-explicit ERIC glossary [12] could be helpful by identifying a wider range of sustainment strategies, which can then be tested in future research.

Limitations

This was a hypothesis generating study using secondary analysis which meant that the original dataset was not specifically collected to identify sustainment approaches. In particular, the data collected did not include details on their specific implementation study type nor implementation strategies used nor did it include data on health outcomes achieved by the intervention and its ongoing sustainment. Furthermore, reliance on publicly available data meant that important features of external support including its type, intensity, duration and whether it changed over time were often not reported. Consequently, we were unable to reliably distinguish static from dynamic supported sustainment and collapsed these into a single supported sustainment category. This limits the extent to which the full typology could be empirically examined in the current study. Future research using prospectively collected data should more explicitly capture how external support is delivered and evolves over time to enable clearer distinction between sustainment approaches. We also assessed sustainment in terms of continued delivery only and were unable to determine whether intervention effectiveness was maintained over time. The small sample size consisting of interventions predominantly delivered in high-income countries limits the generalisability about the nature and strategies used by the interventions for sustainment. Furthermore, the small sample and exploratory nature of this study restricted our analytic approach to simple, univariate analyses which are appropriate for a hypothesis generating study. Future studies could seek to confirm this in larger and more diverse samples that allow for more complex, multivariable analyses can be conducted potentially across different contexts. This study also did not identify how successful these approaches were to achieve sustainment and whether it led to the maintenance of health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study validates, using real world case studies, the existence of two sustainment approaches, that is self-sustainment and supported sustainment in obesity prevention interventions. While self-sustainment approaches are less common, it has mostly been achieved through adopting a commercialisation approach, while supported sustainment has largely been for government initiated interventions which they continue to support. While a number of potential sustainment strategies were identified in this study could further robust testing of their effectiveness is needed.
