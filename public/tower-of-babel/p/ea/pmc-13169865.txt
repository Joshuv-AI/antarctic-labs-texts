Impacts of preoperative kidney function and acute kidney injury on long-term kidney failure after cardiac surgery: a registry-based cohort study
Authors: Dominic Keuskamp, Christopher E. Davies, Robert A. Baker, Kevan R. Polkinghorne, Christopher M. Reid, Julian A. Smith, Lavinia Tran, Jenni Williams-Spence, Rory Wolfe, Stephen P. McDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury is a common complication of cardiac surgery and may lead to kidney failure. In a large sample sourced from national registries, we estimated the risk of kidney failure up to nine years following cardiac surgery and the associations with acute kidney injury, comorbidities and preoperative estimated glomerular filtration rate.MethodsData were linked probabilistically between population-based registries to identify adults with kidney failure (commencement of long-term kidney replacement therapy) following cardiac surgery. Risk of kidney failure accounting for the competing risk of death was estimated from 30 days following surgery using Fine-Gray models. Surgeries from 2010 to 2018 were included (n = 90 605) with follow-up until the end of 2018.ResultsA total of 465 adults (0.51%) developed kidney failure during follow-up and the cumulative incidence at five years was 0.65% (95% CI 0.59, 0.73). The risk of kidney failure increased with decreasing estimated glomerular filtration rate. The rate of any acute kidney injury was 26.2% and all stages were associated with kidney failure. For Stage 3 acute kidney injury compared with none, the adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio of kidney failure exceeded 4.4 at all intervals following surgery.ConclusionAcute kidney injury presented an enduring risk of kidney failure following cardiac surgery, independent of baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate. People comorbid with diabetes also experienced a high adjusted risk of kidney failure. All patients could benefit from perioperative management optimized to prevent acute kidney injury and regular postoperative nephrology follow-up for more than a year to mitigate the burden of kidney disease on the health system.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04922-0.

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) causes a growing global health burden affecting more than one in ten people worldwide [1]. As the fatal end stage of CKD, kidney failure (KF) is treatable with kidney replacement therapy (KRT), however treatment is burdensome to individuals, communities and health systems [2]. Cardiac surgery is one of the higher risk settings for the development of KF, owing in part to acute kidney injury [3].

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and serious surgical complication that reflects a complex interplay of underlying pathology, surgical stress and clinical management [4]. Short-term mortality associated with AKI is high, but survivors may yet develop CKD and KF after the postoperative period [3, 5]. While a gradient of heightened risk of KF across injury severity has been demonstrated, even small postoperative increases in serum creatinine have been associated with 3- to 4-fold increases in long-term risk [3, 6–8].

The long-term prognosis of cardiac-surgery-associated AKI (CSA-AKI) should be considered in the context of baseline kidney function [9]. Candidates for surgery are frequently comorbid with CKD as it increases their cardiovascular disease risk [10]. Other comorbidities of high prevalence in the surgical population, e.g. hypertension and diabetes, are widely acknowledged as risk factors for CKD progression [11] and have been associated with a higher incidence of postoperative KF [8, 12]. Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is the dominant cardiac surgical procedure and is most commonly represented in studies estimating the risk of progression to KF [13]. Some reports suggest more complex procedures, such as valve surgery, may incur a higher risk than CABG [14–16].

Estimating the risk of developing KF among cardiac surgical populations may therefore contribute to effective management of early interventions and postoperative care. However, few robust reports on the long-term postoperative development of KF following cardiac surgery exist, as identified by systematic reviews [3, 13]. This study estimated the incidence of KF (defined by the need for long-term dialysis or kidney transplantation) following cardiac surgery using a large sample drawn from data linkage between two national clinical quality registries in Australia. The association of KF with AKI stage was measured across levels of preoperative kidney function, accounting for comorbidities and surgical procedure type.

Methods

Data source and study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study based on data collected from population-based clinical quality registries. The study population comprised people aged ≥ 18 years undergoing cardiac surgery in Australia between January 2010 and December 2018, as reported to the Australian and New Zealand Society of Cardiac and Thoracic Surgeons (ANZSCTS) Cardiac Surgery Database. The ANZSCTS Cardiac Surgery Database was established in 2001 and by the end of 2018 included 40 contributing hospitals across Australia [17]. Mortality data were sourced through data linkage with the National Death Index. Incident KF was estimated from incident KRT, i.e. all people for whom there was a long-term intention to treat with dialysis or received kidney transplantation, as identified from data linkage with the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant (ANZDATA) Registry. The ANZDATA Registry was established in 1977 and captures data from all people commencing and receiving long-term dialysis or kidney transplantation in Australia and New Zealand since 1963 [18].

Probabilistic data linkage between ANZDATA and ANZSCTS registries was undertaken by a third party (SA-NT DataLink) based on names, dates of birth and death, sex and postal code. Subsequently, manual review was undertaken of those cases coded for preoperative dialysis and/or kidney transplant in the ANZSCTS Cardiac Surgery Database. All data were extracted from the ANZSCTS Cardiac Surgery Database except for dates of KRT commencement, which were extracted from the ANZDATA Registry.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all valid surgeries from 2010 to 2018 in the original cohort (see Fig. 1 for data flow). Cases were then excluded where, as reported to the ANZDATA Registry, KRT had commenced before the date of surgery (N = 1 268) or where the patient was coded as recovered kidney function (9), lost to follow up (1) or deceased (3) before surgery, with the last indicating questionable matches from data linkage. Cases coded in the ANZSCTS Cardiac Surgery Database for preoperative dialysis or with a preoperative estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73m2, indicating KF, were excluded (N = 771). Cases where mortality occurred, KRT commenced, or the study period ended within 30 days of surgery were also excluded (N = 2 897), with follow-up commencing at 30 days. The study cohort numbered 90 605 cases.

Data definitions

Kidney failure was defined as the commencement of KRT, as reported to the ANZDATA Registry. Time -to-event analysis accounted for the competing risk of death and was censored for the end of the study period (31 December 2018). The calculation of eGFR used the 2009 CKD-EPI equation, excluding the race multiplier [19]. This implementation has been routine in Australia given the low “Black race” population and is in line with European recommendations and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines published since the development of the 2021 CKD-EPI equation [20, 21]. Preoperative eGFR was categorized to stages according to KDIGO 2012 criteria [1] and using the last preoperative creatinine value (only available). Postoperative AKI was staged according to KDIGO creatinine criteria as follows: Stage 1, 1.5–1.9 times baseline or ≥ 26.5 mmol/L increase; Stage 2, 2-2.9 times baseline; Stage 3, 3 times baseline or increase to 353 mmol/L or initiation of dialysis [22]. The highest postoperative creatinine value was compared with the last preoperative creatinine value. No further data on the timing of creatinine collection were available from the ANZSCTS Cardiac Surgery Database. Neither did the dataset contain coding for urine output, serum albumin, hemoglobin, bicarbonate, lactate, or inflammatory markers. Variables are defined in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics at the time of surgery were summarized with percentage for categorical variables and median (interquartile range) for continuous variables. Cumulative incidence curves were used to visualize probabilities of incident KF accounting for the competing risk of death. A Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to evaluate the association between KF and measured variables, quantified by subdistribution hazard ratios (‘subhazard’ ratios) [23]. Variables included were sex, age group (18–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69 and + years), presence of comorbidities (hypertension, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease and diabetes), body mass index (BMI, < 25 kg/m2, 25 to < 30 kg/m2, ≥ 30 kg/m2), preoperative eGFR, procedure type (isolated CABG, isolated valve, valve with CABG, aortic procedure, other), ejection fraction estimate (> 45%, 30–45%, < 30%), inotropes use, prolonged perfusion time (> 120 min), red blood cell (RBC) transfusion and postoperative AKI. The primary analysis included all variables as independent covariates. For the secondary analysis, we utilized an interaction term between preoperative eGFR and postoperative AKI to determine whether any AKI-associated risk varied by levels of baseline kidney function. For both analyses, data were restructured and time-dependent weights calculated for computational efficiency [24]. The proportional hazards assumption was tested on the basis of scaled Schoenfeld residuals. To address non-proportionality in the context of competing risks, landmark analysis was used, i.e. cause-specific hazards were modeled at distinct landmark times [25].

By defining KF as the commencement of KRT, we anticipated some underestimation of the true incidence of KF. The ANZDATA Registry does not report non-treated KF, but national Australian estimates indicate that > 90% of KF is treated in all age groups < 60 years [26]. To address this, we undertook sensitivity analyses by utilizing the primary analysis but with only the population (a) < 70 years and (b) < 60 years at the time of surgery.

Data were missing for at most 2.8% of any variable (AKI) and 5.2% of cases had data missing for at least one variable. Missingness was assumed to be at random. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in Stata version 17.1 (StataCorp, TX).

Results

Description of patient cohort

The majority of patients were male (73.7%) and the median age at the time of surgery was 67 years (Table 1). Just over half of procedures (50.8%) were isolated CABG. Hypertension was particularly prevalent (71.9%) among comorbidities, followed by diabetes (28.9%). Postoperative AKI was present for 26.2% of surgeries, with 3.0% of the cohort having experienced stage 3 AKI. Of the stage 3 AKI cases, 53.2% required dialysis.

From 90 605 cardiac surgeries in 2010–2018, 465 (0.51%) people developed KF during a median follow-up time of 3.4 years (interquartile range 1.6, 5.7). The overall cumulative incidence of KF at five years post-surgery was 0.65% (95% CI 0.59, 0.73) (Table 2). Five-year incidence was especially high (> 17%) in patients with preoperative eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73m2, almost ten-fold higher than in those with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2. Among procedure types, incidence was lowest for isolated valve procedures (0.33%), less than half that for isolated CABG (0.88%). That pattern was evident across all age groups and especially marked for those aged under 60 years (Supplementary Table S3). At five years of follow-up, cumulative incidence accounting for the competing risk of death was over 6% for the AKI stage 3 cohort compared with 1.2% for stage 1–2 AKI (Fig. 2).

Multivariable analysis

In the primary analysis, the model initially had non-proportional hazards, so landmark analysis was used. Three models were employed with landmark times set at 30 days (baseline), 1 year and 3 years following surgery. This resulted in intervals of 30 days-1 year, 1–3 years and 3–9 years with cohort sizes of 90 605, 76 945 and 51 419 respectively.

Adjusted risk of KF tended to decrease with increasing age group (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S1). Of the measured comorbidities, only hypertension (1–3 years interval) and diabetes (1–3 years and 3–9 years intervals) remained associated with KF following adjustment in the primary analysis. For diabetes, risk was over 4.5-fold higher than in those with no diabetes at 3–9 years. In the same interval, patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 had a significantly lower risk of KF (subhazard ratio (SHR) 0.62) compared with those with a BMI < 25. Compared with isolated CABG procedures, those undergoing isolated valve surgery had less than 1/3 risk of KF at 3–9 years and those undergoing valve with CABG procedures also had lower risk at that interval (SHR 0.56). No difference in the risk of KF was observed between aortic procedures and isolated CABG. Inotropes use was associated with lower risk of KF at 30 days-1 year and 1–3 years intervals (SHRs ≤ 0.22). Lower ejection fraction (30–45% compared with > 45%) and RBC transfusion were associated with elevated risk, at 3–9 years and 30 days-1 year respectively.

Risk increased with decreasing preoperative eGFR and for those with eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73m2 was over 38-fold those with reference eGFR at any interval. The risk of KF for stage 1–2 AKI was higher than in those with none (all SHRs > 2.5) and was especially high for stage 3 AKI (all SHRs > 4.4).

In the secondary analysis that included the interaction between preoperative eGFR and postoperative AKI, the interaction was significant for stage 1–2 and stage 3 AKI at 3–9 years (Supplementary Material Table S2). For patients with an eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73m2, the relative risk of stage 3 AKI was also attenuated in the first year of follow-up only. The interaction was visually represented with SHRs for levels of eGFR by AKI stage, all with reference to eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2 (Supplementary Material Figure S1).

In the sensitivity analyses which employed the primary analysis but analyzed the population (a) under 70 years or (b) under 60 years at the time of surgery, we found no differences in overall trends and no sizeable differences in SHRs to those found for the full cohort (Supplementary Material Tables S4, S5). Adjusted risk of KF was higher for those aged 18–39 years compared to those aged 50–59 years at 3–9 years following surgery for populations (a) and (b), as it was for the primary analysis.

Discussion

Utilizing data linked between two national clinical quality registries, we had hypothesized that the risk of kidney failure would be higher in patients who had AKI following cardiac surgery. Indeed, we found an increased risk of KF in patients with stages 1–2 and stage 3 of CSA-AKI, independent of measured comorbidities and surgical procedure type. Stage 3 AKI increased the risk of subsequent KF more than four-fold (compared with no AKI) at all intervals following surgery, while even the smaller increase in serum creatinine measured by stage 1–2 AKI increased risk more than two-fold. Baseline kidney function in itself was strongly associated with KF. Preoperative eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73m2 increased risk more than 38-fold compared with reference eGFR, however it significantly attenuated the relative risk of stage 1–2 and stage 3 AKI at 3–9 years following surgery.

Our report was consistent with those from a diverse range of cardiac surgical populations that found even stage 1–2 AKI was strongly associated with the long-term risk of KF [6, 8]. Initiation of dialysis in response to AKI is a distinct component of the measurement of stage 3 AKI and likely contributes to the disproportionately higher risk for that group of requiring long-term dialysis. Additionally, severe AKI is more likely to be associated with acute tubular necrosis, which can lead rapidly to development of KF due to maladaptive repair mechanisms, failed tubule recovery and disordered regeneration.

Baseline kidney function was inversely related to the incidence of postoperative KF, consistent with the few other studies that have measured both parameters in cardiac surgical settings [6, 8, 14, 27]. Although CSA-AKI is a common complication, pathophysiological pathways between AKI, CKD and KF remain to be elucidated and causal relationships are intertwined [4]. Moreover, we found that the relative effect of AKI on KF risk was less pronounced for patients with lower baseline eGFR, a finding consistent with reports from cardiac surgery and critical care settings [6, 28]. However, the absolute risk of KF was so high for patients with low eGFR that this finding should not detract from the essential imperative of avoiding CSA-AKI. Indeed, our analysis demonstrated the enduring risk of AKI and that careful management of volume, blood pressure and bypass time is critical for all patients.

The high risk of KF for cardiac surgical patients with diabetes or hypertension in this study is consistent with others [8, 12]. Both conditions are widely acknowledged as key risk factors for CKD progression and coronary artery disease [10]. Diabetes in particular plays an ever increasing role in the growth of KF, accounting for over a third of incidence in Australia [18, 29]. Moreover, meta-analysis has shown that study cohorts with a higher prevalence of diabetes or coronary artery disease had higher associations between AKI and mortality [3]. Substantially increased risk of KF for patients with diabetes beyond the first year of follow-up is consistent with an accelerated tendency toward KF with chronic disease progression.

The inverse association of BMI with adjusted risk of KF in this study has some precedent. While obesity is a widely acknowledged risk for KF progression in the general population, several studies conversely suggest that lower BMI may be more of a risk in the context of moderate to advanced CKD [30, 31]. Obesity in patients undergoing cardiac surgery has also been persistently associated with lower immediate postoperative morbidity and mortality [32, 33]. The causes for these apparent paradoxes remain unclear. One contributing explanation for the associations between BMI and short- and long-term outcomes could be more vigilant medical care and cardioprotective pharmacotherapy for patients with high BMI. Alternatively, low BMI could be a marker for frailty, malnutrition or cachexia. The unavailability of data on parameters such as those and the relatively small number of patients with a BMI < 18.5 in our study (N = 1 153) did not allow us to disentangle the association between BMI and KF.

We reported a lower risk of KF following valve surgery procedures, which are more complex and typically associated with worse immediate postoperative outcomes than CABG [34]. One US cohort study found the inverse to ours, i.e. that the incidence of postoperative dialysis increased with the complexity of the surgical procedure [15]. However, two studies reporting data from the Swedish Web-System for Enhancement and Development of Evidence-Based Care in Heart Disease Evaluated According to Recommended Therapies reported that the association between AKI and KF risk was similar following CABG and following aortic valve replacement [6, 7]. Patients undergoing CABG are more likely to be older, have higher comorbidity and more established, multivessel coronary disease. These factors predispose them to kidney damage. While these characteristics were accounted for explicitly or by other covariates in our analysis, unmeasured confounders are likely to remain.

The inverse association between inotropes use and kidney failure in the adjusted models for the first two intervals warrants comment as the reverse would be expected. Inotropes use was positively associated with a higher risk of KF in a univariate Fine-Gray model of kidney failure (data not presented) and the event rate was low (Table 2) thus we suggest the negative association found in the multivariable model is artifactual.

The inverse relationship between age and KF risk that we found may seem prima facie counterintuitive, especially for people under 60 years of age where the majority of KF is treated with KRT [26]. However, we suggest that selection for cardiac surgery leads to younger cohorts with relatively more aggressive systemic disease, and subsequently kidney disease. This is supported by the similar risk of long-term mortality after cardiac surgery reported for age groups from 18 to 70 years [35]. Few studies have explicitly reported the relationship between age and KF risk after cardiac surgery, although no significant relationship was found in a prediction model of KF following CABG [12].

With regard to overall KF risk following cardiac surgery in comparison to general population incidence, we note that the reported range of the annual KRT incidence rate for the national population aged 65–69 in 2010–2018 (encompassing the median age at surgery in the study period) was 0.028–0.035 per 100 persons [18, 36]. However, cumulative incidence estimates as reported in this study represent absolute risk within a selected population of patients undergoing surgery and are therefore not directly comparable to population-based incidence estimates across a calendar period.

Strengths and Limitations.

The principal strength of this study is the comprehensive and generalizable dataset sourced from data linkage between two long-standing national clinical quality registries. This study comprises one of the largest reported samples of cardiac surgeries for this type of analysis [3] and the inclusion of people with KF commencing KRT from all clinical units in Australia in the study period.

We acknowledge that, as a retrospective observational cohort study, the associations we found herein are not reliable indicators of causality. The use of secondary data meant that measurement of eGFR and AKI was limited to those variables available from the ANZSCTS Cardiac Surgery Database. Only the immediate preoperative creatinine measure was available, while KDIGO recommends two measures at least three months apart to stage CKD [1]. Acute illness, preoperative hemodynamics or admission factors could have affected creatinine and led to misclassification of kidney function. Preoperative proteinuria and urine output were not available. The highest postoperative creatinine had been recorded at any time after surgery, while KDIGO recommends within 48 h of the index procedure [22]. This could have inflated AKI incidence by capturing non-surgical insults or delayed peaks, leading to misclassification bias.

Given (a) the trend for increasing non-treatment (and therefore measurement) of KF with age and (b) the advanced age of the study cohort, we had noted the potential for bias and addressed this with sensitivity analyses. However, our results suggested this bias did not unduly impact on the findings from our primary analysis. Finally, we acknowledge the underestimation in the true incidence of KF in this study since groups of patients (a) with preoperative dialysis or preoperative eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73m2 and (b) with KF or mortality within 30 days of surgery were excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study from a large cohort sourced from national registries contributes the first population-based evidence of the risk of long-term KF proceeding from cardiac surgery in Australia. The increased risk of KF associated with even stage 1–2 CSA-AKI highlights the critical importance of AKI prevention, while also the rigorous measurement of injury severity in the estimation of risk. The enduring influence of pre-existing CKD or its risk factors (especially diabetes) on the emergence of postoperative KF underscores the importance of early detection of CKD, which remains largely preventable through modifiable risk factors, and long-term nephrology follow-up following surgery. Collectively, these findings reinforce the need for improved perioperative surgical and perfusion management for all cardiac surgical patients with particular focus on reducing the incidence of CSA-AKI. These improvements to clinical care would moderate KF progression and ameliorate the significant burden of kidney disease on the community and the health system.
