Is it safe using organ-sparing surgery in place of complete resection?
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang
Published: 2026

Surgical resection is one of the best approaches in management of various kinds of benign or malignant diseases,1–5 not only offering the definite removal of the disease- and/or tumor-involved tissues or organs, but also providing the tissue-based clinicopathological parameters to comprehensively evaluate the severity and extent of the disease, and accurately predict outcome and subsequently provide the risk-stratification–guided adjuvant therapy.6–9 Additionally, surgical resection itself may play a critical and essential backbone therapy for disease control and offer the chance to achieve the curability, even though many situations may not need a surgical approach.10 However, when multimodality approach for cancer treatment is applied, particularly for those patients with localized and/or isolated primary tumors and/or recurrent tumors with oligometastatic site, surgery may not only offer the enhancement of subsequent and adjuvant therapy but also provide the chance to achieve the potential curability based on removal of all potential chemoresistant strains and decreasing the tumor burden which may enhance the following therapeutic responses.10,11 Recently, due to far-advanced development of biotechnology and deepening understanding of tumor characteristics and tumor microenvironment (TME),12 the cancer treatment has been significantly improved, and the aim of cancer therapy is not only to provide the longer disease-free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS) and even cure the disease but also to consider the better quality of life (QoL).13 Among these, organ reconstruction and/or organ-sparing treatment become more and more popular, and this dream has come true due to the availability of many advanced technologies, such as regenerative medicine, organ transplantation, and three-dimensional–printed custom-made prosthetic reconstructions, etc, which offer the basic or alternative function of damaged organs and/or tissue.13 Therefore, a minimally traumatic organ-sparing approach to establish the physiological and morphological function of damaged tissues and/or organs can be made if accurate and precise preoperative evaluation and an appropriate surgical plan are conducted to achieve this goal, without any need for new but still “developing” advanced technologies.13 However, life-saving is a sacred mission during the disease treatment, particularly dealing with cancer patients. “Oncology safety” is still the most paramount and critical concern, contributing to the biggest challenge and trouble to making any decision.9,10

The publication in the October issue of the Journal of the Chinese Medical Association (JCMA) entitled “Comparison of operative outcomes between proximal and total gastrectomy for proximal gastric cancer,” showed the feasibility of using organ-sparing surgery (proximal gastrectomy [PG]) in the management of patients with proximal gastric cancer (GC).3 Proximal GC (defined as the tumor located in the upper third of the stomach) can be either treated with PG plus double tract reconstruction (PG-DTR) or treated with total gastrectomy plus Roux-en-Y reconstruction (TG-RY) as shown by the authors.3 The authors attempted to compare the operative outcome between two operations, and 263 GC patients (28 for PG-DTR and 235 for TG-RY, respectively) were enrolled.3 The results showed that PG-DTR may take the advantages of fewer post-operation (post-op) hospital stay, better post-op nutrition status (higher post-op/pre-operation [pre-op] body weight ratio, higher post-op/pre-op serum albumin ratio and fewer needs of iron replacement) compared with TG-RY, although PG-DTR had increased the risk of reflux esophagitis and was complicated with fewer number of the retrieval lymph node.3 Based on the aforementioned findings, the authors concluded that PG-DTR was associated with better post-op nutritional status and comparable op complication compared with TG-RY.3 I congratulated the success of their publication and also appreciated their effort to consider the recent much popular concept of using less traumatic approach in the management of various kinds of surgical illnesses.2,13–15 The current study is interesting and worthy of further discussion.

Based on fewer number of the retrieved lymph node in the PG-DTR procedure, the authors failed to explain the “meaning,” since some studies showed the absolute number of the retrieved lymph node may involve in the prognosis.2 Additionally, the authors did not mention the reason why the higher proportion of the patients undergoing PG-DTR needed post-op adjuvant chemotherapy. Moreover, the authors did not mention that the tumor size was significantly smaller in the PG-DTR group. Although some of them did not reach a statistically significant difference between the two groups, the significant difference of background data may influence the interpretation of their results.

As shown by the authors, weakness of patients undergoing PG-DTR included too few number of patients, too short post-op follow-up period, and higher percentage of minimally invasive surgeries (MIS).3 For any introduction of “new technology or new treatment strategy,” as shown by authors as PG-DTR available since July 2018 for treating cancer patients and the fewer number undergoing this new surgical approach (28 PT-DTR vs 235 TG-RY) during the study period (January 2000 and October 2022), although this new technology has been available more than one and half of year, the issue of “oncology safety” may not well accepted in their institute, since the majority of the patients were still treated with conventional standard of care surgical procedure like TG-RY, even though this procedure belonged to the more-invasive and destructive procedures for treating proximal GC patients. The authors seemed to neglect this part. By this viewpoint, I, in person, supposed that only “a few” or very few doctors performed PG-DTR to manage the patients with proximal GC. Since the surgical skills are also an important critical factor to involve in the outcome evaluation,16 the authors may have concerned about this.

In conclusion, although I demonstrate weakness in the current study and the careful interpretation of data to minimize the risk of misjudgment should be done, there is no doubt that the current study is worthy of applause. Multimodality treatment strategy is playing a much important role for treating the cancer patients, since based on its optimal sequencing order and timing,17 it can be applied to cancer patients before the definite therapy, such as using neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) to down-stage of tumor and shrink tumor extent or size of after the definite therapy, playing as a rescued method, such as adjuvant therapy to those patients without adequately definite treatment or with the presence of poor prognostic risk factors which may result in the future therapeutic failure.1,4,6,9,10 All efforts should attempt to provide the less traumatic approach with minimally functional impairment and achieve the better QoL of the remaining life of the cancer patients but of the most importance, guarantee the oncology safety.1,8 Like the early issue18 and the same issue3,4 of the JCMA, all give an evolving treatment to cancer patients using the multimodality treatment strategy to successfully control the disease and maintain the maximal function of the diseased organs and the prognosis of these patients undergoing the multimodality treatment is not inferior to the old believed “standard of care” therapy. As an editorial member, I deeply respect Dr. Lan’s efforts to take care of cancer patients, and their attempts to precisely identify targeted cancer patients who need management are always welcome.
