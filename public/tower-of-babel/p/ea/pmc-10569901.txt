Delayed esophageal anastomotic complication and ramucirumab therapy: A case report
Authors: Robert H Roth, Madison J Malfitano, Matthew Reilley, Linda W Martin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent NCCN guidelines for second‐line therapy in recurrent or metastatic esophago‐gastric cancers recommend the use of VEGF inhibitors such as ramucirumab. VEGF inhibitors have been shown to be associated with gastrointestinal perforation in clinical trials and late colorectal anastomotic leaks in a few case reports. Here, we present a case of late esophageal anastomotic leak in a patient receiving ramucirumab. Case information was obtained from our institution's electronic medical records. The patient was found to have T4N1M0, poorly differentiated invasive adenocarcinoma and subsequently received neoadjuvant chemoradiation followed by hybrid Ivor‐Lewis esophagectomy 6 weeks later. He recovered well with no leak or perioperative complications. The patient had disease progression 9 months postoperatively on CT and PET imaging. Sixteen months after surgery he began paclitaxel and ramucirumab and 16 weeks after ramucirumab initiation, he was found to have an esophago‐pulmonary fistula in the region of the anastomosis. Biopsies were negative for recurrence at the anastomosis. He died one week later from progressive pneumonia despite stenting. In conclusion, this is the only known report of delayed esophageal anastomotic complication associated with ramucirumab. VEGF inhibitor therapies such as bevacizumab have been associated with late (greater than 3 months postoperative) colorectal anastomotic complications including fistulas and leaks. Risk factors that have been associated are perioperative radiotherapy and history of early postoperative leak. These findings raise concern whether VEGF inhibitor therapy should be used in post‐esophagectomy patients with recurrence if these rare but catastrophic events are likely to be terminal.Keywords: anastomosis, esophagus, fistula, ramucirumab

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer accounts for a major burden of global cancer related deaths. 1 , 2 Incidence rates have been rising in Western countries with consistently low survival rates over recent years. 1 Most patients present with dysphagia and already have locoregionally advanced disease. Newer therapies and regimens are changing how this disease is treated. The current standard of care for nonmetastatic esophageal and esophagogastric junction tumors c‐stage T2‐T4aNx is fluorouracil‐based perioperative chemotherapy alone or chemotherapy with 41–50 Gy radiation followed by surgery. 3 Adjuvant nivolumab therapy improves disease‐free outcomes in R0 resected patients without pathological complete response to induction therapy. Despite these improvements, distant recurrence remains common after curative intent therapy.

First‐line systemic therapy for recurrent disease is dependent upon perioperative therapy received and time to recurrence. With recent approval of chemoimmunotherapy combination regimens as frontline treatment and/or adjuvant therapy, patients eligible for second‐line therapy will have shown progression on checkpoint inhibitor (CPI) therapy. There are ongoing trials investigating the addition of perioperative CPIs to chemotherapy that will continue to increase the number of patients exposed to immunotherapy early in their disease course. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendations include the addition of ramucirumab with paclitaxel or as a single agent for second‐line treatment of esophageal adenocarcinoma and GEJ adenocarcinoma. 1 , 2 With increased use of CPI in earlier line settings, ramucirumab use may become more prevalent in the second‐line setting.

Ramucirumab is an antiangiogenic agent that binds to the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor responsible for angiogenesis, inhibiting pathway mediated activation at endothelial cells. Angiogenesis has been shown to potentiate tumor progression and metastasis. 1 , 2 , 4 The addition of ramucirumab in the REGARD and RAINBOW trials has been shown to improve overall survival and progression‐free survival. 5 , 6 There have been multiple reports of gastrointestinal (GI) related complications associated with VEGF inhibitors including GI perforations and early and late colorectal anastomotic leaks. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 There has been no literary evidence that ramucirumab has been associated with esophageal perforations, or esophageal anastomotic complications.

We present a case report of a 59‐year‐old man with an esophageal adenocarcinoma who presented with a late esophago‐pulmonary fistula after starting ramucirumab for recurrent metastatic disease.

CASE REPORT

A 59‐year‐old obese male with a history of myocardial infarction and type 2 diabetes mellitus reported several months of dysphagia, food regurgitation, and 60‐pound weight loss, ultimately leading to hospitalization. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) showed a fungating mass at the distal esophagus, which was a T4N1M0, moderately to poorly differentiated, HER2‐neu negative, invasive adenocarcinoma. During the endoscopy a percutaneous gastrostomy tube was placed and nutritional supplementation initiated. He received 5040 cGy of radiation in 28 fractions and carboplatin/paclitaxel. During this time, he continued with full liquid oral diet and tube feeds via gastrostomy tube. Restaging showed stable disease. He underwent a hybrid Ivor‐Lewis esophagectomy 6 weeks following conclusion of neoadjuvant therapy. 10 The surgical margins were 3.0 cm from the distal resection and 11.5 cm from the proximal resection. Radial margin was negative. There was some treatment response, with downstaging to ypT2N2 poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with signet ring‐cell features. He recovered well and resumed an oral diet after contrast esophagram showed no evidence of leak prior to discharge. Surveillance computed tomography (CT) scan at six‐months showed no evidence of recurrence or metastatic disease. However, his next CT scan 3 months later identified a small left upper quadrant subcutaneous nodule, positive for esophageal adenocarcinoma (HER2‐neu negative, 10% PD‐L1 positivity). Positron emission tomography (PET) also revealed bilateral upper abdominal and left lower neck involvement. He began palliative chemotherapy with capecitabine 11 months after resection.

CT imaging at 14 months post‐resection revealed disease progression within the abdomen; treatment was switched to pembrolizumab. Despite this, his disease progressed over the next 3 months, and paclitaxel and ramucirumab was begun 16 months after surgery. He underwent four cycles of this therapy, receiving 11 doses of paclitaxel and seven of ramucirumab. Approximately 16 weeks after initiating ramucirumab, he was hospitalized for aspiration pneumonia. His most recent dose of ramucirumab was 8 days prior to this hospitalization. A swallow study showed evidence of a fistula between the esophagus, in the region of the anastomosis, and the right upper lobe of the lung (Figures 1, 2, 3). Ramucirumab was discontinued. An EGD showed a mucosal defect at the prior anastomosis that led into a pulmonary abscess cavity (Figure 4). There was no indication of local tumor recurrence, and biopsies in the region of the anastomosis and fistula did not show malignancy. The remainder of the gastric conduit appeared normal. A Merit Medical EndoMAXX 23 mm × 120 mm stent was placed across the defect. Follow‐up esophagram showed distal retrograde reflux around the stent, with ongoing extravasation into the right upper lobe, substantially improved from prior (Figure 5). Initial discussions focused on avoidance of oral intake while this healed and use of either total parenteral nutrition (TPN) or replacement of the jejunostomy (J) tube. Unfortunately, recannulation of the J‐tube could not be accomplished because of multifocal abdominal wall recurrent tumor in close proximity to the J‐tube site. Given the patient's metastatic tumor burden and comorbidities, he was not a candidate for open surgical fistula repair or diversion. After discussion of extremely limited therapeutic options and goals of care, palliation and comfort were prioritized. The patient wanted to attempt to resume oral intake, knowing there was a risk of ongoing aspiration. Unfortunately, he experienced another aspiration event 1 week later and died 26 months from the initial diagnosis, 22 months from resection, due to aspiration pneumonia and respiratory failure.

DISCUSSION

This is the first known report of delayed esophageal anastomotic complication in a patient receiving ramucirumab. Bevacizumab has been associated with late (>3 months postoperative) colorectal anastomotic complications including fistula formation at or distant to the anastomotic site. 7 , 8 , 11 In cases reported by August et al., spontaneous dehiscence occurred more than 1 year postoperatively, within a range of 1–10 months of starting bevacizumab treatment. 8 A literature review by Deshaises et al., reported 18 cases of late colorectal anastomotic complications associated with anti‐VEGF therapies. 11 The main associated risk factors were low anterior resection for rectal cancer, perioperative radiotherapy, and history of early postoperative leak. This group extrapolated these risk factors to be associated with early granulation tissue formation or chronic inflammation and thus poor wound healing at the anastomotic site. 9

Aside from postoperative status, a baseline risk of GI perforation associated with bevacizumab has been reported. Saif et al. discussed multiple clinical trials investigating the safety and efficacy of bevacizumab, most notably the BRiTE study and First BEATrial. 9 , 12 , 13 Incidences of GI perforation were as high as 3.4%, with statistically significant associated risk factors including an intact primary tumor, with the majority of perforations occurring at the tumor site, recent endoscopic procedures, or previous adjuvant radiotherapy. 9 The hypothesized pathophysiological reasoning for these perforations was extrapolated from animal models that showed decreased capillary bed density. 9 , 14

While GI and anastomotic complications have been well‐described for bevacizumab, less is known about the risks associated with ramucirumab. In 2015, Wang et al. performed a meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials to assess the risk of GI perforation associated with ramucirumab. 4 The relative risk of GI perforation for patients taking ramucirumab was 2.56 (95% CI:1.29–5.9), which was statistically significant despite a low incidence of 1.5%. 4 An additional literature review was limited to three abstracts reporting GI complications associated with ramucirumab, with only one being a late anastomotic leak of an ileosigmoidostomy. 15 , 16 , 17 The clinical implications of ramucirumab therapy and these complications is the high reported mortality rate of 29.8%. 4 While there are no evidence‐based guidelines on management of these complications, ramucirumab should be discontinued in the setting of GI perforation per manufacturer's recommendations. 18 In most cases, these catastrophic complications lead to disruptions or indefinite cessation of all systemic therapy in patients with advanced disease. 6 , 8 , 9 , 11 , 13 , 16

To date, there have been no reports of esophageal anastomotic complications related to ramucirumab. The risks of ramucirumab may be extrapolated based on the complications of other anti‐VEGF therapies, such as late colorectal anastomotic leak, and the risk of GI perforations reported in randomized control trials of ramucirumab. In selected patients, there may be increased risk of anastomotic complications associated with ramucirumab use following esophagectomy.

Additionally, studies that investigated ramucirumab therapy such as the RAINBOW and REGARD trials only included a few patients that would have had prior exposure to CPI, whereas many patients are now treated with CPI prior to ramucirumab. Retrospective or prospective observational studies for patients receiving second‐line therapy could help to identify patients at high risk for postoperative leak and other complications, deterring clinicians from initiating anti‐VEGF therapies. With increasing therapeutic options and improving outcomes for these patients, multidisciplinary management of patients will remain important after surgical intervention. Given the risk of severe morbidity with complications post‐esophagectomy, close observation is recommended if starting patients on VEGF inhibitor therapy and warrants further research to risk‐stratify patients.
