ERICA-trio: an outgroup-free deep learning method for topology inference and introgression detection
Authors: Yubo Zhang, Weifan Lv, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic admixture is a widespread phenomenon across diverse organisms. Conventional algorithms for inferring species relationship and detecting introgression often rely on outgroup data, which can be challenging to obtain and may introduce bias. Given the demonstrated efficacy and flexibility of neural networks in topology inference and introgression detection, we developed a deep learning-based method, ERICA-trio, to reconstruct evolutionary relationships among three taxa without requiring outgroup data. We trained and evaluated this network model using extensive simulated data that cover a broad range of evolutionary scenarios and parameter spaces. Our results demonstrate that ERICA-trio achieves accuracy and robustness comparable to the outgroup-dependent model. Leveraging the predicted topological proportions, we employed two strategies to identify genomic regions with potential introgression: one based on topological symmetry, and the other on the proportions of topology corresponding to gene flow. Both approaches were highly effective, particularly for detecting signatures of adaptive introgression. We further applied ERICA-trio to real genomic data from the Heliconius butterflies and successfully identified adaptive introgressed loci associated with mimicry wing patterns. In summary, our work extends the application of deep learning frameworks in evolutionary genomics, and presents a new tool for outgroup-free phylogenetic inference and introgression detection.Keywords: evolutionary relationship, introgression, gene flow, deep learning

Introduction

Inconsistencies between gene trees and species phylogenies are widespread, primarily arising from incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) due to ancestral polymorphism, and from introgression, the transfer of genetic material between species or populations via hybridization and backcrossing [1]. When introgressed alleles benefit recipient species, they can drive adaptive evolution, as evidenced by human adaptation to high altitude [2], mimicry wing-patterns in Heliconius butterflies [3], and toxicant resistance in fish [4]. As introgression is now recognized as a pervasive force shaping the tree of life and adaptive landscapes [5], the precise detection and quantification of historical gene flow events, along with the identification of introgressed loci, have become key objectives in evolutionary genomics.

Among the numerous methods developed for detecting gene flow and introgression, one major category focuses on jointly modelling of demographic history, including divergence times, population sizes and gene flow events. Popular implementations such as G-PhoCS [6], BPP [7], and fastsimcoal2 [8] belong to this category, and these methods tend to be computationally intensive and are generally designed for global introgression analysis rather than for pinpointing specific local introgressed loci. Another category of methods reduces computational complexity by conducting summary statistics for genomic data, e.g. estimating allele frequencies or gene tree proportions. A prominent example is the ABBA-BABA test (D-statistic) that uses a four-taxon comparison to distinguish signals of introgression from those of ILS based on topological asymmetry [9]. Originally developed to infer genome-wide gene flow, the D-statistic has adapted for scanning local introgressed windows across the genome [10]. Another approach, Twisst, identifies local introgression signals by weighing conflicting gene trees [11]. Its extended version, Twisst2, infers ancestral recombination graphs (ARGs) and evaluates topology in sequence, thereby reducing confounding signals from recombination [12]. QuIBL also utilizes gene tree information, primarily employing branch length distributions to infer the presence of global gene flow [13].

Recently, machine learning and deep learning algorithms have been widely adopted in population genetics, leading to the development of various introgression-detection methods [14], including the approaches that utilize population genetic statistics as input (e.g. FILET [15], ArchIE [16], and MaLAdapt [17]), as well as those that directly employ sequence alignment data as input (e.g. ERICA [18], genomatnn [19], and IntroUNET [20]). As supervised learning models, these approaches are typically trained on simulated data and, with the exception of ERICA, usually require predefined demographic histories specific to the studied taxa. Consequently, although these methods show potential for extension to other taxa, the robustness of the models requires further validation. ERICA, on the other hand, is inspired by classical statistics-based methods, e.g. allele-frequency and tree-based approaches. It directly estimates topological proportions from raw sequence data in sliding windows, and subsequently identifies local gene flow signals based on topological discordance, which minimizes dependence on explicit population demographic models [18].

Similar to the D statistic and Twisst, the ERICA pipeline infers the rooted tree topology using data from at least four taxa, including an outgroup. Therefore, the application of these methods can be constrained in practice when a suitable outgroup taxon is unavailable. The allele-frequency based f3 statistic [21] and D3 statistic [22] circumvent this limitation by detecting admixture or introgression with only three populations. However, these methods may be less effective when the data violate their assumptions. For example, D3 is best suited for closely related taxa and is potentially sensitive to substitution rate heterogeneity [22]. Motivated by this requirement and the inherent flexibility of deep learning algorithms, we recognized the potential for developing a new method that likewise operates without an outgroup. Therefore, building upon the original ERICA framework, we developed a novel tool, ERICA-trio, by modifying both the input data and the network architecture. ERICA-trio relies solely on multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) from three ingroup taxa, thereby enabling topology inference and introgression detection without requiring outgroups. We assessed the accuracy and robustness of ERICA-trio across a broader parameter space, and implemented and evaluated two strategies for gene flow detection: one based on topological symmetry and the other based on the proportion of introgressed topologies. Our results demonstrate that ERICA-trio achieves performance comparable to ERICA, while effectively avoiding the issues related to excessive outgroup divergence or the presence of outgroup gene flow.

Methods

Design principles of ERICA-trio

The ERICA pipeline uses a deep learning-based approach for detecting introgression. This method is highly flexible and has been successfully applied to datasets involving four and five taxa [18]. However, it relies on outgroup genotypes, which may limit its applicability in certain scenarios. Given its accuracy on prior datasets and its supervised learning framework, the method shows promise for further extension. To overcome this limitation, we developed ERICA-trio, which does not require outgroup information (Fig. 1). ERICA-trio takes MSA data as input and employs pre-trained convolutional neural networks to infer evolutionary relationships among taxa in sliding windows (Fig. 1A). By evaluating the relative proportions of different topologies, it identifies genomic regions undergoing introgression (Fig. 1B). Specifically, taking three focal taxa as an example, there are three possible rooted tree topologies: ((P1, P2), P3), ((P1, P3), P2), and ((P2, P3), P1). The proportions of these three topologies were used as data labels and training targets for the neural network, with the MSAs of the three taxa serving as input data (Fig. 1A).

Model design of ERICA-trio. A, ERICA-trio infers phylogenetic relationships among three taxa without relying on outgroup data. The CNN model takes MSAs as input, with each taxon represented by eight sequences and a window length of 5 kb. The DNA sequence is encoded as a one-hot numeric array. The model outputs the proportions of the three possible rooted tree topologies, the values of which sum to 1. The demo sequence alignment and input matrix shown in the figure are both 50 bp in length. B, In the absence of gene flow, the species tree topology is the most prevalent, while the two alternative topologies are expected to occur with equal frequency. However, gene flow between P3 and P1 (or P2) asymmetrically increases the proportion of the topology that matches this history, making it more common than the other alternatives.

Based on the predicted topological proportions, two filtering criteria were employed and evaluated to further detect and distinguish gene flow-induced topological changes from ILS in ERICA-trio (Fig. 1B). The first criterion applies a predefined cutoff based on topological proportions, which can be determined using data from scenarios without gene flow or from the scenario with the strongest ILS. The second criterion relies on symmetry, where under the absence of gene flow, the proportions of the two alternative topologies are expected to be equal. Thus, a significant deviation suggests potential gene flow.

Data simulation

To generate the training and test datasets, we used the coalescent simulator ms [23] to simulate gene trees and used Seq-Gen [24] to generate MSA with the GTR nucleotide substitution model. The dataset comprised four taxa (P1, P2, P3, and an outgroup) with eight haplotypes for each taxon. The outgroup was used only for calculating data labels and subsequent comparisons with methods based on four taxa. Population parameters for each simulation were randomly sampled from the parameter space. Specifically, the divergence time of root (tR), (P1, P2) and P3 (t123), and P1 and P2 (t12) were sampled from 3 to 10, 2 to tR–0.2 and 1 to t123–0.2 in units of 4Ne generations, respectively. On average, two-thirds of the data included gene flow between ingroups. The time of gene flow was set to 0.5 to t12–0.2, and the ratio was set to 0 to 100%. The recombination rate (4Neρ) and substitution rate (4Neμ) were set to 0.005 to 0.05, and 0.001 to 0.5 in a log-uniform distribution, respectively. The length of MSA was set to 5 kb. A training dataset of 120 000 MSAs and a test dataset of 12 000 MSAs were generated independently (dataset D1). All parameter combinations and simulation commands are available at figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31916211). The Twisst pipeline [11] was used for summarizing the topological weights of each dataset.

To increase data complexity and better reflect real genomic data, the raw sequence was randomly replaced with ‘N’ to represent missing data, with a proportion ranging from 0 to 40% (dataset D2). In dataset D3, the sample size for each taxon was set to between 1 and 8, with sequences randomly selected from the original samples.

To comprehensively evaluate the robustness and generalization ability of ERICA-trio, we constructed a series of additional test datasets. First, we simulated scenarios with unsuitable outgroups, including cases with gene flow between the outgroup and ingroup, or highly divergent outgroups. In the former, the divergence times tR, t123, and t12 were set to 1, 2, 3, respectively. We modeled gene flow in one direction per scenario, allowing either introgression from the ingroup (P1, P2, or P3) into the outgroup or the reverse. The time of gene flow was set to 0.5, with an admixture proportion of 50%. In the latter, tR was set to 3, 5, 10, 20, and 30, in units of 4Ne generations. For all scenarios, the recombination rate and substitution rate were both set to 0.01, each MSA was 5 kb in length, and 100 replicates were generated per parameter combination.

Second, we introduced missing genotypes as consecutive sites rather than a random distribution, with the proportion per sequence ranging from 0 to 50%, to better simulate realistic patterns in genomic data arising from technical artifacts such as sequencing gaps and alignment failures.

Third, we generated test datasets in which population split times fall entirely outside the range used for model training, including much more recent divergence times. Specifically, tR, t123, and t12 were sampled from 0.3 to 1, 0.2 to tR–0.02 and 0.1 to t123–0.02 in units of 4Ne generations, respectively, while gene flow timing was set to 0.05 to t12–0.02. Other parameters were kept identical to those used in the training set. 1000 5 kb MSAs were generated. All simulation commands are available at figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31916211).

To further assess the efficacy of the method in detecting adaptive introgression, we employed the software msms [25] to simulate scenarios with and without selection. For neutral introgression, gene flow was modeled as a continuous process from P3 to P2 or from P2 to P3, with migration rates (4Nem) set to 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, and 5. For adaptive introgression, selection started at 0.5 × t12 with selection coefficient (2Nes) randomly sampled between 200 and 2000. As the negative control, we used the corresponding neutral and selective sweep scenarios simulated without gene flow, with all other parameters remaining unchanged. Two sets of divergence times were used: one ranging from 1 to 10 and the other from 0.1 to 1, in units of 4Ne generations, corresponding to the deep- and shallow-divergence datasets described earlier. The recombination rates, and mutation rates were set as previously described. The length of each data was set to 50 kb and 100 replicates were generated for each scenario.

Network architecture and model training

MSAs were transformed into two-dimensional numerical arrays via one-hot encoding as input for a convolutional neural network (CNN), with one axis representing alignment sites and the other representing sequences (Fig. 1A). This encoding also accommodates multi-allelic sites without requiring special filtering. Furthermore, ERICA-trio flexibly handles both phased and unphased diploid data. For unphased heterozygous sites, users can either randomly sample a single allele or directly input both unphased sequences. The CNN employs a combined architecture of residual and dense convolutional networks, adapted from the four-taxon ERICA model [18], with an input dimension of 96 × 5000. For the ERICA-trio model training, the datasets were first randomly partitioned, with 90% assigned to training and 10% to validation. Using rooted topological proportions as labels, the loss function—defined as the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted outputs and true labels—was minimized via the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001. The network was trained for three epochs with a batch size of 32. Training was conducted on a single NVIDIA A800 80GB GPU and completed in approximately two hours. During model training, the MAE gradually decreased and eventually stabilized. To enable subsequent comparisons, we also retrained the four-taxon ERICA network using the same dataset but including the outgroup sequences.

Performance comparison between ERICA-trio and other methods

To benchmark our deep learning approach against classical gene tree-based methods in phylogeny inference, we used the RAxML algorithm [26] to construct the maximum likelihood tree for each 500 bp window and calculated the topological weights using Twisst [11]. We also evaluated two ARG-based algorithms, which can jointly infer recombination breakpoints and phylogenetic trees. The Twisst2 workflow employed the sticcs approach for ARG inference, followed by topological weight calculation [12]. For the Bayesian method Singer [27], we generated 10 posterior samples and set the mutation rate for each MSA data according to the simulation parameters. The output of Singer was then converted to a tree sequence format, enabling topological weight calculations via Twisst [11], after which we averaged the weights across the 10 samples. The MAE between the true value and the estimated value was then calculated.

To assess the power in detecting introgression, we compared ERICA-trio with the ABBA-BABA test, one of the most widely used approaches. D and fd statistics were calculated using the tool ‘ABBABABAwindows’ from the package ‘genomics_general’ [10]. In addition, the three-taxa D3 statistic was calculated based on pairwise sequence divergence (dxy), computed using the ‘popgenWindows’ script from the package ‘genomics_general’ [10]. These methods were applied to datasets simulated by msms in the presence and absence of gene flow and/or selection. For each 50-kb window, calculations were performed at 5-kb intervals, with the mean and standard deviation estimated via bootstrapping [28]. Finally, the f3 statistic was calculated using the “qp3pop” function from the R package admixtools 2 [29], with a block size of 5 kb. Standard errors were estimated using the block-jackknife method. Windows with a Z-test P-value below 0.05 were identified as introgressed.

Application of ERICA-trio to real genome data

Resequencing reads for the Heliconius melpomene races (H. m. aglaope and H. m. amaryllis) and H. timareta thelxinoe were obtained from the NCBI SRA database (four individuals per taxon, accession numbers: PRJEB1749 [30], PRJEB11772 [31], and PRJNA73595 [3]). The reads were aligned to the H. melpomene v2.5 genome [32] using Bowtie2 [33]. Genotypes were subsequently called using the GATK v3.7 ‘UnifiedGenotyper’ tool [34]. The consensus sequence alignments were obtained using the ‘vcf2MSA’ tool in ERICA-trio and the trained model was then applied to predict topological proportions for each 5 kb window. All six possible permutations of the order of the three taxa were computed sequentially using the script ‘ERICATrioPrediction_avg.py’, and the mean value of the results was used in subsequent analyses.

Results

The ERICA pipeline was originally designed to infer phylogenetic relationships and detect introgression signals involving three or four target populations with one outgroup [18]. In practice, however, selecting a suitable outgroup for introgression studies can be challenging, since the outgroup must meet two key requirements: no gene flow with the ingroup taxa and an appropriate level of sequence divergence. If gene flow exists between the outgroup and ingroup, the topological structure may be misidentified. For example, in a four-taxon scenario with a species phylogeny of (((P1, P2), P3), O), introgression between P1 and O would cause the clustering of the unaffected taxa (P2 and P3, Supplementary Fig. S1A). Consequently, both the topology- and allele frequency-based method (e.g., the ABBA-BABA test) would erroneously identify this scenario as gene flow between P2 and P3 (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Similar effects have been observed in the case with gene flow between O and P2 (Supplementary Fig. S1A). On the other hand, more distant outgroups may exhibit clearer species boundaries but may also result in reduced alignment rates or a decrease in the number of informative sites. For the ERICA method, excessive sequence divergence may exceed the applicable range of the model, thereby increasing errors. For example, we found that when other parameters remained constant, the model error was significantly influenced by the divergence time of the outgroup (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Given that the deep learning models can infer phylogenetic relationships directly from sequence data without requiring manually specified root nodes or prior information such as derived alleles, we believed that this capability could reduce or eliminate reliance on outgroup data. Therefore, we modified and improved the ERICA method to reconstruct phylogenic relationships without requiring outgroup sequence data. We refer to this modified framework as ERICA-trio (Fig. 1). Specifically, we focused on the case of three ingroup taxa, yielding three possible rooted topologies (Fig. 1A). We employed a neural network to process sequence alignments from the three taxa and predict their topological proportions (Supplementary Fig. S2). The ERICA-trio model was trained and validated on simulated datasets (Supplementary Fig. S3A) with the training data covering a broad range of population genetic parameters to improve the model’s generalization ability. Parameters including the divergence time, the direction, time, and intensity of gene flow, recombination rate, and substitution rate were randomly selected from the predefined parameter space (Fig. 2A, B). To enable direct comparison, we also trained a four-taxon ERICA model on the same dataset (D1) with MSA including outgroup data (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

An independent test set was used to assess model errors. To verify the independence of the test data from the training set, we assessed sequence-level similarity. K-mer distribution analysis revealed minimal overlap, as nearly all k-mers occurred only once across the training set, the test set, and their combined set (Supplementary Fig. S4A). We also selected a subset of sequences from both datasets and calculated the dxy values between them, with dxy exceeding 0.7 across all sampled pairs (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Together, these results confirmed that sequence similarity between the training and test sets was extremely low, supporting the suitability of the test set for the unbiased evaluation of model performance.

Evaluation results indicated no significant difference in MAE between the ERICA-trio and the four-taxon ERICA models. The median values of MAEs were 0.035 and 0.034, respectively. Both were significantly lower than that of the phylogenic inference results based on the maximum likelihood method, which also required outgroup data (median MAE: 0.074), as well as the two ARG-based methods, Twisst2 (median MAE: 0.105) and Singer (median MAE: 0.151) (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S5A). Further analysis indicated that, with the exception of the window-tree-based method, recombination rate had no substantial effect on error. In contrast, substitution rate had a significant impact across all methods (Kruskal–Wallis test P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S5B, C). Both higher and lower substitution rates generally led to increased error, although the variation was relatively small for the deep learning models. Notably, at low substitution rates, Twisst2 exhibited a lower error rate than the window-tree-based method, which was comparable to that of ERICA-trio, while Singer consistently showed the highest error rate across all conditions. These results suggest that by providing rooted tree information during model training, ERICA-trio can effectively infer the topological relationships among taxa and determine the position of the root nodes without relying on outgroup sequences.

To better approximate real genomic data, we first introduced missing sites (dataset D2) and then varied the number of sequences per taxon (dataset D3), thereby creating datasets of progressively increasing complexity (Fig. 2B). These modifications led to a significant increase in model errors for both the ERICA-trio and four-taxon ERICA models, even surpassing the errors of the tree-based method (Fig. 2C, D). This decline in performance resulted from the limitation in model training, which did not cover the complex datasets. Therefore, we retrained the models separately based on the corresponding datasets (D2 and D3, Supplementary Fig. S3C–F), demonstrating that the new models can accurately infer topological relationships despite missing data or variable sequence numbers (Fig. 2E–H). The models trained on dataset D3 achieved median MAEs of 0.045 (ERICA-trio) and 0.046 (four-taxon ERICA), significantly lower than the median MAE of the tree-based method (0.082, Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001) and the ARG-based method Twisst2 (0.111, Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001, Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, the models showed robust performance with no significant difference or slightly lower errors observed on the low-complexity datasets D1 and D2 (Fig. 2G, H), and were therefore employed for subsequent analysis.

On this basis, we evaluated whether ERICA-trio could prevent errors introduced by inappropriate outgroups. Regarding gene flow between ingroup and outgroup taxa, taking the species tree (((P1, P2), P3), O) as an example, gene flow from either P1 or P2 to the outgroup does not alter the relationships among the three ingroup taxa, which remain consistent with the ((P1, P2), P3) topology. In line with this expectation, the proportion predicted by ERICA-trio for this topology under these scenarios showed no significant difference from either the true or predicted values under the null scenario without gene flow (Wilcoxon test, P > 0.05, Fig. 3A). In contrast, outgroup-based analyses showed a significant reduction in the species topology proportion and failed to correctly resolve the relationships among the ingroup taxa under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Alternatively, when the direction of gene flow was from O to P1 or O to P2, ERICA-trio supported clusters of populations without gene flow (P2 and P3 or P1 and P3, respectively) (Fig. 3A). While these results faithfully captured the true phylogenetic relationships of the ingroup taxa, attribution errors may occur due to the presence of ghost gene flow. Moreover, as expected, ERICA-trio was entirely unaffected by the split time of the outgroups (Kruskal–Wallis test, P = 0.69, Fig. 3B), as the outgroup sequences were not utilized in the analysis, suggesting ERICA-trio offers a solution to avoid outgroup bias.

We further evaluated the impact of key simulation parameters on model errors. The errors of ERICA-trio were significantly influenced by substitution rates and data missing rates (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 0.001, Fig. 3C), while the impacts of recombination rates and sample size were relatively minor (no significant difference between groups, Kruskal–Wallis test, P > 0.05, Fig. 3C). A U-shaped relationship was observed between model error and the substitution rate, the parameter determining sequence divergence. Slightly elevated errors were caused by either excessively high or low divergence levels. Similar trends were also observed in the four-taxon ERICA model and the tree-based method, and error also showed a slight positive correlation with the missing rate (Fig. 3C). In nearly all subgroup comparisons, the errors of ERICA-trio were significantly lower than those of the tree-based method and the ARG-based method Twisst2 (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05), with the exception of subgroups at the lower bound of substitution rates (0.001–0.008), where the error marginally exceeded that of Twisst2 (Fig. 3C).

Moreover, although the populations in the input MSA data are arranged in a specific order, this ordering does not theoretically affect their topological relationships in theory. Therefore, we tested the model under different population permutations. Comparisons across all six permutations revealed that the errors of both ERICA-trio and four-taxon ERICA were unaffected by the order of the input populations (Kruskal–Wallis test, P = 0.66 and 0.26, respectively, Fig. 4A and B), indicating that ERICA-trio and ERICA are robust to input order, and thus, no prior assumptions on phylogenetic relationships between taxa are required. We also noted that averaging the topological proportions across all permutations significantly reduced prediction errors (median MAE: 0.043 for ERICA-trio, 0.042 for four-taxon ERICA, Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05, Fig. 4A and B), which may be attributed to the mitigation of random errors through multiple predictions. We employed this approach in subsequent analyses.

We next evaluated the consistency between the ERICA-trio and four-taxon ERICA models. The predicted topological proportions exhibited a strong linear correlation, yielding a regression coefficient of 0.99 and an R2 of 0.96 (Fig. 4C), indicating a high degree of concordance. Moreover, the MAE between the two model (median MAE: 0.026) was significantly smaller than the MAE between either model's predictions and the true values (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001). This indicated that inter-model differences reflect stochastic noise rather than systematic bias, which further supported the validity of the three-taxon ERICA-trio model.

To further assess the robustness of ERICA-trio in topology inference, we evaluated its performance under conditions outside the training distribution. We first examined contiguous missing blocks (Supplementary Fig. S6A), which may more closely resemble the patterns of missing information in real sequencing data. Although error rates increased slightly under this condition compared to randomly distributed missing sites (Dataset D2, Fig. 2E and F), ERICA-trio still outperformed both the window-tree based method and the ARG-based Twisst2 method (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S6B). Furthermore, the error was significantly affected by the overall proportion of missing data. Specifically, once the missing rate exceeded 20%, the error began to rise progressively (Supplementary Fig. S6C).

In addition, on the dataset with shorter divergence times (< 1 × 4Ne generations, Supplementary Fig. S7A), the two ARG-based methods, Singer and Twisst2, exhibited the lowest MAE (median MAE: 0.038 and 0.040, respectively), followed by ERICA-trio (median MAE: 0.050), whose error was significantly lower than those of both the four-taxon ERICA models (median MAE: 0.058) and the window-tree method (median MAE: 0.056) (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S7B). Notably, Twisst2 continued to exhibit relatively high variability, with the highest error observed at the lowest substitution rates (Supplementary Fig. S7C, D). Although the ARG-based methods exhibited a slight advantage in specific ranges of the parameter space, ERICA-trio maintained strong overall robustness. This is particularly evident when considering that ARG-based methods yielded substantially higher errors on the previously analyzed deep-divergence dataset (Dataset D1, Supplementary Fig. S5).

Based on accurate topological information, we evaluated the efficacy of ERICA-trio in introgression detection by simulating gene flow with specific directions and intensities (Fig. 5). Taking the species phylogeny ((P1, P2), P3) (topo A) as an example, the proportions of the two alternative topologies (topo B and C) are low and expected to be equal in the absence of gene flow (Fig. 5A). Introgression between non-sister taxa (e.g. P2 and P3) increases the proportion of the corresponding topology (topo C) (Fig. 5A). Therefore, we employed two approaches to identify potential introgressed windows. First, we set a threshold for the proportion of topo C, and second, we determined whether topo C was significantly more abundant than the other alternative topology (topo B, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.05). The threshold was determined based on the false positive rate (FPR) from data under scenarios without gene flow (FPR < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Overall, the performance of ERICA-trio was highly similar to the four-taxon ERICA model (Fig. 5B). Under neutral gene flow conditions with extremely low migration rates, the approach based on absolute topological proportion showed relatively low detection rates (Fig. 5C). In contrast, the approach based on relatively elevated proportions achieved higher accuracy in the current test data, with improved true positive rates (TPR) despite a slight increase in FPR. Furthermore, ERICA-trio achieved a comparable TPR to the traditional allele-frequency-based statistics (D, fd and the three taxon D3), while maintaining a lower FPR (Fig. 5B, C). For introgression signals under selection, both screening criteria achieved high accuracy (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.05: 98% and FPR < 0.05: 95%) (Fig. 5B, C), demonstrating that ERICA-trio can effectively detect adaptive introgression signals. In contrast to the aforementioned methods, the f3 statistic, originally designed to detect genome-wide admixture, had limited power to capture local introgression signals. For gene flow from P3 to P2, it identified only 12 and 8 of 500 windows under neutral and adaptive scenarios, resulting in extremely low TPRs of 2.4% and 1.6%, respectively.

We also evaluated the model’s performance under scenarios involving recent divergence, with all split times were <1 × 4Ne generations. Due to elevated levels of ILS (Supplementary Fig. S8A), the accuracy of neutral introgression detection declined, with low-intensity gene flow signals being particularly difficult to identify, while detection rates for strong neutral gene flow and adaptive gene flow remained high (Supplementary Fig. S8B, C). We noted that the D, fd and D3 statistics exhibited a similar performance decline under these conditions, suggesting that this sensitivity to elevated ILS reflects a broader challenge faced by existing methods rather than a limitation specific to ERICA-trio. Nevertheless, ERICA-trio still yielded lower FPRs than these allele-frequency-based statistics (Supplementary Fig. S8B, C).

We subsequently applied ERICA-trio to real genomic data of Heliconius butterflies, a classic system with high levels of gene flow. Our analysis focused on two H. melpomene races and H. timareta thelxinoe, which form sympatric co-mimics with H. m. amaryllis (Fig. 6A). The results showed that the species topology (topo A) had the highest proportion, followed by the topology supporting the clustering of co-mimics (H. t. thelxinoe and H. m. amaryllis, topo C, Fig. 6B). The proportion of topo C was significantly higher than that of the another alternative topology (clustering H. t. thelxinoe and H. m. aglaope, topo B) across all chromosomes (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.01, Fig. 6C), supporting the existence of widespread gene flow between H. t. thelxinoe and H. m. amaryllis. The average differences between topo C and topo B ranged from 0.007 to 0.053 across chromosomes, indicating low overall intensity of gene flow. Notably, the Z chromosome exhibited the strongest resistance to gene flow, with the lowest gene flow intensity of all the chromosomes (Fig. 6C). At higher resolutions, 1550 50-kb windows showed an increased proportion of topo C, and among them, 54 windows showed a particularly strong introgression signal, with a topological proportion greater than a threshold of 0.4. The most pronounced introgression signal, in terms of both strength and length, was identified within a 200-kb region upstream of the gene optix, which primarily controls red mimicry color patterns [3] (Fig. 6D). Additionally, two other introgressed windows were located adjacent to the genes cortex/ivory and vvl, which belong to key toolkit loci for wing patterning in Heliconius butterflies [35, 36] (Fig. 6E, F). These results confirmed that the observed phenotype convergence in the co-mimics H. t. thelxinoe and H. m. amaryllis originated from adaptive introgression, consistent with previous findings [3, 30]. In summary, the identification of multiple adaptive introgressed loci in Heliconius further demonstrates that ERICA-trio can effectively detect adaptive introgression in the absence of outgroup data.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed and trained a new network model based on the ERICA pipeline, which only requires ingroup sequence data to infer evolutionary relationships and detect gene flow. Although our present study is centered on three taxa, the framework holds the potential to be adapted to more complex scenarios, such as resolving the 15 possible rooted tree topologies among four ingroup species, as demonstrated by the original five-taxon ERICA model.

Most statistics-based introgression detection methods require an outgroup to rooted trees or determine the ancestral states of alleles. However, when the selected outgroup violates method assumptions, such as the presence of unexpected gene flow, it can introduce systematic errors. For example, the D statistic, the window-tree based method and the four-taxon ERICA model exhibit bias under scenarios with bidirectional gene flow between the outgroup and either the sister group, P1 or P2, leading to misinterpretation of the results (Supplementary Fig. S1A). ERICA-trio is insensitive to gene flow involving the outgroup as the recipient, and bias only occurs when the direction of ghost gene flow is to the ingroups (Fig. 3A), which is similar to the performance of fd statistic. Previous evaluations of the D statistic show that the risk of ghost introgression increases with greater genetic distance to the outgroup [37]. Our results indicate that even without employing outgroups, allele frequency- or topology- based methods struggle to distinguish between ingroup gene flow and ghost gene flow. Therefore, results should be interpreted with careful consideration of the biological context or population history of the studied taxa and through more comprehensive sampling. Other machine learning methods, such as ArchIE [16] and IntroUNET [20], have been proposed to identify introgression from an unsampled ghost population. These methods rely on predefined population demographics and gene flow histories, and assume no introgression to reference populations. The impact of unknown or unsuspected ghost gene flow may still require further investigation.

Based on the current test data, ERICA-trio and four-taxon ERICA achieve broadly comparable accuracy and robustness in topology inference and introgression detection, suggesting the outgroup data is not essential in a wide range of evolutionary scenarios. Our analysis also showed that the D3 statistic, which similarly does not require outgroup data, performed comparably to the D statistic (Fig. 5), which is consistent with previous reports [22]. However, further evaluation suggests that the D3 statistic is more sensitive to heterogeneity in substitution rates [38]. Thus, further verification is needed to determine whether the performance of ERICA-trio and ERICA is affected by differences in evolutionary rates across lineages.

Regardless of the taxon number, our evaluation results demonstrate that neural networks generally display strong generalization capabilities within the ERICA framework. For example, after training, missing data or sample size have a relatively minor effect on topology inference errors. This suggests that model accuracy can be improved by closely approximating real genomic data. However, substitution rates continue to have a substantial impact on model accuracy, with both excessively high and low levels leading to increased error, even when models are trained on data with a broad range of substitution rates. Notably, similar trends were observed in the window-tree-based and ARG-based methods, which exhibited an even greater variance (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating that a single model or method may be insufficient to handle all challenging conditions. Furthermore, there may be a trade-off between the network's applicability across diverse datasets and its accuracy. For instance, models trained on more complex datasets (D2 and D3) show higher error rates than those trained on simpler data (D1), though the difference is marginal. We further show that the model is robust to the input order of populations, despite their sequential arrangement in the network input. Moreover, evaluating multiple permutations of input taxa improves estimate accuracy and may provide a way to assess the variance or bias of results for real data where ground truth is unavailable.

Beyond topology inference, we also evaluated the efficacy of ERICA-trio in introgression detection. Detection accuracy was higher under scenarios with longer divergence times but declined when divergence times were shorter. This decline, which is consistent with observations in other allele frequency-based methods, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing actual introgression from ILS, particularly under conditions of elevated ILS intensity. Nevertheless, even in these challenging scenarios, the distributions of topological proportions still differ between pure ILS and introgression, allowing ERICA-trio to detect high-intensity neutral gene flow and adaptive introgression (Supplementary Fig. S8). Therefore, an elevated proportion of discordant alternative topologies, together with the asymmetry between them, still provide meaningful evidence of potential gene flow, which can be further validated using complementary approaches such as branch length analyses.

In terms of sequence encoding, ERICA-trio currently employs one-hot encoding. This approach represents DNA sequences as lossless but fixed discrete features that are directly fed into the CNN, allowing the network to learn relationships between sequences in its subsequent layers. In contrast, the recent emergence of deep learning-based genomic foundation models (such as DNABERT-2 [39] and the Nucleotide Transformer [40]) can convert sequences into dynamic, context-aware embeddings that capture richer long-range dependencies. However, since the training data for these models are primarily concentrated on specific taxa, their overall cross-species generalization ability in diverse evolutionary contexts remains to be thoroughly tested. Nevertheless, incorporating such trainable, contextual embedding models is a highly promising direction for the future improvement of ERICA-trio.
