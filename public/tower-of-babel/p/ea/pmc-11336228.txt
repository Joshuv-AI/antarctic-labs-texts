Machine learning based storm time modeling of ionospheric vertical total electron content over Ethiopia
Authors: Ayanew Nigusie, Ambelu Tebabal, Firomsa Feyissa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeomagnetic storms can cause variations in the ionization levels of the ionosphere, which is commonly studied using the total electron content (TEC). TEC is a crucial parameter to identify the possible effects of ionospheric variations on satellite communication and navigation. This paper assesses the performance of light gradient boosting machine (LGB) and deep neural network (DNN) machine learning algorithms in modeling ionospheric vertical TEC (VTEC) during geomagnetic disturbances. GPS VTEC data for years 2011–2016 from 13 dual-frequency receiver stations over Ethiopia was utilized. Input parameters for the models were derived from the factors that influence VTEC, such as time, location, geomagnetic activity, solar activity, solar wind, and the interplanetary magnetic field. The LGB model improved the predictions of the DNN model from root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and R2 values of 5.45 TECU, 21%, and 0.93 to 4.98 TECU, 18%, and 0.94 on the testing data, respectively. The two machine learning models significantly outperformed the International Reference Ionosphere (IRI 2020) model during the selected geomagnetic storm periods. This study could provide insight into the impacts of ionosphere variations on satellite communication and navigation systems in the low-latitude ionospheric region.Keywords: Total electron content, LightGBM, Deep neural network, Geomagnetic storm, Hyperband algorithmSubject terms: Space physics, Space physics, Scientific data, Computational science

Introduction

The ionosphere is a layer of plasma that surrounds the Earth and forms a shell-like barrier that extends into the upper atmosphere1. This ionized region, which is composed of charged particles, is very dynamic and undergoes fluctuations in its ionization levels due to various factors, such as solar activity and geomagnetic activity. These fluctuations affect the propagation of radio signals in the region2,3. A geomagnetic storm, which is a temporary disruption of the Earth’s magnetosphere due to a shock wave from the solar wind, may cause significant fluctuations in the ionosphere called ionospheric storms4. Ionospheric storms can result in either positive or negative impacts on the ionosphere. Positive ionospheric storms lead to significant increases in ionization levels, while negative ionospheric storms cause notable decreases5.

Total electron content (TEC), a measurement indicating the total quantity of free electrons within a column of the ionosphere covering a unit area of m2, plays a vital role in characterizing ionosphere behavior and investigating the propagation of radio waves. Regarding how the ionosphere reacts to geomagnetic storms, TEC is a commonly utilized parameter6. Consequently, estimating and predicting TEC accurately is helpful to correct the delay and distortion of radio signals caused by the fluctuations in the ionosphere7. Continuous updates are being made to empirical ionospheric TEC prediction models, including the International Reference Ionosphere (IRI)8–11 and NeQuick12,13 models. Nevertheless, these models are not capable of accurately predicting and describing the dynamic characteristics of ionospheric TEC during geomagnetically disturbed conditions14,15. Machine learning techniques have proven to be effective solutions for precise prediction and forecasting of the ionospheric TEC. These techniques are renowned for their ability to learn from data and extract essential information. They are particularly well-suited for problems that involve multiple variables, especially when linear methods are insufficient to describe a nonlinear system. Machine learning and deep learning approaches are currently extensively employed in various studies related to ionospheric modeling16.

In the African sector, various studies have been conducted in the ionospheric TEC modeling and prediction using machine learning at regional and single station scales17–24. Despite the studies showing good agreement between the models’ predictions and observed values, the accuracy of the models is compromised during geomagnetic storms compared to geomagnetically quiet conditions. This is because the models heavily rely on quiet-time data, which makes up the majority of the data used to train the models. Therefore, it is imperative to build the models using only storm time data to focus on the storm-related information and predict TEC accurately during such conditions25. As a result, studies have been conducted in the African region to model ionospheric TEC only during geomagnetic storms. For instance,26 utilized the empirical orthogonal function (EOF) and neural network techniques for TEC modeling during geomagnetic storms at a single station in Sutherland, South Africa. It was found that the models successfully captured TEC during nighttime and early morning for storms with noticeable TEC responses but did not adequately capture short-term features, TEC enhancement, and depression.

Another study has been conducted by27 to model ionospheric TEC during geomagnetic storms over the entire African sector based on artificial neural networks. The study stated that the developed model performs less effectively in low-latitude and equatorial ionospheric regions compared to mid-latitude regions. The researchers point out that the vast geographic coverage of this model has a detrimental impact on its accuracy in predicting TEC, as the expanse of spatial coverage hinders the model’s predictive capability. As a result, applying machine learning algorithms specifically to model the low-latitude African ionospheric region will improve predictive accuracy during geomagnetic storms as the models fully concentrate on capturing the information from the data on the low-latitude ionospheric region. The low-latitude ionospheric region is highly intricate due to the involvement of a variety of physical and chemical processes, making it challenging to accurately model ionospheric VTEC in the region14. No prior study has been conducted in ionospheric TEC modeling, specifically in the low latitude African ionospheric region during geomagnetic disturbed conditions. To fill this gap, machine learning techniques based on deep neural networks (DNN) and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) are applied for improved storm time TEC prediction in the low-latitude ionospheric region in this study. The LightGBM model, which was utilized in our previous study19, has demonstrated its simplicity in optimization, resulting in efficient and accurate TEC predictions. Ionospheric TEC data from thirteen GPS receiver stations in Ethiopia is utilized in this work. For further validation, the two machine learning models have been compared with the IRI 2020 global empirical model during selected geomagnetic storm periods.

Data and methods

Data and data preparation

In this study, we used GPS TEC data retrieved from 13 dual-frequency GPS receiver stations over Ethiopia. The raw GPS data was obtained from the University NAVSTAR Consortium’s (UNAVCO) website in receiver-independent exchange (RINEX) format. The RINEX data was then processed using the GPS-TEC analysis application software (Version 3.0), developed by Gopi Seemala. This program is used to calculate the TEC at the ionospheric pierce point height, which is considered to be 350 km in the region where this study is performed as recommended by28. To minimize errors caused by multipath effects, only the VTEC values obtained at elevation mask angles greater than 30∘ were considered. The calibrated VTEC data was in a 30-second interval and then averaged to hourly intervals. Because this study focuses solely on geomagnetically disturbed conditions, the data selection relied on the values of the disturbance storm time index (Dst), with the criteria ∣Dst∣ ≥ 30 nT29.

Table 1 presents the list of the locations of the GPS receiver stations and the years of data used in this study. Figure 1 depicts the locations of the stations on the map of Ethiopia. In the figure, the training and validation datasets included data from all GPS stations, excluding data for the years 2012 and 2015 from stations marked with blue stars. The unseen data from the stations marked with blue stars for 2012 and 2015 was used as testing data to assess the performance of models in capturing VTEC variability during geomagnetic disturbances. When choosing the testing data, consideration is given to the varying geographic locations of the stations and the prevalence of geomagnetic storms during the selected years. Generally, the training, validation, and testing datasets make up 60%, 20%, and 20% of the entire data used (63,066 datapoints), respectively. This data splitting ratio effectively balances the amount of training, validation, and testing data, which helps to avoid overfitting by providing enough data for the models to learn effectively while ensuring robust hyperparameter tuning and evaluation. Empirical studies and practical applications favor this split for the data size used in this study, making it a robust choice for machine learning tasks30–32.

Several studies17,18,21,22,27,33,34, have demonstrated input variables for TEC machine learning models are determined by factors that influence TEC variation, such as season, time of day, geographic location, solar activity, geomagnetic activity, solar wind, and interplanetary magnetic field (IMF). In this study, the sunspot number (SN) and solar radio flux at 10.7 cm wavelength (F10.7) were used as input variables to represent the effects of solar activity on VTEC. The planetary index (Kp index) and Dst index were used as inputs to represent geomagnetic activity effects. The spatial variability of VTEC was represented using the geographic latitude (Lat) and longitude (Long). The solar wind plasma speed (SW) and the north-south component of the IMF (Bz, based on the Geocentric Solar Ecliptic System (GSE)) were used as input variables to represent the effects of the solar wind and IMF, respectively. The data for these input parameters was downloaded from the NASA OMNIWEB data set website at hourly intervals. The day of the year (DOY) and the hour of the day (HR) were used to represent the seasonal and diurnal variations of VTEC, respectively.

Description of the models

Machine learning techniques are effective in identifying patterns and connections from historical data to solve problems using a mathematical approach35. In this study, two machine learning algorithms, namely LightGBM and deep neural networks, are employed to predict VTEC variations during geomagnetic storms. The outline of the methodology and construction of these models using the data utilized in this study are presented next. Python programming software (Version 3.10.9) (available at: https://www.spyder-ide.org/) was utilized to build the models and analyze the results.

Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM)

LightGBM, originally developed by Microsoft in 2016, is an efficient implementation of the gradient-boosting algorithm that prioritizes efficiency, scalability, and accuracy to handle large datasets effectively. LightGBM employs decision trees as the base learners in its algorithm. To improve the efficiency of the classic gradient boosting technique, LightGBM applies a leaf-wise tree growth method that includes depth limits, gradient-based one-sided sampling (GOSS), and exclusive feature bundling (EFB) techniques. The leaf-wise tree growth strategy always selects the leaf that will result in the highest reduction in loss when splitting36,37.

In a training set containing n independent and identically distributed instances {x1,...,xn}, for each vector xi of dimension s in the input space χs, in the LightGBM algorithm GOSS preserves all the instances with high gradients while performing random sampling on the instances with small gradients. To offset the impact of only choosing a subset of samples with low gradients, GOSS incorporates a fixed multiplier for data instances with small gradients when calculating information gain. Specifically, GOSS selects the top a×100% instances by arranging the data instances based on the absolute value of their gradients to create subset A. Following that, it randomly selects b×100% instances from the remaining data to form subset B. To calculate the information gain, GOSS multiplies the sampled data with small gradients by a constant 1-ab to focus more on under-trained instances while maintaining the initial data distribution. Finally, the instances are split based on the predicted variance gain V~j(d) over the combined subset A∪B36.

where gi represents the gradient of individual samples, Al={xi∈A:xij≤d}, Ar={xi∈A:xij>d}, Bl={xi∈B:xij≤d}, Br={xi∈A:xij>d}, nlj(d)=∑I[xi∈O:xij≤d], nrj(d)=∑I[xi∈O:xij>d], and 1-ab is employed to normalize the total of the gradients over B. As a result, using the estimated V~j(d) over a smaller subset of instances in GOSS can significantly reduce the computational cost compared to using the accurate variance gain across all instances to calculate the split point36.

EFB is a technique that enables the possibility of creating an almost perfect method for reducing the number of effective features. High-dimensional data is often sparse, allowing for the potential development of a nearly lossless approach to feature reduction. In sparse feature spaces, numerous features are mutually exclusive, indicating that they do not have nonzero values at the same time. Combining these exclusive features into one feature can be done seamlessly. By using a carefully designed algorithm to scan features, the same feature histograms can be produced from combined feature bundles as they would be from individual features36.

The lightgbm (version 3.3.5) Python library was employed to build the model. Machine learning algorithms contain hyperparameters that determine the complexity and structure of the model. Finding the best values for these hyperparameters involves testing different combinations and evaluating performance. The accurate identification of a strong set of hyperparameters is crucial for achieving high performance in machine learning algorithms. The LightGBM model often requires optimizing hyperparameters such as the maximum depth of the trees (max_depth), the number of trees used in ensemble learning (n_estimators), the learning rate to reduce the gradient step (learning_rate), and the maximum number of leaves in each weak learner (num_leaves). The hyperband algorithm was applied to the training and validation datasets to determine the best hyperparameters of the LightGBM model. Deep neural networks

Neural networks are popular machine learning techniques that imitate the learning process of animal neurons. Neural networks are collections of interconnected processing elements, units, or neurons. The network’s processing power is determined by the connection strengths between units, also known as weights, which are adjusted through a learning process using a set of training patterns39. Deep neural network (DNN), also known as a multilayer perceptron, consists of multiple computational layers. DNN includes an input layer, an output layer, and hidden layers in between. The output layer is responsible for performing computations, while the input layer transmits data to the output layer. The computations in the hidden layers are not visible to the user. This type of neural network is called a feed-forward network because information flows in a forward direction from input to output40.

In a neural network, multiple layers are linked together, passing the outputs of one layer as input to the next, where the first layer is the input layer and the last layer is the output layer. The DNN model utilized in this study was built using the Tensorflow (version 2.15.0) Python library and its Keras extension. Discovering the optimal configuration for the DNN model proves to be a significantly more challenging task when compared to fine-tuning the LightGBM model. We have also employed the hyperband algorithm to estimate the best hyperparameters of the DNN model. However, applying the hyperband algorithm to the DNN proved to be challenging due to the lengthy training time. To address this, we defined the hyperparameter ranges for the DNN model and ran the hyperband algorithm with a maximum of 10 epochs with ranges of different parameters. After several iterations, the algorithm identified the combination that resulted in the lowest RMSE on the validation dataset for 10 epochs. This optimal combination of the hyperparameters was then used to train the DNN model for 400 epochs. The best DNN model, which achieved the lowest validation RMSE, was chosen as the resulting model. The differences in performance between the LGB and DNN models at the four testing stations can be attributed to various factors. Primarily, the LGB model can automatically capture and utilize interactions between features, which may differ across stations. If certain features are more informative at one station compared to another, LGB may exploit these differences more effectively than the DNN model, which may require more sophisticated feature engineering45,46. Next, the size of the representative training data at each station also influences performance, with the LGB model performing better with smaller datasets due to its less data-intensive nature compared to the DNN model47,48. Furthermore, model performance is influenced by hyperparameter settings, and LGB, with fewer and simpler hyperparameters, is easier to tune, potentially explaining its better performance at the three stations49. Moreover, LGB is particularly effective with structured data due to its ability to capture complex patterns, making it suitable for datasets with many features or non-linear relationships. In contrast, DNN, while flexible and capable of modeling complex patterns, requires more data and precise tuning to perform well36,50. Lastly, variations in data distribution, such as range, outliers, or noise levels, can impact model generalization, with LGB likely handling these variations better due to its robustness and focus on challenging examples during training36,51.

Discussion

In this study, the comparison of the two machine learning models on the testing dataset revealed that the LGB model yielded favorable results compared to the DNN model. This could be due to the effectiveness of the LGB model with both large and small datasets, as well as the DNN model’s requirement for a significant amount of data to achieve its highest level of accuracy. The comparison of the LGB, DNN, and IRI 2020 models on selected geomagnetic storm periods showed that the LGB model performed better than the other models in most cases. The IRI 2020 global empirical model showed significant shortcomings in accurately representing the fluctuations in VTEC levels throughout the selected storm periods compared to the two machine learning models employed in this study. One frequently mentioned reason for the limited accuracy of the IRI 2020 model is the insufficient inclusion of data from the African region in the development of the model, primarily because there is a scarcity of ionospheric data available from the region52,53. The other reason is that the almost horizontal magnetic field and the eastward current (electrojet) flowing along the magnetic equator create various irregularities and anomalies that complicate accurately modeling this region in the IRI11.

For further validity of the models used in this study, the results have been compared with a previously developed neural network-based storm time ionospheric TEC model over the entire African sector27. The models employed in our study exhibited high levels of accuracy in predicting VTEC. Specifically, the LGB model demonstrated R, RMSE, and MAPE values of approximately 0.97, 5.0 TECU, and 18%, respectively, on the testing data, while the DNN model achieved corresponding values of 0.96, 5.4 TECU, and 21%. In contrast, the storm-time model previously developed for the African sector reported RMSE and MAPE values of 5.8 TECU and 25.7% TECU, respectively, which are higher than the values obtained from both models in our study. As a result, our study achieved superior results compared to the previous model, indicating that machine learning models with limited geographical coverage can effectively capture TEC variations with high accuracy.

Because storm-time machine learning ionospheric TEC models were unavailable in studies carried out in the low-latitude African ionospheric region, our findings are compared with the results of models developed from studies that incorporated data from both geomagnetically quiet and storm conditions on regional and single station scales. In a regional neural network-based model over Ethiopia developed by17, R and RMSE values between 0.94–0.97 and 3.8–6.5 TECU, respectively, were reported on their testing stations. In our study, R and RMSE values in the range of 0.96–0.98 and 4.5–5.7 TECU at our testing stations were obtained, which indicates that sometimes similar and favorable results in other instances were achieved compared to the previous study. In another regional neural network-based model over Nigeria by24, RMSE values ranging from 6.0 to 8.5 TECU were reported, which shows significantly better results were obtained in our study. In a single-station study by19 at Adis Ababa, Ethiopia, the best-performing model yielded R and RMSE values of 0.96 and 5.1 TECU, respectively, which were not as favorable as the results obtained by the LGB model in this study. A study conducted by18 using a single-station feed neural network-based model in Arba Minch, Ethiopia, resulted in R and RMSE values of 0.95 and 6.0 TECU, respectively, which reveals that comparatively accurate results were obtained in our study. Similarly,23 found an RMSE value of 5.7 TECU in another single-station neural network-based model in Mbarara, Uganda, which was higher than the RMSE values achieved in our study. It is important to note that the comparisons are conducted using varying cases and data in these studies, and it can be inferred that the RMSE values are influenced by the absolute TEC values in each study.

Conclusions

This study examines the performance of two machine learning models based on LGB and DNN algorithms for ionospheric VTEC prediction during geomagnetically disturbed conditions. VTEC data from 13 GPS receiver stations over Ethiopia within the years 2011 to 2016 was used. The models employed were built using various input parameters derived from factors that affect VTEC, such as geomagnetic activity, geographic location, solar activity, season, time of day, IMF, and solar wind. Evaluation metrics including R, R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE were utilized to evaluate the performance of the models. The LGB model achieved an R value of 0.969 and a MAPE value of 18%, while the DNN model had an R value of 0.964 and a MAPE value of 21% on the testing dataset. Both machine learning models were able to accurately predict a high percentage of the variations in GPS VTEC data, with the LGB model capturing 94% and the DNN model capturing 93%, which indicates the LGB model yielded favorable results. The LGB model outperformed the DNN model at the Adis, Debk, and Asos stations, while both models showed similar performance at the Nege station. The comparison between the predictions of the machine learning and the IRI 2020 models with GPS VTEC during selected geomagnetic storm periods showed that the machine learning models had significantly better agreement with GPS VTEC compared to the IRI 2020 global model.

This study displayed promising results in applying machine learning techniques for predicting VTEC in the low-latitude ionospheric region during geomagnetic disturbances. This study may be helpful in understanding the impact of ionospheric variability in low-latitude regions, which can affect satellite communication and navigation systems. Enhancing TEC predictions could potentially improve the performance and reliability of these technologies in the region. Additionally, ionospheric scintillation, which can degrade signal quality, is more pronounced at low latitudes. More accurate TEC prediction could aid in anticipating and managing the effects of scintillation on communication and navigation systems. In future work, we aim to explore additional input parameters that reflect the impact of steep electron density gradients, prereversal enhancements, and equatorial ionization anomalies, all of which contribute to intricate ionospheric structures in the low latitude ionospheric region. This exploration is intended to enhance the accuracy of predictions in the region. In a different scenario, the selected time frame (2011–2016) for this study is restricted due to data availability in the area. We believe that expanding the time period and incorporating more stations with abundant recent data in the equatorial region would be advantageous for examining the ionosphere across various solar activity levels. This offers access to a substantial amount of data and a valuable chance to create accurate models using deep learning methods.
