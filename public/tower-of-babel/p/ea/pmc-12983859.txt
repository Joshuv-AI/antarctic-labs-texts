Exogenous NO enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa via the lignin synthesis pathway
Authors: Li-fei Chen, Ying Zhao, Xiao-lin Zhu, Yi-zhen Wang, Xian Wang, Xiao-hong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is a globally important forage legume and the most widely cultivated sown pasture species in China. Drought, as one of the most common abiotic stresses, limits alfalfa growth and development. Nitric oxide (NO), a key signaling molecule, plays an essential role in plant growth, development, and responses to various abiotic stresses. In this study, exogenous NO was applied to alfalfa seedlings under drought stress, followed by physiological and transcriptomic analyses. The results showed that sodium nitroprusside (SNP)-treated alfalfa seedlings grew better than untreated controls (CK), with improved leaf tissue structure. Meanwhile, SNP treatment increased proline content, reduced malondialdehyde accumulation, and enhanced hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity. Under drought stress, lignin content increased in alfalfa seedlings. Following exogenous NO application, lignin content in alfalfa seedlings further increased. RNA-Seq analysis identified 20,183 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in alfalfa seedlings treated with PEG, SNP, or PEG + SNP. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs under drought stress were involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, which regulates lignin biosynthesis and enhances drought tolerance. GO enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs participated in the response to water deprivation, thereby modulating drought stress tolerance and improving drought resistance. Furthermore, we assessed the transcript-level changes in genes induced by phenylpropanoid biosynthesis in alfalfa. Among them, 124 DEGs were identified as participating in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, including 10 up-regulated DEGs (three of which encode key enzymes associated with lignin synthesis), while the remaining DEGs were down-regulated. These findings provide new insights into the transcriptomic mechanisms of SNP-mediated drought adaptation in alfalfa and reveal key pathways contributing to drought tolerance in this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12566-5.Keywords: Alfalfa, Drought stress, Lignin synthesis, NO, Transcriptome

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is a globally important leguminous forage crop [1]. Minly distributed across arid and semi-arid regions of North China and Northwest China, it is known as the “King of Forage” because of its excellent adaptability, high nutritional content, superior quality, high yield, and diverse vitamin and protein profiles [2]. Currently, the effects of abiotic stresses, especially drought stress, have become a bottleneck for agricultural development in many areas [3]. With worsening global climate change, river basins such as the Yellow River experience severe evapotranspiration, leading to declining water resources that greatly impede alfalfa growth. Therefore, in-depth research on alfalfa’s drought tolerance is essential for overcoming natural limits to its cultivation.

Drought is the most severe natural disaster impacting plant growth [4]. Adequate water is crucial for plant growth, development, and reproduction. Water scarcity creates major challenges for plant survival. During growth and development, root systems are very sensitive to changes in water availability. When faced with drought stress, alfalfa responds by increasing the number of lateral roots to absorb more water or promoting primary root elongation to reach deeper soil moisture [5]. Research shows that drought stress greatly reduces water use efficiency in alfalfa, significantly affecting its growth, development, and biomass production. On a physiological level, drought stress lowers stomatal conductance while increasing transpiration rates [6]. This imbalance causes a decrease in leaf relative water content (LRWC) [7]. It happens because stomatal closure limits gas exchange between the plant and the environment, slowly reducing metabolic activities and hindering photosynthesis and carbon fixation [8]. Therefore, improving alfalfa’s drought tolerance is essential for its growth, development, and economic value.

The application of exogenous growth regulators is a key strategy for improving plant drought tolerance [9]. For example, treatment with sodium nitroprusside (SNP) under drought stress effectively enhances drought resistance in bananas [10]. Nitric oxide (NO), as an important signaling molecule, plays an essential role in plant growth, development, and responses to various abiotic stresses [11]. In practice, SNP is commonly used as an NO donor to alleviate plant damage caused by adverse conditions [12]. Growing evidence suggests that NO interacts with abscisic acid (ABA), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), brassinosteroids (BR), and other cellular signaling molecules in response to abiotic stress [13]. NO participates in plant nitrogen metabolism and serves as a fundamental component for the synthesis of key molecules such as proteins and nucleic acids in alfalfa, thereby promoting its growth and development. Under drought stress, NO increases the content of soluble sugars and proline in alfalfa cells, lowers osmotic potential, maintains turgor pressure, and enhances drought tolerance [14]. Moreover, NO promotes root development in alfalfa, improving the efficiency of water and nutrient uptake—a crucial adaptive trait under drought conditions. It also reduces water loss by decreasing ion leakage and transpiration rate, while inducing stomatal closure, thereby further improving drought resistance [15]. Studies have also shown that exogenous NO regulates the activity of key enzymes in the lignin biosynthesis pathway, thereby influencing the rate and distribution of lignin deposition [16]. Specifically, NO modulates enzymatic reactions during lignin monomer synthesis by activating or repressing the expression of related genes, ultimately affecting lignin content and composition.

Lignin is the second most abundant substance in plants and a key component of plant cell walls [17]. It is a phenolic polymer formed by the aggregation of monomers produced through the shikimate pathway, the phenylpropanoid pathway, and the lignin synthesis pathway [18]. Along with cellulose and hemicellulose, it helps provide strength and rigidity to the cell wall. Beyond its structural role, lignin is also crucial for the long-distance transport of water and solutes [19]. Lignin biosynthesis occurs in three main steps: synthesis of monomeric lignin via the cytosolic phenylpropanoid pathway [20], transport of lignin precursors to the apoplast, and polymerization within the cell wall. Its synthesis is compatible with normal growth and can be influenced by biotic and abiotic stresses [21]. Drought can promote lignification, potentially increasing drought tolerance by improving water transport [22] and reducing water loss [23]. Research shows that photosynthesis can boost lignin synthesis; as photosynthetic efficiency rises, lignin synthase activity increases, leading to the formation of drought-resistant structures. Conversely, nitrogen deficiency hampers lignin production, as lignin synthesis genes are downregulated under such conditions, weakening the cell wall and reducing drought resistance [24]. Therefore, controlling the lignin synthesis pathway to strengthen cell walls while boosting drought tolerance is an important goal.

In this study, alfalfa seedlings of the “Sandili” variety were used as experimental materials, with SNP applied as an exogenous NO donor. Following drought stress and exogenous NO treatment, changes in seedling growth, leaf tissue structure, relevant physiological indicators, and transcriptomic profiles were analyzed. This work investigates the physiological and transcriptomic alterations induced by exogenous NO under drought stress in alfalfa. The findings elucidate how exogenous NO enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa by regulating the lignin biosynthesis pathway, thereby providing a theoretical basis for improving drought resistance in this crop.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and processing

The seeds used in this study were obtained from the Gansu Academy of Agricultural Sciences in China. Plump and pest- and disease-free alfalfa seeds (“Sandili”) were disinfected with 0.1% sodium hypochlorite. An appropriate amount of vermiculite and nutrient substrate (1:3, v/v) was added to plastic pots (12 cm in diameter, 10 cm deep). Plants were grown in a growth chamber at 26 °C/18°C (16 h light/8 hours dark), with a light flux density of 400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and relative humidity of 60%. Water content was measured every 2 days. When seedlings reached the 8–10 leaf stage (about 6 weeks old, 15 cm tall), 20 healthy, uniformly growing alfalfa plants were kept per pot. Ten pots were pretreated by spraying leaves with 100 µM sodium nitroprusside (SNP, a NO donor) solution every 24 h for three consecutive days. Untreated controls and SNP-treated plants were randomly assigned to either normal watering or 10% PEG-6000-stressed groups, with equal volumes of PEG solution added to each pot. Thus, four groups were created: (1) untreated, normally watered (CK); (2) SNP-pretreated, normally watered (SNP); (3) untreated PEG-stressed plants (PEG); (4) SNP-treated, PEG-stressed plants (SNP + PEG). Six days after starting treatments, the necessary samples were collected simultaneously and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen to ensure three biological replicates per treatment. The samples were subsequently stored at -80 °C.

Plant growth measurement

When alfalfa plants reached 60 days of age, five seedlings per treatment were harvested. Digital images were captured, and root and stem lengths were measured using ImageJ software. After recording the number of branches and lateral roots, the seedlings were thoroughly washed. The stems, buds, and roots were separated and oven-dried at 80 °C for 72 h to determine dry weights. The root-to-shoot ratio was calculated as root dry weight divided by shoot dry weight. Furthermore, ImageJ was used to measure total leaf thickness (LT), as well as the thickness of the upper epidermis (UET), lower epidermis (LET), palisade tissue (PT), and spongy tissue (SP). All experimental data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 22.0, and figures were generated with Origin 2021.

Measurement of physiological indicators

The endogenous NO content was measured using the Griess reagent method [25]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined according to the method of Hodges et al. [26], and proline content was analyzed following the procedure of Bates et al. [27]. The superoxide anion (O₂⁻) production rate was assayed as described by Elstner et al. [28]. The hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging capacity was evaluated using the 2-deoxy-D-ribose colorimetric method [29]. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content was measured by the potassium iodide (KI) method [30], and lignin content was quantified by ultraviolet spectrophotometry [31]. All experimental data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 22.0, and graphical representations were generated with Origin 2021.

RNA isolation and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from leaf samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and potential contamination were verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. RNA purity was checked using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, USA), and RNA integrity was further precisely assessed with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). The qualified total RNA was then equally divided for two purposes: RNA-seq library construction and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis. cDNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina HiSeq protocol and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform to generate paired-end reads. The raw transcriptome sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI SRA database under the accession number PRJNA478630.

Sequence analysis results: localization and differential expression

Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were initially processed using a custom Perl script. This step involved removing adapter sequences, poly-N reads, and low-quality reads to generate high-quality clean data. The quality of the cleaned data was assessed by calculating Q20, Q30 scores, GC content, and sequence duplication levels. All subsequent analyses were performed using these high-quality data. Transcriptome assembly was carried out with Trinity [32] using the paired-end reads (left.fq and right.fq), with min_kmer_cov set to 2 and other parameters kept as default. Functional annotation of the assembled transcripts was conducted by aligning against the following databases: NR (NCBI non-redundant protein sequences); Pfam (Protein family database); KOG/COG/eggNOG (clusters of orthologous genes); Swiss-Prot (a manually annotated and reviewed protein sequence database); KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes); and GO (Gene Ontology).

Gene expression levels for each sample were quantified using RSEM by mapping the clean reads back to the assembled transcriptome [33]. The read counts obtained for each gene were then used for differential expression analysis between conditions/groups with the DESeq R package (version 1.10.1) [34]. DESeq employs a negative binomial distribution model to statistically determine differentially expressed genes. The resulting P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with an FDR < 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered statistically significant.

Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with the topGO R package. KEGG is a database resource for understanding the higher-level functions and utilities of biological systems—such as cells, organisms, and ecosystems—from molecular-level information, especially large-scale molecular datasets obtained through genome sequencing and other high-throughput experiments. We used KOBAS software to assess the statistical enrichment of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways.

Differentially expressed genes were identified using the criteria |log2Fold Change|≥2 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq software (v.1.5.6). Gene expression patterns were clustered using MeV, Cluster, and Java Treeview software. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed with the Goseq-R package and GlueGO, using a p-value < 0.05 as the cutoff.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

Transcribe 0.5–2 µg of RNA into cDNA using the Evo-MLV reverse transcription kit. Gene expression analysis was conducted using a real-time quantitative PCR instrument (LightCycler 96, Eppendorf, Germany) with a qPCR kit. The reaction mixture contained 1 µL cDNA, 1 µL forward and reverse primers, 10 µL reaction mix, and 8 µL ddH₂O, totaling 20 µL. The reaction program included 95℃ pre-denaturation for 30 s, followed by 95℃ denaturation for 15 s, 60℃ annealing for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. qRT-PCR primers were designed based on differential gene sequence information using the Primer-BLAST online platform (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with three replicates per independent sample. Graphical analysis was performed with Origin 2021, and one-way ANOVA was conducted using SPSS 22.0. Primer sequences for differentially expressed genes are provided in the appendix (Table S1).

Result

Exogenous SNP application induces phenotypic variation in plants

To investigate the role of exogenous NO in modulating alfalfa morphology under drought stress, we applied sodium nitroprusside (SNP) and observed its effects on seedling growth. As shown in Fig. 1A, SNP-treated seedlings exhibited superior growth under drought conditions compared to untreated controls. Specifically, while drought stress alone did not significantly alter root or stem length, the application of exogenous SNP significantly increased stem height in drought-stressed seedlings (Fig. 1B). Moreover, SNP application increased the number of lateral stems by 25.9% under normal conditions and reversed the drought-induced inhibition, resulting in a 34.8% increase over stressed seedlings (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the number of lateral roots was not significantly affected by SNP under normal conditions but decreased by 24.4% under drought stress (Fig. 1D). The root-to-shoot ratio decreased by 13.4% with SNP under normal conditions. Although drought stress alone caused a marked 31.8% increase in this ratio, SNP application moderated this effect, reducing it by 2.4% (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that exogenous SNP alleviates drought-induced growth inhibition in alfalfa seedlings, as reflected by promoted stem elongation, increased lateral stem number, and a moderated root-to-shoot ratio, thereby enhancing drought tolerance.

Morphometric measurements

Research has indicated that plant drought tolerance is associated with the structure of leaf tissues, including the palisade tissue, spongy tissue, and stomata [35]. To determine whether exogenous NO influences the leaf anatomy of alfalfa seedlings, we conducted a histological analysis. As summarized in Fig. 2 and Table S2, exogenous SNP application significantly increased the thickness of the midvein (MVT), palisade tissue (PTT), spongy tissue (STT), upper epidermis (UET), lower epidermis (LET), and the entire leaf (LT) under well-watered conditions, with increases of 22.0%, 38.8%, 37.9%, 35.7%, 68.6%, and 48.8%, respectively. Under drought stress, SNP application similarly enhanced the thickness of these tissues by 11.3%, 10.6%, 31.6%, 45.2%, 25.7%, and 30.9%, respectively. Furthermore, exogenous SNP altered stomatal features. Under normal conditions, it increased stomatal width (SW) and stomatal area (SA) by 36.8% and 27.0%, respectively, without significantly affecting stomatal length (SL). Under drought stress, SNP application increased SL, SW, and SA by 40.5%, 71.7%, and 23.3%, respectively. These results demonstrate that exogenous NO induces structural modifications in the mesophyll tissue and enhances overall leaf thickness, thereby contributing to improved drought resistance in alfalfa seedlings.

Physiological measurement

To further assess the enhanced drought resistance induced by SNP, we analyzed key physiological indicators in alfalfa seedlings. The results demonstrated that PEG-induced drought stress significantly elevated the levels of endogenous NO, MDA, proline, and H₂O₂, increased the superoxide anion (O₂⁻) production rate, and markedly impaired the hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging capacity. Exogenous SNP application under drought stress further increased NO and proline levels and enhanced ·OH scavenging capacity, while it significantly reduced the accumulation of MDA and H₂O₂ and lowered the O₂⁻ production rate. Specifically, compared to their respective controls, SNP treatment significantly increased NO content by 152.7% under normal conditions (CK) and by 52.6% under PEG stress (Fig. 3A). While SNP slightly increased MDA content (8.5%) under CK, it notably reduced MDA by 25.0% under PEG stress (Fig. 3B). Proline content increased with SNP application by 3.1% under CK and by 9.9% under drought (Fig. 3C). Although SNP did not significantly alter the O₂⁻ production rate under CK, it reduced the rate by 23.8% under PEG stress (Fig. 3D). Concurrently, SNP significantly enhanced the ·OH scavenging capacity by 29.7% across conditions (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, SNP treatment consistently reduced H₂O₂ content by 22.1% under both CK and PEG stress conditions (Fig. 3F).These findings indicate that exogenous NO modulates the balance of signaling molecules and oxidative stress markers in alfalfa seedlings. This suggests that NO participates in regulating associated signaling pathways, thereby mitigating drought-induced oxidative damage and improving seedling drought tolerance.

Determination of lignin content

To directly investigate the role of lignin in alfalfa’s drought response and its regulation by exogenous NO, we quantified the lignin content in seedlings under different treatments. As shown in Fig. 4 that drought stress significantly increased lignin content by 4.6% compared to the well-watered control, indicating that lignin accumulation is a physiological adaptation to water deficit. Exogenous NO application under well-watered conditions also significantly induced lignin biosynthesis, resulting in a 2.82% increase. Notably, the combined treatment of drought stress and exogenous NO had a synergistic effect, leading to a pronounced increase in lignin content of 10.2% above the control. These findings demonstrate that alfalfa seedlings respond to drought by enhancing lignin deposition, and this response is further amplified by exogenous NO. This suggests that NO may enhance drought resistance by regulating the lignin biosynthesis pathway, thereby promoting secondary cell wall reinforcement and improving structural stability.

Distribution and expression patterns of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Based on the aforementioned physiological findings, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis to gain deeper insights into the mechanisms by which exogenous NO enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa seedlings. Comparative analysis identified a total of 4,186 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the CK and PEG groups (1,974 up-regulated and 2,212 down-regulated), 12,297 DEGs between CK and SNP (6,467 up-regulated and 5,830 down-regulated), and 3,700 DEGs between PEG and SNP + PEG (1,653 up-regulated and 2,047 down-regulated) (Fig. 5A). Venn diagram analysis further revealed 2,160 DEGs that were common to both SNP treatments under normal and drought conditions, of which 1,293 were up-regulated and 867 were down-regulated (Fig. 5B). Additionally, we identified sets of treatment-specific DEGs: 865 (425 up, 440 down) for PEG, 7,388 (3,172 up, 4,216 down) for SNP, and 1,530 (748 up, 782 down) for SNP + PEG. A clustered heatmap was generated to visualize the expression patterns of these DEGs across different treatment groups (Fig. 5C). The expression profiles in the SNP + PEG group were highly similar to those in the SNP group, though with generally higher expression levels, suggesting that exogenous SNP modulates the transcriptional regulation of these genes to mediate drought resistance. To validate the reliability of the RNA-Seq data, twelve highly expressed DEGs were selected and their expression patterns are displayed in a separate heatmap (Fig. 5D).

Regulatory mechanisms of alfalfa in response to drought

To investigate how differentially expressed genes are distributed across signaling pathways, we conducted KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) annotation and analysis related to drought mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 6. Results show that differentially expressed genes between CK and PEG mainly participate in KEGG pathways such as Circadian rhythm-plant, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, and Carbon metabolism. Some up-regulated genes are involved in Linoleic acid metabolism and Caffeine metabolism, while some down-regulated genes are involved in Vitamin B6 metabolism (Fig. 6A). Between CK and SNP, differentially expressed genes mainly participate in KEGG pathways like the MAPK signaling pathway-plant, plant-pathogen interaction, and plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. 6B). Genes differing between PEG and PEG + SNP are involved in KEGG pathways including plant circadian rhythm, carbon metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. They also include genes involved in stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis, fatty acid degradation, and vitamin B6 metabolism (Fig. 6C). Genes involved in the plant MAPK signaling pathway are differentially expressed among CK, PEG, and SNP treatments. Moreover, genes linked to plant circadian rhythm and carbon metabolism show differential expression among CK, PEG, and PEG + SNP treatments. These findings suggest that differentially expressed genes are involved in various metabolic processes that collectively regulate growth and development in alfalfa seedlings. Additionally, under drought stress, application of exogenous SNP reveals genes involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway.

These results indicate that under drought stress, exogenous SNP application modulates the response of alfalfa seedlings to water deficiency through differential gene expression, thereby enhancing their drought tolerance.

Response of the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway to drought stress

To investigate transcriptomic changes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway under drought stress, we analyzed candidate genes annotated to this pathway in alfalfa (Fig. 8). A total of 124 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis were identified (Fig. 8B). Among these, ten DEGs were significantly up-regulated, including: MS.gene052031, MS.gene28352, MS.gene54571, and MS.gene57369 (all encoding β-glucosidase); MS.gene058752, MS.gene50831, and MS.gene50852 (all encoding cinnamaldehyde dehydrogenase and cinnamoyl-CoA reductase); MS.gene44482 (encoding peroxidase); MS.gene88036 (encoding guaiacol peroxidase); and MS.gene38155 (encoding caffeic acid O-methyltransferase). The remaining DEGs in this pathway were down-regulated. These findings suggest that exogenous NO enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa by modulating the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway.

Among them, 10 genes were downregulated in the transcription data—including MS.gene00981, MS.gene013425, MS.gene022842, MS.gene022053, MS.gene052224, MS.gene062923, MS.gene029816, MS.gene23350, MS.gene24618, and MS.gene072127. Among these, two genes (excluding MS.gene062923 and MS.gene029816) showed discrepancies with RNA-Seq results, whereas the qRT-PCR results for the other 10 genes fully aligned with RNA-Seq data. These findings indicate that drought stress modulates the expression of differentially regulated genes, and exogenous NO exerts a regulatory effect on these genes, thereby influencing alfalfa’s drought tolerance.

Discussion

Alfalfa, an important forage and green manure crop, holds considerable agronomic value in temperate regions and is widely distributed across arid and semi-arid areas [36]. However, its growth and development are frequently limited by various abiotic stresses [37], among which drought or water deficit stands out as the most common and impactful [38]. Drought not only impairs water uptake—leading to a sharp decline in growth rates—but also induces a range of drought-associated metabolic alterations, including osmotic stress, reduced leaf water content, oxidative damage, and stomatal closure. These changes collectively restrict stomatal conductance, disrupt cellular integrity, and lower gas exchange efficiency [39].This study explored the potential of sodium nitroprusside (SNP) to alleviate drought-induced physiological responses in alfalfa. Our results demonstrate that SNP treatment effectively reduced drought-related damage, increasing lignin content promotes the expression of related DEGs, thereby enhancing the drought tolerance of alfalfa. Under drought stress, alfalfa seedlings displayed clear wilting symptoms, along with decreased root length, stem length, and number of primary branches compared to the control (CK). Plant height and root elongation were markedly inhibited. The slowdown in stem and root growth may help redirect metabolic resources toward the production of protective compounds such as osmoprotectants [40]. Concurrently, stomatal closure—an early adaptive response to drought—helps preserve relative water content in leaves [41], though it inevitably compromises photosynthetic performance [42].In this study, drought stress reduced the number of open stomata and increased the thickness of both palisade and spongy tissues—a change we speculate may be linked to lignin accumulation, thereby contributing to enhanced drought tolerance. Another early plant reaction to abiotic stress is the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). While ROS act as signaling molecules that trigger multiple defense pathways within the stress-response network [43], prolonged stress leads to excessive ROS accumulation in the plasma membrane, intensifying lipid peroxidation and raising malondialdehyde (MDA) levels—a widely recognized marker of oxidative damage [44].Consistent with this, our data showed that drought-stressed alfalfa seedlings exhibited elevated levels of NO, MDA, proline, O₂⁻ production rate, and H₂O₂, indicating that ROS overaccumulation may trigger drought resistance mechanisms via signaling pathways. Additionally, drought diminished the seedlings’ hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging capacity. In summary, drought stress severely restricts alfalfa growth and development, underscoring the importance of improving its drought tolerance to support sustainable agriculture.

Nitric oxide (NO), a crucial signaling molecule, is involved in regulating diverse cellular and physiological processes in plants and contributes to defense mechanisms under environmental stress [45]. The application of exogenous NO donors has been shown to enhance stress tolerance in alfalfa through physiological and biochemical pathways [46]. In this study, exogenous NO significantly improved the growth performance of alfalfa under drought conditions. Compared to the control (CK) and polyethylene glycol (PEG) treatments, alfalfa exhibited greater stem.

length and more lateral branches, indicating a more favorable growth phenotype. Furthermore, exogenous NO increased the number of open stomata and improved multiple leaf anatomical traits, with a particularly notable thickening of the palisade tissue. This structural modification is likely a result of NO-mediated regulation of the lignin biosynthesis pathway. As a major component of plant cell walls, lignin enhances wall stiffness and rigidity, thereby improving mechanical strength in stems and increasing resistance to wind and drought. Additionally, lignin deposition promotes root lignification, facilitating deeper root growth into the soil profile, which improves water uptake efficiency and further strengthens drought tolerance. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of lignin in plant resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses [47]. Under drought conditions, plants reinforce cell wall integrity by promoting lignin synthesis, thereby mitigating mechanical damage and water loss [48].

In this study, leaf tissue thickening associated with lignin accumulation likely provided greater structural rigidity, thereby enhancing overall drought resistance. In terms of physiological responses, PEG treatment significantly increased malondialdehyde (MDA) content in alfalfa seedlings, whereas exogenous NO effectively reduced MDA levels. This aligns with the findings of Hogg et al. [49], who reported that NO alleviates membrane lipid peroxidation. Similarly, Sundararajan et al. [50] observed that NO application reduced MDA content and improved drought resistance in tomato seedlings under water deficit, which is consistent with our results. Proline content also increased in alfalfa under drought stress, and exogenous NO further promoted its accumulation, suggesting that NO may help delay tissue dehydration by enhancing osmolyte synthesis and indirectly reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Exogenous NO also significantly lowered the superoxide anion (O₂⁻) production rate and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content, while enhancing hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging capacity. Together, these results demonstrate that NO effectively alleviates drought-induced oxidative damage and improves drought tolerance in alfalfa seedlings. Further evidence suggests that drought-induced lignification is modulated by multiple stress-signaling molecules, including abscisic acid (ABA), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), jasmonic acid (JA), and salicylic acid (SA) [18]. Exogenous NO may interact with these signaling pathways to further regulate lignin synthesis and other physiological processes linked to stress resistance.

Based on transcriptome data, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under different treatment conditions. A total of 4,186 DEGs were detected between the CK and PEG groups, 12,297 between CK and SNP, and 3,700 between PEG and SNP + PEG. The application of PEG and SNP induced substantial changes in gene expression patterns, suggesting that plants activate complex regulatory mechanisms to adapt to or mitigate drought stress by modulating the expression of stress-related genes. The transcriptomic analysis revealed that the SNP + PEG treatment group exhibited a gene expression profile highly similar to that of the SNP group, yet markedly distinct from the PEG group. This strongly suggests that nitric oxide pretreatment may pre-activate a broad core network of abiotic stress responses, thereby conferring cross-tolerance to subsequent drought stress [51]. Specifically, SNP pretreatment is hypothesized to induce the expression of numerous protective genes related to oxidative and osmotic stress—such as those encoding antioxidant enzymes and osmoprotectants—priming the plants into a “pre-adapted” state [52]. When actual drought occurs, the plants can thus fine-tune or amplify these pre-established pathways rather than initiating de novo transcriptional reprogramming. Moreover, for certain core stress-responsive genes, the pre-conditioning signal from SNP and the subsequent stress signal from PEG may produce additive or synergistic effects, leading to further enhanced expression [53]. This provides a plausible molecular basis for the enhanced drought tolerance observed in SNP-pretreated plants. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were primarily enriched in pathways such as the plant MAPK signaling pathway, circadian rhythm, and carbon metabolism. Notably, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway was significantly enriched under SNP treatment combined with drought stress. Given that lignin biosynthesis depends on phenylpropanoid metabolism [54], the enrichment of DEGs in this pathway may contribute to increased lignin accumulation. Thus, we speculate that SNP enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa by regulating DEGs involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, thereby modulating lignin synthesis. GO enrichment analysis further indicated that under drought stress, DEGs are extensively involved in responses to water deprivation, fine-tuning the drought tolerance of alfalfa seedlings. Focusing on the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, we identified 124 associated DEGs. Lignin, as a major end product of phenylpropanoid metabolism, is strictly dependent on this pathway for its biosynthesis. We hypothesize that under drought stress, alfalfa improves drought adaptation by regulating phenylpropanoid synthesis to enhance lignin accumulation. These DEGs encode multiple enzymes that collectively regulate the lignin biosynthesis process. For instance, MS.gene052031, MS.gene28352, MS.gene54571, and MS.gene57369 all encode β-glucosidases, which hydrolyze β-glycosidic bonds to activate glycosylated lignin precursors, thereby playing an essential role in lignin deposition and cell wall reinforcement [55]. MS.gene38155 encodes caffeic acid O-methyltransferase, a key enzyme involved in the methylation of lignin monomers and determination of lignin composition. Its up-regulation suggests that NO signaling may optimize cell wall properties through precise regulation of lignin chemistry, thereby improving drought resistance [56]. Additionally, MS.gene058752, MS.gene50831, and MS.gene50852 encode cinnamaldehyde dehydrogenase (CAD) and cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), respectively. SNP treatment significantly up-regulated the expression of these genes. CCR is recognized as the first rate-limiting enzyme in the lignin monomer biosynthesis pathway, whereas CAD catalyzes the final step of monomer formation. Together, they control the efficiency of lignin monomer synthesis [57]. The up-regulation of these genes indicates that NO signaling directly promotes lignin monomer production by activating key nodes in the biosynthesis pathway. These monomers are subsequently polymerized in the cell wall to form lignin. Previous studies have confirmed that lignin deposition enhances the mechanical strength of the cell wall and improves the water transport capacity of xylem vessels under drought stress [58].In summary, we propose the following mechanistic model: under drought stress, exogenous NO promotes lignin biosynthesis and deposition by up-regulating key genes in the lignin synthesis pathway, such as CCR and CAD. This enhancement ultimately improves drought tolerance in alfalfa by reinforcing cell wall mechanical strength and maintaining the functional integrity of water-conducting tissues (Fig. 10).

Mechanistic model of enhanced drought resistance in alfalfa through exogenous NO-Mediated regulation of lignin biosynthesis

Conclusion

In summary, drought stress significantly inhibits the growth of alfalfa seedlings. However, the application of exogenous SNP markedly alleviated this inhibition. Compared with untreated seedlings under drought conditions, SNP-treated alfalfa seedlings displayed longer stems, increased numbers of lateral roots and stems, and a reduced root-to-shoot ratio. Anatomically, SNP improved structural parameters such as palisade tissue and overall leaf thickness. At the physiological level, SNP treatment considerably altered the levels of key signaling and stress-related molecules. It significantly increased endogenous NO and proline content, enhanced hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity, and concurrently reduced the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), as well as the superoxide anion production rate. These changes demonstrate that exogenous NO mitigates drought-induced oxidative damage, thereby improving drought tolerance in alfalfa seedlings. Lignin quantification further revealed a significant increase in content following SNP application, indicating that exogenous NO enhances drought resistance by regulating the lignin biosynthesis pathway. Transcriptomic analysis supported this finding. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that drought stress induced differentially expressed genes (DEGs) significantly enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, the primary route for lignin synthesis. GO annotation further indicated that these DEGs are involved in the response to water deprivation. Among the 124 DEGs identified in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, 10 were up-regulated—including three genes encoding key enzymes for lignin synthesis, cinnamaldehyde dehydrogenase and cinnamoyl-CoA reductase—while the rest were down-regulated. Collectively, these results demonstrate that exogenous NO enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa by modulating the lignin biosynthesis pathway, effectively counteracting the adverse effects of drought stress on plant growth.
