Glutamine and allergic rhinitis: A bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Shuhua Fan, Zhiwei Guan, Ying Ding, Wenxia Chen, Sujing Su, Han Bai, Le Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glutamine (Gln) has been implicated as a potential protective factor against allergic rhinitis (AR), with supplementation studies suggesting improved patient prognosis. However, the precise relationship between circulating Gln levels and AR risk remains unclear. To address this, we employed bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the causal association between Gln and AR. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for AR (FinnGen dataset finn-b-ALLERGY_RHINITIS; 217,914 samples; 16,380,461 single nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]) and circulating Gln levels (met-d-Gln; 114,750 samples; 12,321,875 SNPs) were obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit Open GWAS database. MR analyses were performed, primarily using the inverse variance weighted (IVW; fixed-effects) method. Sensitivity analyses included the weighted median, simple median, MR-Egger, and maximum likelihood methods. Robustness of the MR findings was further assessed via heterogeneity tests, horizontal pleiotropy evaluation (MR-Egger intercept test), and leave-one-out (LOO) analysis. Additionally, phenotypic association scanning was conducted for both AR and Gln. Forward MR (Gln→AR) revealed a significant negative causal association, identifying Gln as a protective factor for AR (IVW OR < 1; P < .05). Reverse MR (AR→Gln) showed no causal effect (P > .05). Sensitivity analyses confirmed reliability: no significant heterogeneity (Q_P > .05) or horizontal pleiotropy (P > .05) was detected in either direction, and LOO analysis supported result stability. Phenotypic scanning corroborated Gln as a protective factor against AR (OR = 0.874, 95% CI = 0.77047–0.99093, P = .036). Our study suggests that Gln is a protective factor against AR and that AR is not causally associated with Gln.

1. Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a chronic IgE-mediated inflammatory disorder of the nasal mucosa, characterized by the involvement of multiple immune cells and cytokines.[1] Its clinical manifestations – including nasal itching, sneezing, rhinorrhea, and nasal congestion – frequently co-occur with asthma and conjunctivitis.[2,3] Globally, AR affects approximately 25% of children and 40% of adults,[4] with a prevalence of 18.61% among children in China.[5] Notably, the incidence of AR exhibits a continuous upward trend, accompanied by a decreasing age of onset annually. The underlying pathophysiology of AR is complex, involving interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors.[6,7] Observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have established associations between AR incidence and several risk factors, including genetic susceptibility, early-life antibiotic exposure, humid environments, indoor fungal exposure, lifestyle factors, acetaminophen use, and childhood acid suppressant use.[8] Beyond nasal symptoms, AR imposes a significant disease burden. Chronic manifestations can lead to impaired cognitive function, reduced productivity and academic performance, irritability, and neuropsychiatric sequelae such as depression and anxiety.[9] Pediatric AR patients face increased risks of respiratory infections, wheezing episodes, and tic disorders. Further research into AR prevention and management strategies is therefore warranted.

Glutamine (Gln), the most abundant non-essential amino acid in the human body, constitutes approximately 20% of total free amino acids in the bloodstream.[10] It functions as a crucial energy substrate for numerous cell types.[11,12] Beyond its metabolic role, Gln exhibits significant immunomodulatory properties, supporting immune cell energy metabolism, maintaining redox homeostasis, and preserving immune cell homeostasis and function.[13] Emerging evidence suggests an association between Gln levels and the occurrence and progression of AR, with higher Gln levels potentially correlating with improved patient prognosis.[14] Given that B cell dysfunction is implicated in AR pathogenesis,[14] observations of diminished Gln metabolism within B cells and reduced peripheral blood IL-10 levels in AR patients are notable.[14] Mechanistically, Gln cleavage appears essential for maintaining the immune regulatory function of regulatory B cells (Bregs), promoting IL-10 secretion and thereby exerting immunosuppressive effects that may ameliorate AR-associated immune dysregulation.[14] However, traditional observational studies investigating the Gln-AR relationship face limitations, including restricted sample sizes and insufficient consideration of potential confounding factors (e.g., individual characteristics, dietary habits, lifestyle) or reverse causality. Consequently, further research is warranted to definitively elucidate the role of Gln in AR.

MR is an epidemiological method that utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes.[15] Proposed by Katan[16] and conceptually analogous to gene-level RCTs, MR leverages single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) robustly associated with the exposure, typically identified through genome-wide association study (GWAS) as IVs.[17,18] This approach mitigates confounding and reverse causation biases inherent in observational studies.[17,18] Bidirectional MR, employing a two-sample framework,[19] further evaluates potential reverse causality (i.e., whether the outcome influences the exposure), enhancing causal inference. The random assortment of parental alleles during meiosis ensures that genetic associations are largely unaffected by confounding or reverse causation, earning MR the designation of “nature’s RCT.”[20] MR relies on three core assumptions for valid causal inference[17,21]: Relevance: IVs exhibit a strong association with the exposure; Independence: IVs are independent of confounders affecting the exposure-outcome relationship; and Exclusion Restriction: IVs influence the outcome solely through the exposure pathway. While acknowledging inherent limitations, mendelian randomization (MR) offers significant potential for elucidating AR etiology and identifying therapeutic targets.

In this study, we employ bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between AR and Gln, providing novel genetic insights into their potential causal interplay.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source

GWAS summary statistics for AR and Gln were obtained from the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The datasets used were: AR (Outcome/Exposure): finn-b-ALLERGIC_RHINITIS (217,914 individuals; 16,380,461 SNPs); and Gln (Exposure/Outcome): met-d-Gln (114,750 individuals; 12,321,875 SNPs). A bidirectional two-sample MR design was employed: Forward MR analysis: Gln served as the exposure and AR as the outcome; and Reverse MR analysis: AR served as the exposure and Gln as the outcome.

2.2. Data pre-processing

The SNPs significantly associated with the outcome were obtained from the IEU-OpenGWAS database and used as IVs for MR causal inference analysis. The MR analysis rests upon three fundamental premises: a notable correlation was established between IVs and exposure factors; IVs were devoid of any confounding factors; and IVs could only influence outcomes through exposure factors and not through other pathways. The exposure factor data were first retrieved, and IVs (SNPs) were identified using the extract_instruments function from the TwoSampleMR package (v 0.5.6).[22] The significance threshold for selecting IVs was set at P < 5 × 10−8 to ensure strong association with the exposure.[23] The result direction adopted a suggestive threshold (P < 5 × 10−6). To minimize bias due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), SNPs in LD were pruned using the parameters cluster = TRUE, r² < 0.001, and kb = 10,000.[24,25] Eliminate SNPs directly associated with AR (P ≤ .05). The strength of each IV was further evaluated using the F-statistic, SNPs with an F-statistic below 10 were excluded to avoid weak instrument bias.[26] Finally, the harmonise_data function from the TwoSampleMR package (v 0.5.6) was used to align the effect alleles and effect sizes between the exposure and outcome datasets, ensuring consistent and comparable genetic effects across analyses.

2.3. Bidirectional MR analysis

Effect alleles and effect sizes were harmonized using the “harmonise_data” function from the TwoSampleMR package. MR analysis were then performed employing the following methods: inverse-variance weighted (IVW, fixed effects)[27] (primary method), weighted median,[28] simple median, MR-Egger,[29] and maximum likelihood. Subsequently, odds ratios (ORs) were calculated to identify risk factors (OR > 1) and protective factors (OR < 1). Results were visualized using scatter plots, forest plot, and funnel plots.

2.4. Sensitivity analysis of forward and reverse MR analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the MR analyses, sensitivity analyses were performed. Heterogeneity among IVs estimates was assessed using Cochran Q test; a Q-value P > .05 suggested no significant heterogeneity. Potential horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the intercept test from MR-Egger regression, implemented via the “mr_pleiotropy_test” function in the TwoSampleMR package; an intercept P > .05 indicated no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy. Finally, leave-one-out (LOO) analysis was conducted by iteratively removing each SNP and recalculating the causal estimate. The stability of the overall effect estimate across all LOO iterations indicated the absence of influential individual SNPs and supported the robustness of the primary findings.

2.5. Phenotype scanning

This study employed a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis approach. Data were obtained from publicly available GWAS databases. IVs were selected using a P-value threshold (P < 5 × 10−8 for forward MR and P < 5 × 10−6 for reverse MR) and subsequently pruned for LD (r²= 0.001, kb = 10000). The analysis was conducted using multiple methods, including inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, weighted median, simple median, and maximum likelihood, with the IVW method serving as the primary reference. Sensitivity analyses were performed using Cochran Q test, MR Egger intercept test, and leave-one-out analysis. All statistical analyses were carried out using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6) in R.

The causal association between Gln exposure and AR outcome was evaluated using the TwoSampleMR package (P < .05).

3. Results

3.1. Forward MR analysis

After screening, 40 SNPs were selected as IVs from the met-d-Gln dataset to study the effect of Gln on AR using forward MR analysis (Table 1). MR analysis using the five different methods showed that Gln was negatively causally associated with AR and that Gln was a protective factor for AR (IVW model: OR = 0.874, 95% CI = 0.77–0.991, P = .036) (weighted median model: OR = 0.835, 95% CI = 0.701–0.994, P = .043), (MR-Egger model: OR = 0.829, 95% CI = 0.669–1.027, P = .095), (Simple median model: OR = 0.898, 95% CI = 0.714–1.13, P = .359), (Maximum likelihood model: OR = 0.873, 95% CI = 0.769–0.99, P = .035) (Fig. 1A and Table 1). The negative slopes in the scatter plot indicate that higher Gln levels were associated with a lower risk of AR occurrence (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the forest plot showed that an increase in Gln levels reduced the risk of AR development according to the IVW method (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, the symmetrical distribution of the samples along both sides of the IVW line in the funnel plot indicated that the MR conformed to the second law of MR grouping (Fig. 1D).

MR analysis using the five methods showed that AR had no causal relationship with Gln (P > .05) (Fig. 2A and Table 3). The negative slope in the scatter plot indicates that the AR did not directly affect Gln (Fig. 2B). The forest plot showed that AR was not causally associated with Gln according to the IVW approach (Fig. 2C). Lastly, the funnel plot showed that MR conformed to the second law of MR grouping (Fig. 2D).

3.3. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to determine the reliability of the bidirectional MR analysis results. The heterogeneity test results showed an absence of heterogeneity among the samples in the forward and reverse MR analyses (Q_pval > 0.05) (Tables 4 and 5). The horizontal pleiotropy test results showed an absence of a horizontal pleiotropy effect between Gln and AR in the forward MR analysis (P = .526) (Table 6) and between AR and Gln in the reverse MR analysis (P = .609) (Table 7). Finally, the results of the LOO analysis showed the absence of serious bias in the forward and reverse MR analyses, indicating that the results of the bidirectional MR analysis were reliable (Figs. 3 and 4). Altogether, these results indicate that Gln is causally associated with AR occurrence, whereas AR is not causally associated with Gln. Additionally, the results indicated that Gln is a protective factor against AR.

4. Discussion

AR is a type I hypersensitivity reaction mediated by a Th2 immune response, characterized by excessive allergen-specific IgE production in atopic individuals.[30] Upon allergen re-exposure, IgE bound to mast cell surface receptors triggers degranulation, releasing inflammatory mediators (e.g., histamine, leukotrienes) that drive allergic symptoms. Consequently, identifying modifiable risk factors for AR is critical for effective prevention strategies.[31] Current research focuses on elucidating associations between AR onset and specific risk factors. Elevated levels of inflammatory mediators (e.g., macrophage inflammatory protein-1α, tumor necrosis factor-α)[32] and comorbid immune-mediated conditions (e.g., atopic dermatitis, asthma, Crohn disease).[33] are positively associated with AR risk. Conversely, aspirin intake,[34] interleukin-6, and adipocyte fatty acid-binding protein levels demonstrate negative associations with AR occurrence.[35] Standard AR pharmacotherapy includes intranasal corticosteroids, antihistamines, and leukotriene receptor antagonists; however, therapeutic efficacy exhibits significant interindividual variability. Given this limitation, there is increasing emphasis on preventive approaches aimed at modulating immune responses in AR patients to mitigate disease progression and promote remission.

Gln, the most abundant nonessential amino acid in humans, plays a critical role in cellular biosynthesis, energy metabolism, and maintenance of intracellular homeostasis.[36] Metabolized primarily via glutaminolysis, Gln fuels the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation in immune cells to meet their high energy demands.[37] Gln stimulates macrophage and neutrophil function, modulates M1/M2 polarization, and promotes anti-inflammatory responses.[38,39] Furthermore, Gln acts as an immunometabolic regulator in white adipose tissue.[40] Gln depletion shifts cellular metabolism from glutaminolysis to glycolysis, driving nuclear O-GlcNAcylation and pro-inflammatory activation 39. Conversely, elevated Gln levels – potentially mediated by interleukin-6 and IL-1β pathways – can mitigate inflammation, leukocytosis, and glycolytic flux.[40] Paradoxically, while Gln deficiency enhances leukocyte antimicrobial functions (phagocytosis and autophagy),[41] it significantly impairs IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor-α production by activated CD8+ T cells.[41] Given its role in modulating immunity and suppressing inflammation, Gln supplementation represents an established component of clinical therapeutic nutritional support.[42]

Research interest in the pathophysiological roles of Gln and its metabolism across diverse diseases continues to expand. Gln supplementation enhances survival in experimental endotoxin shock models, improves immune and intestinal barrier function, reduces bacteremia incidence, attenuates intestinal mucosal atrophy, and suppresses bronchial asthma pathogenesis.[43,44] Furthermore, Gln alleviates atopic dermatitis by inhibiting cytosolic phospholipase A2 activity.[45] Gln metabolism critically regulates cellular redox homeostasis and modulates key signaling pathways.[46] Circulating Gln may confer neuroprotection, suggesting therapeutic potential for Alzheimer disease prevention and management.[47] Notably, a causal relationship between Gln levels and programmed death ligand-1 expression has been identified, suggesting a potential link to immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance via amino acid degradation pathways.[48]

This study demonstrates a negative causal relationship between Gln levels and AR, suggesting elevated Gln may confer protection against AR development. Mechanistically, Gln modulates AR pathogenesis through multiple pathways: Regulation of B10 Cells: Gln maintains immunoregulatory B10 cell (CD19+IL-10+ B cells) function. AR patients exhibit reduced B10 cell frequency, diminished expression of the Gln transporter ASCT2, and impaired IL-10 secretion 14. Gln metabolism sustains B10 cell function by activating mammalian target of rapamycin signaling,[49,50] which inhibits glycogen synthase kinase-3 phosphorylation – elevated in AR B cells and inhibitory to IL-10 expression – thereby promoting B10 differentiation and IL-10 production to suppress excessive immune responses. Enhancement of Anti-Infective Immunity: Gln is essential for lymphocyte proliferation, cytokine production, macrophage phagocytosis/secretion, and neutrophil bactericidal activity.[42] Supplementation reduces inflammation,[46] bolsters host defense, and lowers infection incidence. As infections are AR risk factors, this enhanced immunity indirectly mitigates infection-triggered AR. Reduction of Early-Life Risk Exposures: Gln may help maintain immune balance by promoting the generation of Treg cells and suppressing the differentiation of T helper 17 (Th17) cells, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses.[50] This immunomodulatory effect could potentially contribute to reduced symptoms associated with infection or inflammation, consequently leading to decreased use of antibiotics and paracetamol. Antibiotic exposure disrupts immature gut microbiota, potentially dysregulating immune development and increasing AR susceptibility.[51–53] Acetaminophen use in children (ages 6–7 and 13–14) is associated with elevated AR risk.[54] By enhancing infection resistance and immune regulation,[14,42,46] Gln offers a potential alternative to these agents, indirectly inhibiting AR initiation and progression.

Utilizing publicly accessible GWAS data for AR and Gln, we performed bidirectional two-sample MR to investigate causal relationships between these traits, supplemented by sensitivity analyses to assess result robustness. Forward MR analysis demonstrated a significant inverse causal effect of genetically predicted Gln levels on AR risk, indicating a protective role for Gln. Conversely, reverse MR analysis found no causal effect of AR on Gln levels. This unidirectional relationship confirms the protective effect of Gln against AR is robust to reverse causality, aligning with established clinical observational evidence.

In the forward MR analysis, using Gln as the exposure factor and AR as the outcome, we selected 294 independent SNPs that were strongly correlated with Gln and were unrelated to AR as IVs. These IVs showed F-statistics of >10, indicating no risk of bias caused by weak IVs. Further MR analysis revealed that Gln showed a causal relationship with AR, with an OR value of <1, indicating that Gln is a protective factor for AR. Furthermore, the IVW method showed a small intercept, indicating that the confounding factors had only a minor impact and that the results of the MR analysis were reliable. Moreover, the negative slope in the scatter plot further confirms that Gln may serve as a protective factor against AR. Altogether, these results indicate that increased Gln levels may reduce the risk of AR, which is consistent with the results reported in clinical studies.

This study has several limitations. First, there are significant geographical variations in the global prevalence of AR, which may be attributed to differences in genetic background, environmental exposures, and lifestyle factors. Since the GWAS data used in this study were primarily derived from European populations, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other ethnic groups, thereby limiting the universality and representativeness of our conclusions. Second, although MR analysis can infer causal relationships, it cannot fully elucidate the specific biological mechanisms and pathways through which Gln influences AR. Third, the protective effect of Gln on AR inferred via MR reflects the influence of genetically predicted lifelong exposure levels; this effect size may not directly correspond to the outcomes of clinical interventions within specific periods, such as short-term nutritional supplementation. Furthermore, despite the use of multiple sensitivity analysis methods, we cannot completely rule out potential IV bias or undetected pleiotropic effects. Finally, differences in phenotypic definitions across databases and potential gene-environment interactions may also have influenced the results.

Future research should be conducted in several directions. First, replication analyses in populations with diverse ancestral backgrounds should be prioritized to examine the robustness of the current findings across ethnicities and to explore the role of gene-environment interactions in AR pathogenesis. Second, experimental studies – including cellular, animal, and human clinical trials – are warranted to investigate the specific molecular mechanisms by which Gln modulates immune cell function. Additionally, RCTs targeting different populations, especially high-risk groups such as children, could evaluate the potential of Gln supplementation in preventing or adjunctively treating AR, and examine variations in efficacy across racial and environmental contexts – particularly the relationship and discrepancy between short-term intervention effects and the lifelong genetic effects identified in this study. Integrating multi-omics data may also help systematically reveal the causal pathways linking Gln and AR. Finally, developing and applying gene-environment interaction MR (GxE MR) will help clarify how Gln interacts with environmental factors such as diet and early-life antibiotic use to influence AR risk, thereby providing a theoretical basis for developing population-specific prevention strategies.

5. Conclusions

Our MR analysis demonstrates that elevated genetically predicted Gln levels are causally associated with reduced AR risk. Reverse MR analysis confirmed the absence of a causal effect of AR on Gln levels, reinforcing the unidirectional protective relationship. Unlike observational studies, MR design minimizes confounding and reverse causality bias. Consistent null findings across pleiotropy-robust methods (MR-Egger, weighted median) and sensitivity analyses indicate no significant horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity, supporting result robustness. These findings suggest that higher Gln exposure may confer clinical benefits in AR management.
