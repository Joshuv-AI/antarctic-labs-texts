A dataset of tabulated results from American ranked-choice voting elections
Authors: Yuki Atsusaka, Jordan Holbrook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the past two decades, a growing number of ranked-choice voting (RCV) elections have been conducted in various jurisdictions across the United States. However, tabulated results of RCV have been reported and stored in widely different styles across places and years, making it infeasible for researchers to perform systematic analyses of vote tabulations. We introduce ARCHIVE: a dataset of standardized tabulated results for over 7600 round-level candidate vote counts from 514 American RCV elections, 2004-2024. To construct the dataset, we develop a methodological procedure based on large language models that semi-automatically collect, standardize, and store candidate vote counts while instantly validating the resulting information. Our dataset releases multiple levels of data, including election metadata, round-level attributes, and candidate-level information with consistent election identifiers, allowing users to address key questions in electoral competition under RCV. Our project and user interface are also available on our corresponding website.

Background & Summary

Single-winner ranked-choice voting (RCV) has been an increasingly popular electoral system that has been adopted in various jurisdictions in the United States1–3. Under RCV, voters can vote by ranking multiple candidates and the majority winner is determined by sequentially eliminating the least popular candidate and transferring voters’ ballots in each round4. By allowing voters to express their preference in rankings and tabulating their first and lower-choice votes, RCV is often expected to provide voters with diverse sets of candidates and more meaningful choices5–12.

Yet, from the perspective of social scientists and election experts, more choices under RCV, along with decentralized American election administration13, create a significant practical problem—there are vastly different ways in which tabulated results are reported, with varying degrees of information that are available for researchers and observers. Figure 1 illustrates this heterogeneity by showing different reporting styles (and file formats) from three contests, including (a) the 2021 New York City Democratic Primary election (html), (b) the 2021 Minneapolis City Council Ward 2 election (pdf), and (c) the 2022 U.S. Senate general election in Alaska (text), respectively. Consequently, it is often challenging to even casually reference the result of any RCV election, let alone performing a comparative analysis of multiple contests under RCV. Indeed, today’s best way to collect election results in RCV would be to independently access the public website of an election office that administers elections and manually extract relevant information. Even if researchers manage to automatically access such websites, tabulated results are not standardized, reported in different file formats, and thus automated data collection is not easily applied.

Graphical Representation of Our Procedure to Construct ARCHIVE.

In this work, we introduce a dataset of standardized tabulated results from American ranked-choice voting elections (ARCHIVE), 2004-2024, to facilitate the study of RCV, electoral systems, and American elections. ARCHIVE enables anyone to report and analyze results for more than 7600 candidate-round observations from 514 American RCV elections in a standardized format. The dataset offers three layers of data, including (a) election-level attributes, (b) round-level variables, and (c) candidate-level features. To construct ARCHIVE, we develop a methodological procedure based on large language models (LLMs) to make the data construction fully reproducible with one click instead of relying on massive manual efforts (except for collecting raw data). Figure 1 graphically summarizes our methodological procedure, in which we (1) manually collect raw tabulated results in varying formats, (2) extract layers of information via LLMs and store them in JSON, and (3) process, compile, and validate data in analysis-ready formats. ARCHIVE is publicly available in the Harvard Dataverse14. Along with the data, we also publish, maintain, and update our LLM-based tools to compile the dataset in our GitHub repository. There, ARCHIVE will be continuously maintained and regularly updated as future RCV contests get reported. Furthermore, our corresponding website (archive-rcv.com) allows users to follow the latest updates and interactively explore our dataset without requiring any coding.

We assume two types of audience for our dataset. Primarily, our dataset speaks to political scientists who study American and comparative politics. For these researchers, especially scholars of electoral systems, data on votes and seats—vote counts and seat shares—provide fundamental information about elections and representative democracy15. In other words, political scientists often seek to analyze the distribution of votes and seats (and functions thereof) and explain their variation. In this context, our work provides ready-to-analyze data for researchers who wish to access vote count-based quantities of interest for their empirical studies, such as candidate vote shares, the number of transferred ballots, and the number of exhausted ballots, among others, at the election, round, and candidate levels. By offering a transparent and consistent coding scheme and election identifiers, our dataset also offers a sampling frame to political scientists who wish to construct their own datasets on RCV.

Secondarily, our dataset communicates with election experts, including practitioners, journalists, and scholars who study RCV elections and electoral systems. By offering comprehensive data on tabulated results as useful references, our work seeks to help such experts achieve their professional goals by reducing the burden of costly data collection. Our dataset not only provides easy access to reported results, but also obtains raw data in a systematic manner, standardizes raw data stored in vastly different formats, and validates the resulting information—all of which can be costly hurdles even for experienced experts and researchers when executed properly. While some researchers may be able to tabulate an RCV election from its cast vote records by developing their own computational tools, there may be no guarantee that the actual tabulation table and such in-house tabulation results will match exactly. As we will detail below, this is because of a set of subtle differences in how ranked ballots are tabulated across and within jurisdictions (e.g., how to define invalid ballots, how to eliminate write-ins, and how many candidates each round can eliminate). Thus, if the goal is to refer to actual reported results, our dataset may offer more externally valid information than in-house tabulation. By integrating actual reported results and our LLM-based procedure, both of which make the dataset more transparent and robust to researcher degrees of freedom, our work seeks to construct an intellectual commons for RCV experts that any parties can continuously utilize and validate.

Our dataset has two general usages: (1) targeted reporting and (2) comparative analysis. First, the dataset functions as an archive of RCV election results for anyone who is interested in RCV, such as researchers, election administrators, political observers, journalists, litigators, and students and followers of American elections. ARCHIVE allows users to see, report, and save the fully tabulated results of any contests at the election, round, and/or candidate levels in a unified format. Our dataset makes it easy for users of any statistical languages (e.g., R, Stata, and Python) to generate such reports in a fully reproducible manner. Moreover, our website allows users to extract election results in the same format by navigating the user interface with no coding requirement. While targeted reporting itself may be the goal in many applications, reported results may also have a few secondary usages. For example, users may use ARCHIVE to validate simulation studies of voting behavior and candidate competition under RCV by comparing simulated and empirical vote shares across rounds6.

Second, with our dataset, researchers can perform comparative analyses of RCV elections. For example, round-by-round results in our data provide fine-grained information about candidate vote shares, ballot transfers, exhausted ballots, undervotes, overvotes, invalid ballots, and the number of rounds, all of which allow users to address a wide range of questions related to electoral competition under RCV7,16–18. Among them, one central question relates to political fragmentation in the electoral system19–21—How many meaningful choices do voters have in American RCV elections? It is often hypothesized that RCV provides more choices for voters than does single-member districts with the plurality rule, which is known to tend to produce two “meaningful” candidates5,8. To address this question, researchers may use ARCHIVE to measure the so-called effective number of vote-winning candidates based on candidate vote shares in each round20,22– 24.

By collecting additional data, analysts may also use our dataset to study the downstream effects of a plethora of factors on various quantities based on vote counts, vote tabulations, and ballot transfers. For example, researchers may merge candidate attributes with our dataset and study how they correlate with ballot transfers (e.g., Do minority candidates receive more ballot transfers than white candidates?). Scholars may also collect district-level information and examine whether such factors relate to tabulated results (e.g., Do red or blue districts have higher proportions of partial rankings than purple districts?). Users can also code details in jurisdiction-level election laws and study how they shape tabulated results (e.g., Does the number of candidates voters are allowed to rank influence the number of rounds and ballot transfers?). Similarly, by integrating with additional variables of interest, researchers may study a range of topics that are central to RCV research, such as, cross-endorsements25, invalid ballots17, incumbency7, negative campaigning26, the number of candidates8, candidate race and gender27, racially polarized voting28, redistricting29, to name a few. We hope that our dataset facilitates such descriptive and observational studies of electoral competition under RCV for our deeper theoretical understanding of the electoral system.

Beyond the two general usages, ARCHIVE offers several specialized utilities. For example, political scientists may use our dataset as a benchmark for merging multiple datasets and variables related to RCV contests. Since RCV elections are non-uniformly adopted across offices, districts, jurisdictions, and states, we develop a coding rule for assigning a unique identifier for each contest. Instead of using ad hoc identifiers for each application independently, scholars may merge any variables with the uniform identifier across studies, which, we hope, will further facilitate the cumulation of our scientific knowledge. Moreover, experts in election administration may use our dataset to examine the consequences of technical details in the implementation of RCV for tabulated results, such as how to define an invalid ballot, how many candidates each round can eliminate, and how to order candidates on the ballot,17,30–36. Meanwhile, our LLM-based procedure may motivate similar data collection efforts outside RCV research. In doing so, our work contributes to the growing body of studies for collecting and validating hard-to-reach data in political science37–42.

Finally, ARCHIVE offers complementary methods and data to the interdisciplinary field of RCV research beyond political science. In the broader research community, several resources for analyzing individual-level ranking data have been available for researchers of RCV and related fields, such as computational social choice. Among them, the collection of cast vote records allows us to analyze how individual voters actually ranked available candidates in American RCV elections43. Relatedly, PrefLib provides a library of preferential data, including data on voter preference in ranking-based electoral systems in multiple countries44. More recently, several Python-based packages offer tools for processing and analyzing such individual-level ranked ballots in RCV, such as pref_voting45, VoteKit46, and Votelib47. These emerging tools allow us to analyze and simulate voting behavior, voter preference, and how different rules may lead to alternative winners.

Our work differs from these studies in several ways. First, our dataset provides aggregated data on actual candidate vote shares and related quantities. That is, ARCHIVE does not contain any voter-level information, which may be useful in many other studies of RCV, and instead covers various candidate-level quantities. Second, our methodology focuses on extracting, standardizing, and validating unstructured data of tabulated results. In this sense, our work addresses a general problem of how to compile analysis-ready tabular data from unstructured raw data, as opposed to a specific task of processing and modeling ranking data—which is important on its own. Third, ARCHIVE allows anyone to explore RCV contests without assuming any software or programming language. While the development of our data is based on Python (see Code Availability), like the above-mentioned resources, users can open and use our dataset in any environment of their choice, including Excel. Our work prioritizes the accessibility of the data, not because we expect that our target users lack coding and technical expertise, but because we assume that they wish to focus on addressing their substantive questions instead of making efforts to construct a similar dataset independently, given limited resources. This way, our goal is not to provide an alternative tool to study RCV replacing existing tools, but to offer a complementary resource for a wide range of users.

Methods

To construct the dataset, we develop a methodological procedure based on LLMs for collecting, standardizing, and storing tabulated results from RCV elections. In this procedure, we transform unstructured raw tabulated results into analysis-ready tabular data. Figure 2 illustrates our workflow. Our primary input data consists of official election results retrieved directly from municipal, county, and state government websites. We begin by manually downloading raw election reports in available file formats (e.g., .pdf, .txt, or .html). Next, we convert these documents into plain text files. We then insert each of the plain texts into a prompt we design for an LLM, which then extracts relevant information from the input file and saves it in a predefined format via JSON. Finally, we turn our JSON file into a set of datasets in tabular formats while validating and postprocessing the data. We implement the procedure in Python, except for the first manual step.

Workflow for Extracting, Standardizing, Storing, and Validating Tabulated Results.

This approach, while novel in its application to RCV, builds on an emerging body of research that uses LLMs to automate and expedite resource-intensive data extraction tasks across various disciplines, including political science, economics, biomedicine, and law48–51. One example in healthcare research shows that LLMs can be used to perform a systematic literature review by extracting and standardizing patient information, including their demographics and health outcomes, from multiple published studies, which would be conventionally done via a manual process52. Similarly, LLMs are being employed for data integration tasks such as schema matching, which involves identifying semantic relationships between data elements across different tables49. Our method applies these advantages of LLMs to the unique challenges of extracting and compiling electoral data.

In this section, we detail each stage of our procedure. We first describe the heterogeneity in tabulated results in RCV. We then introduce a framework for organizing our data, detail our step for downloading and preprocessing raw data, and discuss the prompt we use for the LLM. Finally, we show how we postprocess the output and compile tabular data, construct a unique identifier for each contest, and address the heterogeneity in key concepts.

Heterogeneity in Tabulated Results

A fundamental challenge in studying RCV in the United States is the lack of standardized reporting formats for election results. While recommended practices for releasing RCV election results are available53, tabulated results remain widely heterogeneous. As Fig. 1 illustrates, these styles vary across at least three dimensions:

Jurisdictions: Results are reported differently across jurisdictions. For example, the State of Alaska provides structured, machine-readable PDF files. By contrast, the City of Minneapolis publishes results as separate webpages, in which some results are provided as Excel spreadsheets and others appear as PDFs, depending on the number of rounds and the reporting practices used in a given year.

By contrast, the City of Minneapolis publishes results as separate webpages, in which some results are provided as Excel spreadsheets and others appear as PDFs, depending on the number of rounds and the reporting practices used in a given year.

Offices: Even within the same jurisdiction, reporting styles appear to vary across offices and districts. For example, in Maine, Portland School Board District 3 shows the first round in the most-left column and the final round in the most-right column, whereas District 5 shows multiple rounds in the opposite direction in the same year54.

In addition to the heterogeneity over how results are reported, we also have heterogeneity regarding what information is included. For instance, tabulated results in Minneapolis include explicit ballot transfers counts between rounds, showing how ballots move from eliminated candidates to remaining candidates55. In contrast, the results for San Francisco’s Board of Supervisors contests contain only candidate totals by round with no ballot transfers56. This heterogeneity poses challenges for researchers, journalists, and election administrators seeking to analyze RCV results comparatively and over time. In the absence of standardized structures in raw data, our methodology starts with defining how such data should be collected and organized.

Data Model

To convert unstructured tabulated results into analysis-ready data, we first define a blueprint for our dataset known as a data model. The data model clarifies (a) what information we should extract from raw tabulated results and (b) how such information should be organized in our dataset. Defining the data model is critical for our work because reported results differ widely in terms of the number and type of information available. Even if we only focus on candidate votes across rounds, storing a set of tabulated results in the same way that they are reported requires over 500 spreadsheets with different numbers of rows and columns. Furthermore, such spreadsheets do not allow researchers to easily process, analyze, and visualize information across elections.

We overcome these challenges by considering three datasets with different units of analysis. More specifically, we construct (1) election-level data, where each row refers to a single contest, (2) round-level data, in which each row is a round within a given election, and (3) candidate-level data, where each row corresponds to a candidate within a round (within an election). First, the election-level data store various election metadata in columns, such as jurisdiction, office, year, and election type, to name a few. Second, the round-level data contain round-level attributes, such as the numbers of active ballots and exhausted ballots. Finally, the candidate-level data have candidate-round or candidate-specific variables, such as the number of votes and transferred ballots. This data model gives our raw data a specific structure, which allows us to extract, process, validate, and analyze tabulated results.

Downloading and Preprocessing Raw Data

Once we define the data model, the next step is to collect raw data. We start by accessing publicly available websites that report tabulated results of RCV elections. Many of these websites are maintained by the jurisdictions in which RCV elections are administered (e.g., State of Alaska and City of San Francisco), whereas others are provided by higher administrative entities (e.g., Alameda County for Berkeley, Oakland, and San Leandro). Once we identify the website for each jurisdiction, we maneuver the webpage to discover the exact location (or even a pop-up) where each district’s tabulated results are reported. Our GitHub repository stores the URL for each tabulation table. This first step of our data collection is not automated via LLMs and is performed manually. While we attempted scraping and other automated methods, these approaches failed as tabulated results are reported highly inconsistently across and within the websites.

As highlighted above, tabulated results are published in a wide range of formats, including PDFs, spreadsheets, HTML tables, and plain-text summaries. If we were to rely on manual efforts in extracting relevant information, these original formats may suffice (as “human agents” can see them and extract relevant information from them). In contrast, our procedure uses LLMs for automating data collection. Because LLMs work well with text strings as inputs (i.e., LLMs “see” tabulation tables via texts), we also preprocess raw data by transforming it into a set of .txt files.

For example, for PDF files, we first download original files and then export text from them using a Python library. Meanwhile, for HTML tables, we manually scrape the information and save it in a series of .txt files. For spreadsheets, we take the same procedure. Finally, when results are available in text files, we use the original files with no modification. When we judge that an original file does not contain any identifying information (e.g., jurisdiction name, office, and district number), we manually add such information.

This preprocessing step inevitably loses the “spacial” information that used to be available in the original files (i.e., how different pieces of information are presented in a table format). That is, a reported table is no longer a table but a series of strings (i.e., a single long sentence). Even though this would confuse human coders, our procedure is useful for LLMs. When both official and unofficial reports are available, we prioritize collecting data from official reports.

Prompt Engineering and Execution

After collecting and preprocessing raw data, we now use an LLM to convert unstructured data into the three datasets defined above. To ask the LLM to execute data extraction and organization according our data model, we design a prompt shown in Fig. 3. The prompt consists of three components: (1) a high-level instruction (Parse the…), (2) a formal JSON schema that defines the required fields and how they should be organized (Target JSON…), and (3) preprocessed tabulated results (text) (Input data). When we feed the prompt to the LLM, we replace “Insert Raw Election Data for Extraction” with actual strings for a given election, whereas the other parts stay the same.

Prompt for Extracting and Storing Relevant Information from Tabulated Results.

In the second part, we represent our data model using a system known as a JSON schema. JSON—JavaScript Object Notation—is a widely used file format for representing data in a structured way. It stores information with pairs of labels and values, collectively known as a schema, in a flexible manner. For example, each label (e.g., "year") is an equivalent of a column name in a typical spreadsheet format, whereas each value (e.g., 2021) is a cell value. Unlike spreadsheets, however, JSON allows each label to have its own “spreadsheets” (e.g., "candidates": […]). Our schema has two additional layers defined by "candidates" and "rounds". This way, we organize and store different levels of information (i.e., election metadata, round-level attributes, and candidate-specific data) in a single JSON file.

In the prompt, we further specify how each value should be recorded. For example, "state": "XX" indicates that (a) the LLM must extract the information about the state in which a given election was held and (b) it must be stored as an abbreviated state name. Similarly, "votes": INT means that (a) the LLM must extract a candidate vote count from a given round and (b) the information must be stored as an integer. It is important to highlight that our prompt does not specify from where the LLM should extract these pieces of information based on input tabulated results. In other words, we let the LLM learn how to interpret unstructured tabulated results and store the relevant information per the data model.

We also emphasize that we refined our prompt iteratively—possibly a hundred times—as new jurisdictions, election types, and reporting formats were incorporated. Each new report revealed new differences in layout, terminology, and reporting conventions. As we evaluated different elections, we adjusted our prompt to make sure that all the relevant data fields were extracted and that the output was in a consistent format. New contests contributed to improving the prompt, making it more reliable as a data-extraction and standardization method. This is consistent with other research on harmonizing information from different sources: exposure to heterogeneous data helps reveal inconsistencies and improve the standardization process57.

To implement our automated procedure, we use the OpenAI API with LLM model GPT-4o (the latest model at the time of data collection), with the model temperature set to 0.1 and the maximum token limit set to 15,000. The temperature parameter controls randomness in the model’s responses; setting it to 0.1 minimizes variation and encourages the model to return deterministic and reproducible outputs. The max token parameter specifies the maximum length of the model’s output; a value of 15,000 ensures that the model can return the complete data structure required for heavily-tabulated elections with many candidates and rounds without truncating the output.

Postprocessing and Compiling Tabular Data

Once we complete data extraction, we turn the JSON file into three tabular-form datasets. In addition to compiling extracted data, we also compute multiple variables based on the extracted information. For example, we compute election and candidate identifiers, the number of candidates, the number of rounds, candidate vote shares in percentage points, the number of transferred ballots, and candidate status per round (eliminated or remaining). As discussed below, we also compute several variables for our automated validation.

Finally, we validate our resulting data (see Validation) and correct potential errors, if any. Again, we iteratively refine our dataset by re-designing our prompt, performing validation, and correcting information whenever necessary. While our work partially depends on manual efforts (e.g., downloading raw reports, designing a prompt, etc), our procedure automates all steps involving data extraction and organization by integrating JSON and the LLM.

Unique Identifiers

To ensure consistency and facilitate merging across datasets, we create a standardized approach to constructing unique key identifiers that are reproducible across any dataset containing the same set of identifying variables. By standardizing these keys, we enable reliable joins between multiple tables—such as parsed text results, metadata registries, and historical archives—based on a shared election_id. This design also accommodates both district-based and at-large elections by using At-Large in place of a district number when appropriate.

This structure is flexible and allows the schema to generalize across local, state, and federal election datasets. Additionally, this identifier structure has the advantage of being fully reproducible. That is, any researcher working with past or future RCV datasets can generate the same election_id as long as the same key variables are present. This ensures that new datasets can be aligned with ours in a consistent manner, facilitating replication and validation across future studies.

Heterogeneity in Key Concepts

Finally, beyond formatting, tabulated results vary in how they define and report core RCV concepts. For example, terms describing ballot status—including “exhausted,” “inactive,” “undervote,” or “overvote”—may be used inconsistently. The same outcome may appear under different labels in different places, and some jurisdictions provide no definitions at all. San Francisco publishes a detailed glossary defining terms such as blanks, overvotes, and non-transferable totals58. In contrast, Minneapolis defines only a narrow subset of concepts, primarily undervotes and threshold59. Furthermore, New York City reports categories like active and inactive ballots without providing clear definitions, complicating the analysis of how undervotes or defective ballots are processed60.

Our goal in this project is not to create a universal dictionary of RCV terminology or to reconcile every conceptual difference across jurisdictions. We also do not attempt to determine how each jurisdiction should define ballot categories, nor do we create a crosswalk that maps all local terms into a single national standard. Instead, we focus on extracting a core set of fields that are least subject to interpretation, such as candidate names, round numbers, and vote totals. These elements are generally reported in a straightforward way and can be recovered consistently from the source materials.

To achieve this, we refined our extraction prompts by testing them across a diverse set of jurisdictions rather than relying on any single reporting style. As we processed more than 500 election reports, we improved the prompt to accommodate new layouts and terminology. After extraction, additional post-processing ensured that key identifiers, including office titles and election identifiers, were cleaned and standardized where possible.

Some inconsistencies still remain, reflecting both variation in how jurisdictions report their results and the limits of extracting information from unstructured documents. This shows the importance of our automated validation system, which identifies unusual patterns and flags possible extraction errors. Future work could expand this approach by supplementing the extraction process with jurisdiction-specific documentation that clarifies how local terms should be interpreted.

Technical Validation

We validate our dataset in several ways. First, we validate whether our procedure correctly extracted and compiled the reported tabulation results based on internal logic and external data. Next, we develop and implement a procedure for automated validation. Third, we examine the representativeness of ARCHIVE relative to the population of RCV elections based on additional data.

Technical and Empirical Validation

We begin our validation by performing a set of checks. The goal of these checks is to identify any patterns that should not exist based on internal logic. For example, we examine whether the number of rounds does not surpass the number of available candidates plus one (accounting for write-ins). Similarly, among the remaining candidates, we check whether their vote counts at a given round are not smaller than their vote counts in the previous round. In other words, we check whether monotonicity in votes—votes should not decrease over rounds—holds over rounds. Moreover, we verify that each contest does not have multiple winners. Furthermore, we investigate whether the sum of transferred ballots (negative or positive) must not be positive per round within each contest. With no exhausted ballots, in fact, it must sum up to zero, while it can be slightly negative in the presence of exhausted ballots.

We find that our dataset passes these checks. Figure 4 shows that (a) the number of rounds is always less than or equal to the number of candidates plus one, (b) the proportion that monotonicity (votes should not decrease over rounds) holds is one in all rounds, (c) the proportion of elections with a single winner is one, and (d) the distribution of transferred votes is tightly concentrated around 0, while showing a few negative values (which may happen in the presence of exhausted ballots). For a few cases, however, we also observe that the sum of transferred ballots is positive, for which we encourage users to be cautious when using these cases.

Selected Technical Validation Tests. Note: The top-left panel shows that the number of rounds is valid as all cases are below the dashed line. The top-right graph reports that the proportion of votes that are larger than or equal to those from the previous round is one for Rounds 2-10. The bottom-left panel confirms that the proportion of elections with a single winner is one. The bottom-right validation shows that the sum of transferred ballots per round is mostly either 0 or negative, while reporting a few cases where the sum is positive—candidates gain votes from somewhere unaccounted.

Next, we provide more direct evidence for whether our dataset has the same information that was reported by election administrators. To perform empirical validation, we leverage the reported results in Minneapolis, which have a standardized reporting format in HTML consistently over time. We then wrote a program to scrape candidate vote counts in the first and final rounds, respectively, from all RCV contests in Minneapolis. We focus on the two rounds because the results in intermediary rounds are not reported and thus unavailable. In contrast to our procedure (and likely manual coding), scraping is expected to correctly extract the information we target, thus providing reliable ground truth data. Our scraped dataset contains 590 candidates from 88 RCV elections.

Figure 5 shows our validation results. It visualizes that vote counts in our dataset (x-axis) align with their ground truth values (y-axis), reporting 100% accuracy in both rounds. Since our validation is automated via scraping, it allows us to automatically perform empirical validation as more election results become available in the future.

Each variable takes the value Pass or Flagged to indicate whether the election satisfies the corresponding condition.

Importantly, a flagged result does not necessarily imply that our dataset contains incorrect information or that our extraction procedure failed. Instead, it signals a potential inconsistency between the extracted results and the reported tabulated data. Such inconsistencies may stem from genuine irregularities or ambiguities in the official reports, from variation in how jurisdictions structure their results, or from limitations in automatic extraction. These validation checks help detect whether the extracted results contain irregularities, such as skipped rounds, implausible vote trajectories, or mismatched totals. In particular, round_totals_match_check does not require exact equality; instead, round-level totals and the sum of candidate votes must fall within a 10 percent tolerance to account for jurisdictions that report incomplete or partially aggregated round statistics.

Table 4 reports the distribution of outcomes for each check across our dataset. Overall, our first release appears to pass most of our validation checks. However, we find that a substantial portion of our dataset is flagged based on the final item. It means that the round-level total of votes extracted for the round-level dataset does not match the total number of votes at a given round manually computed based on candidate vote counts. A mismatch does not inherently indicate an issue with extraction; rather, it can arises because jurisdictions differ in how they report undervotes, overvotes, exhausted ballots, and ballots that become inactive mid-tabulation. When these quantities are included in round totals but are not attributed to candidates, the official round total can deviate from the sum of candidate-level counts.

Representativeness

Finally, we analyze the representativeness of our dataset. The representativeness of a dataset is a key issue for applied researchers who wish to make statistical inference about their population of interest. While we made our best efforts to collect data from all single-winner RCV elections from 2004 to 2024, we were not able to cover several elections. For example, we were unable to collect data from elections whose tabulated results were not publicly available, including the 2018 municipal elections in Berkeley, Oakland, and San Leandro.

To investigate the generalizability of one’s study based on our dataset, we examine the representativeness of our dataset with respect to the population of RCV elections that were held in the time period. The key challenge in such an analysis is that there is no data on the census of RCV elections (i.e., we have no sampling frame). We overcome the issue by leveraging the collection of cast vote records—records of how individual voters ranked candidates—compiled by43. That is, we examine the representativeness of our dataset with respect to a reference dataset that contains all elections for which cast vote records are available (as if it is the population of interest) in the same time period.

Figure 6 presents the distributions of RCV races across states by the two datasets. Except for a few contests for which tabulated results were unavailable (e.g., those in California and Vermont), we find that ARCHIVE covers almost all elections whose cast vote records are currently publicly available. This may strengthen the external validity of future studies based on our dataset. Nonetheless, when using our dataset, we encourage users to pay close attention to the representativeness of our data (including our first release and our future versions) relative to their population of interest.

Comparative Analysis: Effective Number of Candidates by Rounds

Finally, ARCHIVE allows users to perform comparative analyses of RCV elections. To illustrate, we examine the number of meaningful candidates in American RCV elections. While this is one of the central problems in political science in general, it may be particularly of interest to RCV scholars since RCV is often expected to provide more meaningful choices than single-member districts with the plurality rule by breaking the so-called lesser-of-the-two-evil mentality5.

To count meaningful candidates, we turn to the concept of the effective number of vote-winning candidates (ENC)20,22–24. ENC is a vote-share-weighted number of candidates, which accounts for the number of contenders while factoring in how “meaningful” each candidate is based on their voter support. For example, consider an election with five candidates. On the one hand, ENC becomes 5 when all candidates receive the same number of votes. On the other hand, ENC is 1 when a single candidate received all the votes, while the other four candidates received no votes.

ARCHIVE provides round-by-round vote counts for all candidates. This allows us to compute ENC for each round in each election. Let vj be candidate j’s vote share in a given round, where j = 1, …, J (with J candidates) in each election. ENC is defined as 1∑i=1Jvj2, where we square candidate vote shares, sum them up, and take its reciprocal. We then compute the round-by-round ENC (along with the actual number of candidates) as follows:
