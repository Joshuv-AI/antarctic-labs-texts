Integrating biometric and multimodal imaging data for early prediction of myopia onset
Authors: Min Hu, Yanfeng Jiang, Zhiwei Luo, Weizhong Lan, Weiwei Dai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyopia is a growing global health concern, and early detection and intervention are crucial for preventing its onset and progression. This study aims to predict myopia onset one year in advance by integrating biometric measurements with multimodal imaging data, including 3D optical coherence tomography (OCT) and color fundus photography (CFP). A dataset of 472 eyes from 347 subjects aged 6–14 years was collected, encompassing demographic information, biometric data, OCT, and CFP. Deep learning models were trained on OCT and CFP images to extract relevant features. A semi-supervised approach was employed to segment the choroid layer in OCT images, and the segmented images were then used to generate Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid thickness value. An XGBoost model was developed to integrate image scores, ETDRS grid values, and biometric data for predicting myopia onset. The model incorporating all available data achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.845 ± 0.050. Permutation importance analysis revealed that spherical equivalent was the most influential variable, followed by CFP scores, OCT scores, and ETDRS variables. The mixed model with multimodal information effectively captured the complex interactions and combined effects of the variables. These findings demonstrate the potential of integrating multimodal data to enhance the accuracy of myopia onset prediction, paving the way for personalized myopia management strategies.Keywords: Myopia onset prediction, Multimodal imaging, Optical coherence tomography (OCT), Color fundus photography (CFP), Machine learningSubject terms: Translational research, Eye diseases

Introduction

Myopia, also referred to as nearsightedness, has become a global public health crisis of epidemic proportions1–3. Current statistics reveal that myopia affects a substantial portion of the global population, with some regions experiencing a higher burden than others. Alarmingly, it is predicted that by 2050, approximately 50% of the global population will be affected by myopia, highlighting the urgent need for effective prevention and management strategies4,5. The financial implications of myopia are staggering, with an estimated $1.7 trillion in global health costs attributed to this condition6,7.

Preventing the onset and progression of myopia requires early detection and treatment of premyopia, a condition that precedes myopia8. Premyopia is a relatively new concept that describes a refractive state between emmetropia (normal vision) and myopia. Premyopia is defined as a refractive error that falls between + 0.75 diopters (D) and − 0.50 D in children where a combination of baseline refraction, age, and other quantifiable risk factors provides a sufficient likelihood of the future development of myopia9. Several studies have emphasized the significance of myopia prevention, especially in children and adolescents10,11. Early detection and treatment in children and adolescents’ premyopia could potentially prevent or delay the onset of myopia12. In addition, detecting premyopia will enable clinicians and researchers to identify children at elevated risk of progression to myopia, thereby allowing timely implementation of preventive strategies. Interventions at this stage could slow or prevent axial elongation and refractive shifts that lead to clinically significant myopia, ultimately reducing the burden of associated ocular complications and healthcare costs.

Recent studies have utilized machine learning techniques to predict myopia progression using longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) and structured clinical data13–15. Lin et al.14 achieved high accuracy in predicting high myopia development within 3–8 years by analyzing a large EHR dataset. Yang et al.15 established an adolescent myopia prediction model based on factors such as spectacle wear, activity time, and ocular biometrics. Varadarajan et al.16 developed a deep learning algorithm that utilized color fundus photography (CFP) to predict the risk of myopia development in children. While these studies have made significant progress, few studies explored the integration of imaging data to further enhance myopia progression prediction models.

Multimodal learning, which has been proven effective in various medical imaging applications17,18, has yet to be extensively explored in the context of myopia progression prediction. Foo et al.19 recently conducted a study that utilized CFP in children to predict the 5-year risk of high myopia. The study achieved promising results, with an area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC) of 0.97, demonstrating the potential of integrating multimodal data for enhanced myopia progression prediction.

The increasing adoption of optical coherence tomography (OCT) in routine ophthalmic examinations provides a valuable opportunity to enhance predictive models. However, related studies have primarily focused on using CFP for myopia progression prediction. The potential of incorporating additional imaging modalities, such as OCT, has not been extensively explored in this context. In particular, the inclusion of OCT has shown promise in enhancing the predictive power of myopia prediction models by providing detailed structural information about the eye20. The introduction of multimodal imaging in myopia progression prediction could reduce the reliance on time-consuming and burdensome methods such as cycloplegic refraction and repeated reviews.

This study aims to predict myopia onset one year in advance by integrating multimodal images (OCT and CFP) with biometric data. While previous studies primarily focused on using biometric data for myopia prediction, and a few recent studies have explored the use of CFP, the potential of combining OCT images with other modalities has not been extensively investigated. This study makes three significant contributions to the field. First, it is the first study to integrate multi-modal data for predicting myopia one year in advance. Second, by comparing the predictive performance of single-modality and multi-modality data, we demonstrate the benefits of combining heterogeneous data sources for myopia prediction. Third, our model achieves high accuracy while maintaining credibility through interpretability analysis. The findings can inform the development of early screening and intervention strategies for myopia prevention.

Methods

Data and materials

The dataset used in this study was collected from students in two primary schools and one middle school in Lining and Yichang, Hubei Province, China. The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of the Aier Eye School of Ophthalmology, Central South University (AIER2018IRB15). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent forms were gained from participants and their parents or other guardians after they had grasped the study protocol and possible consequences of the study. The dataset contains demographic information, biometric measurements (including axial length, corneal curvature, anterior chamber depth, intraocular pressure (IOP), visual acuity (VA), and spherical equivalent (SE) refraction, OCT images and CFP images.

All ocular examinations and OCT imaging were performed by trained professionals using standardized protocols with DRI OCT Triton (Atlantis), Topcon. Each OCT examination contains 128 slices, providing detailed cross-sectional images of the retina. The dataset was carefully curated and preprocessed to remove incomplete or inconsistent records, ensuring data quality and integrity.

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of our analysis, we applied strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. The dataset included only those subjects who had attended at least two visits with intervals of one year or more between visits, and had an initial SE greater than − 0.5 D. Both CFP and OCT images were required to be available for each participant. Subjects undergoing myopia treatment, such as atropine therapy or orthokeratology, were excluded from the study. Additionally, participants with low-quality OCT or CFP images due to issues such as low signal strength, motion artifacts, or inadequate focus were excluded from the dataset. In cases where both eyes of a participant met the inclusion criteria, only the data from the right eye were included in the analysis to maintain independence between observations. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 347 subjects between the ages of 6 and 14 years were included in the final dataset. A summary of the dataset is presented in Table 1.

Development of mixed models

The study design is presented in Fig. 1. First, we enrolled 1,280 adolescents who had OCT taken between 2018 and 2019. There are 347 subjects at least have a follow-up in the second year and have CFP and OCT results. The dataset was then divided into five folds for following evaluation of five-fold cross validation. Of the participants, 56 subjects (79 eyes) had spherical equivalents above − 0.50D in the first year—broadly aligning with a premyopic stage—but progressed to myopia (SE < − 0.50D) in the second year, forming the incident myopia group. The remaining 291 subjects (393 eyes) did not become myopic and thus comprised the control group. The dataset was then divided into five folds for subsequent evaluation using five-fold cross-validation.

Flow chart of the study design.

Following the data preparation, feature extraction was performed using deep learning models. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) were trained on two types of image data: CFP and OCT images. To acquire segmented OCT images, a semi-supervised approach was employed. An ophthalmologist labeled the choroid layer in 2,560 OCT images, which were then used to train a choroid segmentation model based on U-Net architecture21. The labeling process involved annotating the upper and lower boundaries of the choroid. The segmentation model was trained and validated using 5-fold cross-validation (Additional file 1). Subsequently, the trained model was applied to the remaining OCT images in the dataset to generate segmented images with labeled choroid layers. The segmented choroid of OCT slices was then used to generate ETDRS grid thickness values. Image processing techniques were applied to the segmented choroid images to calculate the thickness of each region in the ETDRS grid. These generated ETDRS grid values were then incorporated as features in the predictive model to assess their contribution to the prediction of myopia onset in the second year.

For image-based classification of second-year myopia onset, a ResNet-50 model was adopted as the base model to distinguish between the premyopia and control groups. Scores were generated using the trained models in a five-fold cross-validation scheme. In each fold, the training set consisted of four folds, and the remaining fold served as the test set, ensuring that scores were generated only on the held-out fold. Image enhancement techniques, including filtering, exponential enhancement, and linear enhancement, were performed to improve the quality and enhance the visibility of important features in the images. Additionally, image augmentation techniques such as resizing all images to a standard size of 512 × 512 pixels, rotating all photos randomly between − 10 and 10 degrees, and horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5 were used to widen the diversity of the training data and improving generalization of the model to reduce overfitting.

The proposed model was trained using demographic biometric data, the ETDRS grid obtained from segmented choroid, and the image scores obtained from the CNN models. A gradient boosting decision tree (XGBoost) was employed to integrate these features. The biometric data, which includes weight, height, BMI, VA, axis, and IOP, is incorporated into the predictive model. The ETDRS grid thickness encompasses the thickness of different regions, including nasal inner (NI), nasal outer (NO), temporal inner (TI), temporal outer (TO), inferior inner (II), inferior outer (IO), superior inner (SI), superior outer (SO), central fovea (CF), and the average thickness of overall regions.

Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator, a Bayesian optimization method that models the probability of hyperparameters leading to improved performance22. Through TPE optimization, hyperparameters including the learning rate (0.01–0.1), maximum tree depth (1–50), number of estimators (100–1500), subsample (0.1–0.9), and subsample ratio of columns (0.1–0.9) were systematically adjusted.

A 5-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to compute the metrics such as the AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1 score. These metrics offer a thorough evaluation of the model’s predictive performance and aid in determining the best feature combination for myopia onset prediction. We conducted Student’s t-tests to assess differences in ETDRS thickness between premyopia and control groups.

After training and optimizing the mixed models, we utilized permutation importance to visualize the contribution of each variable in predicting the 1-year myopia risk. Permutation importance measures the decrease in model performance when a single variable is randomly shuffled, thereby breaking its association with the target variable. Variables with higher permutation importance scores are considered to have a greater influence on the model’s predictions. By ranking the variables based on their permutation importance, we can gain insights into the relative significance of different biometric and image-derived features in predicting myopia onset.

All experiments were conducted on a server with Intel Core i5-10600KF, using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 24GB GPU for training and validation, with 32GB RAM.

Results

These ETDRS grid thickness values were generated in semi-automatic manner using our pre-trained U-Net choroid segmentation model. The findings of the student’s t-test reveal that the premyopia group’s choroid thickness is substantially thinner than the control group in five of the nine sectors in the ETDRS grid: II, SI, SO, TI, and TO. These results indicate that the premyopia group has thinner choroid thickness than the control group, despite not having developed myopia (i.e., SE > -0.5D) in the first year. The observation emphasizes the potential role of choroid thickness as a precursor to myopia.

For the image models, we evaluated the performance of CNN models trained on OCT images, CFP images, and a machine learning model using ETDRS macular thickness values generated from segmented OCT images. The CNN model trained on OCT images achieved an AUROC of 0.612 ± 0.022. The CNN model trained on CFP images demonstrated better performance, with an AUROC of 0.703 ± 0.026. The machine learning model utilizing ETDRS macular thickness values generated from segmented OCT images achieved an AUROC of 0.598 ± 0.031. The CNN models trained on OCT and CFP images outperformed the model using ETDRS variables alone, indicating that the deep learning approach can extract more informative features from the images. Furthermore, the higher performance of the CFP model compared to the OCT model suggests that CFP images contain more predictive information for myopia onset prediction than OCT images.

For the mixed models, we focused on various combinations of features, including base variables, AL, SE, OCT scores, CFP scores, and ETDRS macular thickness. The mixed model incorporating all variables (base variables, AL, SE, OCT scores, CFP scores, and ETDRS macular thickness) achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 83.88 ± 5.29%, sensitivity of 84.59 ± 10.75%, specificity of 84.40 ± 6.64%, and an AUROC of 0.845 ± 0.05. The model utilizing base variables, AL, SE, ETDRS, and CFP scores, also demonstrated strong results, with an accuracy of 82.16 ± 5.88%, sensitivity of 84.59 ± 10.75%, specificity of 84.40 ± 6.64%, and an AUROC of 0.835 ± 0.05.

Discussion

Permutation importance analysis

In our study, the permutation importance analysis in Fig. 5 revealed that SE was the most influential variable in predicting myopia onset, followed by CFP scores and OCT scores generated by the CNN models. This finding underscores the critical role of refractive error in the development of myopia and highlights the importance of regular eye examinations and early detection of refractive changes in children and adolescents. The prominence of SE in the model’s predictions suggests that monitoring and managing refractive error should be a primary focus in myopia prevention strategies as suggested in other studies23,24.

Permutation importance of the mixed model.

The high ranking of CFP and OCT scores indicates that incorporating advanced imaging techniques into myopia screening protocols could provide valuable additional information for identifying individuals at high risk of myopia onset. CFP can reveal early signs of myopia-related changes in the retina, such as tilted optic discs and peripapillary atrophy, which may precede significant refractive shifts25–27. Similarly, OCT imaging can detect subtle changes in choroidal thickness and retinal layers that have been associated with myopia development28–30. These variables in the permutation importance analysis suggests that models incorporating this information can lead to more accurate predictions of myopia onset.

Compared to SE, CFP scores, and OCT scores, the ETDRS variables—which represent choroidal thickness measurements in various regions of the retina—had a less significant impact on the prediction of myopia onset. Although choroidal thickness change has been linked to the development of myopia, it may not be as strong a predictor of myopia onset as other factors in this specific population and timeframe. Nevertheless, the relative importance of these variables may vary based on the stage of myopia progression and the individual characteristics31,32. Further research is necessary to fully understand the complex interactions between choroidal thickness, other ocular biometrics, and environmental factors in the progression of myopia.

Comparison and interpretation of mixed model

As shown in Table 2, biometric data alone, including base variables and ocular parameters such as AL and SE, achieves an AUROC of 0.783. The best-performing model, which incorporates all available data (base variables, AL, SE, OCT, segmented OCT, and CFP images), achieves an AUROC of 0.845. The introduction of multimodal images, including OCT and CFP, significantly improves the classification performance of the models for predicting myopia onset.

This result underscores the importance of leveraging multiple data sources to capture the complex nature of myopia development and improve predictive accuracy. By comparing single-modality and multimodal models, we demonstrated that integrating multiple data sources (e.g., biometric, CFP, OCT) significantly improved predictive performance.

Figure 6 illustrates the distribution of SE, AL, OCT scores, and CFP scores in the premyopia and control groups. The distribution of SE is clearly separated between the two groups, indicating their stronger influence on differentiating between premyopia and control cases. In contrast, AL, CFP scores, and OCT scores, have less impact on distinguishing between the groups. Although the impact of ETDRS variables appears less significant in the single variable analysis, the permutation importance analysis suggested that the mixed model effectively captured the complex interactions and combined effects of these variables with other predictors, thereby leveraging the ETDRS information to enhance the overall predictive performance.

Inspection of important variable in mixed models.

The superior performance of the mixed models can be attributed to their ability to extract and integrate complementary information from various data sources. While biometric data, particularly SE and AL, provides essential insights into the refractive status and ocular dimensions, imaging data offers additional information about the structural and morphological changes in the retina and choroid that may precede or accompany myopia onset. By combining these diverse data types, the mixed models can better capture the intricate relationships between ocular parameters and myopia development, leading to more accurate predictions and enhanced clinical decision-making.

Integrating CFP and OCT for enhanced myopia onset prediction

Fundus images can reveal early signs of myopia-related changes in the retina, such as tilted optic discs, peripapillary atrophy, and retinal pigment epithelium changes. Studies have demonstrated that these morphological alterations precede significant refractive shifts and serve as early indicators of myopia development19,26. The inclusion of CFP scores in our model suggests that Fundus images contain valuable predictive information that can predict myopia onset.

Although fundus images can predict myopia development, they are only based on information about the retina. It’s difficult to provide detailed insights into the structural changes in the choroid, which have been associated with myopia development. Changes in choroidal thickness, for example, have been linked to alterations in the scleral extracellular matrix, which can influence the axial elongation of the eye and lead to myopia33,34. Previous studies have shown that choroidal thinning, particularly in the macular region, are early signs of myopia onset and progression28. By detecting choroidal changes, OCT imaging can offer predictive information about an individual’s risk of developing myopia. Therefore, OCT and CFP contain more information than individual CFP, which may further enhance the ability to predict the occurrence of myopia.

To our knowledge, this is the first time that CFP and OCT have been combined to predict the occurrence of myopia. The incorporation of OCT and fundus images in our mixed model significantly improved the prediction of myopia onset, highlighting their potential as valuable tools for early detection and intervention. The mechanisms underlying the predictive value of OCT and fundus images may be related to the complex interplay between ocular structures and refractive development. By leveraging the complementary information provided by fundus imaging modalities, our mixed model can better capture the early signs of myopia onset and progression, enabling more effective screening and intervention strategies.

Limitations

While our study demonstrates the potential of multimodal data integration for predicting myopia onset, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. One limitation is the small sample size and the findings from our study may not be generalizable to other ethnicities. A larger, more diverse dataset would enhance the generalizability of our findings and provide more robust evidence for the efficacy of the proposed approach.

While our study incorporated various imaging modalities, including OCT and CFP, other potential predictive factors were not considered. Previous research has established a strong association between parental myopia and lifestyle factors, such as time spent outdoors and near work, with the development and progression of myopia35–38. Future studies should aim to integrate these additional factors to develop even more comprehensive and accurate predictive models.

Another limitation is the short follow-up period, with only one year follow up in this study. As myopia development is a progressive condition, longer-term research is required to examine how well the models perform over time and how well they can forecast the growth of myopia.

Our study sample may have inherent selection bias, as participants were required to have both OCT and CFP images of sufficient quality, potentially excluding individuals with ocular conditions or image artifacts. In addition, excluding participants undergoing myopia control treatments (e.g., atropine or orthokeratology) may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Future studies should aim to include more diverse cohorts, potentially spanning multiple geographic regions and ethnic backgrounds, to improve external validity.

Despite these limitations, our work lays the groundwork for future investigations into the application of multimodal data integration for the evaluation of myopia risk and emphasizes the potential for the improvement of individualized myopia prevention and treatment. In future work, incorporating lifestyle factors (e.g., time spent outdoors, near work activities) and genetic predispositions may enhance predictive accuracy and personalize prevention strategies. Extending the follow-up period beyond one year would also provide insights into the model’s ability to predict long-term myopia progression and stability of the risk factors identified.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the value of integrating multimodal data, including OCT and CFP images, with biometric information to predict myopia onset in adolescents. The proposed approach achieved high accuracy and provided insights into the relative importance of different predictive factors. Future research should focus on incorporating additional predictive factors and evaluating the long-term performance of the models to further advance the early detection and prevention of myopia. These findings underscore the potential of multimodal data integration to improve myopia risk assessment and guide personalized intervention strategies.
