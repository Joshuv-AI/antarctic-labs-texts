Prognostic value of ventilatory efficiency in hypersensitivity pneumonitis
Authors: Worawat Chumpangern, Paolo Spagnolo, Pailin Ratanawatkul, Michael P Mohning, Evans R Fernández Pérez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVentilatory efficiency (VE) from cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is a valuable prognostic metric for evaluating cardiopulmonary diseases. However, its relationship with clinical outcomes in hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) remains unclear. We aimed to determine whether VE correlates with disease severity and predicts mortality in HP.Research questionIn patients with HP, does VE correlate with disease severity and predict mortality?MethodsThis retrospective cohort study (2009–2019) included patients with non-fibrotic (nfHP) and fibrotic HP (fHP) and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), which served as the comparison group. CPET variables, including VE/carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2), were assessed across various forced vital capacity (FVC%) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO%) ranges. Multivariate logistic regression identified predictors of 5-year mortality and survival was assessed with Kaplan-Meier plots.Results164 patients were analysed (mean age of 65.6±12.0 years, 54.3% male). 25 had nfHP, 66 fHP and 73 IPF. Overall, an increase in the VE/VCO2 slope and nadir was observed as the DLCO% decreased, but not with a decrease in FVC%, while the intercept remained unchanged. Patients with pulmonary hypertension (35%, 57/164) and fHP or IPF had significantly higher VE/VCO2 slope and nadir when compared with patients without pulmonary hypertension or nfHP, respectively. A VE/VCO2 slope ≥42 predicted survival (HR 3.65; 95% CI 1.04 to 12.80). Restricting the analysis to patients with HP showed similar results (HR 5.49; 95% CI 1.19 to 33.54).InterpretationIn patients with HP, elevated VE/VCO2 is associated with the presence of pulmonary fibrosis, PH, reduced DLCO and a higher mortality risk. A VE/VCO2 slope threshold of 42 may serve as a practical prognostic marker for stratification.Keywords: Exercise, Interstitial Fibrosis

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study shows that elevated ventilatory efficiency/carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2) slope and nadir are associated with pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension and declining diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, but not with forced vital capacity impairment. A VE/VCO2 slope of ≥42 predicts 5-year mortality in both HP and IPF, with HP showing a fivefold increased risk. The VE/VCO2 intercept remained normal across groups, indicating inefficiency occurs only during exercise.

Introduction

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) represent two of the most prevalent forms of interstitial lung disease (ILD) encountered in pulmonary clinics globally. A subset of patients diagnosed with HP may progress to develop pulmonary fibrosis.1 2 Similar to patients with IPF, individuals with fibrotic and non-fibrotic HP commonly experience dyspnoea.3 While dyspnoea is a primary symptom for both IPF and HP, accompanying comorbidities, such as pulmonary hypertension, can exacerbate this symptom, further limiting physical activity and significantly diminishing quality of life and independence over time.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) provides valuable insights into functional capacity, cardiovascular function, ventilatory efficiency (VE) and pulmonary gas exchange in patients with ILD. Among the key metrics evaluated, VE—measured as the ratio of minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production (VE/VCO2)—is frequently compromised in individuals with ILD. This impairment indicates that patients must engage in higher levels of ventilation to expel the same quantity of VCO2. Consequently, this inefficiency contributes to the experience of breathlessness and limits exercise capacity.

To date, only two studies have examined the CPET characteristics in HP. A small case (n=28) control (n=13) study4 and a cohort of 51 HP patients, both describing multifactorial limitations to exercise.5 Nonetheless, the role of ventilatory inefficiency in the risk assessment and prognostic evaluation of ILD has been limited to IPF.6 However, HP has a fundamentally different pathobiology from IPF and prognostic thresholds derived from IPF or other fibrotic ILDs cannot be assumed to be directly applicable. Furthermore, there exists a significant gap in the literature regarding the relationships among VE, lung function impairment and pulmonary hypertension in individuals presenting for initial evaluation diagnosed with HP with and without pulmonary fibrosis. Moreover, unlike IPF, HP spans a non-fibrotic to fibrotic spectrum, enabling examination of ventilatory inefficiency in relation to the presence or absence of fibrosis within a single disease entity. We hypothesised that ventilatory inefficiency would be associated with pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension and gas exchange impairment, and would independently predict mortality in HP. Hence, the primary aim of the study was to investigate patients with and without fibrotic HP, and IPF as a comparison group: (1) the relationship between ventilatory inefficiency, pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary hypertension, stratified by forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and (2) the relationship of ventilatory inefficiency and survival.

Methods

Study design and population

While the main goal was to evaluate CPET VE in HP, cases of IPF were analysed as disease comparison group. The National Jewish Health ILD research database was queried for consecutive adult patients referred for evaluation with HP and IPF who had a maximum cardiopulmonary test between 2009 and 2019 as part of their initial referral evaluation. The diagnosis of HP and IPF was determined through a structured multidisciplinary discussion that used all available data at the time of the initial assessment in the ILD clinic, which served as the baseline data for all analyses.

The presence of pulmonary fibrosis was defined by the presence of reticular abnormality and/or traction bronchiectasis and/or architectural distortion, and/or honeycombing on high-resolution CT at time of diagnosis.

For baseline and inclusion in the study, pulmonary hypertension assessments, pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and CPET performed within 30 days of diagnosis were retrieved from the research database:

All patients underwent transthoracic echocardiography; pulmonary hypertension was defined by right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) ≥35 mm Hg or peak tricuspid regurgitant velocity (TRV) ≥2.8 m/s. 14 patients also underwent right-heart cardiac catheterisation, with pulmonary hypertension confirmed by mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) ≥25 mm Hg and mean pulmonary wedge pressure (mPCWP) ≤15 mm Hg) or a transthoracic echocardiogram (RVSP ≥35 mm Hg or peak TRV ≥2.8 m/s).7 Patients with post-capillary pulmonary hypertension identified on right-heart catheterisation (mPCWP >15 mm Hg) were excluded; however, no patients in the cohort met this exclusion criterion.

PFTs (n=164), including spirometry, maximum voluntary ventilation and diffusion capacity, were conducted following the guidelines set by the American Thoracic Society and the European Respiratory Society.8 9 The reference equation is outlined in earlier publications.10 All indices from the PFTs (FVC% and DLCO% predicted) were categorised into four stages based on z-scores: preserved: >−2.6, mild: −1.65 to −2.5, moderate: −2.51 to −4.0 and severe: <−4.10.11

Every patient (n=164) underwent room-air maximal cardiopulmonary exercise testing using a bicycle ergometer at our facility, following the same standardised protocol. A radial arterial line was placed before the test. The ramp protocol was endorsed, consisting of 3 min of pedalling at zero workload and an incremental workload range of 5–40 watts, while maintaining a pedal speed of at least 60 rpm, until the test termination criteria were met. Then, the recovery phase was initiated. Physiological data were collected. Oxygen uptake (VO2, STPD), VCO2 production (STPD), ventilation (VE, BTPS), tidal volume, respiratory frequency (fR) and end-tidal CO2 partial pressure (PETCO2) were measured breath-by-breath (Ultima Cardio2 System, Minnesota, USA). Arterial oxygen saturation was monitored using a pulse oximeter (SpO2). The anaerobic threshold (AT) was identified by the V-slope method along with supporting ventilatory and pulmonary gas exchange criteria. The physiological VD/VT was calculated by using PETCO2 and PaCO2 data from arterial blood gas.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics of baseline and longitudinal data were generated. The normality of frequency distributions was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Values are presented as means±SD, unless stated otherwise. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. To evaluate differences between proportions, a χ2 test for homogeneity was employed. Differences between means were analysed using either the unpaired t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the data. The analysis of variance with Tukey’s method was used for comparisons across varying degrees of FVC and DLCO% predicted ranges.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, including calculation of the area under the curve, was conducted to assess the discriminative significance of the variables, identifying optimal cut-off points by using Youden’s method. Specifically, the optimal VE/VCO2 slope and nadir thresholds for predicting 5-year mortality were determined by maximising the sum of sensitivity and specificity from the ROC curve. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival times between groups. Vital status was ascertained on December 31, 2024.

HRs and their 95% CIs were calculated using Cox proportional hazards analysis. Candidate predictors for the multivariable model were selected from clinically relevant baseline variables including demographics (age, sex), pulmonary hypertension status, pulmonary function parameters and CPET variables (table 1). Prior to model building, Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was performed to address multicollinearity; no significant collinearity was identified among candidate predictors (all correlation coefficients <0.7). Predictors were included in the multivariable model if their univariate relationships with 5-year mortality met a significance threshold of p<0.10. CPET variables were evaluated as both continuous and dichotomised forms; the dichotomised forms were entered into the multivariable model given their stronger univariable associations and clinical interpretability. After establishing a full model that included all candidate predictors, we applied bidirectional stepwise elimination to derive a reduced model. The final multivariate Cox proportional hazard model comprised independent predictors of 5-year mortality, with the criterion for retaining covariates set at p<0.05. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

VE is associated with the presence of pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary hypertension

Compared with non-fibrotic HP (n=25), fibrotic HP and IPF patients (n=139) had an increased VE/VCO2 slope and nadir with a relatively constant intercept (figure 3d–f and online supplemental table 1). Restricting the analysis to patients with HP showed similar results (table 2). Likewise, patients with pulmonary hypertension (n=57) also showed elevated VE/VCO2 slope and nadir, in contrast to those without pulmonary hypertension (n=107). VE predicts survival

Table 1 shows the key predictors of 5-year mortality, as determined through both univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. The 1-minute heart rate recovery, VE/VCO2 slope and VE/VCO2 nadir were significant predictors. VE/VCO2 nadir was excluded from the multivariate analysis due to collinearity with VE/VCO2 slope.

The Kaplan-Meier curve depicting the relationship between the VE/VCO2 slope, VE/VCO2 nadir and 5-year survival is presented in online supplemental figure 1. 17 (10.3%) died within 5 years (5 with fibrotic HP and 12 with IPF). Median survival was 21 months. The optimal cut-off point for the VE/VCO2 slope, determined using Youden’s index, was 42, which best stratified the cohort into high-risk and low-risk groups. For the nadir VE/VCO2, the cut-off point was established at 36.5. Restricting the analysis to patients with HP revealed that a VE/VCO2 slope greater than 42 distinguished low-risk and high-risk groups (HR 5.49; 95% CI 1.19 to 33.54, online supplemental figure 2).

Discussion

In a cohort of patients with non-fibrotic HP, fibrotic HP and IPF, we found that exercise ventilatory inefficiency was: (1) significantly associated with the presence of pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary hypertension, (2) worsened significantly with declining DLCO and (3) identified patients at greater risk of poor outcome. Specifically, we found that a baseline threshold VE/VCO2 slope of ≥42 predicts mortality in these patients.

Although not the main focus of our study, our multivariable regression model indicated that a 1-minute heart rate recovery of ≤11 beats is a significant predictor of mortality. Other studies have also shown that this metric correlates with clinical outcomes in cardiopulmonary diseases.13 14 This abnormal response may stem from an impaired autonomic reaction post-exercise, chronotropic incompetence due to right heart dysfunction and hypoxaemia leading to sympathovagal imbalance. Indeed, decreased heart rate recovery was notably more pronounced in patients with pulmonary hypertension (online supplemental table 4), as well as in those with fibrotic HP and IPF, compared with those with non-fibrotic HP (online supplemental table 1).

The findings of this study align with earlier research indicating that patients with ILD exhibit an elevated VE/VCO2 slope and nadir. This phenomenon can be attributed to a combination of increased physiological dead space, compensatory tachypnoea and heightened ventilatory drive due to hypoxaemia.15 16 Indeed, in contrast to non-fibrotic HP, patients with fibrotic HP and IPF, who have greater baseline disease severity, displayed a higher ventilation-perfusion ratio and a lower arterial VCO2 tension during maximal exercise (online supplemental table 1). We also observed that the VE/VCO2 intercept was normal and constant in our patient cohort, independent of the presence of pulmonary fibrosis or pulmonary hypertension, suggesting there is no excessive ventilation at rest, as the factors described above, particularly increased dead space ventilation, contributing to ventilatory inefficiency in ILD, become more pronounced during exercise. In addition, a significant number of patients in our study (57/164, 35%) had pulmonary hypertension, which can further exacerbate ventilatory inefficiency due to an increased V/Q mismatch and blunted cardiac output during exercise.

A key finding of this study is that VE/VCO2 demonstrated a stronger correlation with a progressive decline in DLCO compared with a progressive decline in FVC, likely because both VE/VCO2 and DLCO are sensitive measures of gas exchange across the alveolar-capillary membrane, whereas FVC primarily measures lung volume.

Previous studies, particularly those involving patients with IPF—who were included as a comparison group in our study—have demonstrated that exercise ventilatory inefficiency serves as a pathophysiological marker in various cardiopulmonary diseases. This study represents the largest analysis validating this finding among patients with HP, presenting for initial ILD evaluation, while controlling for the presence of lung fibrosis and pulmonary hypertension, which are known to be important predictors of poor outcome.

Baseline lung function has been demonstrated to predict survival in cohorts with HP. Specifically, a lower FVC% and DLCO% are associated with increased mortality.17–19 While baseline metrics, such as pulmonary function tests, provide a static measure of lung capacity, evaluating exercise VE offers a dynamic and sensitive assessment of disease severity and progression, which can enhance the tools available for risk stratification in patients with HP. While CPET requires specialised equipment, trained personnel and greater resource utilisation compared with simpler functional assessments, it provides unique dynamic physiological information not captured by pulmonary function tests. CPET is most practical at referral centres where the majority of ILD patients are evaluated. For settings where CPET is unavailable, the 6-minute walk test may serve as an initial screening tool, with CPET reserved for cases requiring detailed physiological characterisation.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design and that we do not specifically account for post-CPET treatment effects on survival. Second, our cohort lacked information on the specific causes of death. Third, the non-fibrotic HP subgroup (n=25) is relatively small, reflecting the clinical reality that non-fibrotic HP represents a minority of referrals to tertiary ILD centres; however, statistically significant differences in VE were observed despite this smaller subgroup size. Fourth, although pulmonary hypertension contributes to elevated VE/VCO₂, the VE/VCO₂ slope threshold of ≥42 predicted mortality independent of pulmonary hypertension status. Nonetheless, parenchymal fibrosis and pulmonary vascular disease are pathophysiologically interrelated and difficult to fully separate. Future studies incorporating serial haemodynamic assessments may help delineate their individual contributions to ventilatory inefficiency and outcomes. Fifth, although all patients had reportable echocardiographic values including measurable tricuspid regurgitant jets, echocardiographic estimation of pulmonary pressures may overestimate or underestimate true haemodynamic values compared with right heart catheterisation, which was performed in only 14 patients. Also, although echocardiographic estimation of pulmonary pressures may overestimate or underestimate true haemodynamic values compared with right heart catheterisation, which was performed in only 14 patients, our echocardiographic criteria (RVSP ≥35 mm Hg or peak TRV ≥2.8 m/s) remain aligned with current guidelines, including the revised 2022 European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society6 and 2024 World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension haemodynamic definition20 lowering the mPAP threshold from ≥25 to >20 mm Hg, as echocardiographic screening thresholds were unchanged. Moreover, all 14 patients who underwent right-heart catheterisation had mPAP ≥25 mm Hg, exceeding both thresholds, and VE/VCO₂ predicted outcomes independently of pulmonary hypertension status, indicating that neither potential echocardiographic misclassification nor the updated definition would affect patient classification or study conclusions. Finally, while the prognostic value of VE at levels ranging from >34 to >46 has been reported to predict survival in IPF, to the best of our knowledge, the prognostic significance of this metric has not been previously investigated in HP. Although this is the first study to provide a practical threshold for VE/VCO2 that includes patients with HP, the results should be replicated in an independent cohort.

Conclusions

We found that in a cohort of patients with non-fibrotic HP, fibrotic HP and IPF undergoing room-air maximal CPET using a bicycle ergometer, ventilatory inefficiency, indicated by an elevated VE/VCO2 slope and nadir, was associated with the presence of pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, reduced DLCO and an increased risk of mortality. These findings suggest that a VE/VCO2 slope threshold of 42 or higher may serve as a useful prognostic marker to help stratify patients into low-risk and high-risk groups.
