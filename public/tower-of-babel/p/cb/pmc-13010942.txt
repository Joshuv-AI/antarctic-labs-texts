Integrated GC–MS, LC–MS/MS metabolomics, and transcriptomics analysis of meat quality differences between marketed Wuzhishan and Duroc-landrace-Yorkshire pigs
Authors: YN Wang, M Li, MH Li, ZQ Jiang, LM Wei, F Wang, Z Chao, LL Chen, HL Liu, QW Liu, RP Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting China’ s pork market transformation and high-end sector bottlenecks, this study employed 6 WZS pigs (experimental group) and 6 DLY pigs (control group), aged 7–8 months. After slaughter, longissimus thoracis samples were collected for meat quality and molecular mechanism analysis using multiple technical methods. The results showed that compared with DLY pigs, (1) they exhibited distinct meat quality advantages, including higher muscle fiber density, a larger proportion of Type II A muscle fibers, and higher L* and a* values (P < 0.01), as well as lower drip loss and shear force (P < 0.05); (2) their pork contained more abundant sulfur-containing volatile substances, with superior umami, saltiness, and lower astringency. Omics analyses identified 12 DFMs, 361 DMs, and 2279 DEGs, these substances induce differences in meat quality by regulating relevant pathways. Integrated multi-omics found 46 core regulators. WZS pigs excel in flavor and tenderness, and China’ s pork industry development.Keywords: Meat quality traits, Flavor substances, Metabolomics, Transcriptomics, Wuzhishan pigs

Introduction

With the continuous development of China's economy, consumers' demand for high-end pork has shifted from “having meat to eat” to “enjoying high-quality meat”, which is driving the pork market into an era of comprehensive quality-oriented consumption. High-end pork must possess excellent sensory characteristics, nutritional value, and flavor quality, while complying with national health and safety standards. Internationally renowned breeds such as the Spanish Iberian black pig are typical examples (Fernández-Barroso et al., 2020). Currently, China's pork market is undergoing a structural transformation from “quantity-oriented” to “quality-prioritized”. Against this backdrop, the development of local pig breeds and industrial upgrading have become key directions to break through market bottlenecks (Chen et al., 2023).

Taking the WZS pig (also known as “Five-foot Pig”) as an example, as a rare indigenous pig breed unique to China, it not only carries the germplasm genes of traditional pig farming in southern tropical regions but also forms distinct meat quality advantages different from commercial crossbred pigs, thanks to its unique biological characteristics and breeding model (Min et al., 2014). In terms of breed traits, WZS pigs have a compact and robust body shape; coupled with the environmentally friendly “semi - confined + semi - free - range in mountain forests” breeding model, their muscle tissue accumulates flavor substances through moderate exercise, avoiding the rough meat texture caused by intensive farming of commercial pig breeds (Chen et al., 2023). These advantages result in distinct quality features: WZS pork has a bright red color, evenly distributed marbling, tender and juicy texture, and rich flavor. A study using GC–MS detection found that the number of volatile aroma components in its muscle is 12–15 more than that in DLY pigs (a commercial crossbred of Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire). These substances collectively form the unique “fresh, fragrant, and tender” flavor system of WZS pork (Wu et al., 2022). Moreover, despite its relatively high market price, the repurchase rate of WZS pork remains above 60%. This not only reflects consumers ‘recognition of “high-quality local pork”, but also confirms the irreplaceability of WZS pigs, which represent China's local pig breeds, in the high-end pork market (Min et al., 2014).

In pork quality evaluation, traditional methods focus on apparent traits (e.g., meat color, marbling) and nutritional components (Zhou et al., 2024). However, the application of emerging technologies such as electronic tongues, electronic noses, and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) has provided objective support for the quantitative analysis of flavor compounds (Lippolis et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2023). In the field of molecular biology, metabolomics and transcriptomics are core tools for deciphering the mechanisms underlying meat quality formation (Jarles & Ward, 2021; Johnson et al., 2024). For instance, a study targeting pork drip loss conducted transcriptome sequencing on the longissimus thoracis (LT) of Duroc × (Landrace × Yorkshire) pigs with extreme drip loss levels at 24 and 48 h post-slaughter. Eventually, 9 key genes were identified, offering new insights into the mechanism of drip loss and molecular breeding for pork quality improvement (Johnson et al., 2024). Another study focused on intramuscular fat content and meat color redness: it analyzed individuals with extreme phenotypes among 147 crossbred pigs, and finally hypothesized that the SOX9/CEBPB/PPARGC1A axis regulates lipid metabolism and myoglobin redox reactions, shedding light on the coordinated regulatory mechanism of meat quality (Kumar et al., 2024). Additionally, researchers used lipidomics and flavoromics to establish a meat quality characteristic fingerprint for Laiwu pigs and Beijing Black Six pigs, successfully screening 55 lipid molecules and 13 volatile flavor compounds as potential characteristic markers for black pork (Hou et al., 2023). The integrated application of multi-omics has become a core approach to deciphering meat quality mechanisms and driving industrial upgrading (Zhan et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024).

Against this background of growing demand for high-quality pork and the urgent need to explore the unique quality traits of local Chinese pig breeds, this study employed purebred WZS pigs as the research subject and market-dominant DLY pigs as the control group. By systematically comparing carcass traits, pHysicochemical meat quality indicators, and flavor characteristics between the two breeds, and integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, this study comprehensively deciphered the molecular basis of meat quality traits in WZS pigs. This research not only provides comprehensive foundational data for the quality evaluation of local pig breeds and a scientific reference for consumers in selecting high-quality pork.

Method

Animals and sample information

This animal experiment was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Medicine, Hainan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (No. ISA-HNXMSY20240606). This experiment selected 12 healthy and disease-free pigs (7–8 months old, male and female half) among which 6 were WZS pigs (purchased from Hainan Nongxiangmu Ecological Agricultural Development Co., Ltd.) and 6 were DLY pigs (purchased from Hainan Nongken New Hope Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd.). On the day before slaughter, they were transported to designated slaughterhouse (Dingan, Hainan Province) to rest, drink and fast. The facilities and process of the slaughterhouse met the requirements of the Institute of Animal Care and Use Committee. Pigs were stunned by electrocution at slaughter facility, followed by exsanguination, using standard industry protocols. Within 45 min (min) after the animal's death, longissimus thoracis (LT) muscles were harvested from the segment spanning the 7th to the last thoracic vertebrae, and were subsequently used for meat quality measurements and relevant subsequent molecular experiments. After removing visible fascia, fat, and connective tissue, the muscle was divided into two portions. One portion was refrigerated at 4 °C for subsequent meat quality analysis, while the other was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C ultra-low temperature environment for subsequent omics research.

Determination of meat quality

Determination of slaughter performance indicators

After 12 h (h) of fasting, all the experimental pigs were weighed with an electronic scale and the pre-slaughter weight (Body weight) was recorded. All pigs were slaughtered according to GB/T 17236–2017 Operating Procedures for Pig Slaughtering: after electrical stunning and exsanguination, carcasses underwent scalding, dehairing, and evisceration with removal of the head (transected at the atlanto-occipital joint), hooves (carpal/tarsal joints), tail, and viscera, while retaining leaf fat and kidneys. Carcass weight was immediately measured using an electronic hanging scale. Each carcass was split sagittally along the spinal midline, and subcutaneous backfat thickness (Backfat thickness) was determined at three sites on the left half—the thickest shoulder point (3rd–4th thoracic vertebrae), the last rib (13th rib), and the lumbosacral junction (last lumbar/first sacral vertebra)—using vernier calipers perpendicular to the skin surface (precision: 0.01 cm), with the mean value recorded. At the 13th rib, the LT was transversely sectioned; its cross-sectional outline was traced onto filter paper, and eye area measurement: long diameter × short diameter × 0.7, where long diameter represents maximal fiber-aligned length and short diameter the perpendicular maximal width, with 0.7 as a standard correction factor.

Meat quality measurements

Meat quality parameters were assessed using standardized protocols. pH values were measured at 45 min post-slaughter at three central points of the longissimus thoracis (LT) using a calibrated handheld pH meter (testo 205, Germany; pre-calibrated with pH 4.01 and 7.00 buffers), with triplicate stable readings averaged as final pH; Meat color was evaluated via a 3NH colorimeter (China) calibrated with standard white/black plates, recording L*, a* and b* values near pH measurement sites in triplicate, each site was assessed three times, and the mean values were calculated. Water-holding capacity (WHC) was determined by trimming muscle samples into 2.0 × 1.0 × 3.0 cm3 blocks, weighing (initial weight, W₀), suspending in conical flasks at 4 °C for 48 h, blotting surface moisture with absorbent paper, reweighing (final weight, W₁), and calculating drip loss as (W − W₁)/W₀ × 100%. Shear force was measured using a C-LM3B digital tenderometer (Tenovo, China) by cooking 30 g muscle samples in an 80 °C water bath until core temperature reached 70 °C, cooling to room temperature, cutting along fibers into nine 2.5 × 1.0 × 3.0 cm3 strips, and shearing at <5 mm/s with the mean of nine replicates recorded in Newtons (N); Intramuscular fat (IMF) content was quantified via a FoodScan 2 near-infrared analyzer (FOSS, Denmark) after homogenizing 30 g samples into paste, evenly spreading in sample dishes, and averaging triplicate measurements.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining and ATPase staining

The LT was quickly immersed in neutral polyformin fixative, and then tissue sections were prepared. The specific process included fixation, embedding, paraffin sectioning, HE staining, ATP enzyme staining and frozen sectioning. For the tissue sections of each experimental pig, Case Viewer software (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) was used to randomly select 3 non-overlapping fields of view for observation under 200× magnification. Meanwhile, the average cross-sectional area and diameter of muscle fibers within the selected fields of view were measured, and muscle fiber density (unit: fibers/mm2; defined as the number of muscle fibers per unit area) was calculated based on the measurement results. The corresponding measured values of each field of view were recorded. The experiment was set with 6 biological replicates (n = 6). During data processing, the average value of the measured data from the 3 fields of view of each pig was first calculated, and this average value was then used as the valid data for a single biological replicate in subsequent statistical analyses. ATPase Dye solution set (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) can effectively divide muscle fibers into type I and type II. Three random fields were selected by Case Viewer software to count different numbers of muscle fibers, and the proportion of each fiber type was calculated using the formula: Proportion (%) = (Number of Type I fibers / Total number of fibers) × 100%.

Amino acid detection

The amino acid content in the LT was determined in accordance with GB 5009.124–2016: muscle samples were first freeze-dried at −80 °C, ground, and sieved through an 80-mesh sieve to remove impurities; an accurate 1.0 g of the sieved sample was weighed into a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) hydrolysis tube, followed by the addition of 10 mL of 6 mol/L analytical-grade hydrochloric acid. The tube was frozen at −20 °C for 3–5 min, then subjected to 3 cycles of vacuum evacuation and filling with 99.999% high-purity nitrogen (to exclude oxygen), before being sealed and hydrolyzed in an electric heating blast constant temperature oven at 110 ± 1 °C for 22 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered into a 50 mL volumetric flask (with washing solutions incorporated), made up to volume with ultrapure water, and vortexed; a 1.0 mL aliquot of the diluted hydrolysate was dried under reduced pressure (0.08–0.1 MPa, 40 °C–50 °C), the residue was washed with ultrapure water and evaporated to dryness again, then dissolved in 1.0–2.0 mL of pH 2.2 sodium citrate buffer solution (Cat. No. C1013, Solarbiio, Beijing, China; pre-filtered through 0.22 μm membrane), and the final solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain the test sample. Detection was performed using an S—433D automatic amino acid analyzer (Sykam, Germany) with a Sykam LCA K06/Na cation exchange column (4.6 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm), gradient elution with sodium citrate buffers (pH 3.20, 4.30, 9.40) at 0.4 mL/min, column temperature of 55 °C, ninhydrin derivatization, detection wavelengths of 570 nm (most amino acids) and 440 nm (proline), and 20 μL injection volume; calibration was conducted with a 2.5 mmol/L Sykam mixed amino acid standard (R2 > 0.999). Quality control included reagent blank controls and a standard addition recovery test (recovery rate 90%–110%); quantification of amino acid concentration in sample test solution by peak area using external standard method, results were expressed as amino acid content per 100 g wet muscle (mg/100 g). Electronic nose and electronic tongue detection

After homogenizing the muscle tissue, place it in a self-sealing bag and incubate it in a 40 °C water bath until the core temperature reaches 40 °C. Subsequently, accurately weigh 20 g of the sample, transfer it to a 50 mL headspace vial, and seal the vial tightly. Then, an electronic nose (Model PEN3, Airsense Analytics GmbH, Germany) was used to detect the volatile substances in the LT. This device is equipped with 10 independent metal oxide sensors, and the sensor models correspond to the response characteristics of different volatile substances, respectively. Details are shown in Table S4. The detection parameters were set as follows: sampling interval: 5 s, flushing time: 55 s, zero calibration time: 5 s, pre-sampling time: 5 s, measurement time: 70 s.

Accurately weigh 30 g of sample, mix it with 150 mL of distilled water at a ratio of 1:5, and stir thoroughly in a food processor. Transfer the mixture into a centrifuge tube and centrifuge at 8000 rpm (rpm) for 10 s. Finally, collect the supernatant into a sample cup and use an electronic tongue (Model: SA402B Plus-EX, Brand: Insent, Origin: Japan) to detect relevant indicators. This device simulates the working principle of human taste buds, uses taste-sensitive sensors to capture the characteristics of taste substances, and converts them into analyzable electrical signals. Different sensors correspond to different taste indicators (such as bitterness, astringency, umami, saltiness, sweetness and other relevant parameters). Ultimately, these indicators are presented as specific numerical values, which are used to reflect the taste characteristics of the sample.

Determination of volatile compounds by HS-SPME-GC–MS

Approximately 500 mg of each sample was accurately weighed and transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial for HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis. A mixed standard solution of n-alkanes (C7–C40, 20 μg/mL) was separately prepared for retention time calibrationHS-SPME extraction was performed under controlled conditions: samples were equilibrated at 60 °C with pulsed agitation (450 rpm, 5 s on/2 s off) for 15 min, followed by adsorption onto a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Supelco, USA) exposed to the headspace for 40 min; the fiber was then thermally desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C for 5 min (preconditioned at 250 °C for 2 h before initial use and cleaned for 10 min between runs). GC separation utilized a DB-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID, 0.25 μm; Agilent, USA) with helium carrier gas (≥99.999%) at 1.0 mL/min constant flow, under splitless injection mode at 230 °C and 1.5 min solvent delay. The oven temperature program initiated at 30 °C (hold 3 min), ramped at 4 °C/min to 230 °C (hold 5 min). MS detection employed electron impact ionization (70 eV) with ion source/quadrupole temperatures at 230 °C/150 °C, scanning m/z 20–650 at 5 spectra/s in full-scan mode, then GC–MS analysis was performed.

The raw GC–MS data (in .D format) were converted into analysis basic file (abf format) by AnalysisBaseFileConverter software for rapid data retrieval, and then imported into MS-DIAL software for pretreatment. The algorithm was used to extract “model peaks” from chromatograms, remove background noise, and achieve qualitative and quantitative analysis of compounds by matching the similarity of retention time, fragment ion mass spectra with the self-built database.

LC-MS/MS condition and metabolite identification

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) coupled with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) and a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus/Thermo QE plus mass spectrometer (Waters/Thermo, Waltham, USA).

Approximately 30 mg of LT was homogenized with two steel beads in 400 μL ice-cold methanol-water (4:1, v/v) containing 4 μg/mL mixed internal standards, followed by 2-min grinding in a precooled (−40 °C) mill. The homogenate underwent ultrasonication in an ice-water bath for 10 min, incubation at−40 °C for 2 h, and centrifugation at 13,000 ×g (20 min, 4 °C); 150 μL supernatant was transferred to LC vials for dual-polarity LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic separation employed an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA) at 45 °C with mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid/water) and B (acetonitrile) at 0.35 mL/min flow rate, using 5 μL injection volume. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q Exactive Plus HRMS (Thermo Scientific, USA) with HESI source operating at 320 °C under dual spray voltages (+3.8 kV/−3.0 kV), sheath/auxiliary gas flows of 35/8 arb, full MS resolution of 70,000 (MS1) and 17,500 (MS/MS), and scan range m/z 100–1500, then LC-MS/MS analysis was performed.

The raw data were processed using the metabolomics software Progenesis QI v3.0 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization. Compound identification was performed using The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Lipidmaps (v2.3), METLIN database, and the local LuMet-Animal3.0 database.

Screening and functional enrichment of differential flavor compounds and differential metabolites

To evaluate the stability and distribution characteristics of samples, we first performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the data extracted from the flavor metabolomics and metabolomics. Meanwhile, Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to characterize the differences in metabolic profiles between different sample groups. A t-test was applied to analyze the differences in variables between the two sample groups, and key differential metabolites were screened out using the criteria of P < 0.05, | log2Fold Change | ≥ 1. The screened differential metabolites were mapped to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database for metabolic pathway enrichment analysis, so as to explore the main metabolic pathways they are involved in and their biological functions.

RNA-seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from muscle samples using the mirVana™ miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero Gold kit (Illumina, California, USA) was used to perform the following procedures: Ribo-Zero Deplete and Fragment RNA, Synthesize First Strand cDNA, Synthesize Second Strand cDNA, Adenylate 3′ Ends, Ligate Adapters, Enrich DNA Fragments, and purification. Thereafter, 1 μL of the sample was loaded onto an Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, California, USA), and finally, the length and quality of the library were confirmed based on the analysis results.

Raw reads were processed by removing adapters and low-quality reads, and clean reads were aligned to the Sus scrofa reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) using HISAT2. The average clean rate of raw reads was 96.55%, with mean Q30 value and GC content reaching 94.18% and 53.87%, respectively, and the average total mapping rate was 90.11%, indicating that the data quality met the requirements for subsequent analysis. Gene expression levels were then quantified using HTSeq-count technology based on reads mapped to protein-coding genes, with expression levels normalized as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). Genes with mean counts >2 were retained for further analysis. Key differentially expressed genes were screened based on the criteria of P < 0.05, | log2Fold Change | ≥ 1, and then imported into Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG databases for functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes. Pathways with P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched, providing insights into biological processes and molecular mechanisms underlying muscle trait differences.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from 12 muscle samples using the Trizol method, with the specific procedures as follows: 100 mg of tissue was placed into a grinding tube containing 1 mL of Trizol and grinding beads, which was then placed in a JXFSTPRP-32 grinder (Jingxin, Shanghai, China) and ground 6 times at a frequency of 60 Hz for 60 s each time. After complete grinding, the mixture was allowed to stand for 10 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and 0.2 mL of chloroform was added per 1 mL of Trizol, followed by vortexing for 15 s, standing at room temperature for 3 min, and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min (with the deceleration set to 0). The supernatant after centrifugation was then transferred to a new centrifuge tube, and 0.5 mL of pre-cooled isopropanol was added per 1 mL of Trizol. After vortexing for 15 s and incubating at −20 °C for 30 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was washed with 1 mL of 75% ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 7500 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min; the supernatant was discarded, and this washing step was repeated. The RNA precipitate was then air-dried for approximately 10 min, dissolved in an appropriate amount of DEPC-treated water, and the RNA concentration was finally measured using a NanoPhotometer® N50 (Implen, Germany). Reverse transcription PCR was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Shanghai, China) to synthesize cDNA. The thermal cycling parameters were 42 °C for 2 min, followed by 37 °C for 15 min, and then 85 °C for 5 s (s). After extraction and synthesis, the RNA and cDNA samples were stored in a − 80 °C refrigerator. qRT-PCR was performed using the QuantiNova SYBR PCR Mix Kit (QIAGEN, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol for the CFX96™ Real-Time System (BIO-RAD, USA). The thermal cycling parameters were 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 2 s, annealing for 10 s, then 65 °C for 5 s, and finally 95 °C for 5 s. The quantitative results were calculated and analyzed via the 2-ΔΔCt method, with n = 6 biological replicates (one independent porcine individual per muscle tissue sample) and three technical replicates performed for each sample. Data visualization was subsequently conducted using GraphPad Prism, where error bars in the figures indicate the standard error (SE). β-actin was used as the housekeeping gene. The information about the primers is listed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0 software. Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare experimental groups and the data are represented as mean ± standard deviation, P < 0.05 (*), indicating a significant difference; P < 0.01 (**), a highly significant difference; P < 0.001 (***), an extremely significant difference. Pearson's correlation analysis was given for correlation analysis, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a strong correlation was designated when the absolute value of the correlation coefficient met | r | > 0.7. In the multi-omics correlation analysis, the screening criteria for core regulatory factors were as follows: P < 0.001, | r | > 0.8. Data are represented as mean ± standard deviation from 6 biological replicates (one porcine individual is defined as one biological replicate).

Results

Comparative analysis of meat quality traits in longissimus thoracis (LT) between WZS and DLY pigs

The results showed that WZS pigs presented significantly inferior growth performance compared to DLY pigs across multiple growth indicators. Specifically, body weight, carcass weight, carcass length, backfat thickness and loin eye area of WZS pigs were all notably lower than those of DLY pigs (Fig. 1a–1d, P < 0.01). Results of H&E staining showed that the muscle fiber density of WZS pigs was higher than DLY pigs; ATPase staining results demonstrated that the number of Type I (slow-twitch oxidative) muscle fibers did not differ significantly between WZS pigs and DLYpigs; in contrast, WZS pigs exhibited a greater quantity of Type IIA muscle fibers and a smaller quantity of Type IIB muscle fibers. (Fig. 1e). In terms of morphological characteristics, compared with DLY pigs, WZS pigs had smaller cross-sectional areas of muscle fibers (P < 0.01) and a higher proportion of oxidative muscle fibers (Type I and Type IIA) (Fig. 1f–1g); meanwhile, WZS pigs presented characteristics of slower growth rate and denser muscle structure.

Meat quality assessments showed that WZS pigs had lower intramuscular fat (IMF) content than DLY pigs (P < 0.05), but exhibited superior meat color with higher lightness (L*) and redness (a*) values (Fig. 1i) (P < 0.01). This color advantage is likely driven by variations in myoglobin content and muscle tissue oxygenation, as myoglobin concentration directly influences meat redness and light reflectance (Zhang et al., 2020). Notably, WZS pig muscles had significantly lower drip loss rates and shear force values than DLY pigs (Fig. 1k-1l), indicating better WHC and tenderness (P < 0.05) (Table S3). This correlates with their high muscle fiber density and small fiber diameter, as smaller fiber diameters are typically linked to improved meat tenderness.

Flavor phenotypic analysis via amino acid determination, electronic nose, and electronic tongue

Further analysis of the proportional structure of amino acid composition reveals that although the concentrations of individual amino acids differed significantly between DLY and WZS pigs, the proportions of umami amino acids, sweet amino acids, and bitter amino acids in the total amino acids are not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that, in terms of the overall proportion of amino acids, the framework of flavor - forming substances in the two types of pigs maintains a certain degree of consistency, without any fundamental proportional imbalance. However, the two breeds exhibited significant differences in the absolute concentrations of amino acids—a factor that directly influences the actual flavor profile of the meat. Among these amino acids, the absolute concentrations of umami amino acids (such as glutamic acid and aspartic acid) and sweet amino acids (such as glycine and alanine) in DLY pork are significantly higher than those in WZS pigs (P < 0.05). As key substances constituting the core flavor of meat, the increase in the concentrations of these two types of amino acids can directly act on taste perception, thereby enhancing the mellow taste of DLY pork when it enters the mouth. Notably, while the flavor is improved, the concentration of bitter amino acids (such as branched - chain amino acids like leucine, isoleucine, and valine) in DLY pork also shows a significant upward trend (P < 0.05), and this change may affect the overall flavor balance to a certain extent (Table 1).

Principal component analysis of electronic nose data further validated the authenticity of breed-specific odor differences. For the electronic nose PCA model, PC1 contributed 66.8%, PC2 contributed 16.4%, and the cumulative contribution rate is 83.5%, indicating that the model can effectively reflect the overall odor characteristics of samples (Fig. 2a). From the PCA scatter plot, data points of the same variety were highly clustered, confirming the stability of electronic nose detection. In contrast, sample points from the two breeds were distinctly separated demonstrating inherent differences in the volatile odor profiles between WZS pigs and the outbred three-way cross DLY pigs. The abundance of volatile odor compounds in cooked meat directly determines its aroma, with the response values of W1W (sulfides) and W2W (organic sulfides) sensors serving as key indicators. Detection data showed that the W1W and W2W response values were extremely significantly higher than in WZS pork than in DLY pork (P < 0.01; Fig. 2b; Table S4). Since sulfide compounds corresponding to the W1W sensor are the core signal sources for electronic nose to identify meat aroma. This result indicates that WZS pork releases more abundant sulfur-containing volatile substances after cooking, and its unique meat aroma intensity is significantly better than that of DLY pigs. The response values of W1W and W2W sensors significantly favored WZS pigs, directly confirming their content advantages in sulfides and organic sulfides. These compounds serve as key flavor precursors that contribute to the baking aroma and caramel-like fragrance of meat.

To further characterize taste differences, PCA was performed on samples using an electronic tongue. For this model, PC1 contributed 37.4%, PC2 contributed 24.1%, and the cumulative contribution rate was 61.5%, indicating that the electronic tongue can comprehensively reflect the taste information of the samples (Fig. 2c). Specifically, WZS pigs showed significant advantages in the response values of umami, saltiness, and umami aftertaste, while the astringency response values were extremely significantly lower than those of outbred DLY pigs (P < 0.01). This result is completely consistent with the radar chart analysis (Fig. 2d, Table S5), revealing that the advantage of WZS pork in taste balance lies in its high umami and low astringency, which makes its taste more mellow and soft.

To systematically explore flavor metabolite differences in LT between WZS pigs and DLY pigs, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) was used foru comprehensive qualitative and quantitative analysis. A total of 87 metabolites were identified (Table S6), with organic oxygen compounds (25.35%), saturated hydrocarbons (22.54%), and fatty acids (15.49%) as the major classes (Fig. 3a). Among these, carbonyl compounds (11.27% of total), alkanes (22.54%), and fatty alcohols (9.86%) constituted the primary subcategories (Fig. 3b). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the dataset showed the first two components explained 24.1% and 12.6% of total variance, respectively, preliminarily distinguishing sample clusters (Fig. 3c). Supervised orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was further applied to eliminate interference and enhance model predictability (Fig. 3d). The OPLS-DA model exhibited robust performance with R2Y (explained variance) of 0.954 and Q2 (predictive ability) of 0.76, confirming its reliability for subsequent analysis (Fig. 3e).

Additionally, we further conducted a correlation analysis between the screened differential flavor metabolites and phenotypic data (Table S10). The results showed that 2,3-butanediol, isopropoxyethyl carbamate, diethylmethylcarbamodithioic acid methyl ester, and 5-(1-methylethyl)bicyclo[3.1.0]hexan-2-one exhibited a significant positive correlation with a* and b* (color indices). This indicates that an increase in the content of these flavor substances is accompanied by more distinct redness and yellowness of meat products, which is directly associated with meat freshness and sensory acceptability—consistent with previous research conclusions that the oxidation state of myoglobin affects meat color and the formation of flavor substances. In contrast, these metabolites showed a significant negative correlation with intramuscular fat (IMF), drip loss, shear force, loin eye area, and backfat thickness (Faustman et al., 2010). Among these metabolites, 2,3-butanediol itself has a moderate fruity aroma and is a key flavor substance in fermented foods; its negative correlation with tenderness-related indices further confirms the inherent link between flavor quality and meat texture (Shi et al., 2025). In contrast, prenol showed an extremely significant positive correlation with drip loss, shear force, and loin eye area, but a significant negative correlation with a* and b* (Fig. 4c–4d). Drip loss reflects the water-holding capacity of meat, and shear force is directly related to tenderness. This correlation pattern implies that an increase in prenol content may be accompanied by a decrease in muscle water-holding capacity and tenderness; meanwhile, the negative correlation with color indices suggests that the accumulation of prenol may be associated with a decline in meat freshness. Similar negative correlation patterns between volatile substances and meat physicochemical properties have also been observed in previous relevant studies (Estévez et al., 2003).

Chemical classification of all identified metabolites revealed that the top three superclasses in terms of content were Lipids and Lipid-like Molecules (32.62%), Organic Acids and Derivatives (20.82%), and Organoheterocyclic Compounds (16%) (Fig. 5a). Among Lipids and Lipid-like Molecules, fatty acyls accounted for the highest proportion (58.5%) (Fig. 5b). Multivariate analysis confirmed significant differences in muscle metabolism between the two pig breeds. PCA showed that the first two principal components explained 24.1% and 12.6% of the total variation in muscle metabolites, respectively (Fig. 5c). Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) further verified the distinct clustering of muscle metabolites between the two breeds (Fig. 5d), with excellent model validation parameters (R2Y > 0.9, Q2 > 0.5), indicating that the model was reliable and suitable for subsequent analyses (Fig. 5e).

Using P < 0.05, | log2Fold Change | ≥ 1 as screening criteria, with DLY pigs as controls, 361 significantly differential metabolites were identified in the muscles of WZS pigs, among which 200 were up-regulated and 161 were down-regulated (Fig. 5f, Table S12). Lipids and lipid-like molecules accounted for the highest proportion (34.9%) among these differential metabolites. Hierarchical clustering analysis of the top 50 metabolites with the most significant differences (including amino acids, lipids, nucleotides, carbohydrates, and organic acids) further distinguished the muscle metabolic characteristics of the two pig breeds (Fig. 5g). Notably, the levels of L-argininium(1+) (FC = 30.95) and L-valine (FC = 2.16) in the LT of WZS pigs were relatively high (Table S11), and the levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) were also elevated, such as eicosapentaenoic acid (FC = 2.98), PC(20:5/0:0) (FC = 10.20), and PC(22:6/0:0) (FC = 4.75). These metabolites are known to contribute to pork quality differences. Specifically, upregulated quinic acid and alginic acid may exert multi-faceted effects on WZS pork quality by regulating muscle energy metabolism, antioxidant capacity, and fat deposition (Fig. 5h).

The differential metabolites were imported into the KEGG database for enrichment analysis, resulting in a total of 103 enriched pathways (Table S13). Among these, metabolic pathways accounted for the highest proportion (46.6%). Based on the screening criterion of P < 0.05, 18 significantly enriched pathways were further identified (Table S14). Among the significantly enriched pathways, amino acid metabolism pathways stood out, specifically including arginine and proline metabolism, histidine metabolism, lysine degradation, arginine biosynthesis, and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism pathways. These pathways are directly involved in the synthesis, decomposition and transformation of amino acids in muscle, and are core factors affecting muscle meat quality (such as tenderness and accumulation of flavor precursor substances). In addition, lipid metabolism pathways (e.g., glycerophospholipid metabolism) were also significantly enriched. They are closely related to the fat deposition pattern and fatty acid composition in muscle, which determine the juiciness and flavor intensity of muscle. The muscle-specific metabolic differences revealed in this study clearly explain the essential differences in the muscle metabolic networks between WZS pigs and DLY pigs at the molecular level. This provides a key metabolic molecular basis for further analyzing the formation mechanism of pork quality differences between the two pig breeds, and also offers clear research targets for the subsequent targeted improvement of pork quality by regulating metabolic pathways (Fig. 5i-5j).

To clarify the regulatory role of differential metabolites (DMs) in pork quality, correlation analysis was performed between DMs and pork phenotypic traits (Table S15). The results showed that among the detected differential metabolites, a total of 18 differential muscle metabolites were positively correlated with core meat quality indicators (backfat thickness, loin eye area, drip loss, shear force, and intramuscular fat content, IMF), while another 16 differential muscle metabolites were positively correlated with meat color indicators (redness value a*, yellowness value b*, and lightness value L*). This finding suggests that variations in the content of muscle metabolites are closely associated with pork quality characteristics. Among these metabolites, four key differential metabolites—1-methyl-L-histidine, alginic acid, PE, 20:4/18:0, and quinic acid—exhibited a consistent regulatory trend: they were all significantly negatively correlated with backfat thickness, loin eye area, drip loss, shear force, and IMF, while being significantly positively correlated with meat color redness value a* and yellowness value b* (Fig. 5k, Table S15). Further mechanistic speculation indicates that these metabolites can exert a synergistic positive effect on pork quality through multiple pathways, including reducing myoglobin oxidation, inhibiting excessive fat deposition, and optimizing energy metabolism. Specifically, they not only decrease shear force to improve meat tenderness and reduce drip loss to enhance WHC but also maintain myoglobin stability to improve meat color brightness, demonstrating significant potential for pork quality enhancement. In contrast, 4-trimethylaminobutyraldehyde and N-acetyl-5-hydroxytryptamine showed obvious negative regulatory characteristics: both were significantly positively correlated with loin eye area, drip loss, and IMF, while being significantly negatively correlated with meat color redness value a* (Fig. 5k, Table S15). These metabolites may disrupt the spatial structure of myoglobin, leading to reduced redness and dull color of pork.

Collectively, these results confirm that variations in muscle metabolites are key factors contributing to differences in core quality indicators of pork LT, including tenderness, WHC, color, and intramuscular fat content.

Transcriptomics in longissimus thoracis (LT) of WZS pigs and DLY pigs

To investigate the molecular mechanisms responsible for meat quality differences between WZS and DLY pigs, RNA-seq was performed on 12 longissimus thoracis (LT) samples (6 samples per breed). After quality control, a total of 166.7 Gb of high-quality clean reads were generated, with an average of approximately 13.9 Gb per sample—providing sufficient depth for subsequent gene expression analysis (Table S16). Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to evaluate the overall variation in gene expression profiles between the two breeds. As illustrated in the PCA score plot, WZS and DLY samples were clearly clustered into two distinct groups with no inter-group overlap. This result indicated significant differences in the LT transcriptomes of WZS and DLY pigs, validating the effectiveness of the group design for differential expression analysis (Fig. 6a). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using the criteria of |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. A total of 2279 DEGs were identified, including 996 genes that were significantly upregulated and 1283 genes that were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6b, Table S17). To verify the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) data, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted on 25 randomly selected DEGs. The relative expression levels of these genes, as determined by qRT-PCR, were consistent with the RNA-seq results (Fig. 6c). This high degree of consistency confirmed the accuracy and reproducibility of the DEG profiles identified in the RNA-seq analysis.

We performed cluster analysis on the top 50 most significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) ranked by P-value (Fig. 6d). The results showed that there were 43 downregulated genes (including MMP16, THY1, and CHD3) and 7 upregulated genes (including LOC106509183, AQP1, and IYD). Among these DEGs, genes such as those in the collagen family (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL5A1, and COL5A2), MMP16, FAP, and ADAMTS2 are mainly involved in regulating the content of collagen in muscle, the processing of collagen precursors, and the efficiency of collagen degradation, thereby influencing the tenderness and chewiness of the LT. In contrast, genes including IGF2, DHCR24, and INSIG1 affect the flavor and juiciness of pork by regulating the deposition of intramuscular fat (IMF). Through the synergistic effects of multiple pathways, these genes collectively drive significant differences in the quality of the LT between WZS pigs and DLY pigs. Ultimately, this gives rise to the core quality advantages of WZS pork—“higher tenderness, richer flavor, and better appearance”—making it more aligned with consumers' preference for high-quality pork.

GO functional enrichment analysis was performed on the 2279 DEGs obtained through screening. The results showed that these DEGs were collectively enriched in 3537 metabolic pathways, among which growth-related pathways accounted for the highest proportion (65.9%) and served as the core category regulating gene functions (Table S18). Using P < 0.05 as the threshold for significant enrichment, 188 significantly enriched pathways were further identified (Table S19). The top 5 key pathways, sorted by ascending P-value, were: cell adhesion, adaptive immune response, extracellular matrix organization, regulation of immune response, and collagen catabolic process (Fig. 6e). By regulating the remodeling of muscle tissue structure, the efficiency of collagen degradation, and the balance of immune responses, these pathways directly act on the structure and function of the LT, thereby influencing core meat quality traits such as tenderness, juiciness, and color (Neuhoff, & Christiane., 2014; Li et al., 2024). Concurrent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that this set of DEGs was mapped to a total of 228 metabolic pathways (Table S20). Among these, growth-related pathways remained dominant (35.9%), and pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism exhibited significant enrichment characteristics. Using P < 0.05 as the screening criterion, 13 key regulatory pathways were finally determined, including steroid biosynthesis, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Table S21). By participating in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway, these pathways synergistically regulate the antioxidant capacity and developmental process of muscle tissue. Ultimately, through metabolic networks, they translate differences in gene expression into the unique meat quality traits of WZS pigs (Fig. 6f).

To clarify the molecular mechanism underlying the differences in longissimus thoracis (LT) quality between the two pig breeds, we first performed a correlation analysis between the 119 genes enriched in key pathways and phenotypic data (Table S22). Given the large number of genes, we screened the top 50 genes with the most statistically significant associations by ranking them in ascending order of P-values and visualized their associations with phenotypic traits (Fig. 5g). Among these 50 key genes, we found that their associations with meat quality traits could be divided into two main categories: 30 genes showed positive correlations with 5 core meat quality indices, while the other 20 genes exhibited positive correlations with meat color parameters. Specifically, the ALDH2, GCAT, PYCR, CYP5, HACD, and KMO genes were positively correlated with longissimus thoracis (LT) traits such as loin eye area, drip loss, shear force, and intramuscular fat content (IMF), while showing significantly negative correlations with the meat color parameters a* and b*. These genes not only directly regulate the morphological characteristics and tenderness of the longissimus thoracis (LT) but also alter the visual characteristics of meat color, serving as an important molecular basis for distinguishing the LT quality between the two pig breeds. In contrast, the LOC100739101 gene was significantly negatively correlated with loin eye area and IMF, but significantly positively correlated with meat color a* and b* values. Additionally, the PGM2L1 and LPIN1 genes were significantly negatively correlated with backfat thickness, yet significantly positively correlated with meat color L* value. These genes contribute to reduced fat deposition in the LT of WZS pigs, further amplifying the degree of difference in LT quality between WZS pigs and DLY pigs. Collectively, these findings provide key insights into the molecular mechanism underlying meat quality differences between WZS and DLY pigs.

Discussion

Pig carcass and meat quality are synergistically influenced by genetics, nutrition, and management, with genetics dominating breed-specific differences in carcass composition and meat quality (Wang et al., 2023). This study compared Hainan local WZS pigs and introduced DLY crossbreds, and the results aligned with previous findings on “high-quality meat” traits in Chinese local pigs (Wu et al., 2022). Specifically, for carcass traits, DLY pigs—selected long-term for “high growth and lean percentage” loci—have larger bodies and significantly larger loin eye areas (P < 0.05), suiting commercial high-lean output (Zeng et al., 2024). However, local miniature WZS pigs have unique strengths: relatively higher lean percentage and thinner backfat, consistent with Meishan pig study (local breeds have smaller bodies but more balanced carcass fat, enabling targeted breeding improvements) (Li et al., 2020). Chinese local pigs, shaped by long natural/artificial selection, link carcass traits to meat quality, which serve as key genetic resources for high-quality pork production (Huang et al., 2020). In meat quality, WZS advantages are supported by muscle fiber data: significantly higher L* and a* (P < 0.05), lower shear force (P < 0.05)—tied to smaller fiber area and higher type IIA slow-twitch fibers. Furthermore, relevant studies have shown that an increase in oxidative muscle fibers is associated with improved meat quality, specifically manifested by a reduction in drip loss, which makes the meat more tender (Guo et al., 2010). Generally, local pigs have thinner fibers, higher density, and more slow-twitch fibers, enhancing tenderness (Chen et al., 2018). WZS pigs inherited these traits: fiber structure affects color, tenderness, flavor, and WHC, with slow-twitch fibers stabilizing color. Moreover, WZS's high type IIA fibers aid energy metabolism/lipid deposition, optimizing intramuscular fat and flavor. In contrast, DLY's high type IIB fast-twitch fibers cause strong anaerobic metabolism, insufficient flavor precursors, and poorer color/tenderness. WZS excels in color, tenderness, and flavor. Despite exhibiting inferior carcass performance compared to DLY pigs, its favorable lean meat distribution and superior meat quality align with consumer demands, supporting local pig genetic resource use and high-quality pork industry development.

Electronic noses and electronic tongues, as sensory simulation tools, do not merely detect single flavor components (e.g., umami amino acids) but capture the comprehensive fingerprint information of volatile and taste-active substances in meat (Lippolis et al., 2015). Such instruments are widely used in pork flavor analysis; for instance, they have been applied to the rapid detection of the overall flavor of Ningxiang pork (Yang et al., 2021). In this study, we conducted detections on cooked WZS pork. The results showed that the response values of sensors W1W and W2W were relatively high, and WZS pigs exhibit significant advantages in the response values of umami, saltiness, and umami aftertaste, indicating that the contents of sulfur-containing compounds and aromatic compounds in WZS pork were higher. These volatile flavor substances can synergistically enhance the perception of umami taste through the “aroma-taste interaction” effect. For DLY pigs, although the absolute content of umami amino acids is higher, their bitter amino acid content is also high. Bitter amino acids have a masking effect on umami taste, which weakens the actual umami perception detected by the electronic tongue (Wang et al., 2020). Additionally, relevant studies have demonstrated that the Maillard reaction and fat oxidation are core factors contributing to pork flavor formation (Myers et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2022). This is consistent with the increased content of Carbamodithioic Acid, Diethyl-, Methyl Ester detected in the LT of WZS pigs in this study, further confirming the material basis for its flavor advantages. Furthermore, flavoromics analysis of the LT of WZS pigs revealed an upward trend in 2,3-butanediol content and a downward trend in isopentanol content. Studies have shown that 2,3-butanediol can enhance the sweet or milky aroma of pork and improve flavor complexity, while a decrease in isopentanol content may reduce the fishy odor caused by oxidation (Dong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). These specific metabolites not only optimize the overall flavor profile of WZS pork but also create a more favorable chemical environment for the presentation of umami taste, enabling the electronic tongue to detect a stronger umami signal. In contrast, DLY pigs may lack such flavor substance support, and the high content of bitter amino acids further offsets the contribution of umami amino acids to the taste response.

The premise that the ratios of umami and bitter amino acids to total amino acids are not significantly different between the two breeds further indicates that the difference in umami response values is not determined by the proportion of umami amino acids. Instead, it is the specific combination of taste-active substances, volatile flavor compounds, and the regulation of flavor formation pathways (moderate Maillard reaction and fat oxidation) in WZS pork that shapes its unique umami taste advantage detected by the electronic tongue. Correlation analysis showed that these metabolites were strongly associated with pork color (P < 0.001) and tenderness, and could serve as key indicators for evaluation. This “aroma-taste” synergistic effect enhances consumers' positive perception of pork flavor. In summary, the moderate Maillard reaction, moderate fat oxidation, and the specific changes in DFMs collectively shape the unique flavor of WZS pork, thus clarifying the mechanism underlying its flavor advantages.

To systematically clarify the material basis of the unique meat quality of WZS pigs, this study identified DMs in WZS pork through metabolomic analysis, and further explored their regulatory roles in meat quality formation by associating them with key meat quality traits. Studies have shown that amino acids, lipids, and organic acids are core factors influencing meat quality formation. The 34 DMs identified in this study include 3 amino acids, 5 amino acid derivatives, 8 lipids, and 4 organic acids. Among them, L-valine (a branched-chain essential amino acid) affects the tenderness and taste of pork by participating in protein synthesis in muscles (Xu et al., 2022). Aminoadipic acid, on the other hand, regulates amino acid metabolism networks, influences protein synthesis and degradation, and ultimately acts on pork quality traits (meat color and WHC) (Belal & Shim, 2016). Some relevant studies have pointed out that volatile compounds such as aldehydes and ketones produced by fat oxidation during cooking are the main sources of meaty aroma (Sohail et al., 2022). Substances identified in this study, including PE (20:4/18:0), PC (20:5/0:0) and eicosapentaenoic acid, are all involved in lipid metabolism: Phosphatidylethanolamine regulates intramuscular fat content by affecting adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage, thereby optimizing flavor and tenderness; eicosapentaenoic acid influences flavor changes in pork during storage and processing by altering the oxidative stability of fats (Song et al., 2017; Wood et al., 2004). Furthermore, organic acids also play an important role. Quinic acid and succinic acid identified in this study are involved in multiple metabolic pathways in pigs (Zhai et al., 2024; Arnold & Finley, 2023). As an intermediate product of energy metabolism, quinic acid may interfere with the tricarboxylic acid cycle and affect meat quality formation; succinic acid regulates muscle pH by participating in cellular respiration, thereby influencing meat color, tenderness, and shelf life (Arnold & Finley, 2023). The coordinated upregulation and downregulation of these DMs may collectively contribute to the tenderer texture and brighter red color of WZS pork, potentially highlighting the key role of differential metabolites in shaping the unique meat quality of WZS pigs. In-depth research on these metabolites and their mechanisms is expected to provide new targets for pork quality improvement and evaluation, ultimately facilitating the high-quality development of the pork industry.

In the field of transcriptomic research on pork quality, numerous studies have provided key insights into the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying meat quality traits, laying a theoretical foundation for exploring the characteristic quality of local pig breeds (Wang et al., 2024). A study conducted RNA sequencing and differential expression analysis on the LT of Iberian pigs, identifying a total of 200 DEGs. Among these, transcription factors including GF1, VGLL3 and PPARG were confirmed as core candidate genes regulating meat tenderness (Fernández-Barroso et al., 2020). Another multi-omics integrated analysis comparing commercial crossbred pigs and local pig breeds further screened 6 core genes involved in regulating intramuscular fat deposition, such as PPARG, SREBF1 and ACACA. The coordinated regulation of these three genes determines the accumulation of intramuscular fat in pork, ultimately influencing the flavor richness and juiciness of pork (Yu et al., 2023). Focusing on WZS pigs, this study performed transcriptomic analysis associated with meat quality traits on their LT, identifying 119 DEGs related to meat quality traits. This further reveals the molecular specificity underlying the quality formation of the LT in WZS pigs. Among these DEGs, genes such as FADS2, PGM2L1, ALDH2, KMO, and CYP51 exhibit a unique characteristic of “negatively regulating meat color (L*, a* and b*) while positively regulating tenderness (Drip Loss, Shear Force and IMF)”. Specifically, FADS2 can improve meat flavor and tenderness; although it shows a negative correlation with a* and b* at the transcriptional level, it actually contributes to maintaining meat color stability (Kasai et al., 2022; Renaville et al., 2013). In contrast, genes including CYP4F55, ADH1C, MAOB, PNMT and LPIN1 in the LT of WZS pigs display an expression pattern of “positively regulating meat color while negatively regulating tenderness”, which further enriches the gene network for meat quality regulation. Meanwhile, their negative correlation with shear force suggests that it may indirectly reduce tenderness by affecting muscle collagen fibers (Ovilo et al., 2006). To accurately screen the core regulatory genes for the LT quality of WZS pigs, this study adopted more stringent screening criteria (P ≤ 0.001, |r| > 0.8), ultimately obtaining 37 key DEGs (including ACSL6, ADH1C, AMDHD1, AOX2 and ARG2). Through the synergistic and antagonistic effects of multiple metabolic networks, these genes further optimize the meat flavor of the LT in WZS pigs. Specifically, the coordinated optimization of meat quality traits is achieved through multi-dimensional regulation of lipid metabolism network (FADS2, ACSL6, CYP51), amino acid metabolism network (KMO, AMDHD1, ARG2), glucose metabolism network (PGM2L1), and redox regulation network (ALDH2, AOX2). These findings not only provide a molecular basis for the conservation and utilization of WZS pigs as germplasm resources but also offer potential gene targets for the precise improvement of pork quality.

Leveraging its technical advantage of integrating multi-dimensional molecular data, multi-omics integrated analysis has become a core approach for deciphering the complex molecular mechanisms governing pork quality formation, accumulating substantial research foundations in this field (Song et al., 2023). However, existing studies still have notable limitations: most focus on binary correlation analysis between “metabolites and meat quality” or “genes and meat quality,” or only explore a single attribute of meat quality (e.g., muscle fiber type, intramuscular fat content) (Liu et al., 2024). They fail to establish systematic associations between “multi-dimensional molecular indicators (such as genes and metabolites) and multiple meat quality traits (such as flavor, tenderness, meat color, and water-holding capacity),” which prevents the complete revelation of the interconnected regulatory networks underlying meat quality formation and makes it difficult to fully elaborate the molecular essence of meat quality phenotypic differences (Li et al., 2025). To address the aforementioned research gaps, this study took WZS pigs as the research object and conducted systematic correlation analysis between 4 DFMs, 14 DMs, 37 DEGs, and meat quality traits. The results showed that: DFMs exerted a core impact on meat flavor, with positive correlations with W1W and W2W (electronic nose signals) and a negative correlation with astringency; most DMs (including 3 amino acids and their derivatives, 4 lipids and lipid-like molecules) exhibited negative correlations with shear force and drip loss, and positive correlations with meat color; furthermore, DEGs (such as ADH1C involved in amino acid metabolism, BCKDHB in carbohydrate metabolism, and ACSL6 in lipid metabolism) helped clarify the genetic regulatory mechanisms of the aforementioned metabolic processes. Compared with existing studies, the multi-level association system of “DFMs-DMs-DEGs-meat quality traits” constructed in this study could more completely present the pathway of “genetic regulation-metabolic change-phenotypic manifestation” in meat quality formation. It overcame the analytical limitations of “single molecular dimension or single meat quality trait” in traditional studies and achieved a transition from “local mechanism deciphering” to “systematic network construction.”In summary, through the design of precise correlations between multi-dimensional molecular indicators and multiple meat quality traits, this study revealed the molecular mechanisms underlying meat quality formation in WZS pigs more comprehensively. This research not only addressed the shortcomings of previous studies but also identified DFMs, DMs, and DEGs as multi-level molecular targets for pork quality improvement. It provided a theoretical basis for the coordinated optimization of multiple pork traits (flavor, tenderness, and meat color) and held important practical significance for promoting the transformation of the pork industry toward high-quality development.

Although this study has achieved valuable results in analyzing the key molecular markers of WZS pork quality and their regulatory roles, it still has certain limitations that need to be focused on and addressed in subsequent research. First, this study only took the LT as the research object. This makes it difficult to capture the metabolic interaction network among multiple tissues, resulting in the inability to fully reveal the comprehensive regulatory mechanism of pork quality formation. Second, there is a limitation in the verification scope of candidate markers. The 4 DFMs, 14 DMs, and 37 DEGs identified in this study have not been verified in larger-scale and more diverse scenarios. Therefore, in future research, we should expand the dimension of tissue research to fill the cognitive gap of the current single-tissue research and provide a more comprehensive molecular explanation for meat quality formation. In addition, it is necessary to strengthen the verification of candidate markers, design large-scale and multi-environment validation experiments, and confirm the robust regulatory functions of the 4 DFMs, 14 DMs, and 37 DEGs, so as to provide a solid theoretical basis for the subsequent improvement of WZS pork quality. Addressing the limitations of single tissue focus and limited validation scope in the current study is not only a necessary step to improve the research on the molecular mechanism of WZS pork quality, but also a key bridge to promote the transformation of multi-omics results from basic research to industrial application. Ultimately, it will provide more accurate scientific support for the protection and utilization of high-quality traits of local pig breeds and the high-quality development of the pork industry.

Conclusion

In conclusion, WZS pigs represent a unique genetic resource with superior flavor, tenderness, and nutritional value, underpinned by distinct metabolic and transcriptional profiles. Their divergence from DLY pigs reflects evolutionary trade-offs between production efficiency and meat quality, offering insights for balancing these traits in future breeding programs. The identified markers and pathways provide a molecular basis for conserving and improving local pig breeds, ensuring sustainable development of high-quality pork production.
