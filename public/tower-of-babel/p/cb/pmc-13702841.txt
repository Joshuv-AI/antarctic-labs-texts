Waxing and waning pulmonary infiltrates in a patient with hypertrophic pachymeningitis: Solving a diagnostic mystery
Authors: Abdullah Mobeireek, Ghayda Alhifthi, Shamayel Ahmed, Saeed Bohlega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertrophic pachymeningitis (HP) is a rare chronic fibro-inflammatory process that leads to thickening of the dura mater in the central nervous system. It might be idiopathic or secondary to several causes including infections, malignancy, autoimmune conditions or sarcoidosis. Pulmonary involvement in HP is a rare association that poses a diagnostic dilemma, particularly as to whether the pathologic process is idiopathic or secondary to another systemic disorder. The case of a 50-year-old woman with HP and recurrent pulmonary infiltrates is presented with a discussion on the systematic diagnostic approach and management of relapses. Although pulmonary involvement in secondary HP is well recognized, it can occur rarely in the idiopathic type. The pathogenetic mechanisms are likely related to a systemic immune process that deserves further research.

Background

Hypertrophic pachymeningitis (HP) refers to a chronic fibro-inflammatory process that leads to thickening of the dura mater in the central nervous system.[12] As a result, patients can present with a variety of neurological manifestations, including headache, weakness, seizures, ataxia, and other deficits.[12] Incidence is rare; for example, the crude prevalence estimated to be 0.949/100,000 in Japan.[3] It might be idiopathic or secondary to several causes including infections, malignancy, autoimmune conditions, or sarcoidosis.[1245678] Although the idiopathic type is a diagnosis of exclusion, it is likely to be related to an immune systemic process.[89]

When a patient with HP presents with respiratory symptoms and abnormal thoracic imaging, the clinician faces a diagnostic puzzle. Philosophers have cautioned against the use of “Occam’s razor” i.e. taking a simple route in invoking a causal relationship.[10] It is more prudent that clinicians exclude the known causes, and if none are found, they can speculate on the possible etiology. Herein, we present such a case with a discussion on the possible pathogenetic mechanism and therapeutic options.

Case Report

A 50-year-old female with a history of HP was referred by neurology for the evaluation of exertional dyspnea and pulmonary infiltrates in 2022. She denied cough, sputum, chest pain, fever, and weight loss.

Her initial presentation was in 2017, with a complaint of recurrent headaches, mainly around the ear and temporal region, that were followed by diplopia and hearing loss on the left side. Examination then showed few cranial nerve palsies of the left 5th, 6th, 7th, and 8th nerves. Magnetic resonance imaging showed nodular thickening of the dura with heterogeneous enhancement and a left cerebellopontine angle lesion extending to the left carotid sheath, internal auditory canal, jugular foramen, and ventral aspects of the brain stem [Figure 1]. Positron emission tomography-fludeoxyglucose (FDG) scan showed no focal FDG activity, and a surgical biopsy of the lesion was then performed. Based on all the findings, a diagnosis of idiopathic pachymeningitis was made. At that time, she had no respiratory symptoms and chest radiograph was normal. She was treated with azathioprine 100 mg once daily and prednisone 5 mg once daily, after which she remained in remission.

On examination, she was afebrile, with a blood pressure of 126/75 mmHg, pulse rate of 88 bpm, respiratory rate 25 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 93% on room air. Examination of the chest showed bilateral vesicular breath sounds. Systematic examination was otherwise unremarkable.

The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary meeting and after a thorough review, it was concluded that since specific causes were ruled out, the likely diagnosis was idiopathic HP with pulmonary involvement. The patient was managed with intensification of her corticosteroids therapy and maintained on azathioprine 100 mg daily. Subsequently, her symptoms improved as well as imaging, and corticosteroid dosage was tapered. However, on follow-up, she had few relapses of her pulmonary disease that required temporary escalation of steroids. In her last relapse in 2024, she underwent a repeat CT-guided biopsy of a new suspicious lesion in the right lower lobe that showed identical pathology to the biopsy performed in 2022, and no evidence of malignancy or an opportunistic infection. On her latest follow-up, 12 months following her relapse, she remained in clinical and radiological remission.

Discussion

Patients with HP might present with a variety of pulmonary manifestations, including interstitial lung disease, nodules, masses, and tracheal stenosis.[45678] With the recent advances in serologic testing and tissue sampling, many systemic disorders were reported as etiologic factors, including infection, malignancy, the different types of the vasculitides, IgG 4-related disease, and sarcoidosis.[245678] Such testing is imperative to initiate the appropriate specific therapy. However, despite significant advances since 1869, when HP was first described,[2] extensive testing might still fail to demonstrate a specific etiology. This was the situation in the presented case, wherein the diagnosis remained a mystery and therefore conveniently labeled as “idiopathic”. A literature search yielded only one similar case of a 47-year-old male with idiopathic HP who developed a pleural-based pulmonary nodule, with an identical histological picture to HP.[9] However, no relapses were reported over 3 years’ follow-up while on immunosuppressive therapy. We concur with the authors that this condition is likely an immune mediated disorder, based on the similarities in the pathological process exhibiting lymphocytic inflammation and fibrosis, and the response to immunosuppressive therapy. In addition, since idiopathic HP shares a number of features with myeloperoxidase-antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) related HP, Yokoseki et al. speculated that idiopathic HP is associated with an undetected ANCA or other autoantibodies.[8] Authors also suggested what starts as limited vasculitis might evolve to a more generalized type of vasculitis,[8] although this did not occur in our patient after a follow-up for several years.

Furthermore, relapses can occur, with a different radiological picture, which may present a new diagnostic dilemma to the clinician. In such situation, it is prudent to determine whether the process is a genuine relapse, or another diagnosis such as an opportunistic infection secondary to immunosuppressive medications, malignancy, etc., Another cycle of investigations might be repeated to exclude such entities, which was the case in our patient.

The main limitation of our observations is the fact that is based on a single case report. However, this is because of the extreme rarity of this condition and we hope that this report will alert researchers to recognize and report similar cases in the future.

Conclusion

Pulmonary disease in HP requires a thorough exclusion of several disorders by means of imaging, serology, tissue sampling, and multidisciplinary collaboration. A diagnostic algorithmic approach is recommended to facilitate this process, and close follow-up is essential to monitor progress and detect relapses. Few cases remain idiopathic, but the similarities in the pathological process in both the central nervous and the pulmonary systems, and the response to immunosuppressive therapy suggest a systemic immune-mediated disorder that deserves further research.
