Association Between Plasma Omega-3 and Omega-6 Fatty Acid Levels and Atrial Fibrillation: A Large-Scale, Real-World Retrospective Study in Japan
Authors: Naotake Yanagisawa, Kazutoshi Fujibayashi, Tetsuro Miyazaki, Hiroyuki Daida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim: The relationship between omega-3 and omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and atrial fibrillation (AF) remains unclear. This retrospective study aimed to investigate the association between plasma levels of omega-3 PUFAs (eicosapentaenoic acid [EPA], docosahexaenoic acid [DHA]) and omega-6 PUFAs (arachidonic acid [AA] and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid [DGLA]) and the prevalence of AF. Methods: After excluding patients who were prescribed icosapent ethyl (IPE) or were younger than 40 years, data from 9,178 patients at Juntendo University Hospital with plasma EPA, DHA, AA, and DGLA measurements obtained between April 2017 and January 2021 were analyzed. The plasma PUFA levels were divided into quartiles, with the lowest quartile used as the reference. Multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to assess the association between the PUFA levels and AF. Results: The median age was 69 years (interquartile range [IQR], 59–77 years), and 60.6% of the participants were male. AF was diagnosed in 908 (9.9%) patients. After adjusting for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), higher plasma levels of EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, AA, and DGLA were significantly associated with a lower risk of AF (allP＜0.001). In contrast, no significant associations were observed between the PUFA ratios, including (EPA+DHA)/AA, EPA/AA, DHA/AA, and DGLA/AA, and AF. Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed linear inverse associations for EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, and AA and AF, whereas a nonlinear association for DGLA and AF was observed. Conclusion: Within the physiological ranges, higher plasma levels of EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, AA, and DGLA were independently and inversely associated with AF risk. However, extremely high EPA concentrations, achieved through pharmacological interventions, have been suggested to be associated with an increased risk of AF.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common type of chronic arrhythmia, and its symptoms include palpitations, shortness of breath, chest pain, a sense of near syncope, reduced exercise tolerance, and fatigue. However, approximately 10%–40% of such patients are asymptomatic 1) . The incidence of AF increases with age, and it is estimated that approximately 9% of adults aged ≥ 65 years are affected 2) . Several animal studies have reported that long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) have antiarrhythmic properties 3 - 5) . In contrast, human observational studies 6 - 10) and randomized controlled trials 11 - 14) have shown inconsistent results. However, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials on omega-3 PUFA supplementation reported that the risk of AF was associated with the supplementation dose, with trials using ＞1 g of omega-3 PUFA showing a higher risk 15) .

Aim

This study aimed to investigate the potential associations between plasma omega-3 and omega-6 PUFAs and AF in a real-world, hospital-based population.

Methods

Patients and Outcome

The study population included all outpatients and inpatients who visited Juntendo University Hospital between April 2017 and January 2021 and had recorded measurements of four plasma fatty acids (eicosapentaenoic acid [EPA], docosahexaenoic acid [DHA], arachidonic acid [AA], and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid [DGLA]); the total number of patients was 10,073. Measurements of these fatty acids were performed for patients with dyslipidemia, atherosclerotic disease, or suspected atherosclerosis, for screening or follow-up purposes.

As the present study aimed to evaluate the association between the baseline plasma FA levels and AF, patients who had been prescribed icosapent ethyl or other omega-3 FA ethyl esters (n = 293) were excluded. Furthermore, as the incidence of AF increases with age and younger adults may develop AF due to different factors, such as genetic predisposition or structural heart disease, including congenital defects, and because only 13 cases of AF occurred among patients aged ＜40 years (n = 602) in this cohort, these patients were excluded from the analysis because of the potential impact on the validity of the study’s interpretations. Consequently, a total of 9,178 patients were included in the final analysis.

The presence or absence of AF was ascertained solely based on the disease name (ICD-10 code) documented in the patient’s electronic medical records up to January 2021.

Measurements

The measurement parameters evaluated in this study included patient age (at the date of examination), sex, body mass index (BMI), and fatty acid fractions (EPA, DHA, AA, and DGLA). PUFA fractions were measured in plasma samples using gas chromatography. The sum of EPA and DHA was combined as the EPA+DHA fraction, and the ratio of these PUFAs to AA was also calculated.

Statistical Analysis

For baseline characteristics, continuous variables are presented as medians and first and third quartiles, whereas categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Data for all participants were summarized, including the presence or absence of AF. The relationship between AF and each PUFA fraction was analyzed using multiple logistic regression, in which the PUFA fractions were categorized based on quartiles, with the lowest quartile used as the reference. Prior to the main analysis, we assessed whether age modified the association between plasma PUFA levels and AF. No significant interaction was found. Therefore, the interaction term was not included in the model. The odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated, and the p-value for the trend was calculated. Three models were analyzed in this study. Model 1 included only PUFA groups. Model 2 included sex and age as covariates and Model 3 included BMI in addition to the covariates in Model 2. For BMI, 3,249 missing values were imputed using multiple imputations. To examine whether there was a nonlinear relationship between the PUFA concentration and AF risk, restricted cubic spline logistic regression was performed with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the PUFA distribution. The nonlinearity of the relationship between PUFA fractions and AF was examined by comparing a model with only linear terms to a model with both linear and nonlinear terms using a likelihood ratio test. In this study, a significance level of 5% (two-tailed) was used, and p-values of ＜0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software program (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 10,073 patients met the inclusion criteria; after exclusion, 9,178 patients were included in the analysis. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1 . The patients had a median age of 69 years (interquartile range: 59–77 years) and a sex distribution of 5,565 males (60.6%) and 3,613 females (39.4%). The median plasma concentrations of EPA, DHA, AA, and DGLA in the entire cohort were 52.9 (interquartile range [IQR]: 33.5–81.1) for EPA, 122.4 (IQR: 96.5–152.2) for DHA, 190.2 (IQR: 158.3–225.6) for AA, and 33.2 (IQR:25.3–42.2) for DGLA.

A total of 908 participants (9.9%) had AF (AF group) and 8,270 did not (non-AF group). The patients in the AF group were older (non-AF: 69 years vs. AF: 71 years, p＜0.001) and had a higher proportion of males (non-AF: 59.8% vs. AF: 68.6%, p＜0.001). Regarding the levels of individual PUFAs, EPA (non-AF: 53.2 vs. AF: 49.3, p＜0.001), DHA (non-AF: 123.2 vs. AF: 116.5, p＜0.001), AA (non-AF: 191.5 vs. AF: 176.2, p＜0.001), and DGLA (non-AF: 33.5 vs. AF: 31.3, p＜0.001) were all significantly lower in the AF group. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the EPA/AA, DHA/AA, or other PUFA ratios.

Association of the Plasma PUFA with the Risk of AF

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the plasma PUFAs and AF. The results are summarized in Table 2 . Three models were compared in the analysis. Model 1 included only PUFAs; Model 2 included sex and age as covariates in Model 1, and Model 3 included BMI as a covariate in Model 2. For each PUFA (EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, AA, and DGLA), an increase in the plasma PUFA levels was associated with a reduction in the prevalence of AF in all models. In contrast, no statistically significant associations were found between the (EPA+DHA) to AA ratio, EPA to AA ratio, DHA to AA ratio, or DGLA to AA ratio. Furthermore, to investigate whether there is a potentially nonlinear relationship between the plasma PUFAs and the AF risk, restricted cubic spline curves were plotted for each PUFA fraction ( Fig.1 ) . No statistically significant nonlinear relationships were observed for EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA or AA. However, for DGLA, a nonlinear relationship was observed, with a reversal of risk occurring when the plasma concentration reached 40 µg/mL ( Fig.1E ) .

Discussion

This study showed that higher plasma levels of EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, AA, and DGLA were associated with a lower prevalence of AF. Furthermore, a restricted cubic spline analysis showed that EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, and AA had a linear association with AF, whereas DGLA showed a non-linear relationship. The results of the present study were generally consistent with those of previous cohort studies, showing that the omega-3 PUFA concentrations are inversely associated with AF. For example, a meta-analysis of 17 prospective cohort studies reported that higher blood concentrations of docosapentaenoic acid (DPA), DHA, and EPA+DHA were associated with a 7–11% reduction in AF risk 2) . Similarly, the Cardiovascular Health Study observed that higher levels of omega-3 and DHA were significantly associated with a lower risk of AF 16) . However, the magnitude and significance of these associations have not been entirely consistent across studies. For example, our findings showed a robust linear inverse association for EPA, which contrasts with some previous reports that found no significant reduction for EPA alone 2 , 10 , 16) . Regarding omega-6 PUFAs, we identified a significant inverse linear association for AA and a non-linear inverse association for DGLA. While a large meta-analysis 17) and a Finnish cohort 18) reported no significant associations for AA or DGLA, our results align with the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis 19) and a Japanese retrospective study 20) that reported inverse trends for these fatty acids. The mechanisms underlying these inconsistent findings remain to be elucidated. However, several multifactorial elements may be involved. First, the protective effects of EPA and omega-6 PUFAs may be more pronounced in specific populations, such as the Japanese, who have a high baseline fish intake, or within particular physiological concentration ranges. Second, methodological differences, including the use of plasma versus erythrocyte membrane measurements, may influence the strength of the observed associations as intraindividual variability and correlations differ by fatty acid type 21 - 24) . Overall, these inconsistencies likely reflect a complex interplay among ethnic background, dietary habits, fatty acid measurement methods, and residual confounding.

A critical point of discussion is the divergence between observational studies and recent large-scale randomized controlled trials 12 - 14) . While our cohort showed a protective association, multiple RCTs and meta-analyses 15) have reported that high-dose EPA supplementation significantly increases AF risk in a dose-dependent manner. The reasons for this divergence between observational cohort studies and some randomized trials are still a subject of active debate. However, a complex array of factors likely contributes to these discrepancies. The biological effects of omega-3 fatty acids appear to depend on both dose and physiological context. At dietary or physiological levels, EPA is gradually incorporated into cardiomyocyte membranes, where it may stabilize the ion channel activity and suppress triggered arrhythmias 25 , 26) . In contrast, high-dose pharmacologic EPA can rapidly elevate circulating free fatty acid levels 27) , potentially altering ion channel dynamics and shortening action potential duration—changes that may facilitate reentry-based arrhythmias 26) . This dose-dependent duality may partly explain the divergent findings across studies. Recent evidence suggests that omega-3 fatty acids incorporate into the myocardial membrane and increase its fluidity, which in turn modulates the activity of mechanosensitive ion channels, such as piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel protein 1 (PIEZO1) 28) . In particular, a DHA-dominant environment may lead to sustained PIEZO1 activation, thereby promoting calcium influx and atrial fibrosis, which could contribute to the induction and maintenance of AF. Furthermore, the impact of omega-3 fatty acids on the autonomic nervous system may follow a similar dose-dependent pattern. High doses have been reported to increase vagal tone in a dose-dependent manner 29) . While low-level vagal stimulation can be cardioprotective and reduce AF risk, excessive vagal activation at high doses may increase susceptibility to AF. The discrepancies may also reflect differences in study populations. Randomized trials often enroll high-risk patients receiving intensive medical therapy. For instance, in statin-treated individuals, reduced membrane cholesterol combined with rapid high-dose EPA incorporation may exacerbate electrical instability. Collectively, a U-shaped association between the omega-3 fatty acid intake and AF risk has been proposed 7 , 28 , 30) , with intermediate doses associated with a lower risk and very low or high doses associated with an increased risk. Because prescription EPA users were excluded from our study, circulating omega-3 levels likely reflected habitual dietary intake within a low-to-moderate range. The inverse association observed in our cohort may therefore represent a reduced risk of AF at higher circulating omega-3 concentrations within this physiological range.

One of the main strengths of this study is its large sample size, which improved the reliability and validity of the results. With almost 9,000 participants, the findings are more likely to be generalizable and reflect real-world conditions. In addition, the use of real-world clinical data from Japan provides practical insights that can be applied to local healthcare settings.

One limitation of this study was its retrospective design, which prevented the establishment of causal relationships. Although associations were observed, causality remained uncertain. Another limitation is the reliance on ICD codes for diagnosing AF, which may introduce some misclassification bias and affect the accuracy of the results. Additionally, this study was adjusted for only age, sex, and BMI and did not account for other potential confounders, including prior cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, genetic predisposition, and lifestyle factors. Finally, some missing data could have affected the results, even after using multiple imputation methods.

Conclusion

This study found a negative correlation between the physiological plasma concentrations of EPA, DHA, EPA+DHA, AA, and DGLA and the prevalence of AF, suggesting that higher levels of these fatty acids within this range may lower the risk of AF. However, it should be noted that while these baseline ranges appear to be protective, extremely high EPA concentrations—such as those achieved through pharmacological interventions—may paradoxically induce AF. This implies that omega-3 and omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids may play a nuanced role in AF prevention depending on their concentration, warranting further research on their mechanisms and clinical implications. Further investigation through prospective cohort studies and large-scale RCTs is required to confirm these findings and to determine the optimal therapeutic ranges.
