Insights From the UK on Fibromuscular Dysplasia in Pregnancy
Authors: Sharmilee Rengarajan, Henry HL Wu, Rajkumar Chinnadurai, Jenny Myers, Edward Lake, Aine de Bhailis, Philip Thomas, Amit Herwadkar, Constantina Chrysochou
Published: 2025

Introduction

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a rare vascular disease which leads to small-to-medium sized arterial stenosis, and commonly aneurysms, dissections, and tortuosity.1 The most common symptom of FMD is hypertension.2,3 Although the exact prevalence of FMD is not well-established, FMD is estimated to be present in approximately 0.4% of the adult population, with an average age of diagnosis of 30 years (focal) and 43 to 52 years (multifocal).1,3 Given that FMD predominantly affects women of childbearing age, there is an urgent need to understand its implications on pregnancy. Data relating to pregnancy in women with FMD remain limited at present because of the rarity of the condition. Pregnancy outcomes in FMD are strongly determined by the extent of vascular involvement, proteinuria, and control of blood pressure.4,5 The presence of FMD should not be regarded as a contraindication to pregnancy because women with FMD can still have successful pregnancies with appropriate monitoring and management.4,5

Here, we detail three selected cases with educational value from our nationwide cohort of patients referred to the Salford Royal Hospital-based UK national FMD clinic, to highlight the nuances within this topic. We also present retrospective observational data in relation to pregnancy outcomes within our cohort of patients from the UK national FMD clinic over > 5 years (Discussion, Table S1 and S2).

Case Presentations

Three Case Studies From the UK National FMD Clinic Study Cohort

Case 1

The first case is a 28-year-old who was initially noted to be hypertensive while on oral contraceptive pill at the age of 18 years, necessitating discontinuation. At age 28, she was found to be hypertensive during pregnancy and required a caesarean delivery at 33 weeks because of worsening hypertension. Subsequent investigations revealed a smaller right kidney (8.1 cm (right kidney) versus 11.8 cm (left kidney)) and right renal artery multifocal FMD. Her body mass index was noted to be 30 kg/m2. Considering that she required 3 agents for blood pressure control and mild left ventricular impairment was observed in the echocardiogram, she underwent right renal artery angioplasty at the age of 31 years, which achieved an excellent effect with cessation of all 3 antihypertensive medications.

The patient underwent prepregnancy counselling and subsequent surveillance scans to assist with pregnancy planning. The previously-treated focal area of FMD in the proximal renal artery had remained widely patent (Figure 1a). There was a subtly presenting new occurrence of multifocal FMD at the right distal artery, which was not considered to be of clinical significance. The left renal artery appeared normal. The patient has gone on to have another successful pregnancy at the age of 33 years, with no events of hypertension and normal intrauterine growth observed on scans throughout pregnancy.

Case 2

The second case is a 21-year-old woman with a history of smoking who presented to the clinic with hypertension, and subsequently found to have tight focal right renal artery stenosis and FMD. Features of left ventricular hypertrophy were noted on echocardiogram. The patient underwent angioplasty to excellent effect and her antihypertensive medications were discontinued. She subsequently underwent three further angioplasty procedures on the right renal artery (Figure 1b). The third angioplasty procedure was complicated by renal artery dissection, which was managed conservatively. A fourth renal angioplasty was unfortunately complicated by renal artery trauma requiring right nephrectomy. She remained at chronic kidney disease stage G3 following this event; however, her blood pressure was well-controlled with no new FMD-associated manifestations on the unaffected left renal artery.

The patient became pregnant at the age of 28 years. Her blood pressure remained in normal ranges throughout her entire pregnancy with fetal growth restriction observed on her scans. However, she experienced severe antepartum hemorrhage at 29 weeks of pregnancy because of a low-lying placenta, requiring preterm birth; a complication not considered to be linked to her FMD. Fortunately, both the patient and her baby have been clinically stable following delivery. The patient’s blood pressure remained stable at < 140/90 mm Hg, she did not require antihypertensive medications to control her blood pressure, and her estimated glomerular filtration rate remained stable at 56 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at her last follow-up appointment.

Case 3

The third case was a patient with bilateral renal artery FMD diagnosed following a diagnosis of severe early onset preeclampsia during her first pregnancy at the age of 22 years, which resulted in a stillbirth at 23 weeks of pregnancy. The patient was counselled about having a renal artery angioplasty because of severe left renal artery stenosis with mid and distal right renal artery beading; however, she subsequently presented to the obstetric services at 6 weeks of gestation during her second pregnancy before undergoing this procedure. Cerebrovascular FMD was excluded. Her blood pressure remained uncontrolled despite escalation to three antihypertensive agents. Following an FMD multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient underwent a “rescue” left renal artery angioplasty at 20 weeks of gestation, which resulted in an excellent clinical response (Figure 1c). Following close maternal and fetal surveillance, a caesarean delivery was performed at 30 weeks indicated by fetal growth restriction with abnormal Dopplers; maternal blood pressure remained below treatment thresholds. Recovery for the patient and the outcome for her baby were uncomplicated. At 9 months postpartum, the patient’s blood pressure was well-controlled at 118/77 mm Hg without the need for prescription of antihypertensive therapy, and her estimated glomerular filtration rate was > 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2.

Discussion

The largest report to date describing pregnancy outcomes in FMD populations is from the European-International FMD registry.4 Data from 534 pregnancies in 237 patients showed that 40% of women experienced pregnancy-related complications, the most frequently observed complications being gestational hypertension (25%) and preterm birth (20%), whereas preeclampsia was reported in 7.5% of patients. In this study, 1 patient experienced arterial dissection and another patient had an aneurysm rupture. When compared with patients without pregnancy-related complications, patients with complicated pregnancies were younger at the time point of FMD diagnosis and had a lower prevalence of cerebrovascular FMD (30% vs. 52%; P = 0.003); however, they were more likely to undergo renal revascularization (63% vs. 40%, P < 0.001). These findings emphasize the need to screen hypertensive women for FMD to ensure revascularization before pregnancy if indicated, and to ensure regular follow-up during pregnancy. Screening for cerebrovascular FMD (i.e., carotid and cerebral) has the added benefit of risk assessment for any underlying aneurysms.6 The risk of cerebrovascular complications may be superadded by a hypercoaguable state resulting from pregnancy, in addition to aneurysm growth or dissection because of gestational hypertension provoked by FMD.7

Data from our cohort displayed similar observations to the findings from the European-International FMD registry. Notably that in 185 pregnancies in 83 women, 96% of cases of FMD in childbearing women were only detected postpregnancy (Table S1 and S2). Patients with complicated pregnancies were younger at the time point of FMD diagnosis (40 vs. 53 years; P < 0.001), and more so the time point of symptoms suspicious for FMD (39 vs. 53 years; P < 0.001) (Table S3). The adverse outcomes noted during or postpregnancy across both studies indicate the importance of prepregnancy screening and diagnosis of FMD. A prepregnancy FMD diagnosis would enable prepregnancy counselling, an individualized birth plan and coordinated multidisciplinary involvement of care. Elective prepregnancy angioplasties can result in a safe and event-free pregnancy, as highlighted from Case 1. In Case 3, we report, to the best of our knowledge, the only case in the literature to date of “rescue” angioplasty in an FMD scenario which resulted in a preterm but healthy birth. Further investigations to optimize the safety of intrapregnancy angioplasty as a bail-out treatment option is needed. Close postpartum monitoring of patients with FMD is required, because the risks of vascular complications such as arterial dissections or aneurysms remain elevated in these patients.

In Table 1,8,9 we summarize teaching points relating to the care of patients with FMD in the context of pregnancy. Although stringent monitoring is indicated, it should be emphasized nevertheless, considering that both the European-International FMD registry data and findings from our cohort demonstrated a low incidence of preeclampsia and arterial complications during pregnancy in the FMD cohort compared with the general population, that pregnancy remains a safe option for this patient group.

Conclusion

Based on the current evidence, it is reasonable to suggest that women of childbearing age with acute onset or chronic hypertension without traditional risk factors (i.e., age ≤ 35 years, normal body mass index, left ventricular hypertrophy identified on echocardiography, have been taking > 2 antihypertensive medications) should be screened for renal artery FMD. Early diagnosis and timely angioplasty on an individual risk-benefit basis before subsequent pregnancies would be important measures to significantly reduce the prevalence of pregnancy-related complications in women with FMD. Furthermore, a known diagnosis of FMD may allow earlier identification of vascular complications arising peri-partum. Bail-out antepartum angioplasty can be considered in specific cases with careful risk-benefit analysis.
