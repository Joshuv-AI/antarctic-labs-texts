Can digital-green technology integration reduce corporate energy consumption? - Empirical evidence from Chinese patent data
Authors: Jie Wu, Zhiqing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBased on the sample of Chinese A-share listed companies from 2012 to 2023, this paper identifies digital-green technology integration (DGTI) patents by matching the IPC codes from the Key Digital Technology Patent Classification System (2023) and the Green Technology Patent Classification System. It explores the direct impact and indirect mechanisms of DGTI on corporate energy consumption (CEC). The findings indicate: (1) DGTI significantly reduces CEC, and this conclusion remains robust after a series of robustness tests. (2) DGTI reduces CEC by improving the efficiency of capital and labor allocation. Furthermore, environmental uncertainty mitigates the inhibitory effect of DGTI on CEC. (3) Heterogeneity analysis shows that the inhibitory effect of DGTI is stronger in regions with advanced digital infrastructure and higher carbon intensity, and is more pronounced in high-tech and non-heavily polluting industries, as well as among firms with lower ESG performance and higher digitalization levels.

Introduction

Global climate change, energy scarcity, and rising costs have made the reduction of corporate energy consumption (CEC) a central pillar of low-carbon transformation. In China, enterprises consume over 70% of national energy and therefore constitute the primary arena for achieving the “Dual Carbon” goals. The government has responded with decisive policy measures. On 29 May 2024, the State Council released the 2024–2025 Action Plan for Energy Conservation and Carbon Reduction, requiring a 2.5% annual decline in energy intensity per unit of GDP and mandating transformative upgrades in high-energy sectors including steel, petrochemicals, and building materials. The 14th Five-Year Plan (2021–2025) further sets a binding 13.5% energy-intensity reduction target relative to 2020 levels, while emphasizing efficiency improvement as a key component of industrial restructuring and high-quality economic growth. Despite these advances, firms still face persistent barriers in technology adoption, resource reconfiguration, and compliance, which highlights the urgent need for innovative drivers capable of achieving deep and cost-effective reductions in energy consumption.1,2

Digital-green technology integration (DGTI) is defined as the synergistic fusion of digital technologies with green technologies to advance sustainable outcomes, primarily by enhancing energy efficiency, environmental governance, and eco-innovation.3 The Implementation Guidelines for Coordinated Digitalization and Greenization Transformation (2024), issued by the Central Cyberspace Affairs Commission, explicitly promotes DGTI to accelerate industrial energy efficiency and low-carbon upgrading. DGTI has a significant impact on CEC through three primary channels. First, digital tools enable real-time monitoring of energy flows and predictive analytics, allowing firms to identify high-consumption nodes and guide the targeted deployment of green technologies.4 Second, DGTI accelerates energy-saving innovation by lowering R&D costs and risks through digital simulation, knowledge sharing, and rapid technology diffusion. Third, it establishes intelligent, data-driven energy management systems that shift from reactive control to proactive, whole-process optimization, achieving sustained and substantial consumption reduction.5

The existing literature primarily focuses on the impact of either digital technology or green technology on corporate performance or environmental outcomes. On one hand, scholars argue that digital transformation can significantly enhance the quantity and quality of corporate green technology innovations,6,7,8 improve green total factor productivity,9 and promote green development through mechanisms such as optimizing resource allocation and alleviating financing constraints.1,10 On the other hand, current research also deeply explores the drivers of corporate sustainable development performance, such as policy, market, and internal capabilities.11,12,13 However, most of these studies treat digital and green technologies as separate, independent dimensions, lacking attention to their deep synergy and integration. Although most studies have historically treated digital and green technologies as distinct dimensions, a nascent but growing stream of work has begun to investigate their coupling or synergistic effects, particularly in reducing regional carbon emissions and improving corporate environmental performance.14,15 Nevertheless, systematic firm-level evidence on the direct impact of DGTI on CEC reduction, along with a comprehensive exploration of its underlying mechanisms, remains limited. Such an analysis will not only enrich the theory of corporate innovation in the context of the digital economy but also provide practical guidance for enterprises in formulating DGTI strategies and for governments in optimizing relevant policies.

Using a sample of Chinese A-share listed companies from 2012 to 2023 and identifying firm-level DGTI through patent IPC co-classification, this study empirically examines the direct effect and indirect pathways of DGTI on CEC. The marginal contributions are 3-fold: First, rather than treating digital and green technologies as independent innovation domains, this study provides firm-level evidence on how their technological integration influences CEC reduction, thereby extending the emerging literature on digital-green coupling. Second, this study constructs a refined patent-based measure of DGTI by systematically matching digital and green IPC classifications using the latest official patent classification systems, improving upon existing proxy-based approaches such as keyword counts or indirect indicators. Third, by examining the mediating roles of capital and labor allocation efficiency and the moderating effect of environmental uncertainty (EU), this paper extends the mechanistic understanding of how DGTI translates into energy-saving outcomes under different organizational and environmental conditions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature on DGTI and CEC. Section 3 develops the theoretical analysis and proposes the research hypotheses. Section 4 describes the research design, including the econometric models, variable definitions, and data sources. Section 5 presents the empirical results, including baseline regression analysis, robustness checks, mechanism tests, and heterogeneity analyses. Section 6 concludes the paper and discusses the main findings, theoretical contributions, practical implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Results

Literature review

Digital-green technology integration

While some studies explore the role of DGTI in improving corporate environmental performance and optimizing urban resource management,3 research remains limited. Therefore, this review draws on broader literature examining how digital technology drives green development, focusing on three main areas: the promotion of green innovation, the improvement of environmental performance, and the underlying mechanisms of transformation.

First, corporate digital transformation serves as a critical catalyst for green technology innovation. It significantly boosts the quantity, quality, and overall performance of corporate green innovations and facilitates the inter-firm diffusion and transfer of green technologies.6,7,8,16 From a supply chain view, downstream customers’ digital transformation can also drive upstream suppliers’ green innovation through both compulsion effects and knowledge spillovers.17

Second, digitalization plays a crucial role in achieving tangible environmental outcomes. Research indicates that a high degree of DGTI has a significant positive impact on corporate environmental performance, helping firms more effectively respond to environmental pressures from stakeholders.3 At a macro level, the development of the digital economy is a key driver of urban green total factor productivity.9 Furthermore, digital technology coupled with environmental regulations boosts urban green efficiency,5 while digital-green innovation synergy drives regional carbon reduction.15

Finally, scholars have extensively investigated the internal mechanisms through which digital technologies facilitate green development. The literature highlights several key pathways. One primary mechanism is resource allocation optimization. Digitalization alleviates financing constraints for green projects, restructures labor by integrating digital skills, and directs capital toward R&D and digital infrastructure.1,10 Another important pathway is the facilitation of knowledge and information flows, whereby digital platforms enhance internal knowledge sharing for green innovation and mitigate information asymmetry in the market.15,18 A third mechanism involves promoting industrial structure upgrading, guiding industries toward cleaner and more efficient models, thereby serving as an important channel for improving green productivity.10

Corporate energy consumption

CEC is critical for global sustainability, with recent literature exploring its determinants across three core dimensions: external institutional factors, technological factors, and corporate governance characteristics.

First, external institutional factors are powerful external drivers. Government climate risk perception reduces corporate fossil energy consumption and intensity via strengthened environmental regulation and higher-quality energy shifts.19 Regulatory policies such as corporate average fuel economy standards aim to promote energy conservation, but their effectiveness is complicated by factors such as the rebound effect.20 Green finance regulation enhances corporate renewable energy utilization by easing financing constraints and promoting corporate digitalization.21 In contrast, climate risk presents a more complex and nonlinear challenge, showing an inverted N-shaped relationship with CEC that is moderated by green technology innovation.7

Second, technological factors serve as important drivers of improvements in corporate energy efficiency. Digital empowerment boosts corporate energy efficiency by fostering specialization, labor division, and improving supply chain coordination.22 Similarly, supply chain digitalization promotes energy efficiency by facilitating the quantity, quality, and persistence of green technology innovations.14 AI has a dual effect: it can reduce energy consumption via technological innovation and digital transformation,23,24 but its energy-intensive nature means its positive impact depends on factors such as human capital levels. Specifically, low human capital may lead AI to increase CEC, and this effect is weakened (but not eliminated) when human capital exceeds a certain threshold.25

Third, corporate governance characteristics play a moderating role. Board gender diversity correlates positively with renewable energy consumption, in line with the critical mass theory that requires two or more women for a significant impact.26 Similarly, female executives promote lower energy consumption intensity by stimulating green innovation.27 Firm-level environmental governance is also crucial: It weakens the link between total energy consumption and carbon emissions, and strengthens the positive effect of clean energy adoption.28

Despite these advances, several important gaps remain in the literature. First, although recent studies have begun to examine the interaction between digital technologies and green development, systematic research on DGTI remains limited. Most studies analyze digital transformation or green innovation separately, while relatively few consider their synergistic integration at the firm level.

Second, research on CEC has largely focused on institutional factors, technological progress, and corporate governance. However, little attention has been paid to how the joint application of digital and green technologies shapes firms’ energy use.

Third, the mechanisms through which DGTI affects CEC remain insufficiently explored, particularly from the perspective of resource allocation efficiency. Moreover, the moderating role of EU in this relationship has received limited scholarly attention.

Theoretical analysis and hypotheses

Digital-green technology integration and corporate energy consumption

From the resource-based view (RBV), a firm’s sustained competitive advantage arises from the strategic orchestration of valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (VRIN) resources.29 DGTI enables firms to reconfigure technological and operational resources under dual digital and green transformation pressures, thereby achieving substantial reductions in energy consumption.30 Specifically, digital technologies provide data processing, connectivity, and decision-support capabilities, while green technologies focus on energy efficiency and clean production; their integration allows firms to embed environmental objectives directly into digitally enabled operational systems, generating synergistic efficiency gains.

First, DGTI reduces energy consumption by optimizing production processes and resource allocation. Digital technologies such as IoT, big data analytics, and AI-driven control systems enable real-time monitoring and precise identification of energy-intensive segments, eliminating idle running, overheating, and inefficient scheduling.31 Green technologies, including energy-efficient equipment, waste-heat recovery, and renewable energy substitution, replace high-energy inputs and processes, and digital technologies further enhance their effectiveness through real-time monitoring and adaptive optimization, creating a multiplicative effect that significantly lowers total energy consumption.32

Second, DGTI accelerates the development and adoption of energy-saving innovations through enhanced dynamic capabilities.33 Digital simulation, machine learning, and collaborative platforms reduce the time and cost of developing low-energy materials and processes, while digital knowledge networks facilitate rapid diffusion and recombination of energy-saving technologies across organizational boundaries.34,35 Through this interaction, digital technologies improve the efficiency of green R&D and experimentation, while green technologies provide concrete application scenarios for digital solutions, embedding lower energy consumption into core technological capabilities.

Third, DGTI supports the establishment of intelligent, data-driven energy management systems that institutionalize energy consumption reduction. By integrating digital twins, cloud computing, and blockchain, firms achieve full-lifecycle, real-time tracking and predictive control of energy consumption across the value chain.36 When combined with green technologies such as smart energy storage and automated demand-response systems, digital platforms enable coordinated optimization of energy generation, storage, and consumption, achieving systematic and sustained reductions in energy use. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1: DGTI significantly reduces CEC.

The mediating role of resource allocation efficiency

Resource allocation efficiency serves as a pivotal mediator linking firms’ internal resource bases to reduced energy consumption, embodying the core mechanism for operational optimization under dual digital and green transformation pressures. Agency theory highlights that ownership-control separation exacerbates information asymmetries and goal divergences, inflating agency costs and distorting resource deployment.37 DGTI mitigates these frictions through data-driven decision-making, transparent oversight, and intelligent scheduling, thereby channeling resources toward energy-conserving activities.

Importantly, improved allocation efficiency reflects a structural optimization of capital and labor rather than a simple reduction in inputs. By curbing inefficient allocations such as over-investment and excess employment, DGTI releases resources that can be redirected toward energy-efficient technologies, green innovation, and intelligent energy management.

The influence of DGTI on capital allocation efficiency manifests in dual pathways, both converging on energy consumption abatement. First, DGTI bolsters the analytical precision of energy-efficient investment selections. Digital tools aggregate financial metrics, policy signals, and real-time energy profiles to appraise green initiatives’ viability, forecasting returns while preempting risks from informational gaps.38 Green technologies furnish granular inputs on energy footprints and emission profiles,39 which digital analytics convert into predictive models, directing capital toward low-consumption projects and curtailing wasteful expenditures.

Second, DGTI fortifies capital deployment monitoring and hazard mitigation. Blockchain and IoT integrations yield immutable, traceable ledgers of fund trajectories, while cloud platforms enable visualized, real-time audits of energy project execution.40 This system reduces oversight costs and prevents opportunistic deviations, avoiding misallocations that increase energy use. Machine learning further refines allocations by iteratively reweighting investments based on consumption data, embedding adaptive, energy-minimizing protocols that sustain long-term reductions.

Thus, the reduction in over-investment indicates that firms avoid inefficient or energy-intensive projects and reallocate capital toward energy-saving equipment, green technologies, and environmental R&D, thereby improving energy efficiency rather than simply reducing investment levels.

Similarly, DGTI’s effects on labor allocation efficiency bifurcate, yielding compounded energy savings. Human capital theory underscores optimal workforce deployment as essential for productivity gains.41 DGTI advances this by automating energy-intensive tasks - via robots and AI controls - liberating personnel for high-value roles in green R&D and efficiency analytics, thereby elevating overall energy productivity.42

Concurrently, DGTI refines labor forecasting and alignment. Big data and AI dissect competencies, performance trajectories, and energy-project demands, facilitating precise talent mapping and upskilling.43 Continuous monitoring of skill-energy linkages detects surpluses or deficits, prompting agile reallocations that minimize idle labor and reduce energy overruns. This precision curtails human resource redundancies, channeling expertise toward consumption-lowering innovations and operational refinements.

Accordingly, the reduction in excess employment reflects the elimination of redundant positions in low-productivity segments while reallocating human capital toward energy management, environmental monitoring, and green innovation activities. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H2a: DGTI reduce CEC by optimizing capital allocation efficiency.

H2b: DGTI reduce CEC by optimizing labor allocation efficiency.

The moderating role of environmental uncertainty

EU, encompassing market volatility, technological flux, and policy instability,44 shapes the effectiveness of DGTI in reducing CEC. Contingency theory posits that organizational efficacy hinges on aligning internal capabilities with external exigencies.45 Under heightened uncertainty, firms may face challenges in leveraging DGTI for resource reconfiguration, thereby diminishing reductions in energy consumption due to disrupted adaptive strategies.18

First, EU can undermine the effectiveness of DGTI by complicating informational acuity and decisional agility. Digital technologies aim to capture signals from energy markets, regulatory shifts, and supply disruptions, while green technologies offer low-consumption alternatives such as renewable integrations.4,46 However, in volatile environments, the abundance of noise and unreliable data may hinder this integration, reducing the ability to recalibrate energy procurement and usage in real time, and weakening preemptive efficiency gains.1

Second, EU hinders agile resource allocation, where DGTI’s potency in energy minimization is attenuated. Contingency theory underscores the imperative for supple resource mobilization amid turbulence.45 DGTI aims to streamline capital and labor toward energy-efficient R&D and automated processes, mitigating misallocations that exacerbate waste. Yet under high uncertainty, erratic pivots and unpredictable shifts can lead to misaligned allocations, limiting reductions in energy consumption.35

Third, EU disrupts the imperative for strategic-environmental congruence, limiting DGTI’s simulation prowess. Digital twins and scenario modeling are intended to forecast energy trajectories under flux, integrating green optimizations to refine operational paradigms.47 In uncertain milieus, this alignment may fail due to unpredictable variables, reducing proactive adjustments and diminishing sustained energy savings.19 Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: EU negatively moderates the negative effect of DGTI on CEC.

Variable measurement

Explained variable - corporate energy consumption (CEC)

Following Li et al. (2025) and Lee et al. (2022),14,25 this paper measures CEC from multiple resource categories to assess energy use efficiency and environmental impact. Energy inputs are categorized into water consumption (10,000 tons), electricity consumption (10,000 kWh), coal usage (10,000 tons), natural gas usage (10,000 m3), gasoline usage (10,000 tons), diesel usage (10,000 tons), and district heating (10,000 GJ). Each category captures distinct aspects of operational energy demands, enabling a holistic evaluation of resource utilization.

To derive a unified metric, individual categories are converted to standard coal equivalent (10,000,000 tons) using coefficients from the Energy Statistics Division of the National Bureau of Statistics in the China Energy Statistical Yearbook: water (0.0002429), electricity (1.229), coal (0.7143), natural gas (13.3), gasoline (1.4714), diesel (1.4571), and district heating (0.03412). Finally, the CEC is measured by the total amount of standard coal equivalent, and the specific calculation formula is as follows:(Equation 1)CEC=Water∗0.0002429+Electricity∗1.229+Coal∗0.7143+Naturalgas∗13.3+Gasoline∗1.4714+Diesel∗1.4571+Districtheating∗0.034121

Explanatory variable - digital-green technology integration (DGTI)

Existing research still suffers from significant limitations in measuring DGTI. On one hand, some studies measure digital or green technology in isolation based on patent data,8,47 overlooking the cross-cutting and integration features of the two technologies at the firm level, which makes it difficult to accurately capture the actual degree of synergistic empowerment. More recently, a few studies have attempted to measure the coupling between digital and green technologies. For example, Liu et al. (2025) construct a coupling index between digital and green technology innovation based on the number of digital economy patents and green technology patents,15 while Li et al. (2025) identify digital-green patents by matching digital economy IPC codes with the WIPO green patent list.3

However, these approaches still have limitations. Specifically, the identification of digital technology patents in these studies is largely based on industrial classifications of the digital economy, which mainly reflect industry-level digital activities and may lead to relatively coarse identification of firm-level digital technologies. On the other hand, a few scholars identify digital economy patents by extracting relevant IPC codes based on the Statistical Classification of Digital Economy and Core Industries (2021),3,35 but these patents mainly reflect the number of patents within the core digital economy industries and fail to precisely focus on the firm level.

To improve the accuracy of measurement, this paper systematically identifies DGTI patents at the firm level by cross-matching the Green Technology Patent Classification System and the Key Digital Technology Patent Classification System (2023) issued by the China National Intellectual Property Administration (CNIPA). Compared with existing approaches based on industry-oriented digital economy classifications, these official patent classification systems focus directly on specific technological domains, allowing a more precise identification of both digital and green technologies and their integration at the firm level. The DGTI level is then measured based on these identified patents. The specific measurement procedure is as follows:

First, we download detailed patent data for enterprises from 2012 to 2023 from the CNIPA, including patent titles, abstracts, IPC codes, and application/grant years. We then match the patent applicants with the list of A-share listed companies to form a detailed patent dataset for listed firms.

Second, we extract the IPC codes related to green technology from the Green Technology Patent Classification System and match them with the corporate patent IPC codes to identify green technology patents at the firm level. After screening and cleaning, we obtain approximately 320,000 green technology patent records.

Third, we further extract the IPC codes related to digital technology based on the Key Digital Technology Patent Classification System (2023) and cross-match them with the aforementioned green technology patent IPC codes. A patent is classified as a DGTI patent if its IPC code appears simultaneously in both the green technology and digital technology classification systems. Considering that some patents have multiple IPC codes, we classify a patent as a DGTI patent if any of its IPC codes meets the integration criterion. Ultimately, we identify 77,288 DGTI patents.

Finally, we use the natural logarithm of the annual number of DGTI patent applications by the firm as the measure for the corporate DGTI level. Mediating variable-resource allocation efficiency

Following Ni & Wang (2022),48 we divide corporate resource allocation efficiency into capital allocation efficiency (IE) and labor allocation efficiency (LE).

Capital allocation efficiency (IE)

Capital allocation efficiency is measured using the over-investment indicator based on the investment efficiency model proposed by Richardson (2006).49 Specifically, we first estimate the firm’s expected level of investment by regressing current investment on a set of firm characteristics that capture investment opportunities and financial conditions:(Equation 2)Investi,t=β0+β1Growthi,t-1+β2Levi,t-1+β3Roai,t-1+β4Agei,t-1+β5Sizei,t-1+β6Investi,t-1+∑Indu+∑Year+εi,tIn this model, Investi,t represents the firm’s fixed asset investment scaled by total assets at the beginning of the year. Growthi,t-1 denotes the growth rate of operating revenue, Levi,t-1 is the leverage ratio measured by total liabilities divided by total assets, Roai,t-1 represents return on assets, Agei,t-1 denotes firm age, and Sizei,t-1 is measured by the natural logarithm of total assets. Investi,t-1 captures the persistence of investment behavior, while industry and year fixed effects are included to control for industry-specific and time-specific factors.

The residual term from this regression reflects the deviation between the firm’s actual investment and its expected investment level. When the residual is positive, the firm is considered to engage in over-investment, indicating inefficient capital allocation. Therefore, the over-investment variable (IE) is defined as the positive residual from the above model, while negative residuals are set to zero. A larger IE value indicates lower capital allocation efficiency.

Labor allocation efficiency (LE)

Labor allocation efficiency is measured by excess employment. First, we estimate the firm’s normal employment level based on firm characteristics using the following regression model:(Equation 3)Employeei,t=β0+β1Sizei,t-1+β2Capitali,t-1+β3Growthi,t-1+∑γIndu+∑λYear+εi,tIn this model, Employeei,t represents the firm’s employment scale measured by the ratio of the number of employees to total assets. Sizei,t-1 denotes firm size measured by the natural logarithm of total assets, Capitali,t-1 represents capital intensity measured by the ratio of fixed assets to total assets, and Growthi,t-1 is the growth rate of operating revenue. Industry and year fixed effects are included to control for structural differences across industries and time periods.

Based on this model, the estimated value represents the firm’s normal employment level. Excess employment (LE) is then calculated as the difference between the firm’s actual employment and the estimated normal employment level. A larger LE value indicates a higher degree of excess employment and therefore lower labor allocation efficiency.

Moderating variable - environmental uncertainty (EU)

Following Zhao & Wang (2025) and Liu & Song (2025),50,51 we measure EU using the industry-adjusted coefficient of variation of abnormal firm sales revenue. This indicator is suitable because a firm facing no uncertainty (EU) exhibits stable sales without fluctuations; greater sales volatility directly reflects external unpredictability arising from policy changes, technological developments, and industry competition that affect a firm’s operating environment. The model is constructed as follows(Equation 4)Salei=α0+α1Year+εwhere Year = 1 (four years ago) to Year = 5 (current year). The residuals ε represent abnormal sales fluctuations due to external shocks. EU is then computed as EUi=σ(ε)Salei divided by the industry-year median. Larger values indicate higher EU.

Data sources

This paper selects A-share listed companies in China from 2012 to 2023 as the research sample. The starting year of 2012 is chosen because “digitalization” was first included in the government work report, indicating increased national emphasis on digital development from that year onward. We then screen the sample data, excluding firms with severe missing values, firms classified as ST or PT, and retaining only those in normal trading status. We also exclude firms in the finance, computer, and electronic information industries. The specific data sources are as follows: (1) Listed company lists, daily stock trading data, and financial control variables are sourced from the CSMAR database (http://data.csmar.com). (2) Detailed DGTI patent data are sourced from the CNIPA Patent Search Platform (https://pss-system.cponline.cnipa.gov.cn/conventionalSearch). (3) Energy consumption data are derived from energy consumption information in listed corporate annual reports, social responsibility reports, and investor relations activities (https://www.cninfo.com.cn). The final sample comprises 6,392 “firm-year” observations.

Stylized facts analysis of digital-green technology integration

This section conducts a stylized facts analysis on the annual changes, core technology word cloud, and annual evolution of thematic words for DGTI patents to reveal the characteristics of DGTI.

Annual changes in DGTI patents

Figure 2 illustrates the annual trends in the number of DGTI patent applications and grants from 2012 to 2023. Overall, the number of DGTI patents in China shows a trend of initial increase followed by a decline between 2012 and 2023. The number of applications grew from 3,148 in 2012 to 10,927 in 2020, with an average annual growth rate of approximately 14.7%. The number of grants also rose from 2,701 to 7,784 in 2020. A brief dip in both applications and grants occurred in 2014, possibly due to the incomplete nature of the green technology classification system and policy support at that time. After 2015, driven by national policies promoting green and low-carbon development alongside the digital economy, DGTI activity significantly increased. Particularly between 2016 and 2020, policy incentives, industrial capital investment, and improvements in the R&D environment collectively fueled the rapid growth in applications. However, both applications and grants have shown a marked decline since 2021, dropping to 6,832 and 2,443, respectively, in 2023. This reflects a period of adjustment for DGTI activities. This change may be related to the disruption of R&D and supply chains caused by the COVID-19 pandemic; while the initial phase of the pandemic promoted short-term growth in some digital-green projects, subsequent market uncertainty and cost pressures inhibited sustained corporate investment. Furthermore, stricter patent examination, higher quality requirements for applications, and a shift in corporate innovation strategy from “quantity expansion” to “quality improvement” are also important reasons for the decline in patent grants after 2020. Overall, DGTI exhibits characteristics of phased fluctuation and structural adjustment, influenced by both policy drivers and changes in the economic environment.Figure 2Annual changes in the number of patent applications and grants for the integration of digital green technologies

Word cloud analysis of DGTI

Based on Figures 3 and 4, the high-frequency words for DGTI can be categorized into two thematic clusters: “digital enablement” and “green/low-carbon.” The digital enablement cluster features keywords such as “control,” “storage medium,” “online,” “intelligent,” “automatic,” and “prediction,” focusing on utilizing algorithms, data, and AI technology to achieve real-time sensing and dynamic optimization. The green/low-carbon cluster is represented by terms such as “flue gas desulfurization,” “denitrification,” “catalytic oxidation,” “wet method,” “halogen-free,” “photovoltaic” and “double-glass,” covering three major areas: air pollution control, clean energy, and energy storage/material conservation. Specifically, green scenarios demand high-precision and real-time responses to varying operating conditions. For example, desulfurization and denitrification processes require automatic adjustment of agents and airflow based on coal quality and load, while photovoltaic cell output fluctuates with the environment. Digital technology achieves optimization through the “data-model-computing power” pathway: sensor arrays and edge computing acquire status information, machine learning predicts pollutant concentrations or power curves, and intelligent control algorithms dynamically adjust ammonia injection or maximum power point tracking. This synergy achieves a simultaneous improvement in green performance and economic efficiency. Conversely, green technology also promotes the industrial application of digital technology. Keywords such as “lithium-ion,” “polymer,” and “read-only storage medium,” indicate that battery management systems deeply couple chips, materials, and algorithms, significantly enhancing the stability and reliability of digital systems in industrial settings. “Double-glass modules” and “halogen-free backsheets” optimize the material system, reducing the potential induced degradation effect and providing a highly stable carrier for data collection in photovoltaic power stations. Overall, the innovation trajectory of DGTI is moving from single-point control to systemic synergy, utilizing modularization and digital twin technology to achieve integrated management of source, grid, load, and storage, forming a closed-loop green governance system of “process-algorithm-feedback.” This marks the acceleration of green manufacturing into a new phase of intelligence and systematization.

Annual thematic analysis based on LDA model

To identify the core themes of DGTI and their evolutionary patterns, we apply the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model to analyze the text of corporate DGTI patents from 2012 to 2023. The procedure is as follows: First, patent data are imported, stop words are loaded, and the patent titles and abstracts undergo Chinese word segmentation, including the removal of stop words, punctuation, and low-frequency words to ensure the purity of the corpus and the validity of the analysis. Second, the processed text is divided by year to construct a text feature matrix based on word frequency. The LDA model is then trained separately on the corpus for each year to capture the thematic distribution characteristics over time. In the topic extraction stage, the LDA model is set to 10 topics, and a fixed random seed is used to ensure the reproducibility of the results. After training, the top 10 high-frequency words for each topic are extracted as representative terms. Finally, by comparing the annual thematic words, we analyze the dynamic evolution trend of DGTI.

The specific results are shown in Data S1. From 2012 to 2015, the focus was on photovoltaics (solar energy, battery, backsheet), materials, and exhaust gas treatment (desulfurization, adsorption, purification), reflecting an early stage driven by component and material improvements. From 2016 to 2019, themes related to power electronics and system integration significantly increased, with terms such as energy storage, inverter, charging, power grid, and monitoring/control appearing, indicating a shift in research focus toward grid connection, energy storage, and vehicle energy management. After 2020, the themes gradually shifted toward large-scale manufacturing and intelligent operation and maintenance, highlighting modularization, encapsulation, electrode plates, inspection, monitoring, and predictive maintenance. Simultaneously, the frequency of terms related to recycling and material regeneration increased, suggesting that circular utilization has entered the supply chain focus. The period 2021–2023 further shows the emergence of carbon governance and high-end manufacturing refinement (CO2 capture, fuel cells, electrode manufacturing) alongside system-level innovations such as modularization and robotics. Overall, the thematic evolution reflects a logical progression from breakthroughs in single materials and components to system integration, industrialization, and quality orientation, suggesting the maturation of the technological trajectory from exploratory research toward engineering, commercialization, and circular sustainability.

This suggests that DGTI inhibits corporate CEC by enhancing labor allocation efficiency, enabling firms to more effectively utilize human resources to support green technology innovation and the transition of production models toward less energy-intensive operations, thereby validating hypothesis H2b.

Moderation effect test

To examine the influence of EU on the relationship between DGTI and corporate CEC, we conduct the following moderation effect tests. Column (4) of Table 7 shows that the regression coefficient of DGTI remains significantly negative, confirming its inhibitory effect on CEC. The coefficient of the interaction term between DGTI and EU (DGTI∗EU) is significantly positive, which supports hypothesis H3. This indicates that EU attenuates the inhibitory effect of DGTI on corporate CEC. EU reshapes corporate strategic priorities and resource allocation patterns. Under highly uncertain environments, firms tend to emphasize operational flexibility and risk buffering rather than efficiency optimization, which may weaken the energy-saving effects of digital technologies. Prior research shows that EU can redirect digital transformation toward risk diversification and adaptive responses instead of efficiency improvement.52

High-tech industry heterogeneity

The inhibitory effect of DGTI on CEC is found to be highly significant and robust in the high-tech industry (HIGH_TECH), whereas it is statistically insignificant in the non-high-tech industry. This divergence stems from the congruence between the operational architectures of high-tech industries and the requisites of DGTI. High-tech sectors, predicated on iterative technological paradigms and data-intensive processes, enable the efficacious translation of DGTI into CEC reductions through amplified innovation capacities and systemic resource optimizations. Non-high-tech industries, anchored in conventional operational modalities, encounter elevated assimilation barriers, thereby attenuating the CEC implications of DGTI.

Heavily polluting industry heterogeneity

The results indicate that the inhibitory effect of DGTI on CEC is most significant in non-heavily polluting industry (NON_HIGH_POLLU), but statistically insignificant in heavily polluting industry (HIGH_POLLU). This pattern reflects structural differences in technological adaptability across industries. Non-heavily polluting industries typically possess more flexible production systems, allowing digital technologies to enhance energy efficiency through process optimization and real-time management. In contrast, heavily polluting industries are characterized by capital-intensive infrastructures and strong technological path dependence, where energy efficiency improvements often rely on costly equipment upgrades rather than managerial optimization alone. Consequently, the environmental benefits of DGTI may be constrained in the short term. Similar industry heterogeneity has been documented in prior studies on digitalization and energy efficiency.53,54

ESG performance heterogeneity

The DGTI coefficient is significantly more negative in the low ESG performance (LOW_ESG) group than in the high ESG performance (HIGH_ESG) group. This heterogeneity reflects differences in marginal improvement potential along the ESG continuum. Firms with weaker ESG performance often exhibit deficiencies in environmental management and governance practices. In such contexts, digital technologies can substantially enhance transparency, monitoring capacity, and data-driven environmental management, thereby generating greater marginal improvements in energy efficiency. Conversely, firms with higher ESG performance typically operate more mature sustainability frameworks, which reduces the incremental benefits of DGTI.55

Digitalization level heterogeneity

The inhibitory effect of DGTI on CEC is more significant for firms with a higher digitalization level (HIGH_DIG) than for those with lower digitalization levels (LOW_DIG). This phenomenon derives from the synergistic interplay between antecedent digital proficiencies and the integrative imperatives of DGTI. Firms possessing advanced digitalization endowments, encompassing cohesive informational architectures and proficient human capital, orchestrate DGTI within augmented operational matrices, thereby catalyzing amplified CEC reduction trajectories through sophisticated analytical and adaptive modalities. Firms at rudimentary digitalization stages, conversely, confront allocative trade-offs in infrastructural priming, which constrain the specialized CEC affordances of DGTI and precipitate more attenuated developmental progressions.

Discussion

This paper utilizes a sample of A-share listed companies in China, identifying DGTI based on patent data. We systematically investigate the direct impact of DGTI on CEC and its underlying mechanisms. The main findings are as follows: (1) DGTI significantly reduces CEC. By embedding digital technologies such as big data and AI into energy management and production optimization, firms can effectuate a substantial reduction in resource consumption and improve energy efficiency. (2) DGTI inhibits CEC by enhancing capital allocation efficiency and labor allocation efficiency. DGTI enables firms to more precisely identify and evaluate energy-saving projects, avoiding blind over-investment and optimizing capital utilization. Simultaneously, through automation and intelligent production management, DGTI reduces excess employment, leading to optimal labor allocation. (3) EU attenuates the inhibitory effect of DGTI on CEC. This suggests that in a high-uncertainty environment, the unpredictable nature of the market and policy landscape disrupts the stable operational conditions required for DGTI to fully optimize energy usage. (4) The inhibitory effect of DGTI on CEC exhibits significant heterogeneity across regions and industries. At the regional level, the inhibitory effect of DGTI is more pronounced for firms in regions with high digital infrastructure and high carbon emission intensity. At the industry level, the inhibitory effect is significantly present in both the high-tech industry and non-heavily polluting industry. Furthermore, at the firm level, the inhibitory effect is more pronounced for firms with low ESG performance and those with high digitalization levels.

Theoretical implications

This paper offers several theoretical contributions to the literature: First, this paper enriches the theoretical framework of the RBV and dynamic capability theory by exploring how DGTI reduces CEC. Existing studies have primarily examined RBV and dynamic capabilities in the contexts of digital transformation, green innovation, or environmental performance separately.9,10,15 However, few have systematically investigated how the synergistic integration of digital and green technologies jointly enhances corporate green practices. By incorporating capital allocation efficiency and labor allocation efficiency as mediating mechanisms, and EU as a moderating factor, this paper reveals the internal logic through which DGTI reduces CEC. It provides new insights into how digital-enabled resource reconfiguration and dynamic learning mechanisms foster sustainable competitiveness, thereby extending the explanatory boundaries of RBV and dynamic capability theory in the digital-green integration domain.

Second, this paper advances the methodological framework for measuring DGTI. Existing studies still face limitations in measuring DGTI. On the one hand, some research measures digital or green technologies separately based on patent data,5,8 which overlooks the cross-domain integration between the two technologies and therefore fails to capture their synergistic interaction at the firm level. On the other hand, a few recent studies attempt to measure the coupling between digital and green technologies. Liu et al. (2025) construct a coupling index based on the number of digital economy patents and green technology patents,15 while Li et al. (2025) identify digital-green patents by matching digital economy IPC codes with the WIPO green patent list.3 However, these approaches largely rely on digital economy classifications derived from industry standards, which may lead to relatively coarse identification of firm-level digital technologies. To address this limitation, this paper measures DGTI by cross-matching the “Green Technology Patent Classification System” and the “Key Digital Technology Patent Classification System (2023)” at the IPC level, allowing more precise identification of patents that simultaneously exhibit digital and green technological attributes.

Practical contributions

Based on the above conclusions, this paper proposes the following targeted policy and practical implications: First, governments should strengthen top-level design for digital-green integrated development and accelerate the construction of high-standard digital infrastructure, especially in regions with high carbon emission intensity. For high-tech and heavily polluting industries, special funds and pilot demonstration projects should be established to guide enterprises to embed AI, big data, and IoT technologies into energy management and production processes, achieving precise energy conservation and efficiency improvement. Enterprises, particularly those with high digitalization levels, should increase R&D investment in DGTI, strengthen independent innovation in core digital-green technologies, and build differentiated green competitive advantages through integrated innovation.

Second, policymakers should improve the efficiency of capital and labor allocation by establishing unified green information disclosure standards and digital supervision platforms to reduce information asymmetry and resource misallocation. Regulatory authorities can use blockchain and big data technology to achieve real-time monitoring and dynamic optimization of CEC and resource allocation behavior. Enterprises should fully leverage digital tools to accurately evaluate energy-saving projects, avoid blind investment and excess employment, and form a resource-efficient green operation mechanism.

Finally, in environments of high uncertainty, the government should enhance policy stability and predictability, reduce frequent adjustments in environmental and industrial policies, and create favorable external conditions for the sustained effectiveness of DGTI. At the same time, differentiated support policies should be implemented for different regions and industries: providing stronger fiscal, tax, and credit incentives in high carbon emission intensity regions and high-tech sectors.

Limitations of the study

Despite the valuable findings, this paper has several limitations that provide opportunities for future research. First, the measurement of DGTI is primarily based on patent data, which, although objective and verifiable, may not fully capture the actual implementation and performance outcomes of technological integration within firms. Future research could incorporate full-text patent mining or combine patent data with textual analysis of annual reports regarding digital-green strategic alignment to construct a more multidimensional measure of integration quality. In addition, future studies could incorporate multidimensional data sources - such as green project disclosures, ESG reports, or digital infrastructure investments - to provide a more comprehensive assessment of DGTI and its operational effectiveness.

Second, this paper focuses on Chinese A-share listed enterprises, which may limit the generalizability of the results across different institutional and industrial contexts. The unique policy environment, government intervention, and digital governance mechanisms in China could influence the relationship between DGTI and CEC. Future research could extend the analysis to cross-country or industry-level comparisons to explore the heterogeneous effects of institutional quality, environmental regulation, and market competition on DGTI outcomes.

Third, although this paper identifies mediating and moderating mechanisms, it does not capture the dynamic evolution of these mechanisms over time. Future studies could employ panel vector autoregression, time-varying coefficient models, or machine learning approaches to uncover the dynamic feedback between technological integration and CEC. In addition, future research could further explore the potential diffusion and spillover effects of DGTI across firms, particularly along supply chains or industry networks.56 Moreover, inspired by the multi-factor identification framework proposed by Ahmadi et al. (2025),57 future studies could systematically examine and prioritize multiple candidate mechanisms linking DGTI to CEC, thereby providing a clearer understanding of the relative importance of different channels and constraints.

Method details

Data collection

This paper selects A-share listed companies in China from 2012 to 2023 as the research sample. The starting year of 2012 is chosen because “digitalization” was first included in the government work report, indicating increased national emphasis on digital development from that year onward. We then screen the sample data, excluding firms with severe missing values, firms classified as ST or PT, and retaining only those in normal trading status. We also exclude firms in the finance, computer, and electronic information industries. The specific data sources are as follows: (1) Listed company lists, daily stock trading data, and financial control variables are sourced from the CSMAR database. (2) Detailed DGTI patent data are sourced from the CNIPA Patent Search Platform. (3) Energy consumption data are derived from energy consumption information in listed corporate annual reports, social responsibility reports, and investor relations activities. The final sample comprises 6,392 “firm-year” observations.

Measuring digital-green technology integration (DGTI)

This paper systematically identifies DGTI patents at the firm level by cross-matching the Green Technology Patent Classification System and the Key Digital Technology Patent Classification System (2023) issued by the China National Intellectual Property Administration (CNIPA). Compared with existing approaches based on industry-oriented digital economy classifications, these official patent classification systems focus directly on specific technological domains, allowing a more precise identification of both digital and green technologies and their integration at the firm level. The DGTI level is then measured based on these identified patents. The specific measurement procedure is as follows:

First, we download detailed patent data for enterprises from 2012 to 2023 from the CNIPA, including patent titles, abstracts, IPC codes, and application/grant years. We then match the patent applicants with the list of A-share listed companies to form a detailed patent dataset for listed firms.

Second, we extract the IPC codes related to green technology from the Green Technology Patent Classification System and match them with the corporate patent IPC codes to identify green technology patents at the firm level. After screening and cleaning, we obtain approximately 320,000 green technology patent records.

Third, we further extract the IPC codes related to digital technology based on the Key Digital Technology Patent Classification System (2023) and cross-match them with the aforementioned green technology patent IPC codes. A patent is classified as a DGTI patent if its IPC code appears simultaneously in both the green technology and digital technology classification systems. Considering that some patents have multiple IPC codes, we classify a patent as a DGTI patent if any of its IPC codes meets the integration criterion. Ultimately, we identify 77,288 DGTI patents.

Finally, we use the natural logarithm of the annual number of DGTI patent applications by the firm as the measure for the corporate digital-green technology integration level. A partial list of DGTI patent identification results is shown in Table 1.
