Peri anal region is an overlooked location for pilonidal recurrence after surgical excision and closure – A case series
Authors: Somin Jo, Claire Abrajano, Bill Chiu
Published: 2024

Background

Pilonidal disease (PD) is a chronic skin infection in which hair follicles near the gluteal cleft and sacrococcygeal region get trapped with keratin or hair [1]. When loose hairs become trapped in the region and penetrate the skin, a foreign body reaction is initiated and leads to pit formation. PD can present as an abscess or chronically draining sinus [2]. The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons recommends excision and primary repair, excision with healing by secondary intention, and flap-based procedures for patients with chronic PD [[3], [4], [5]].

Surgical excision involves excision down to the sacrococcygeal fascia, removing skin, sinuses, and pits present. The resulting wound can be closed primarily or left open to heal by secondary intention [6]. Alternatively, the wound can be closed by flap-based procedures, such as the Karydakis technique: An elliptical incision is made off the midline towards the affected side, dissecting the sinus with a scalpel up to the sacral fascia; the skin and subcutaneous tissue on the other side of the incision are then used to close the sacral fascia and space created from excising the sinus [7]. The Karydakis technique prevents the formation of a midline wound while flattening the gluteal cleft [8]. The Limberg flap makes a rhomboid incision over the midline that includes the affected area and removes the skin, subcutaneous fat, and the pilonidal sinus all the way down to the sacral fascia. An incision extending from a corner of the rhomboid-shaped excision is then used to free up skin on the buttock so that the flap can cover the excision [9]. The Bascom cleft lift closure marks the excision margins where the buttocks touch together, makes an elliptical midline incision that encompasses the affected area including the pilonidal sinus, and pulls a skin flap from the less affected side to close the wound with sutures [10].

PD patients still report recurrences after flap procedures despite performing better than wide excision operations [5]. Although little in the current literature specifically looks at the causes of failed flap procedures in PD, general flap procedures involving a unit of tissue transferred to a different location are proposed to fail if the rearranged skin flap has challenges maintaining its blood supply [14]. Patients who failed flap procedures have been referred to our Pilonidal Care Clinic, and we have often encountered recurrent disease in the perianal region. When looking at perianal pilonidal disease and treating with cleft lift procedures, Immerman reported that wounds in the perianal region or on the edge of the anus were a significant predictor of a failed cleft lift, requiring subsequent revision [15,16]. The perianal wounds were frequently obscured by hair and unrecognized by patients or even the referring physicians. In this report, we describe the clinical course of a series of pilonidal patients with recurrent disease and perianal wounds.

Case presentation

Patients presented to our Pilonidal Clinic were included in this report if they had previously received at least one surgical excision procedure for pilonidal disease prior to our care and continued to be symptomatic. Patients were considered symptomatic if they had pain, drainage, or swelling at the gluteal cleft. Patients who underwent only incision and drainage procedure were excluded. All patients were counseled on good hygiene and received local wound care. Local wound care consisted of daily sitz baths and application of Medihoney® (Leptospermum honey, McKesson, Irving, TX, USA) for open wounds or Triad® paste (Coloplast, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for wounds with excess moisture. AQUACEL® Ribbon Dressing was applied over wounds > 1 cm deep.

All patients were started on regular manual and laser epilation. Laser epilation was performed using a LightSheer Quattro™ diode laser (Lumenis, Yokneam, Israel). The laser epilation area comprised of the gluteal cleft at midline and extended 5 cm bilaterally, superiorly to the border of the buttock, and anteriorly to 5 cm from the anus. Laser epilation sessions were conducted at 6–8 week intervals for a minimum of six sessions. Patients were selected for surgical intervention only when they had an abscess, pits with symptoms (pain, erythema, drainage, or embedded hair), or a granuloma/secondary draining sinus. Patients with abscesses receive incision and drainage procedure, while those with symptomatic pits or a granuloma/secondary draining sinus underwent pit-picking and excision of the granuloma/secondary draining sinus. Not all patients with an open wound underwent surgical intervention and only those with wounds > 3 cm were taken to the operating room for debridement. No wound was closed with sutures.

Patient D: 22-year-old male with six-year history of pilonidal disease, underwent wide excision of pilonidal sinus and a tissue transfer flap to close the wound. The wound took about six months to heal and he was referred to our Pilonidal Care Clinic for laser epilation. However, two years later, he developed a small pit/wound opening at the peri-anal region, consistent with a recurrence. He underwent a pit-picking (Gips) procedure [11] and hair was removed from the pilonidal sinus. His wounds closed after four weeks, and he has remained symptom-free.

Patient E: 23-year-old male with pilonidal disease first underwent a limited cleft lift procedure, but post-operatively the wounds would not heal. He had a second excision and flap reconstruction procedure a few months later, but the wounds continued to stay open and drain. He was referred to our Pilonidal Care Clinic and was found to have an open wound from the previous operation at the superior gluteal cleft as well as an unrecognized peri-anal wound. He was treated with regular manual and laser epilation as well as local wound care. All wounds closed after six weeks.

Discussion

All five PD patients described had pilonidal sinus in the perianal region after prior wide excision and flap closures. Their recurrent symptoms included persistent open wound with drainage and pain. Upon referral to our clinic, each patient was started on regular manual and laser epilation at the gluteal cleft region. The removal of hair at the perianal region often exposed a pilonidal sinus that was previously unrecognized. Only one patient underwent excision of the perianal pilonidal wound/pit, while the remaining four patients successfully healed the perianal wound without additional surgery.

There is still much debate over which surgical procedure is the best at treating PD given the high post operative recurrence rates and severe wound complications [6]. While wide excision is a simple method that has been commonly used to surgically treat PD for many decades, studies have shown that this method leads to longer wound healing time and more pain in patients [11]. In a systematic review, the American Pediatric Surgical Association Outcomes and Evidence-Based Practice Committee recommends limiting the choice of wide excision due to high failure rates [12]. Literature comparing various flap-based procedures to wide excision found that flap-based procedures had lower recurrence rates and faster healing times, but no one flap-based procedure has been found to be significantly better than the others [12,13].

There is limited information in the literature as to why perianal PD seems to be harder to manage post-excision. Beal et al. noted that the perianal PD management was complex due to poor wound healing due to the close proximity of the wound to the anal verge [17]. Additional insights on poor wound healing at the perianal region can be gleaned from non-PD wounds: surgical incisions treating perianal hemorrhoids and fistulas are difficult to heal due to spasming of the anal sphincter, closed space, risk of contamination, difficulty of consistent observation of the wound and properly applying wound care to the area [18]. Perianal wound care is especially challenging due to the naturally moist environment of the anus that retains bacteria [19]. Careful consideration of proper wound care post-excision of perianal PD sinuses should be prioritized.

Lastly, we have found consistent hair removal at the perianal region could be very effective at healing the perianal wound, often without additional surgical excision. This observation was consistent with other papers that have reported success in using laser epilation to decrease recurrent PD [20,21]. More importantly, the low recurrence rates in these perianal PD patients were similar to our prior report that adopted a standardized treatment protocol for PD [22]. Therefore, it is worth emphasizing the major impact of regular manual and laser epilation and standardized treatment in managing PD.

Conclusion

Pilonidal perianal pit formation after previous surgical excision and flap closure can be obscured by hair, resulting in recurrent pain and drainage. The perianal PD can be successfully managed with a regular manual and laser epilation and selectively chosen to undergo pit-picking (Gips) procedure for persistent disease.
