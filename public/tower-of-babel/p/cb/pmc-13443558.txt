miR-29a-3p regulates SPARC to inhibit ferroptosis and promote cell proliferation in gastric cancer
Authors: Pengwei Liu, Chiyi He, Lin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of cell death, is a promising therapeutic target in cancer. Secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC) contributes to the pathogenesis of gastric cancer (GC); however, whether SPARC modulates ferroptosis in GC cells remains unclear. Ferroptosis-related genes in GC were identified through bioinformatics screening. Ferroptosis was induced (Erastin) or inhibited (Ferrostatin-1), followed by analyses of GSH, MDA, and SOD activity. mRNA and protein expression was quantified via qRT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, and western blotting. The miR-29a-3p/SPARC interaction was confirmed by a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Cell proliferation was assessed using MTT and EdU assays. In vivo effects of SPARC on tumor growth and ferroptosis were evaluated in a xenograft mouse model. SPARC was identified as a ferroptosis-related gene in GC. Its expression was significantly lower in GC tissues than in adjacent normal gastric tissues and was negatively correlated with tumor size and differentiation. Low expression was an independent predictor of shorter overall survival in GC patients. Functionally, SPARC attenuated GC cell proliferation by promoting ferroptosis. SPARC was identified as a direct downstream target of miR-29a-3p, which inhibited ferroptosis in GC cells by suppressing SPARC expression. Furthermore, we demonstrated that SPARC overexpression significantly suppressed GC tumor growth and induced ferroptosis in vivo. This study suggests that miR-29a-3p directly targets and represses SPARC expression, which may promote GC tumor proliferation by inhibiting ferroptosis in cellular models. The miR-29a-3p/SPARC axis may represent a potential therapeutic target for GC, but its clinical translational relevance requires further validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55231-3.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health challenge, ranking fifth for both incidence and mortality worldwide, with an estimated 968,350 new cases and 659,853 deaths according to 2022 GLOBOCAN data1. Eastern Asia exhibits the highest regional incidence of GC2. Recent advancements in digestive endoscopy, surgical techniques, optimized chemotherapy regimens, and molecularly targeted therapies have improved the survival duration and quality of life substantially for patients with GC. However, an incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis limits progress in reducing mortality rates3,4.

Ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death distinct from apoptosis and necroptosis in its morphological, genetic, and biochemical features, is characterized by the iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides5. This lipid peroxidation generates excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD); these products can react with DNA or proteins, exerting toxic effects6. Solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11/xCT), the catalytic subunit of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system xc−, serves as the primary transporter for extracellular cystine. By promoting cystine uptake, glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis, and GPX4-mediated detoxification of lipid peroxides, SLC7A11 inhibits ferroptosis7. Therefore, cystine depletion or pharmacological inhibition of SLC7A11-mediated cystine uptake (e.g., via erastin) triggers ferroptosis8. The antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) plays a central role in ferroptosis; it neutralizes lipid hydroperoxides into lipid alcohols via GSH, thereby maintaining membrane lipid homeostasis and suppressing ferroptotic cell death9. Collectively, the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis constitutes the core regulatory machinery of cellular defense against ferroptosis10.

Accumulating evidence implicates ferroptosis as a critical regulator in the pathogenesis and malignant progression of diverse cancers11–14, with particular relevance to gastric carcinogenesis15. GPX4 inhibition exacerbates lipid peroxidation cascades, promoting ferroptosis in malignant cells16. Li et al. demonstrated that CST1 facilitates GC metastasis by suppressing ferroptosis through the OTUB1-mediated stabilization of GPX417. Similarly, Wang et al. revealed that the upregulation of GPX4 via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in GC cells confers ferroptosis resistance18.

Secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC), also known as osteonectin, is a calcium-binding matricellular glycoprotein. It modulates extracellular matrix dynamics, coordinates growth factor activity, and mediates cellular-stromal communication19. Accumulating evidence indicates that SPARC plays a significant role in the development of various malignancies20–22. Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that SPARC enhances hepatocellular carcinoma cell responsiveness to chemotherapeutic agents by modulating ferroptosis23. However, whether SPARC regulates ferroptosis in GC progression remains to be explored.

In this study, we investigated the clinical significance of SPARC in GC progression and explored its potential role in regulating ferroptosis and tumor proliferation in vitro and in vivo.

Materials and methods

Data collection and candidate gene screening

Transcriptomic expression profiles for stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) were obtained from the TCGA-Xena database (http://xena.ucsc.edu/), including data for 375 tumor samples and 32 paired adjacent normal tissues. Differential expression between normal gastric tissues and STAD tissues was analyzed using the DESeq2 package in R, setting |logFC| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 as thresholds for significance. Ferroptosis-related genes were aggregated from databases (PMID:32760210, doi:10.3389/fonc.2020.590861, FerrDb, MSIgDB, and GeneCards). Differentially expressed ferroptosis-associated genes identified in STAD tissues were retained for subsequent analyses. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (P < 0.01) were conducted to evaluate prognostic significance.

Patients and specimens

This retrospective cohort study included 111 consecutive patients with GC who underwent radical gastrectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College between January and December 2022. Patients with histopathologically confirmed primary gastric adenocarcinoma who underwent curative gastrectomy with lymph node dissection were included. Those who had received neoadjuvant therapy (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy) prior to surgery were excluded. Postoperative follow-up data were retrospectively collected for a median duration of 28 months (3 to 39 months). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College (Approval No. LLSC-2024-62) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulatory guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Cell lines and culture

Human gastric cancer cell lines (BGC-823, MGC-803, MKN-45 and AGS) and the normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, and a humidified atmosphere).

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR

Total RNA was isolated from tissue samples and cultured cells with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara Biotechnology, Kusatsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences employed in this study were as follows: SPARC, forward, 5′-TGAGAATGA GAAGCGCCTGG-3′, reverse, 5′-TGGGAGAGGTACCCGTCAAT-3′; GAPDH, forward, 5′-GAAAGCCTGCCGGTGACTAA-3′, reverse, 5′-GCCCAATACGACC AAATCAGAG-3′; miR-29a-3p, forward, 5′-CGCGTAGCACCATCTGAAAT-3′, reverse, 5′-AGTGCAGGGTCCGAGGTATT-3′; U6, forward, 5′-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3′, reverse, 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3′. A quantitative analysis was performed using the SYBR Green I method (Takara Biotechnology, Kusatsu, Japan) and relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCT) method.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from tissues and cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto PVDF membranes (Beyotime). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-SPARC (1:2000, 66426-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000, YT8130; ImmunoWay, San Jose, CA, USA), anti-GPX4 (1:2000, 67763-1-Ig; Proteintech, China), anti-FTH1 (1:5000, 60875-5-Ig; Proteintech), and anti-GAPDH (1:1000, ab181602; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) Detection Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using microwave-mediated heating. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Following blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-SPARC (1:1000, 66426-1-Ig; Proteintech), anti-SLC7A11 (1:100, YT8130; ImmunoWay), anti-GPX4 (1:1000, 67763-1-Ig; Proteintech), anti-FTH1 (1:250, 60875-5-Ig; Proteintech), and anti-Ki-67 (1:2000, 28074-1-AP; Proteintech). Subsequently, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime). Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (Maixin Biotechnology, Fuzhou, China), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining of nuclei and permanent mounting. Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system based on staining intensity and distribution. Staining intensity was graded as follows: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). The staining distribution was assessed by the percentage of positively stained tumor cells and categorized as: 0 (0–10%), 1 (11–50%), 2 (51–75%), or 3 (> 75%). The composite immunoreactivity score (IRS) was calculated by multiplying the intensity score by the distribution score. Specimens were categorized based on their IRS as follows: negative expression (IRS ≤ 1) and positive expression (IRS > 2). Positive expression was further stratified as low expression (IRS ≤ 3) or high expression (IRS > 3).

siRNA and plasmid construction

The full-length human SPARC transcript was synthesized and cloned into the pLV3-CMV vector (OE-SPARC) using restriction enzyme-mediated subcloning. Lentiviral particles containing either OE-SPARC or the empty vector control (OE-NC) were packaged in 293 T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; 11668-019), following standard viral production protocols. SPARC-targeting siRNA duplexes (siRNA-SPARC) and negative control siRNA (siRNA-NC) were synthesized by General Biol (Anhui, China). The following SPARC siRNA sequence was employed: 5′-CAAGACCUUCGACUCUUCCUU-3′24. AGS and MKN-45 cells were transduced with the viral supernatant supplemented with 8 µg/mL polybrene and incubated for 48–72 h. Stable transfectants were selected and maintained using 2.5 µg/mL puromycin for 4 weeks prior to functional validation. To confirm overexpression or knockdown, cells were harvested and SPARC expression was analyzed at the mRNA level by quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) and the protein level by western blotting. A synthetic miR-29a-3p mimic (hsa-miR-29a-3p mimics) and scrambled negative control mimic (NC-mimic) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The sequence for the has-miR-29a-3p mimic was as follows: forward, 5′–UAGCACCAUCUGAAAUCGGUUA–3′, reverse, 5′–ACCGAUUUCAG AUGGUGCUAUU–3′. miR-29a-3p expression levels were quantified using qRT-PCR.

Methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium (MTT) Assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells/well in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Following overnight incubation, cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 with siRNA-SPARC, siRNA-NC, OE-SPARC (pLV3-CMV-SPARC), OE-NC (empty pLV3-CMV vector), hsa-miR-29a-3p mimic, or NC mimic. Plates were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere for 24–72 h; medium was refreshed every 24 h for incubations extending beyond 48 h. MTT solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China; 5 mg/mL in PBS) was added (10 µL/well) and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The supernatant was carefully aspirated, and formazan crystals were dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO (Solarbio) with gentle shaking. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a commercial EdU assay kit (Beyotime; C0071S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 × 105 cells per well were plated in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h to allow adhesion. Cells were pulse-labeled with 500 µL of 10 µM EdU solution for 2 h at 37 °C and washed three times with PBS. Subsequent steps included fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (GenStar, San Francisco, CA, USA, VA11410; 15 min), and incubation with the Click-iT reaction cocktail (30 min, room temperature, protected from light). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti-S inverted fluorescence microscope system.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the interaction between miR-29a-3p and SPARC, wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) fragments of the SPARC 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR), encompassing the predicted miR-29a-3p binding sites, were cloned downstream of the Renilla luciferase gene in the psiCHECK2 dual-luciferase reporter vector (General Biol, Chuzhou, China). The resultant recombinant plasmids (designated psiCHECK2-SPARC-3’UTR-WT and psiCHECK2-SPARC-3’UTR-MUT) were individually co-transfected into 293 T cells with either the hsa-miR-29a-3p mimic or negative control mimic (NC mimic) using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h of incubation, transfected cells were lysed. Luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), with the Renilla luciferase signal normalized to the firefly luciferase signal for each sample.

Measurement of the glutathione (GSH) content

GSH levels were measured using a GSH Detection Kit (BC1175, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The GSH content was calculated as follows: GSH (µg/mg prot) = [(ΔOD + 0.0058)/0.0014]/Cpr, where Cpr represents the protein concentration of the sample.

Measurement of the malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration

MDA levels were measured using an MDA Detection Kit (BC0025, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The MDA concentration was calculated as follows: MDA (nmol/mg prot) = 53.763 × (ΔA532 – ΔA600)/Cpr, and Cpr is the sample protein concentration.

Determination of the superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme activity

SOD activity was measured using a commercially available SOD Activity Assay Kit (BC5165, Solarbio) and was quantified as follows: SOD Activity (U/mg prot) = [10 × Inhibition Rate/(1 – Inhibition Rate)]/Cpr, where Inhibition Rate represents the percentage inhibition of chromogen reduction, and Cpr denotes the sample protein concentration.

Mouse xenograft model

Twelve 4 to 5-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (BALB/c-nu) were purchased from the Animal Experimental Center of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, Anhui, China). The mice were randomly allocated into experimental (OE-SPARC) and control groups (n = 6 per group). For the tumor xenograft procedure, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen delivered via a nose cone. Subsequently, mice received subcutaneous injections of OE-SPARC-transduced AGS cells or vector-transduced AGS cells (1 × 107 cells/mouse) into the right axillary region. Tumor dimensions (length and width) were measured twice weekly using digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated as: V (mm3) = (length × width2)/2. All mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia (5% isoflurane) at nearly 3 weeks post-injection. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wannan Medical College (Approval No. WNMC-AWE-2024123) and performed in strict compliance with institutional ethical guidelines. Reporting is in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.2.1). Data were visualized using the R package ggplot2. Categorical variables were compared using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables employed Student’s t-tests. Survival distributions were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors. All in vitro assays included triplicate replicates. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of Prognostic Ferroptosis-Related DEGs in STAD

TCGA-STAD transcriptomic profiles were obtained from the Xena platform, including data for 375 tumor samples and 32 matched adjacent normal tissues. Using DESeq2 (|log2FC| > 1, adjusted P < 0.05), we identified 10,329 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between gastric tumors and normal tissues (Fig. 1a–b). Ferroptosis-related genes were compiled from multiple sources (PMID:32760210, doi:10.3389/fonc.2020.590861, FerrDb, MSIgDB, and GeneCards), resulting in a reference set of 461 genes. To expand the limited core ferroptosis regulator set, we performed a Pearson correlation analysis (r > 0.8, P < 0.05) and identified 1, 611 genes that were positively correlated with these ferroptosis-related genes. The intersection between these genes and the DEGs yielded 459 ferroptosis-associated DEGs for further analyses (Fig. 1c). Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses (P < 0.01) revealed 38 ferroptosis-related genes that were significantly related to STAD survival outcomes, including ABCA6, BGN, DUSP1, EZH2, SPARC, and ZFP36 (Table S1).

Based on a review of relevant literature23,25,26, DUSP1, SPARC, and ZFP36 were selected for further investigation. We assessed their mRNA expression levels in gastric cancer MKN-45 and AGS cells treated with Erastin, a ferroptosis inducer. Erastin treatment downregulated the ferroptosis-associated genes SLC7A11 and GPX4, confirming successful ferroptosis induction (Fig. 1d). Compared with levels in the control groups, SPARC was significantly upregulated (P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively), whereas DUSP1 was downregulated (P < 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively). ZFP36 expression did not differ significantly between Erastin-treated and control cells (Fig. 1e). These findings suggested that SPARC and DUSP1 might be involved in the ferroptosis process. Owing to the more pronounced change in SPARC expression compared with DUSP1, SPARC was selected for further functional analyses.

SPARC downregulation in GC tissues is associated with poor clinical outcomes

qRT-PCR and western blotting were used to evaluate SPARC expression in GC cell lines and the normal gastric mucosal cell line GES-1. SPARC expression was significantly lower at both mRNA (Fig. 2a) and protein (Fig. 2b) levels in GC cell lines than in GES-1 cells. These findings were further preliminarily validated in 7 paired clinical samples of primary GC tissues and adjacent normal mucosa, where SPARC mRNA and protein levels were consistently lower in tumors (Fig. 2c–d).

A Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significantly shorter median overall survival (OS) in patients with low SPARC expression than in patients with high SPARC expression (P < 0.001; Fig. 2f). Both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified SPARC downregulation as an independent risk factor for reduced OS (univariate: P = 0.001; multivariate: P < 0.001; Table 2). To further explore the prognostic value of SPARC expression in different clinical stages of GC, we performed a stratified survival analysis based on pTNM stage. The 111 GC patients were divided into early-stage group (Ⅰ+Ⅱ stage, n = 61) and advanced-stage group (Ⅲ+Ⅳ stage, n = 50). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis combined with Log-rank test revealed that the correlation between SPARC low expression and shorter OS was more pronounced in the advanced-stage subgroup (P < 0.001, Fig. S1b), whereas the correlation was not significant in the early-stage subgroup (P = 0.094, Fig. S1a). These results suggested that SPARC may serve as a potential prognostic stratification biomarker for patients with advanced GC.

SPARC-induced ferroptosis inhibits GC cell proliferation in vitro

To investigate the effects of SPARC upregulation on GC cell proliferation and ferroptosis, AGS and MKN-45 cells were transfected with OE-SPARC or OE-NC. Initial qRT-PCR and western blotting analyses confirmed that OE-SPARC transfection elevated SPARC expression significantly. Subsequent treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 reduced SPARC levels compared with those in OE-SPARC-transfected cells prior to Ferrostatin-1 exposure (Fig. 3a). MTT and EdU assays revealed that SPARC overexpression suppressed proliferation significantly (P < 0.001; Fig. 3b–c), an effect reversed by Ferrostatin-1 treatment (Fig. 3b–c). To validate these findings, GC cell lines were transfected with SPARC-specific or NC siRNAs (Fig. 4a). Consistent with expectations, after SPARC knockdown, proliferation was increased significantly compared with that in controls (P < 0.001; Fig. 4b–c), and Erastin treatment decreased the proliferative capacity (Fig. 4b–c).

To further investigate the role of SPARC in regulating ferroptosis in GC cells, we measured GSH, MDA, and SOD levels, and the expression of key ferroptosis-related proteins, including GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1. GSH, SOD, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 levels were significantly lower in OE-SPARC-transfected GC cells than in controls (P < 0.05), while MDA levels were significantly higher (P < 0.05). Ferrostatin-1 attenuated these OE-SPARC-induced alterations (Fig. 3d–g). Consistent findings were observed in the SPARC siRNA group; GSH, SOD, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 levels were significantly increased (P < 0.05), MDA levels were significantly decreased (P < 0.05), and Erastin treatment attenuated these alterations (Fig. 4d–g).

These results demonstrated that SPARC may promote ferroptosis by downregulating ferroptosis suppressors (GPX4, SLC7A11, FTH1), thereby inhibiting proliferation in GC cells. Notably, in 7 paired human GC and adjacent normal tissues, SPARC, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 were all significantly downregulated in GC tissues (all P < 0.05) (Fig. S2a–c). Further correlation analysis in the TCGA-STAD dataset (375 GC tissues) showed SPARC expression was positively correlated with GPX4 (r = 0.214, P < 0.001) and FTH1 (r = 0.187, P < 0.001) (Fig. S3a–b), which was opposite to the in vitro negative regulatory relationship.

SPARC-induced ferroptosis suppresses GC proliferation in vivo

To validate our in vitro findings, nude mice were subcutaneously injected with AGS cells stably expressing either OE-SPARC or OE-NC vectors and monitored for nearly 3 weeks. The initial body weights of the mice in the control and OV-SPARC groups were 21.4 ± 0.7 g and 22.2 ± 2.1 g, respectively. The final weights were 26.1 ± 1.1 g and 26.5 ± 2.7 g, respectively. Tumors in the OE-SPARC group exhibited significantly lower volumes and weights compared with tumors in the control group (P < 0.001; Fig. 5a–c). Immunohistochemical analyses further revealed markedly increased SPARC expression alongside decreased levels of Ki-67, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 in OE-SPARC tumors (Fig. 5d).These data suggested an inverse regulatory relationship between SPARC and these ferroptosis suppressors in vivo.

miR-29a-3p targets SPARC to regulate GC cell proliferation and ferroptosis

A previous bioinformatic analysis using TargetScan suggested that SPARC is a direct target of miR-29a-3p (Fig. 6a), prompting us to investigate its regulatory role in GC. We observed a significant inverse correlation between miR-29a-3p and SPARC expression levels in seven fresh frozen GC tissues (r = −0.763, P < 0.05, Fig. 6b). To further validate this interaction, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay using 293 T cells. After transfection with miR-29a-3p mimics, cells carrying the wild-type SPARC 3’UTR showed significantly suppressed luciferase activity compared with cells carrying the negative control (NC) mimic (P < 0.05, Fig. 6c). Neither the NC nor a mutant SPARC construct group showed altered reporter activity (P > 0.05, Fig. 6c), indicating that miR-29a-3p directly targets SPARC.

We next assessed SPARC expression in AGS cells transfected with NC mimic, miR-29a-3p mimic, OE-SPARC, or a combination of OE-SPARC and miR-29a-3p mimic. Compared with that in controls, SPARC after miR-29a-3p overexpression was significantly downregulated at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6d). The miR-29a-3p mimic effectively attenuated the effects of SPARC overexpression, reinforcing the inverse relationship. Functionally, miR-29a-3p overexpression promoted cell proliferation, whereas SPARC co-expression mitigated this effect (Fig. 6e–f). Additionally, miR-29a-3p upregulation increased GSH, SOD, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 levels while reducing the MDA content (Fig. 6g–j), and these changes were reversed by SPARC overexpression.

To validate these findings, we performed parallel experiments using the NC inhibitor, miR-29a-3p inhibitor, siRNA-SPARC, or miR-29a-3p inhibitor plus siRNA-SPARC. As anticipated, miR-29a-3p inhibition elevated SPARC expression significantly and counteracted the effects of siRNA-induced SPARC knockdown (Fig. 6d). Functionally, miR-29a-3p suppression impaired cell proliferation, an effect rescued by SPARC silencing (Fig. 6e–f). Moreover, miR-29a-3p inhibition reduced GSH, SOD, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 levels while increasing MDA (Fig. 6g–j), and these changes were attenuated upon SPARC knockdown. Of note, although functional assays suggested that miR-29a-3p promotes proliferation and inhibits ferroptosis in GC cells, its basal expression level was paradoxically lower in GC cell lines (including MKN-45 and AGS) than in the normal gastric mucosal cell line GES-1 (Fig.S4).

Discussion

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent regulated cell death mechanism marked by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and disruption of redox homeostasis, has emerged as a promising target in cancer treatment, especially for GC. Recent studies have demonstrated the multifaceted roles of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of GC, with significant effects on tumor growth, metastasis, and the development of chemoresistance17,27,28. Additionally, SPARC has been shown to contribute to tumorigenesis through diverse mechanisms. For example, SPARC induces M2-to-M1 macrophage repolarization and promotes pancreatic tumor cell apoptosis, thereby suppressing tumor progression29. SPARC increases 5-fluorouracil chemosensitivity by regulating the epithelial–mesenchymal transition and apoptotic pathways in GC30. Furthermore, diminished SPARC expression in GC-associated fibroblasts drives stemness transformation and 5-fluorouracil resistance and is associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients with GC31. Nevertheless, the role of SPARC in modulating ferroptosis during GC progression remained largely unexplored. In this study, we sought to investigate whether and how SPARC might be involved in ferroptosis regulation in GC in vitro and in vivo.

We first identified 459 ferroptosis-related DEGs between GC tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumorous tissues by screening public databases. Univariate Cox proportional hazards analyses identified significant associations between 38 of these genes, including SPARC, and OS. To investigate the specific relationship between SPARC and ferroptosis in GC, we treated MKN-45 and AGS cells with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin. qRT-PCR demonstrated a significant increase in SPARC mRNA levels compared with those in untreated controls, supporting its involvement in ferroptosis. Furthermore, SPARC was significantly downregulated in GC tissues, and this downregulation was strongly correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including a larger tumor size and poorer differentiation. SPARC low expression emerged as an independent prognostic factor for significantly shorter OS. Furthermore, stratified analysis of 111 GC patients revealed that SPARC low expression had a strong correlation with poor OS in advanced-stage (Ⅲ+Ⅳ) patients, which implied that SPARC could be a specific prognostic stratification biomarker for advanced GC. Taken together, consistent downregulation of SPARC at both the transcript and protein levels was observed in 7 fresh human GC tissues, providing preliminary evidence that SPARC dysregulation may serve as a potential biomarker for adverse clinical outcomes in GC. These findings may aid in identifying advanced GC patients at elevated risk and offer a potential marker for personalized prognostic evaluation.

To further investigate the functional role of SPARC, we modulated its expression in vitro and in vivo. SPARC upregulation inhibited GC cell proliferation and enhanced ferroptosis. Simultaneously, levels of SLC7A11, GSH, and GPX4 decreased markedly. SPARC downregulation had the opposite effects. These data suggested that SPARC overexpression might induce ferroptosis partly through the SLC7A11/GPX4 signaling pathway in cellular and xenograft models. This observation aligns with a recent study highlighting the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis as a key regulator of ferroptosis in GC metastasis32. Similarly, SHARPIN promotes cholangiocarcinoma progression and suppresses ferroptosis via the p53/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway33. However, mRNA analysis in 7 paired human GC and normal tissues revealed that SPARC and these ferroptosis related genes (SLC7A11, GPX4 and FTH1) were all significantly downregulated in GC tissues, which contradicts our initial hypothesis based on in vitro and in vivo. Given the limited sample size, this observation remains preliminary. Larger, multicenter clinical cohorts are therefore warranted to validate the clinical relevance of SPARC and ferroptosis. Furthermore, correlation analysis of the TCGA-STAD dataset showed SPARC mRNA expression was positively correlated with GPX4 and FTH1 in GC tissues, directly opposing the negative regulatory relationship observed in our GC cell line models. These discrepancies indicate that the in vivo regulatory network is far more complex and context‑dependent. Although the expression of these genes was unexpectedly decreased in cancer tissues, this observation suggests that the overall ferroptosis level is elevated in GC. This implies that inhibiting GC cell growth by promoting ferroptosis might be a feasible therapeutic strategy.

A better understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing ferroptosis may help identify novel therapeutic targets in GC. Various microRNAs (miRNAs) have been identified as key regulators of ferroptosis. For instance, miR-522 suppresses ferroptosis in GC, contributing to acquired chemoresistance34. Similarly, miR-432-5p targets CHAC1 to inhibit ferroptosis and confer chemoresistance in prostate cancer35. However, whether miRNAs modulate SPARC to influence ferroptosis in GC has not been previously reported. In our study, we identified a negative correlation between miR-29a-3p and SPARC expression in GC tissues and confirmed that miR-29a-3p directly binds to SPARC. Furthermore, miR-29a-3p mimic transfection downregulated SPARC expression and inhibited ferroptosis in GC cells. SPARC overexpression in GC cells with elevated miR-29a-3p levels significantly attenuated the inhibitory effect on ferroptosis. Conversely, miR-29a-3p inhibition increased SPARC expression and induced ferroptosis. Intriguingly, basal miR-29a-3p expression was lower in GC cell lines than in normal GES-1 cells despite its proposed oncogenic role. This paradox cannot be simply attributed to cellular adaptation.The underlying reason for this inconsistent expression–function relationship remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

In summary, this study identifies a functional role of the miR-29a-3p/SPARC axis in regulating ferroptosis and cell proliferation in GC. While the interaction between miR-29a-3p and SPARC has been documented in other cancer types, our findings suggest that this axis may influence ferroptosis through the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway in GC. Notably, our mechanistic model is not fully supported and even partially contradicted by clinical findings. Growing evidence suggests that microRNA-mediated regulatory axes rarely operate in isolation, but instead function within highly context-dependent networks that shape tumor behavior at multiple levels. In gastrointestinal malignancies, specific miRNAs have been shown to modulate key downstream targets to influence proliferation, invasion, and treatment response, often with effects that vary depending on cellular and microenvironmental context36. At the same time, emerging studies highlight that tumor-associated signaling pathways and regulatory molecules can exert divergent roles across experimental systems37, suggesting that mechanistic conclusions drawn from in vitro models may not fully capture the complexity observed in human tumors.

However, this study had several limitations. First, although our results indicate that SPARC may regulate ferroptosis through the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway, we only detected classic ferroptosis markers, which cannot fully clarify its detailed mechanism. Further mechanistic research is therefore required to elucidate the SPARC-mediated ferroptosis regulation. Second, ferroptosis is regulated by a complex network involving multiple players. Inducing ferroptosis is a well-established mechanism through which tumor suppressors, such as p53, BAP1 and the epigenetic factor MLL4, exert suppressive effects, at least in part38. It has been reported that SPARC may regulate p53 to influence apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in cervical cancer39. p53 is known to induce ferroptosis primarily through the transcriptional repression of SLC7A1140. In contrast, SPARC inhibits the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis to drive ferroptosis in GC cells. We speculate that SPARC and p53 may synergistically regulate ferroptosis and form a coordinated signaling network. Our future work will focus on their direct interaction and transcriptional regulation.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that reduced SPARC expression is inversely correlated with a larger tumor size and poorer differentiation in GC, and may serve as an independent prognostic factor in GC patients. Notably, our in vitro and in vivo data suggest that SPARC may play a role in regulating ferroptosis, which could in turn influence GC cell proliferation and tumor growth. The observed negative correlation and mechanistic analyses indicated that miR-29a-3p may directly target SPARC to inhibit ferroptosis in vitro. These findings link the miR-29a-3p/SPARC axis to ferroptosis-related phenotypes in GC models, while its clinical relevance remains to be fully elucidated.
