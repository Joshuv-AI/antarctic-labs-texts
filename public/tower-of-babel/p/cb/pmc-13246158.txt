Next generation technologies for CRISPR-based epigenome and transcriptional modulation
Authors: Rithu K. Pattali, Nikita S. Divekar, James K. Nuñez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Technologies for editing epigenetic modifications and controlling transcription in mammalian cells have revolutionized targeted gene perturbation, functional genomics, and basic research. By avoiding the generation of DNA breaks, epigenome editing serves as a safe and precise approach for altering gene expression and has emerged as a promising platform for therapeutic applications. The advent of CRISPR has contributed significantly to the expansion of the existing toolkit for programmable modulation of epigenetic and transcriptional states. This review highlights recent discoveries in engineering novel tools for epigenome editing and transcriptional modulation through rational design, high throughput screening methods, and mutational scans, which leverage the endogenous reservoir of chromatin and transcriptional effectors for targeted gene repression and activation. We also discuss the therapeutic potential of epigenome modulators and highlight the key challenges that need to be addressed to improve their safety and efficacy. Advancing our understanding of the complex mechanisms driving gene expression and overcoming current limitations will pave the way for the development of novel technologies that advance fundamental research and translational applications.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of genome editing has led to the development of genomic perturbation technologies for both basic research and therapeutic applications. In particular, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) - CRISPR-associated proteins (Cas) nucleases, base and prime editors, integrases, and recombinases have revolutionized the ability to rewrite the genomes of mammalian cells with high precision and programmability [1]. Alongside advances in genome editing, CRISPR-based technologies have been developed to regulate gene expression in mammalian cells. These approaches function by modulating epigenetic modifications instead of introducing DNA breaks or permanently changing the genomic sequence. Epigenome editing technologies have been used widely for targeted transcriptional repression and activation of genes, functional genomics, and recently as potential therapeutic modalities for perturbing disease-associated genes [2,3].

Platforms for targeted epigenome editing rely on a DNA binding module (e.g., TALE, zinc fingers orcatalytically inactive CRISPR-Cas effectors) combined with an epigenetic modulator that can either enzymatically edit epigenetic modifications (e.g., DNA or histone methyltransferases or acetyltransferases) or act as scaffold proteins that recruit endogenous epigenetic and/or transcriptional modifiers (e.g., transcriptional corepressors and co-activators) (Figure 1). For example, combining catalytically dead dCas9 with the de novo DNA methyltransferase enzymatic complex DNMT3A-DNMT3L can directly add DNA methylation at CpG dinucleotides when directed to gene promoters, leading to long-term, heritable transcriptional silencing of target genes [4,5]. Conversely, targeting gene promoters with dCas9 fused to TET1 promote oxidative DNA demethylation at these loci and reactivate genes silenced by promoter methylation [6] (Figure 1a). Hence, although epigenome editing was first described before the advent of CRISPR, the programmable nature of RNA-guided Cas proteins has greatly advanced the use of these technologies in mammalian cells.

In this review, we use the term epigenome editing to describe programmable approaches that modulate gene expression through the recruitment of chromatin-associated effectors and subsequent alteration of the epigenetic landscape at a target locus. Examples include effectors that enzymatically catalyze epigenetic changes, such as DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) that deposit DNA methylation marks [5], and TET1 that initiates the removal of DNA methylation [6]. In addition, some effectors act indirectly as scaffolds, such as the well-characterized KRAB domain that functions as a scaffold to recruit the KAP1 co-repressor to promote repressive chromatin modifications, including H3K9me3 [7]. In contrast, transcriptional activators such as VP64 or VPR primarily function through their direct interactions with the transcriptional machinery, including RNA polymerase II and coactivator complexes. While these transcriptional effectors can strongly modulate gene expression, they do not typically induce stable or heritable epigenetic modifications at the targeted locus. Although both classes are often grouped under the broader CRISPRa/i umbrella, they differ substantially in mechanism, durability, and dependence on endogenous chromatin maintenance pathways. Detailed discussions of the mechanistic distinctions between direct and indirect modes of epigenome editing can be found in a recent review [8].

One direct use of epigenome editing technologies in eukaryotic cells is for site-specific transcriptional repression (CRISPR interference or CRISPRi) or activation (CRISPR activation or CRISPRa). The state-of-the-art CRISPRi platform tethers dCas9 to the Krüppel associated box (KRAB) domain from the zinc finger protein family that recruits epigenetic repressor proteins to establish H3K9me3 at target gene promoters, eventually resulting in transcriptional repression [9,10]. Since its advent, CRISPRi has been a transformative technology that offers advantages over nucleases or RNAi-based gene silencing. For example, CRISPRi is not associated with DNA break-associated cytotoxicity and can induce fully penetrant silencing, whereas nucleases depend on unpredictable DNA repair pathways and can result in heterogeneous and undesirable repair outcomes. An additional advancement is the experimental derivation and computational prediction of active sgRNAs for productive CRISPRi activity that are highly potent in mammalian cells and have led to the development of compact sgRNA libraries for functional genomics [11]. As such, CRISPRi/a technologies have been applied widely for in vitro and in vivo mouse models and have advanced platforms for single cell CRISPR perturbation methods like Perturb-seq and CROP-seq [12–14].

Recently, major breakthroughs in the field have discovered novel transcriptional repressors and activators that can modulate gene expression in mammalian cells. The central goals of these studies include expanding the gene targeting landscape for transcriptional modulation and understanding the direct effect of epigenome modifiers on transcription. For example, repression mediated by the KRAB domain can be further enhanced by combining it with the epigenetic regulator MeCP2 [15]. Here, we review recent work to discover next generation transcriptional repressors and activators that have expanded the available toolkit for modulating the epigenome and gene expression in mammalian cells. We also discuss recent advances that profile the safety and therapeutic applications of epigenome modulators. Lastly, we highlight the challenges and future directions in this rapidly evolving field.

Discovery of novel epigenome engineering tools using high throughput screens

Proteins that participate in chromatin regulation in eukaryotic cells provide a rich source of effectors that can be harnessed to build synthetic tools for modulating gene expression. Early efforts to discover new effectors for transcriptional editing relied on low throughput cloning and testing of individual dCas9 fusions in arrayed formats. Although this strategy has produced robust tools for transcriptional repression such as DNA methylation modifiers and KRAB domains from the ZNF10 [9] and ZIM3 [16] proteins, they are often laborious and require multiple rounds of optimization and validation. To overcome these limitations, recent studies have pioneered high throughput platforms to test hundreds to thousands of effectors in a pooled screening format, thereby enabling the discovery of novel effectors that can program transcriptional repression or activation (Figure 2). These studies are bolstered by advancements in pooled oligonucleotide synthesis and sequencing methods that allow for computational design and subsequent experimental testing of complex effector libraries across different cellular contexts.

The first demonstration of scalable screening to systematically study the function of chromatin factors in mammalian cells involved the generation of oligonucleotide libraries encoding 5093 effector domains (each 80 amino acids in length) [17]. Termed HT-recruit, the library was integrated into a pool of cells to target a synthetic reporter that drives the expression of a surface marker protein that can be magnetically isolated, followed by sequence identification of effectors that modulate reporter activity. This approach yielded novel repressor and activator domains across 600 effector proteins, including some of which lacked domain annotations. Using the same pooled platform, the study performed the most extensive mutational scan of the KRAB repressor domain that revealed variants with enhanced repression. These results highlight the potential of using mutational libraries to engineer chromatin effector variants with improved efficacy, similar to the engineering of DNMT3A for long-term gene silencing. A subsequent HT-recruit screen identified KRAB domains from the ZNF705 and ZNF471 proteins that exhibited stronger repression than the canonical KRAB domain from ZNF10, thereby enhancing CRISPRi capabilities in pooled functional genomics screens [18].

Systematic pooled screens have also evaluated how effector function is influenced by different biological or technical variables, such as cell-to-cell variability, differences in the target loci, and the choice of the the DNA-binding domain (DBD), such as comparisons between rTetR and dCas9 [18]. This was exemplified in a HT-recruit screen with previously described libraries, where endogenous genes were targeted across 17 different biological contexts. Interestingly, the screen revealed less variability in activator function when recruited using the rTetR system, while the dCas9 format uncovered additional activation domains, such as the HLH domain from HAND2. Effector function also showed strong cell type dependence. For example, the KIBRA WW domain led to 91 % activation in K-562 cells but only 3 % in HEK293T cells. In contrast, repressor domains were more consistent across different cellular contexts. Notably, the screen also identified effectors with bifunctional domains that could act as activators or repressors, such as the HLH domain, when targeted to different promoters. These findings support recent reports of the context dependent activities of epigenome and transcriptional effectors [19,20], highlighting the importance of accounting for such variability in engineering synthetic epigenome modifiers.

The power of HT-recruit has been further applied to screen over 100,000 protein fragments from human transcription factors and chromatin regulators, as well as over 60,000 protein tiles from viral genomes leading to the discovery of hundreds of new transcriptional effector domains [21,22]. Collectively, these high-throughput large-scale screens, whether conducted using tiled domain sequences or full-length protein domains, represent powerful strategies for dissecting the function of chromatin regulators by diving into the complexity of gene regulation.

Combinatorial synthetic repressors enhance programmable gene repression

Epigenetic regulation in mammals is a highly complex process that involves the coordination of large multi-protein complexes. For epigenome editing, prior studies have demonstrated that combining effectors with different biochemical functions, such as DNMT3A, DNMT3L and KRAB can enhance hit-and-run heritable silencing of endogenous genes [5,23,24]. Inspired by the endogenous interplay between chromatin regulators, several groups have performed high-throughput screens to investigate the biochemical functions of combinatorial chromatin regulators in epigenetic repression.

One such study carried out a systematic high-throughput screen to assess the pairwise combination of 8400 effector domains (activators, repressors, or dual function effectors) and their effects on gene regulation [25]. The study revealed that weak or moderate activators can act synergistically to enhance transcriptional activation, while repressors typically act additively. Interestingly, when repressors were combined with activators, the repressors frequently overrode the effects of the activators. Leveraging this, a synthetic transcription factor was engineered by fusing FOXO3, a dual-function effector, to the strong repressor KRAB, separated by a drug-inducible cleavable domain. This system elucidated that while FOXO3 alone activated a minimal promoter, the recruitment of KRAB overrode FOXO3’s activating effects, resulting in transcriptional repression. Thus, this study highlights the potential of utilizing combinatorial synthetic fusions to recapitulate the complexity of endogenous gene regulatory systems. Another high-throughput platform investigated the combinatorial effects of chromatin regulators (over 1900 pairs) in yeast [26]. Key insights were uncovered, such as the synergistic activity of weak activators, like Mediator subunits, and the overriding effects of SNF11, a chromatin remodeler that can partially resist repression. Furthermore, the large high-quality datasets that were generated were used to train machine learning models to accurately predict outcomes for combinatorial effector recruitment.

Some groups have extended high-throughput screening strategies utilizing full-length protein domains. For example, the COMBINE platform systematically evaluated over 50,000 pairwise combinations of epigenetic effector domains, each fused to dCas9 and introduced into cells harboring a reporter system [27]. This screen led to the identification of approximately 6500 effector pairs that significantly modulated transcription. In addition, this study also generated a rich dataset mapping how different effector combinations influence gene expression. One of the top hits from this screen was a KRAB-L3MBTL3 fusion that demonstrated substantially enhanced gene silencing compared to KRAB alone, reflecting strong synergistic activity between the two domains (Figure 3a).

Another platform, termed COMET, employed a large CRISPR-based screening platform to test constructs composed of combinations of 2–6 full length effector domains for targeted transcriptional modulation at endogenous human gene loci [28]. A total of 110,000 unique combinations were tested, preserving full domain integrity and resulting in the identification of over 5800 significant hits that could modulate transcription. Among the combinatorial repressors tested, the top hit was an MBD2-CDYL fusion. MBD2 is hypothesized to recruit the repressive NuRD complex, whereas CDYL interacts with G9A to promote the deposition of H3K9me1/2 marks, resulting in strong synergistic repression (Figure 3b).

Advances in programmable gene activation

While technologies for programmable transcriptional repression have expanded rapidly in recent years, methods for gene activation remain limited due to the complex nature of transcriptional activation in mammals. This challenge is exacerbated by the gene-dependent nature of gene activators and the fact that each target gene has a distinct maximum transcriptional output.

Traditionally used synthetic dCas9-based transcriptional activators are often derived from viral transactivation domains, such as the widely used dCas9-VPR [29], which are larger and immunogenic, making their delivery challenging and potentially toxic to mammalian cells. To address these limitations, a recent study designed synthetic activation domains from human sequences. Termed CRISPR-DREAM, this platform involved designing and systematically testing multipartite human mechanosensitive transactivation domains across various genes, many of which demonstrated robust activation in mammalian cells [30] (Figure 3c). These compact and human-derived editors can potentially reduce immunogenicity and hold great promise for future therapeutic applications.

In parallel to these synthetic activators, epigenome editors that harness endogenous mechanisms such as recruitment of TET enzymes to promote DNA demethylation have demonstrated high efficacy in activating both endogenous genes and synthetically repressed reporters [23,31,32]. Recently, dCas9-TET1 was used to activate an epigenetically silenced maternal allele to compensate for deletions of the paternal allele that cause a neurological disorder called Prader–Willi Syndrome (PWS) [33]. Transient delivery of dCas9-TET1 in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-based models led to the stable activation of downstream genes. This effect was maintained following differentiation into neurons and restored expression comparable to the the activity of the normally expressed paternal allele.

Consistent with these findings, an independent study also used a dCas9-SunTag-TET1 system to target the PWS imprinting control region (PWS-ICR) in PWS patient-derived iPSC models [34]. This approach led to robust DNA demethylation and reactivation of multiple paternally expressed genes including SNRPN, SNORD116, and MAGEL. Importantly, reactivation of these genes was maintained following differentiation of the iPSCs into hypothalamic organoids, demonstrating durable epigenetic reprogramming across cell fate transitions. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses further revealed partial restoration of gene expression programs associated with PWS pathology, supporting the functional relevance of targeted demethylation at the imprinting control region. Together, these studies highlight the capacity of TET-based epigenome editing to induce stable, locus-wide reactivation of imprinted genes in human disease models.

Recent work has also demonstrated that combining TET1 with additional activation domains further enhances its gene-activating activity. For example, combining TET1-dCas9 with VPR and p64-Rta leads to robust gene activation compared to TET1 alone, suggesting that RNA Pol II recruitment enhances gene activation [23]. Similarly, incorporation of additional domains such as HSF1 further enhances transcriptional activity relative to TET1 alone, potentially by enabling more efficient RNA Pol II recruitment. However, a boost in gene activation does not necessarily translate to sustained gene activation. This was illustrated when systematic comparisons of chromatin regulators in human iPSCs with activating histone modifiers such as DOT1L, p300 or PRDM9 could only transiently reactivate expression from DNA-methylated reporters [35]. In contrast, TET1-mediated demethylation enabled stable and durable gene reactivation. Together, these findings suggest that while transcriptional activators can transiently boost expression, removal of DNA methylation by TET enzymes is required for long-term maintenance of reactivated gene states in certain cellular contexts [36].

In parallel to these approaches, systematic, large-scale pooled screens have also informed the design of synthetic transcription activators. For example, the previously described HT-recruit platform led to the identification and ranking of several activation domains that were functional across different cellular contexts, target loci and DBDs [18]. These findings led to the development of a novel synthetic tripartite activator, termed NFZ, which is composed of activation domains NCOA3, FOXO3, and ZNF47.NFZ performed comparably to dCas9-VP64 and achieved comparable or higher levels of activation than the more potent synthetic activator dCas9-VPR when delivered as lentivirus (Figure 3d). Notably, NFZ is a compact activator at just 145 amino acids, and its efficacy was further validated in a CAR-T cell therapeutic context, demonstrating its translational potential. Together, these advances have expanded the growing toolkit of transcriptional activators for programmable gene activation.

Therapeutic use of epigenome modulators

In recent years, programmable epigenome editing has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential. A recent highlight is the development of the CHARM system composed of either a ZFP or dCas9 fused to a histone tail and DNMT3L [37]. CHARM (with ZFP) is compact, enabling its delivery via AAVs for silencing Prnp, a gene implicated in prion disease. Administration of CHARM to mice brain led to an 80 % reduction in prion protein levels with sustained silencing for at least 13 weeks. Epigenome editing has also been harnessed to silence MGMT, a gene whose downregulation sensitizes glioblastoma cells to chemotherapy [38]. In a study, delivery of CRISPRoff, a single fusion of DNMT3A-DNMT3L-dCas9-KRAB, as mRNAs encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNP) enabled long-term MGMT repression in primary cells and xenograft models [23,39]. Furthermore, CRISPRoff mediated multiplexed editing of four target genes sensitized the cells to a chemotherapeutic agent. In another example, a ZFP based variant of CRISPRoff (catalytically dead DNMT3A-DNMT3L-ZFP-KRAB) delivered as mRNA encapsulated in LNPs to mouse liver downregulated Pcsk9, a clinical target for cholesterol homeostasis [24] (Figure 3e). This epigenome editor, called evolved engineered transcriptional repressor (EvoETR), resulted in a ~50 % decrease in Pcsk9 expression that persisted for up to a year. Following partial hepatectomy, a procedure that induces liver regeneration, Pcsk9 silencing was found to be durable, indicating a successful one-shot delivery. Similarly, another group demonstrated that their epigenetic editor (EE), a single fusion of DNMT3A-DNMT3L-dCas9-KRAB, enabled silencing of Pcsk9 for up to a year in mice, with gene expression restored by dCas9-TET1 [40]. Finally, targeting PCSK9 in non-human primates was well tolerated and resulted in a 90% reduction in its expression.

A unique example of applying epigenome editing into the clinic involves targeting viruses that cause chronic liver infections. Two biotechnology companies, Tune Therapeutics (TUNE-401) and nChroma Bio (CRMA-1001), have demonstrated durable epigenetic silencing of hepatitis B virus (HBV) in mouse and non-human primate models and have advanced these approaches toward first-in-human clinical trials. By targeting both HBV DNA integrated into the host genome and the episomally maintained covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), these programs aim to achieve sustained repression of viral transcription. This highlights epigenetic silencing of chronic liver viruses as a future therapeutic direction [8].

In addition to programmable gene silencing, therapeutic applications of programmable gene activation are also emerging. A recent study used an AAV-delivered SadCas9-VP64 CRISPRa system to upregulate the Scn2a gene in a mouse model of SCN2A haploinsufficiency, a genetic cause of neurodevelopmental disorders including autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, and epilepsy [41]. Upregulation of the remaining functional Scn2a allele during adolescence rescued intrinsic and synaptic deficits in neocortical pyramidal neurons. Furthermore, systemic AAV treatment protected Scn2a heterozygous mice from chemoconvulsant-induced seizures. Together, this work establishes CRISPRa as a potential therapeutic for disorders driven by haploinsufficiency and highlights the potential of programmable gene activation for treating neurological diseases. Collectively, these studies highlight the therapeutic potential of durable and programmable epigenome editing platforms.

Key challenges

Despite the rapid advancement of epigenome and transcriptional modifiers, several key challenges and considerations remain in their application for basic and translational use. In this review, we discuss current barriers and propose future directions to accerelate progress in the field.

Off-target effects

A central goal of CRISPR-based technologies is to maximize on-target efficacy while minimizing off-targeting effects [42]. While the efficiency and on-off target ratio for genome editing technologies have been profiled extensively, similar comprehensive profiling of epigenome modulating platforms have lagged. With the expansion of the epigenome editing toolkit, there is an increasing need to assess its efficacy, specificity and safety.

Gauging the specificity of epigenome editors begins with profiling on-target chromatin modifications, such as chemical modifications on histone tails or DNA modifications. For example, the specificity of histone modifying epigenome editors at the target site can be measured using assays like ChIP [43], CUT&RUN [44], or CUT&Tag [45], for which antibodies that recognize specific epigenetic modifications on histones have been validated extensively. To assess changes in chromatin accessibility, ATAC-seq serves as a robust and reliable method [46]. For DNA methylation-based editors, locus specific targeting is assessed typically using bisulfite sequencing or Enzymatic Methyl sequencing (EM-seq), although novel long-read sequencing assays are being developed to directly profile DNA methylation without chemical conversion [47].

While on-target profiling provides insights into specificity, it is critical to measure the off-target effects to characterize the unintended outcomes of epigenome editing. For epigenome editing, off-target binding within gene bodies or non-genic genomic regions may be less detrimental than the DNA breaks induced by Cas9 nucleases/nickases at these sites [48–51]. Nevertheless, large-scale studies have shown that CRISPRi and CRISPRa can elicit off-target or toxic effects with a similar proportion of sgRNAs as observed for CRISPR knockout approaches, underscoring that transcriptional perturbations alone can have substantial functional consequences [52]. Off-target binding of epigenome editors to promoters or enhancers remain a concern as it can lead to unintended transcriptional responses [53]. This was highlighted in a study that evaluated the on-off target ratio in mammalian cells using dCas9-based DNA methylation fusions accompanied by targeting and non-targeting gRNAs [54]. While all editors induced targeted DNA methylation, most also triggered widespread off-target methylation regardless of guide specificity except for CRISPRoff, which showed the least off-target activity. These differentially methylated regions were found in GC-rich, promoter-like regions. In some cases, the off-target effects from certain editors persisted up to 30 days post-transfection. RNA-seq revealed modest but widespread transcriptomic changes for both targeting and non-targeting guides, with the latter eliciting more pronounced effects, suggesting methylation independent responses. Overall, this sheds light on how off-target effects in dCas9-based epigenome editing are not solely driven by sequence homology but can arise due to the promiscuity of the DBD such as Cas9 scanning the genome. Genome-wide counterparts of ChIP, methylation and chromatin accessibility assays remain crucial for profiling these off-target effects.

Overexpression of chromatin effectors in cells presents another layer of risk due to the potential to cause unintended global epigenetic changes and toxicity. For example, a comparison of different dCas9 repressor effectors in K-562 cells revealed cellular toxicity associated with the SID-dCas9-KRAB (Kox1), which was accompanied by widespread transcriptomic perturbations [11]. In contrast, Kox1 and Zim3 KRAB domains on their own did not induce similarly pronounced global cellular defects in this cellular context. Interestingly, subsequent studies have demonstrated that the tolerability of SID-dCas9-Kox1 can vary across cell types. For example, in A549 cells, long-term constitutive expression of dCas9-Kox1 and SID4x-dCas9-Kox1 did not impair cellular proliferation and was instead associated with modest increases in growth rates relative to control cells [55]. Together, these findings highlight that the impact of chromatin effector overexpression can be cell-type dependent, likely reflecting differences in endogenous chromatin states and transcriptional regulatory networks. Similarly, overexpressing the catalytic domain of DNMT3A has been linked to toxicity [37,56]. One strategy to circumvent this issue is by utilizing effectors that recruit endogenous chromatin modifiers, thus obviating the need to overexpress potentially toxic proteins. This was demonstrated with the CHARM system that recruits endogenous DNMT3A, enabling DNA methylation at the target site without directly overexpressing it [37]. Additionally, CHARM was further engineered with a self-inactivating module that silences its own expression after achieving targeted gene repression, thereby limiting prolonged activity and potential off-target effects.

Delivery

Delivery remains a major challenge as epigenome editors are generally large due to the size of the chromatin effectors. This limitation can potentially be addressed by using compact scaffold repressor domains like KRAB [9], which can recruit larger complexes (KAP1-SETDB1) capable of depositing repressive histone marks. Similarly, activating domains such as VP64, which when fused to dCas9, can recruit RNA polymerase and promote gene expression (Figure 1b). Furthermore, hits from the aforementioned screens for compact epigenome editors can further improve their delivery [18]. Another important factor to consider is that variations in the composition and positioning of nuclear localization signals (NLSs) can significantly influence nuclear import efficiency and chromatin residency of DNA-binding epigenome editors, thereby impacting epigenetic editing outcomes. These effects are likely to be particularly important in therapeutic contexts where chromatin accessibility and duration of exposure are often limiting.

The inherently disordered nature of chromatin modifiers complicates their in vitro purification for ribonucleoprotein (RNP) delivery. To address this, virus-like particles (VLPs) have emerged as a promising alternative delivery modality. Recent work highlights a protein-based delivery platform known as RENDER which utilized VLPs to deliver epigenome editors [57] (Figure 3e). RENDER CRISPRoff was used to successfully reduce Tau, a protein associated with various neurodegenerative diseases in iPSC-derived neurons. In general, VLPs have been applied for other CRISPR technologies and continues to be engineered for cell type-specific delivery [58–60]. Another example is the development of MAPLEX, a photoinducible exosome-based system for delivering dCas9-DNMT3A RNPs. In this system, light activates the cleavage of a photo-cleavable protein called mMaple3, mediating the release of dCas9-DNMT3A from the exosomal membrane into recipient cells. MAPLEX was used to target the β-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (Bace1) promoter enabling epigenome editing of Amyloid βeta (Aβ) in Alzheimer’s mouse models [61].

Context-dependency

One limitation in the field is the lack of comparisons of existing tools under matched conditions. Studies often evaluate editors using different target genes, cell types, delivery modalities, and DNA-binding architectures, making direct comparisons tricky. As a result, the relative efficacy, durability, or specificity of epigenome editors across studies often must be inferred. Cellular context is another important determining factor influencing the efficacy of epigenome and transcriptional modulation. For instance, stem cells are less permissive to durable DNA methylation than somatic cells, potentially due to high levels of activating H3K4me3 at CGIs (CpG islands) and active TET-mediated demethylation [35,56]. Thus, factors such as chromatin context and the cell-specific abundance of interaction partners can influence the efficacy of epigenome editing. Additionally, it is important to consider the influence of cis-regulatory contexts. In embryonic stem cells, the underlying DNA sequence and TF motifs seemingly play a role in shaping the response to specific epigenetic marks. For instance, YY1 motifs attenuated the repressive effects induced by H2AK119ub and H3K9me2/3, while E-box motifs buffered the effect of activation marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K27ac [20]. Strikingly, targeted installation of H3K36me3 at a reporter lacking CTCF motifs did not alter transcription, whereas introduction of a CTCF motif at the same genomic position rendered the locus sensitive to H3K36me3-mediated repression, resulting in potent silencing. This highlights the importance of the role of local cis sequences in overriding or amplifying the transcriptional effects induced by an epigenome editor.

Together, these insights underscore the need for careful and rational design of epigenome editors, alongside comprehensive validation of their on and off-target effects. Additionally, consideration of cellular contexts and cis regulatory elements is crucial to enhance their efficacy, specificity, safety and deliverability.

Conclusions and future directions

The converging advancements in genomics and genome engineering have produced robust tools for modifying the epigenome and controlling transcription of human genes at unprecedented pace. In the past 2–5 years alone, the development of high-throughput platforms has identified new effectors for programmable transcriptional repression and activation, thereby greatly advancing the toolkit for reprogramming epigenetics and transcription. The rapid pace of this field is evidenced by the therapeutic application of DNA methylation-directed silencing of genes in animal models to silence disease-relevant genes. Furthermore, pioneering biotechnology companies have been launched in this space with a focus to apply epigenome modulators for therapeutic use in the treatment and management of a variety of diseases [62].

As the field progresses, a mechanistic understanding of the effectors utilized for epigenome and transcriptional modulation remains essential. In addition to this, it is also necessary to systematically evaluate the potential off-target activities and cytotoxicity of epigenome editing. For example, DNA breaks induced by CRISPR nucleases have been associated with cell viability defects and chromosomal loss and rearrangement [48,49]. Given the complexity of epigenetic regulation, the consequences of off-target effects and its unintended consequences remain unknown, making this an active area of research. A rigorous head-to-head comparison of current epigenome and transcriptional modulators is therefore needed to define their relative advantages, limitations, and cellular impact. Addressing these challenges and barriers in the field will further advance the use of these technologies for fundamental research and therapeutic applications.
