Barriers to Radiomics Adoption for Urological Cancer Diagnosis in Low-Income and Middle-Income Countries: A Perspective from Pakistan
Authors: Awais Ayub, Hanan Mudassar, Maida Rizwan
Published: 2025

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the recent article by Yao et al,1 “Deep Learning Applications in Clinical Cancer Detection,” published online on July 18, 2025, in Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Digital Health. The authors rightly highlighted the importance of radiomics and artificial intelligence in oncology, and the relevant barriers to adoption in low-resource settings. We would like to add the perspective of a low-resource country facing substantial challenges in the diagnosis of urological cancers due to limited access to advanced detection technologies.2,3 In our region, patients often present at advanced stages of bladder, kidney, and prostate cancers due to delayed diagnosis. On the contrary, many patients undergo unnecessary biopsies. These late-stage presentations and complications from biopsy procedures contribute to increased morbidity, poorer outcomes, and add considerable burden on an already strained health care system.

Radiomics could be a game-changer here because it has immense potential in cancer care, such as screening, staging, treatment planning, and predicting recurrence.1 It may also help reduce unnecessary biopsies and holds promise for early detection.1 However, implementation of radiomics in these settings faces several technical and infrastructural challenges.3

The absence of locally validated radiomics platform is one issue. Most studies on radiomics have been conducted in high-income countries, with limited evaluation in low-income and middle-income countries.4,5 Artificial intelligence models from Western populations may not generalize effectively to other populations due to genetic and environmental variations, making local validation essential.4

Second, the lack of trained personnel and infrastructure further hinders the deployment of these technologies.2,3 Most radiomics models have been trained on 3T multi-parametric magnetic resonance imaging, which may not be applicable in many low-resource centers that typically have basic 1.5T systems or limited imaging sequences due to cost or availability. Hence, even open-source tools become impractical in such centers.

Lack of funding is another challenge, as health care systems in these countries are already burdened with infectious disease priorities, and radiomics is often seen as a low priority.2,3

To bridge this digital divide, we advocate for the development of open-source radiomics workflows compatible with low-cost scanners, accessible imaging alternatives, and cloud-based radiomics platforms to mitigate local hardware limitations.

We call for targeted investments in radiomics research with multicenter participation across low-resource countries for pooled datasets. There is a dire need for international partnerships focused on training, capacity building, and infrastructure support in such countries. These measures are essential to prevent the widening of disparities in cancer care.

In conclusion, radiomics holds enormous promise, but its full impact can only be realized if it is made accessible globally.
