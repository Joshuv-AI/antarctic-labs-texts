Implementation model for a national learning health system (IMPLEMENT-National LHS): a concept analysis and systematic review protocol
Authors: Jean-Baptiste Gartner, Boutheina Benharbit, Géraldine Layani, Maxime Sasseville, Célia Lemaire, Frédéric Bergeron, Catherine Wilhelmy, Matthew Menear, André Côté
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite efforts and repeated calls to improve the organisation and quality of healthcare and services, and in view of the many challenges facing health systems, the results and capacity to adapt and integrate innovations and new knowledge remain suboptimal. Learning health systems (LHS) may be an effective model to accelerate the application of research for real quality improvement in healthcare. However, while recognising the enormous potential of LHS, the literature suggests the model remains more of an aspiration than a reality.Methods and analysisTo reach a fine understanding of the implementation of the concepts involved in LHS, we will use a hybrid method which combines concept analyses with systematic review methodology. We will use a two-step analysis, a content analysis to analyse the definitions, uses and attributes of the concept and a systematic review to analyse the concept’s implementation mechanisms. We will search eight databases and grey literature and present a broad synthesis of the available evidence regarding design, implementation and evaluation of LHS in a multilevel perspective. We will follow the latest Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis statement for conducting and reporting a systematic review. Two reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria followed by full-text screening of potentially relevant articles for final inclusion decision. Conflicts will be resolved with a senior author. We will include published primary studies that use qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods. The assessment of risk of bias will be made using the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool.Ethics and disseminationThis systematic review is exempt from ethics approval. The results formulated will highlight evidence-based interventions that support the implementation of a national LHS. They will be of particular interest to decision makers, researchers, managers, clinicians and patients allowing finally to implement the promising proposal of LHSs at national scale.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42023393565.Keywords: organisation of health services, quality in health care, health policy, systematic review

Introduction

Despite repeated calls for more than 20 years to improve the organisation and quality of healthcare in countries such as the USA1–3 and Canada,4 as well as internationally by the WHO,5 6 suboptimal performance and inadequate use of resources persist.7–9 This includes significant levels of iatrogenic harm and inappropriate care,10 as indicated by patients receiving only half the care recommended by clinical guidelines.11 These problems stem as much from organisational, professional and data silos12 as from the failure to innovate and integrate evidenced-based knowledge in routine clinical practice.13 Pressures to improve the quality of care have sparked interest in health system redesign initiatives to meet public expectations and deliver greater social value.14 Indeed, improving health systems is one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time15 and modern systems that can integrate knowledge in daily care more efficiently have the potential to better contain costs and improve population health.10

The learning health system (LHS) model may be an effective model to accelerate the application of research for quality improvement in healthcare.16 17 Proposed in 2007 by the Institute of Medicine (now National Academy of Medicine) during the course of a series of workshops,18–26 this model has generated much enthusiasm in North American and worldwide.27–33 LHSs were defined as systems where ‘science, informatics, incentives, and culture are aligned for continuous improvement, innovation, and equity—with best practices and discovery seamlessly embedded in the delivery process, individuals and families as active participants in all elements, and new knowledge generated as an integral by-product of the delivery experience’.34 According to Friedman and colleagues,35 LHSs aim to generate new data from patient data and patient experiences, create knowledge from these data and then feed knowledge back into practice, thus generating a continuous learning cycle. This learning cycle has since become a central feature of several conceptual frameworks of varying degrees of complexity,36 37 from a simple three-step approach35 to more complex processes involving multiple steps or concepts.28 37 Regardless of the lens used to understand the learning cycle, its real-world implementation remains a challenging endeavour. The implementation process raises questions about how data and knowledge will be managed, how change will be evaluated and managed, what outcomes are deemed important and to whom’s priority do they respond. There are also questions about how decisions are made and who participates in them, with the LHS model encouraging a greater involvement of patients, families and communities in the design and improvement of care.38 Finally, the LHS model calls for the mobilisation of a wide range of researchers from different disciplines and a redefinition of the roles and responsibilities of professional actors, including physicians.39 Its application in the healthcare context involves an interdisciplinary approach39 combining knowledge of management science, evaluation science, data science and clinical science. To date, despite 15 years of effort, many authors argue that LHS remains more of an aspiration than reality,15 29 40 even speaking of a‘theoretical approach’.41 Indeed, although several examples of application have been reported, there are few concrete applications of the whole approach at the level of a healthcare system,42 43 either because the multilevel approach allowing a system-wide implementation is not addressed,44 or because the learning cycle is rarely implemented as a whole. In practice, its application has been gradual and siloed, notably focused on medical specialties.41 In addition, its application with a national-scale vision16 36 implies adaptations and learning at several levels.44 This is why, despite real efforts to clarify the concept and propose conceptual frameworks, guidance is needed for a valid and effective implementation model of LHS at the national scale which enables the systematic integration of research knowledge into clinical practice.45 While some work has focused on the operationalisation of the concepts involved in LHS,29 38 the formulation of an implementation model goes far beyond operationalisation. Indeed, implementation science, also known variously as knowledge translation or the science of knowledge mobilisation46 47 aim precisely at taking into account the integration of knowledge into practice by focusing on the mechanisms of uptake by the actors. Moreover, this approach recognises that implementation challenges cannot be seen as a localised problem for individual adopting organisations, but should be seen as global intervention with effects at the level of health systems,48 49 supporting the importance of developing a system thinking approach to the integration of knowledge and evidence.47 This is why, it is timely to define an implementation model of national LHS integrating a multilevel approach that are beneficial to all health system stakeholders, managers, professionals and are conceptualised with and for patients and that clearly responds to how to implement the concepts associated with the model.

A preliminary search for existing systematic reviews on the topic has been conducted in Cochrane Database, JBI Database of Systematic Reviews and Implementation Reports and PROSPERO. LHS have already been the subject of few reviews, but they have been limited in scope, consisting of an inventory,29 a proposed conceptual framework,28 a focus limited to a specific context such as primary care,32 or a particular aspect such as ethics,50 clinical analytics,51 the effects of LHS52 or community relationship.53 To the best of our knowledge, only two literature reviews aimed to clarify the use of the concept and its operationalisation.38 54 However, all two were limited to the organisational level without addressing the regional and national level necessary to integrate services in a cross-sectoral approach. Similarly, no distinction is made between the operational and organisational levels. Finally, while one of the two does not present any methodological part,54 the other used only two bibliographic databases (PubMed and Embase), focused only on conceptual models and not on empirical implementation examples and retained only 17 articles.38 Thus, no literature review has treated the implementation of a health system-wide learning health system, yet plebiscite,16 55 and specified the implementation and evaluation mechanisms and the roles of the different stakeholders in a multilevel and cross-sectoral approach.

Objectives

This systematic review aims to define an implementation model of LHS, considering administrative, organisational and social realities in a multilevel approach. Therefore, our systematic review of the literature will focus on the following research questions:

What are the prerequisites for designing and implementing a learning health system at operational, organisational, regional and national level?

Methods and analysis

To reach an understanding of the implementation of the concepts involved in LHS, we will use a hybrid method which combines a systematic review methodology with a concept analysis.56 57 We opted for a systematic review because it offers a robust and more comprehensive method than narrative or scoping review methods, especially as the number of empirical articles on LHS implementations seems to be growing strongly in recent years. Although the systematic review methodology provides a robust guide for searching, selecting and synthesising the literature in a systematic, transparent and reproducible way,58 an analysis of qualitative data can be strengthened with the use of complementary methods. Concept analysis59 aims specifically to clarify a concept and its uses including a semantic field linked to a specific theoretical framework. Once the concept has been defined, it enables an in-depth analysis of the use of the concept, the associated mechanisms and interventions, their implementation and the results obtained. This approach uses eight steps to: (1) select the concept; (2) determine the purposes of the use; (3) identify all uses of the concept; (4) determine the defining attributes; (5) identify model cases of applications; (6) identify additional cases; (7) identify antecedents as prerequisites and consequences as outcomes and (8) define empirical referents. The first phase will be to carry out a content analysis of the literature, including grey literature, in order to respond to steps 1 to 4 of the conceptual analysis. The data collected will be used to map the definitions present in the literature and the uses and attributes of the concept. The second phase will consist of a systematic review of the literature on the concept’s implementation mechanisms. Thus, using the methodologies of a systematic review and a conceptual analysis, we will first be able to highlight the different definitions of the concept as well as its uses and attributes. Then, in a second step, we will be able to appropriately identify the resources needed for implementation, the phases and mechanisms of implementation of the various interventions and the evaluation tools and methods associated to answer our three research questions.

We will follow the latest PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement for conducting and reporting a systematic review.60 Consistently, this protocol follows the PRISMA-P 2015 statement61 62 and the PRISMA-P 2015 checklist is provided (see online supplemental table 1). We have registered the present protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 21 January 2023. The review process will be completed within 6 months of protocol registration.

Eligibility criteria

The first phase, the content analysis, will consider all types of material, scientific articles or grey literature, which contribute to or develop the definition of the concept, its use or its attributes. Then, the second step, the systematic review, will consider all qualitative, quantitative or mixed-method primary research studies without methodological restrictions. Our search will focus on peer-reviewed empirical articles which mobilise a clearly described scientific methodology. Therefore, descriptive studies that describe the experience or the effects of the experience in the form of a report without any clear mention of the analysis methodology will be excluded. In addition, books, doctoral or master’s theses and abstracts from conference presentations will be excluded due to time and resource limitations. To guide study selection for the systematic review, we will use PICOS criteria (Participant(s) or population, Intervention(s), Comparator(s), Outcome(s) Settings) consistent with the objectives.

Participants or population

No restrictions. This review will consider all studies that include or focus on all types LHS actors such as, but not limited to patient, caregiver, provider, manager, researcher, decision maker, etc.

Interventions/phenomena of interest

This review will focus on studies that contribute to the implementation of the LHS model resulting in contributions on the prerequisites for the design, the implementation or improvement of the concept at one or more levels (operational, organisational, regional and national level) or the evaluation of the results following the implementation of the concept. Thus, theoretical or conceptual articles that do not include any implementation aspects will be excluded. In the same way, studies which attempt to describe LHS, or only describe features of an LHS, without using this terminology will be excluded.

Comparator

No restrictions. So, we will be open to any comparisons the literature contains.

Outcomes

We will consider all outcomes reported in the studies that will be included, from the stars to the neglected.63

Settings

The setting is healthcare systems, which are conceptualised as multilevel systems involving governance, operators and actors within, including patients. The context studied is then, composed of healthcare providers and governance in any geographic area. Healthcare providers include all providers of primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary care. These providers can be both healthcare organisations (general practice clinics, hospitals, intensive care units, emergency departments, long-term care facilities, etc.) and individual providers (physicians, nurses, administrators, pharmacists, etc.). All types of studies will be included (ie, studies using qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods).

Information sources

We developed two search strategies, in collaboration with a Health Sciences Librarian specialising in systematic reviews of the healthcare literature. First, we developed a dedicated search strategy, for the content analysis, aimed at capturing the articles and grey literature involved in defining the concept, its uses and attributes. An initial search strategy was therefore launched in Google scholar. Second, for the systematic review, we develop a search strategy to identify published peer-reviewed studies. We performed initial pilot searches in MEDLINE and CINAHL in order to assess the relevance of the reports retrieved on the basis of their titles and abstracts. This allowed us to refine our search strategies to ensure the capture of more relevant information. A comprehensive search strategy was launched on 15 November 2022, in the eight following databases: Medline (OVID), Embase (Embase.com), CINAHL (EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), Web of Science, ABI/Inform (ProQuest), Business Source Premier (EBSCO) and Google Scholar.64

Search strategy

For the first phase, the content analysis, a dedicated search strategy was launched with no restrictions on languages or dates. Limited to the first hundred references, the search returns 400 results (see online supplemental table 2). For the second step, the systematic review, different search strings have been designed and applied to each of the databases. The complete search strategy was tested by the Health Sciences Librarian. No study period restrictions were applied to the search, nor were there any restrictions based on language or geography. As our team is made up of several researchers who speak several languages (at least English, French, German, Spanish and Italian), for all other languages, the full text will be analysed by a translator specialising in scientific literature. The complete search strategy across the eight databases is provided (see online supplemental table 3). The search returned 4935 records that have been imported into the Covidence software, an Internet-based software that facilitates collaboration among reviewers during the study selection process, consisting of 2085 unique studies after automatic removal of duplicates. In addition, we will search the reference lists of included studies or relevant journals or authors identified by the search, as well as those of any literature reviews that will be excluded from the selection process.

Study records

For the systematic review, the team will develop and test screening questions and forms for levels 1 (title and abstract) and 2 (full text) assessments based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Citation abstracts and full-text articles will be uploaded with screening questions to Covidence. Before the formal screening process, following the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions,65 a calibration exercise will be performed with all participants in the screening phase to pilot and improve the screening questions. A pilot test to validate the selection process will be carried out on 5% of a random sample of all studies. Variability in reviewer assessment will be documented using Kappa.66

Further, we will provide training to new members of the review team not familiar with Covidence. Pairs of reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. In case of disagreement, an exchange between the two reviewers on the key points of the eligibility criteria will be undertaken. For the studies where disagreement persists, a senior reviewer will resolve the conflict after analysis and discussion with the two reviewers. Review author pairs will then independently screen the full-text articles for final inclusion decision. In case of disagreement, the same process as for the title and abstract selection will be used. Reasons for exclusion will be recorded. For the sake of consistency, one of the authors will screen all studies at all stages.

The reference lists of all included studies will be screened for additional studies, as well as those of all literature reviews rejected at level 1 (titles and abstracts screening). Finally, all selected studies will be presented to a group of experts on the concept who will seek to identify any key articles missing from the list. Any new studies identified at this stage will be screened against the eligibility criteria. We will present the selection process in a PRISMA 202060 flow diagram.60

Risk of bias for individual studies

The assessment of risk of bias for the systematic review will be made using the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), a tool adapted to systematic reviews of empirical studies including qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods.67 The MMAT can facilitate the critical appraisal process in systematic reviews of mixed studies by providing methodological quality criteria for different study designs. The blank grid is provided to help understand the tool (see online supplemental table 4). Each domain of the tool will be assessed by two independent authors. In case of disagreement, an exchange on the key points will be undertaken. Where disagreement persists, a senior reviewer will resolve the conflict after analysis and discussion.

Data extraction

Descriptive numerical summary analysis follows the systematic review guideline60 and will systematically extract the followed items: Reference (APA style), Title, First Author country, Year, Case country, Study design, Study settings, LHS name/institution, Population/Sample size, Study parameters and level of analyses (operational, organisational, regional and national level) and Definition of LHS retained.

Data for both qualitative and quantitative analysis will be extracted by two independent authors using a customised form. Again, for consistency, one author will review all included studies. The data extracted will follow the concept analysis guideline59 and systematically extract the followed items: Related concepts, Concept uses and objectives, Prerequisites or Inputs, Patients role, Professionals roles, Structural and organisational context of the system and organisations, Modes of operation, Coordination structures, Information management, Outputs, Performance dimensions, Performance indicators and Results obtained (qualitative and quantitative outcomes). Findings, and their illustrations, will be extracted and assigned a level of credibility. Authors of papers will be contacted to request missing or additional data, where required. The quality of the included studies will be evaluated and reported using the MMAT.67

Data synthesis

We will use a concept analysis framework59 as a data analysis framework. In addition, all information will be synthesised by level of the implementation (operational, organisational, regional and national level). We will use a convergent synthesis design68 to synthesise qualitative, quantitative and mixed-method results. Thus, using a thematic synthesis procedure, we will synthesise the objectives and prerequisites for the implementation of the LHS concept. We will then summarise and describe methods and approaches designed to implement the concept focusing, at a minimum, on the patient role, the professionals’ roles, the structural and organisational context of the system and organisations, the modes of operation, the coordination structures and the information management. Finally, the empirical referents, consisting of outputs and outcomes, will be the subject of a thematic synthesis of qualitative data and quantitative data with study subgroups gathered around the theme and differentiated according to the type of intervention and level of implementation in a descriptive manner when statistics and calculation methods are available. In this way, the effectiveness of the different implementation methods can be supported by the quantitative results obtained in the evaluation phase. We will also highlight the context in which the empirical studies were produced (the countries in which they were carried out), to understand any problems and incompatibilities that may exist for certain national systems. The heterogeneity of the outcomes of the different studies should not, in our opinion, allow for a meta-analysis. Finally, we will compare the results obtained from the content analysis with those from the systematic review, highlighting the gaps between the approach and its implementation, and possibly formulating a roadmap to achieve its full potential.

Ethics and dissemination

This systematic review is exempt from ethics approval because the work carried out will be based on published documents.

This project aims to produce an implementation model to provide a comprehensive guide to the implementation of a national LHS. Addressing an important gap in the literature, this systematic review will provide much needed knowledge regarding LHS design, implementation and evaluation at national scale. The findings will be disseminated in a related peer-reviewed journal and presented at conferences. The results should provide a roadmap for the implementation of a health systemwide LHS by specifying the implementation strategies at the different levels of a national health system. In addition, it will provide specific indications on the results of its implementation and the associated evaluation methods, enabling the focus of resources and energy on the implementation of a valid and effective model. Thus, the results formulated will highlight evidence-based interventions and implementation strategies that support the implementation of a national LHS. They will be of particular interest to decision makers, researchers, managers, clinicians and patients allowing finally to implement the promising proposal of LHSs at a national scale. Finally, the results will be used to develop a number of pilot projects at different levels and in several countries, with the aim of implementing national learning health systems.
