A new cross-disciplinary model for early integration of palliative care for patients with incurable cancer – an intervention development study
Authors: Johanna Swenne, Mette Stie, Torben Frøstrup Hansen, Britt Rasmussen, Kristine Nielsen, Jens Søndergaard, Ricko Damberg Nissen, Lars Henrik Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with incurable cancer face complex physical, psychological, social, and existential challenges. Early integration of palliative care (PC) has proven essential to improve quality of life and well-being, yet implementation remains inconsistent and often delayed. To address this gap, we developed the Cross-PALL model, a cross-disciplinary approach to integrate PC across oncology and primary care.MethodsFollowing the Medical Research Council’s framework for complex interventions, we used a participatory design approach to develop the model in collaboration with key stakeholders, including patients, their relatives, healthcare professionals from oncology, primary care, municipal services and representatives from voluntary organizations. Development involved iterative phases of evidence synthesis, needs assessment, field studies, collaborative workshops, and prototyping. For each of these phases, various methods and activities are applied to enhance genuine participation from all collaborators.Implementation determinants were continuously identified using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, and a logic model was constructed to link determinants, implementation strategies, mechanisms, and outcomes.ResultsThe Cross-PALL model is structured as a standardized care pathway designed to ensure systematic, person-centered, and coordinated PC. Key components include early initiation of PC at diagnosis, routine patient-reported outcome assessments, decision aids, navigation support, structured inter-sectoral communication, and integration of advance care planning.ConclusionThe Cross-PALL model offers a feasible, scalable, evidence-informed approach to strengthen early PC integration in oncology. By combining implementation science with participatory design, the model aims to enhance relevance, sustainability, and continuity of care, ultimately supporting patients’ quality of life and well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02048-6.Keywords: Palliation, Participatory design, Development, Model of care, Quality of life

Background

A diagnosis of incurable cancer brings profound physical, emotional, social, and existential challenges. Healthcare authorities have a clear responsibility to provide continuous support and guidance throughout the illness trajectory. Palliative care (PC) is widely recognized as an essential approach, with the primary aim of improving quality of life (QoL) for patients and their families. This is achieved through the early integration of person-centered care, comprehensive assessment, and targeted management of physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs [1].

The question is no longer whether PC should be integrated early in the course of incurable illness, but rather how this integration can be achieved in routine practice [2–4]. Despite international and national recommendations [5, 6], PC services are still introduced late, typically in response to escalating symptoms or crises [7]. At this stage, patients and their families are frequently already distressed, and therefore become passive recipients of care rather than engaging actively in care planning. Early PC seeks to move beyond a reactive model toward a proactive, supportive approach that emphasizes coping, shared decision-making, and advance care planning alongside disease-directed treatment [8, 9].

While specialist PC services have demonstrated positive effects on patient outcomes [10–12], reliance on specialist teams alone limits scalability and sustainability. Consequently, basic PC is increasingly regarded as a shared responsibility across healthcare sectors, involving oncologists, general practitioners (GPs), municipal services, and other health care professionals (HCP) within their routine clinical practice [13]. However, implementing structured, early PC across sectors remains challenging due to fragmented responsibilities, variable competencies, and entrenched perceptions of PC as synonymous with end-of-life care [14].

Implementation science offers a structured approach to addressing these challenges by focusing on how evidence-based interventions can be integrated into everyday clinical practice [15–17]. Rather than developing additional clinical evidence for early PC, there is a growing need for models that address the organizational, professional, and contextual factors influencing implementation across care settings.

Person-centeredness is a cornerstone of PC and requires attention to both QoL and well-being—concepts that are related but distinct [18]. QoL primarily reflects how illness affects daily functioning, whereas well-being encompasses broader existential dimensions such as meaning, purpose, and acceptance [19]. Recognizing this distinction has important implications for early PC models: while symptom management remains essential, supporting well-being ensures that care addresses the full human experience of living with incurable cancer [20].

Actively involving patients with incurable cancer and their relatives in the development of PC interventions may therefore be particularly important. Although patient involvement in PC is increasingly encouraged, such approaches remain uncommon, often due to concerns about patient vulnerability [21]. However, these concerns are frequently unfounded, and research suggests that participation can be both feasible and meaningful for patients with advanced illness [22]. Therefore, involving patients, relatives, and relevant HCPs in intervention development can help ensure that new care models are grounded in lived experiences, reflect individual values and preferences, and remain feasible within the realities of everyday clinical practice.

Guided by these considerations, we developed the Cross-PALL model—a new cross-disciplinary, person-centered approach to early PC integration across oncology and primary care—using a structured intervention development process informed by implementation science with a user-involvement approach.

Aim

The aim of this paper is to describe the pre-implementation development of the Cross-PALL model, including its underlying rationale and implementation strategies, and to illustrate how implementation science and user involvement can inform the development of early PC models in routine clinical practice.

Method

Design

The Medical Research Council (MRC) guideline of developing and evaluating complex interventions was used as framework for overall guidance of the study [23, 24]. It comprises four phases: development, piloting, evaluation and implementation [23]. This paper reports on the development phase. Within the MRC framework six core elements are emphasized and to be addressed continuously in an iterative process; (1) context, (2) develop, refine and (re)test program theory, (3) engage stakeholders, (4) identify key uncertainties, (5) refine intervention and (6) economic consideration [23].

The participatory design (PD) approach was integrated to engage key stakeholders and ensure that the users’ needs were represented and addressed in the model [25, 26]. The stakeholders (putative, potential or future) should be invited to cooperate as partners with the researchers and designers during an innovation process to ensure that the result meet their needs and is usable [25, 26]. Participatory approaches generate ownership as a precondition for sustainable change. It is a process of mutual learning through equal cooperation. Thereby ensuring the feasibility and relevance of the intervention in clinical practice and increase its chances of successful implementation and sustainability.

The integration of the two first phases of PD, “needs assessment and field studies” and “idea generation and modelling process”, into the MRC development phase. Through interviews, observations, and collaborative workshops, patients, relatives, and HCPs contributed experiential knowledge that informed both the content and structure of the model.

Implementation determinants and uncertainties, identified throughout the development process were systematically examined using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [27], addressing MRC elements related to context, key uncertainties and economic considerations. Following agreement on the final intervention construct, a programme theory was articulated using a logic model [28], linking identified determinants with implementation strategies, mechanisms, and intended outcomes, thereby supporting transparency and implementation readiness.

The development of the Cross-PALL model is reported in accordance with the Guidance for Reporting Intervention Development Studies in Health Research (GUIDED) checklist [29], which supports transparent reporting of the rationale, context, and iterative processes underpinning intervention development. The final model is described using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist [30], ensuring a detailed and reproducible description of the intervention components, delivery, and implementation considerations.

Setting

The Cross-PALL model was developed at the Department of Oncology at Lillebælt Hospital, University Hospital of Southern Denmark, in collaboration with the Research Unit of General Practice at the University of Southern Denmark and stakeholders from the municipalities of Vejle, Kolding, Middelfart, and Fredericia, covering a population of approximately 310,000 inhabitants [31].

Denmark has a tax-funded healthcare system providing universal and equitable access to care. All residents are registered with a general practitioner (GP), who serves as the primary point of contact and gatekeeper to specialized services [32]. The Department of Oncology at Lillebælt Hospital provides care for adult patients with a range of solid tumors and serves a geographically defined catchment area of approximately 400.000 inhabitants of the Region of Southern Denmark [33].

Basic PC are regarded as a core responsibility of all HCP in both primary and secondary care, integrated into their routine clinical practice. Access to specialized PC services, which include PC units, teams, and hospices, is provided through referrals [4].

Participants

Collaboration group

Patients diagnosed with incurable cancer, receiving medical treatment from the outpatient ward at the Department of Oncology, and their relatives were invited to participate in the development of the intervention, by being part of the collaboration group along with HCPs form the different sites involved. We also invited a consultant from the Danish Cancer Society due to their voluntary work for oncological patients and their relatives. A total of six patients, four relatives, thirteen HCPs (four from the primary sector and nine from the secondary sector) and one consultant from The Danish Cancer Society participated in the collaboration group. The patients were purposeful sampled to ensure variation in diagnosis, sex and time living with incurable cancer. All the participating HCPs accepting to participate had some interest and experience in palliative patients.

Patients were identified and invited by the first author based on predefined inclusion criteria. Eligible patients were adults diagnosed with incurable cancer and affiliated with the oncology department for ongoing evaluation of life-prolonging treatment options. Patients received oral and written information about the study, and participation was voluntary. No participants withdrew from the study after consenting to participate.

To address potential power imbalances between patients, relatives, and HCPs during the process in the collaboration group, structured facilitation strategies were used. These included working in smaller, mixed or homogeneous groups depending on the task, use of visual and creative methods to support expression, and facilitated plenary discussions aimed at ensuring that patient and relative perspectives were actively elicited and respected.

Research group

The interdisciplinary research group included two oncology physicians, an oncology nurse, a GP and an anthropologist. All were clinically active, except for the anthropologist, and all were involved in research. The oncology physicians comprised the chief physician, who was also an associate professor, and the head of the research unit at the Department of Oncology, who was a clinical professor. The GP is professor and head of the Research Unit of General Practice at the University of Southern Denmark. All members had extensive experience in developing and evaluating interventions within the health care system. The group was consulted in every phase and step of the intervention development which ensured leadership anchoring in the project.

All members of the interdisciplinary research group contributed analytical perspectives informed by their respective professional backgrounds. Together, the group engaged in reflexive discussions and joint interpretation of data, with particular attention to human aspects of care, including lived experience, meaning making, and interactions between patients and the HCS. This collaborative analytical approach ensured that multiple perspectives informed the development of the Cross-PALL model rather than privileging a single disciplinary lens.

Two health innovation consultants with expertise in health care architecture and design innovation, were connected to the project to facilitate the development and modelling process of the intervention. They cooperated with the research group and were needed as they hold highly developed skills that are relevant at larger levels of scope and complexity [26, 34].

Stakeholders

Leaders representing the GPs, the oncology department, the specialized PC unit and the municipalities were informed about the project and collaboration agreements were made.

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations were central to the development of the intervention, ensuring respect for patient autonomy, beneficence, and non-maleficence. Participants’ preferences, values, and wishes guided the design, while confidentiality and secure handling of personal data were strictly maintained. The research was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki [35]. Hence, the participants had the right to withdraw without any consequences for their future treatment.

Patients participating in the workshops were considered potentially vulnerable due to their incurable cancer. Particular attention was therefore paid to creating a safe and supportive workshop environment. Workshops were scheduled at times and locations familiar to participants, often coordinated with other scheduled appointments at the hospital. Sufficient breaks and flexibility to accommodate individual needs were ensured. Patients were encouraged to participate together with relatives if they wished, both to enhance comfort and to support shared reflection.

Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed that they could refrain from contributing to specific activities or discussions, take breaks, or withdraw from the workshop at any time without explanation. Workshop facilitators remained attentive to verbal and non-verbal cues of discomfort and explicitly reminded participants of these options when needed.

Through ongoing reflexivity and situational ethical awareness, facilitators navigated sensitive topics related to illness, death, and dying with care. This included openly and respectfully exploring participants’ readiness to engage in difficult experiences and adapting the depth and pace of discussions accordingly, to avoid pressing participants into topics they were not prepared to address.

Phase 1: identifying the evidence base

The first phase had two aims; (1) to systematically gather and synthesize existing knowledge relevant to the intervention, and (2) identify challenges, needs, wishes, barriers and facilitators from the users [23, 26].

Literature review

A scoping review was conducted to investigate similar interventions - models of cross-sectorial integration between primary care and oncology specialists that have been developed to promote early and basic PC [36]. Six models were identified, indicating a limited and heterogeneous evidence base. Across models, recurring components included early identification, education, structured communication, advance care planning, coordination, multidisciplinary collaboration, and involvement of informal caregivers. Key facilitators were trust, shared goals, and communication, while barriers related to fragmented care pathways, unclear roles, and contextual differences. These findings informed the Cross-PALL model by emphasizing early initiation, clear cross-sectoral roles, standardized communication, and systematic coordination as core design principles. The findings also reflected the importance of a continuous PC approach [36].

Needs assessment

Individual semi-structured interviews with patients diagnosed with an incurable cancer were performed with the aim of exploring the lived experiences of patients in the initial phase of an incurable cancer [37]. Hence the methodological approach is grounded in the phenomenology-hermeneutical tradition [38]. A secondary analysis was made on the collected data, with the aim to investigate how patients experience their encounter with the HCS in the early palliative phase, and what significance it has for their lived life [39].

The findings demonstrated that patients newly diagnosed with incurable cancer experienced profound disruption, uncertainty, and existential distress in the early phase following diagnosis. Patients expressed a strong urge to regain control and maintain normality and therefore engaged in a process of navigating their new circumstances moving towards reconciliation of their new situation, characterized by decisive action, reassessment of life priorities, and evolving self-perception. Simultaneously they often struggled with difficulties navigating an increasingly complex healthcare system and experienced unmet needs related to anticipatory guidance, continuity, and support for existential concerns, and found it challenging to engage in future-oriented discussions early in the illness trajectory [37, 39].

These findings informed the Cross-PALL model by underscoring the importance of the acknowledgement and assessment of patients’ existential experiences to support sense-making, navigation and adequate care planning aligning with their needs and wishes. Furthermore it can help HCPs to learn where the patients are in their illness journeys towards reconciliation, which is important to time conversations about the future in a way that aligns with patients’ readiness and lived realities.

Field studies

To understand the HCP’s practices, formal observations and individual and group interviews, were conducted with potential users, to map a generic illness trajectory for patients with incurable cancer at a practical, low-level detail using a service blueprint [40]. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants who contributed to the mapping. Relevant policies, guidelines and reports were studied and held against the current practice. Mapping the illness trajectory through service blueprinting highlighted multiple discontinuities in information flow, unclear transitions of responsibility between sectors, and missed opportunities for proactive coordination. The analysis further revealed that approaches to basic PC were highly person-dependent, reflecting varying understandings of what basic PC entails and when a patient is considered palliative. HCPs generally acted to the best of their ability within their respective roles and organizational positions; however, communication across sectors was often insufficient, and expectations of one another’s responsibilities were frequently misaligned. Together, these findings underscored the need for standardized communication content, predefined cross-sectoral consultations, and clearer integration of GP and municipal services.

Phase 2: Idea generation and modelling process

Potential and future users were brought together to work-shops with the aim to facilitate mutual learning and a joining reflection process to exchange knowledge and ideas about current and future practice inspired by the ‘Future Workshop’ method [26, 41]. All participants from the collaboration group were invited to participate in the workshops.

1st Workshop

The findings from the first phase provided the foundation for the first workshop, which consisted of different group work followed by plenary discussions. It was conducted inspired by the world-café’s seven design principles [42]. Eighteen participants from the collaboration group participated in a four-hour session. They were divided into three groups. Every group included at least one patient, one nurse from the department of oncology, one nurse from the municipality, and one physician.

In the first group work, the method of stakeholder mapping with created personas was used to create a wow effect among the participants and illustrate how overwhelming the encounter with ‘the system’ can be [43, 44]. The personas represented the actors (e.g. HCP) from the different sites of the HCS. This served as a starting point for a discussion on how participants perceived the other actors, as well as their own role.

In the second group work a customer journey mapping method was used [45]. Two patient trajectories, from time of diagnosis to death, were built using storytelling based on research data, connecting findings and insights. After a detailed description, the participants should go through the trajectory, reflecting over questions like what was working and what was not, why the patients and the relatives acted like they did and what they felt. Following the group works, reflections in plenum were facilitated to generate ideas and identify determinants to improve the HCP’s work with the fictive patients.

Data from the workshops, consisting of notes and drawings from the participants, photos and observations, were analyzed using a thematic approach [46, 47]. The findings are presented in Table 2. Initial coding and categorizing were conducted by the innovation consultants and subsequently discussed and refined through consensus-based interpretation with members from the research group having participated in the workshop. Analytic rigor was supported through reflexive discussions and triangulation across data sources and development phases.

Currently, the patients and their relatives spend very little time at the hospital compared to the duration of the process. The lived life is at home – not at the hospital.

Some terms and word used in a palliative care illness trajectory has to be demystified. The have to be made clear and easier to understand by removing confusion or mystery surrounding it.

Ideas:

• Introducing infected terms from the beginning and “planting seeds”.

• Using common language

• Talk about death and dying as a natural part of the illness trajectory

• Show interest in and approach every aspect of the patients’ life.

The patients often experience a fluctuating trust to the HCPs. Lack of trust to the GP is often mentioned in relation to being diagnosed. The person in question never gets to know if their relationship to a patient is disrupted, and therefor never has a chance to reach out and talk to their patient.

The patients do not know how to use their GP and for what. When trying to reach out, it is difficult to get in touch. One relative said “We cannot get through to our GP, resulting in that our attempts and interest are dying out”.

“I prefer talking to the nurses” is a common citation among the patients and their relatives. They do not see the physicians as having time for talking of their lived life, nor the relevance it can have for them.

The patients and their relatives have many presumptions towards the municipality and the services, especially home-care services, they can offer.

Continuity of care is a common desire but difficult to execute. The patients has a desire to be met by the same HCP every time they show up, which would build trust to the system.

As HCPs, we have to be better to lift each other up. To positively influence our patients trust to the system and individual HCPs. The advantages with the many possibilities within the HCS and society should be highlighted for the patients. It could bring hope!

The relation to the GP is critical due to continuity of care in the Danish HCS and therefore has to be established/worked on from the start.

Ideas:

• Correspondence to the GP about the patient’s perceived relation and level of trust (from the oncologist).

• Help to establish contact to the home-care and the GP after being seen the first time in the outpatient clinic at the department of oncology.

• Inter-disciplinary and inter-sectoral appointment in the patient’s home.

Collaborative prototyping

After the first workshop, the design process moved from the abstract to the concrete through reflections [48]. Through a constant movement between the three levels of abstraction; Vision, Operative Image, and Specification, a process of collaborative prototyping was established [26]. Implementation determinants and uncertainties were identified using the CFIR [27]. Potential areas for improvement in the future model were explored, and solution concepts were outlined and explored, using ‘How might we’- questions [49]. The two health innovation consultants and the first author, in collaboration with the research group, through an iterative process, led the process.

2nd Workshop

The aim of the second workshop was to work with the developed prototypes and mockups of solution concepts. Seventeen participants from the collaboration group gathered for another four-hour session. The workshop was divided into two sessions. In the first session the HCPs and the patients and the relatives worked in separate groups. The HCP discussed the content of a service blueprint, outlining a patient illness trajectory, including a sketch of cross-sectoral information-flow with details on content and timing. The patients and the relatives explored possibilities in a potential folder, aiming to help them to navigate the illness trajectory, through sketching [44].

In the second session all gathered to explore the use of and generate ideas regarding communication tools within the service blueprint. Data (notes and drawings from the participants, photos and observations) from the workshop were analyzed, using a thematic analysis approach as previously described [46, 47]. 3rd Workshop

As a result of the second workshop, a third additional workshop had to be arranged to work separately on a patient decision aid (PDA) prototype according to the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IDPAS) criteria [50]. The PDA is aimed at helping the patients to understand their options regarding treatment and ‘plant a seed’ for palliative care conversations to make an informed choice of treatment options [51]. Potential and future users from the collaboration group (n = 8) participated in a two-hour session, elaborating on the content and layout of a prototype.

The process of collaborative prototyping continued as an iterative process, with refinement of the model in response to accumulated data. Potential users (n = 12) that had not participated in the development process accessed the different components of the model for feasibility and to inform further refinements. With feedback and adjustments, the components were made acceptable, balanced and adequate.

Discussion

The aim of this paper was to describe the development of a comprehensive PC model, targeted at patients with incurable solid cancer and their relatives. Guided by the MRC framework for complex interventions with the integration of a PD approach, the model was developed through a systematic, step-by-step process in which existing research evidence was translated into clinical practice through collaboration with potential and future users. Hence, the complexity of the intervention and its multiple interacting components were made explicit and allowed for critical reflection throughout the process.

The process of development: co-creation

Applying a Participatory Design (PD) approach to the development of the Cross-PALL model revealed important tensions between the participating user groups. Patients, representing themselves primarily as persons rather than roles, articulated deeply personal experiential and existential concerns, which often required them to draw on inner, emotional, and vulnerable aspects of their lived experience. In contrast, HCPs contributed from clearly defined professional positions within the healthcare system, drawing on established biomedical frameworks, institutional responsibilities, and professional certainty, without the need to disclose personal or private experiences. These differing positions and forms of representation meant that patients became, and remained, more vulnerable participants in the co-design process.

This phenomenon “professional certainty” is referred to when HCPs are confident in the belief that they are already providing optimal care [53]. This can inadvertently create a barrier when participation in co-creation processes as any suggestions for change may be perceived not as improvements but as challenges to one’s competence or moral intent, potentially leading to resistance. Overcoming professional resistance requires efforts that not only acknowledge clinicians’ expertise but also addresses professional identity and emotional responses [54].

Tensions between the participants become especially evident in discussions about PC as a concept. Professional certainty, while grounded in clinical expertise and a desire to provide safe and effective care, came at times to dominate when communication occurred in ways that could be perceived as inaccessible to the patients, reflecting that we still operate within a paradigm of a selectively paternalistic and at times hierarchic clinician system [55].

Acknowledging and working through these tensions between these roles, positions and hierarchies, to uphold the democratic approach of PD, was a central component of the development process. Rather than seeking to eliminate disagreement, the PD allowed divergent perspectives to be surfaced, negotiated, and integrated into the final model. This reflexive process strengthened the relevance and feasibility of the Cross-PALL model by ensuring that it remained sensitive to both clinical realities and patients’ lived experiences.

Nevertheless, existing power imbalances and professional cultures inevitably shaped the interactions between participants. These different starting points for contributing with knowledge and experiences therefore call for different prerequisites to enable equitable participation within PD processes. Engaging patients in research has been argued to be highly relevant [56, 57] but can be problematic [58]. Consistent with previous observations, patients are most willing to participate in research [59]. Their contributions to our process might have been optimized had we involved the patients in our collaboration group differently with more comprehensive reflections of the character of their contribution beforehand as recommended [60, 61]. However, by paying attention to the process and being flexible during the workshops we managed to create safe space for the participants to more fully engage them in meaningful reflections and create a sense of ownership which is a precondition for change [62, 63].

The process of development: modelling

Across CFIR domains, several determinants highlighted variation in how basic PC was conceptualized and communicated. After investigating and elaborating with the different understandings of PC, it became evident that PC in our setting should be understood as an approach that permeates the entire HCS in encounters with patients to meaningfully influence the patients’ illness trajectory. By placing professional person-centered care [64] — of which the PC approach is essential at its core [65] — at the center of how healthcare services should be delivered, there should be no need to use the wordings ‘palliative’ which we found to be stigmatizing. We argue that PC is an approach that cannot be reduced to one or more isolated interventions or clinical services as constructed by the HCS [66, 67]. By applying this perspective on PC and using it as a strategy, we intend to overcome a well-known barrier to PC integration and influence the existing normative discourse of not only the care for the incurable ill persons, but also the prevailing discourse around death and dying [68, 69].

The SCP were developed to support this approach by reducing limiting, person-dependent practices and attitudes, and supporting less experienced HCP, thereby ensuring that all patients are, at a minimum, offered the same level of care [70]. The pathway was integrated into the existing framework of the HCS and aligned with the working routines at the different sites. By avoiding add-ons to the HCPs workload and focusing on a systematization of well-known procedures and approaches, the identified resistance to change, which is a well-known barrier to implementation [71], are intended to be minimized.

Across CFIR domains, several determinants highlighted patients’ difficulties navigating a fragmented HCS, including limited understanding of professional roles and unclear responsibility transitions between sites, mirroring previous findings [72]. Our study further showed that HCPs often had the same experiences, where the lack of a shared language and inconsistent and poor coordination and communication across the different sites constrained continuity of care. In response, the navigation tool Find vej i sundhedsvæsenet was designed to support patients’ orientation and sense-making by illustrating a typical illness trajectory without fixed time points, thereby avoiding normative assumptions about disease progression. The tool visually presents key HCPs roles, clarifies who may assist with what, and includes explanations of commonly used terms. Importantly, PC is framed not as a discrete service or referral, but as an approach to care that is integrated across settings and stages of illness. Moreover, CFIR determinants related to patients’ uncertainties, emotional distress, and difficulty articulating concerns informed the design process of the tool, which also aims at supporting patients in articulating what matters to them and facilitating expectation alignment with HCP. By functioning as a shared reference point, the tool is intended to support a common language across sectors, reduce reliance on individual professional discretion, and strengthen continuity despite organizational fragmentation.

Patient road maps represent a novel and emerging approach to promote patient-centered care by aiming to engage patients earlier in the care trajectory and provide anticipatory guidance [73]. Within the context of PC, comparable tools remain sparsely described in the literature. The development of Find vej i sundhedsvæsenet was an effort to translate identified implementation determinants—at both individual and setting levels—into a practical, person-centered design solution to support both patient and HCPs in an early and basic PC integration.

To further enhance co-ordination and communication between sites, our model has incorporated mandating specific touchpoints for follow-ups and transitional consultations in the primary sector. This proactive approach is meant to support and strengthen the HCPs relations with their patients and thereby ensuring smoother transitions and reducing crisis and perceived chaos later [8]. Furthermore, standardized phrases within the electronic medical records are used to enhance relevant, timely and understandable communication across sites. This structured approach aligns with previous research [74], which emphasizes the use of standardized communication activities to ensure that everyone is included and has a shared understanding of the patient’s condition and care plan as it has shown to more likely engage HCPs [75].

The integration of tools such as PDAs and systematic needs assessments (PRO questionnaires) with a continous application are not only well-know strategies to reduce variation in care but are also used to enhance patient involvement and empowerment [76, 77]. Together with conversations about life and thoughts about the future (e.g. ACP), these components support an approach aligning with a PC approach in everyday clinical practice and have been acknowledged as components of high-quality PC [78].

Strength and limitations

This study has demonstrated how the MRC framework with an integrated PD approach successfully guided the development process of the Cross-PALL model. The complexity of early and integrated PC was allowed to be unfolded and reflected upon in an iterative process, supported by CFIR.

The integration of the model within existing framework and workflows was made to make the model more feasible and to enhance the chances of sustainability and development of the achievements beyond its ending. All the HCPs from the different sites accepted the model in principle and their shared sense of ownership and responsibility is likely to facilitate implementation and foster a sense of security for both patients and their families, as all professionals work towards the same goal.

The study also has limitations. The participants in our collaboration group cannot be considered fully representative, as the patients, relatives, and HCPs as a population is extremely diverse. It was obvious that the HCPs who chose to participate had previous experiences with, or a special insight and interest in, PC and were highly motivated to bring PC onto the agenda not only in their own practice, but also within the broader healthcare organization. There was an absence of male HCPs, and GPs were underrepresented relative to their role in the illness trajectory. Therefore, acceptability must be in focus in the piloting of the model. While facilitation strategies were used to encourage open dialogue, social desirability bias cannot be ruled out in group-based workshops, as participants could have been inclined to express views perceived as acceptable within the group or aligned with professional or research expectations.

The process of going through the many steps in the MRC framework with the PD approach is time consuming. It has taken three years from the literature review (2022) to the piloting of the new model (2025). Having sufficient time to be thorough in one’s work is an advantage; however, given the rapid global development of PC and the influence of political interests, it is essential for the researcher to stay up to date and continuously integrate the latest recommendations into their work.

Moreover, the Cross-PALL model was developed within a GP-based HCS characterized by universal coverage and clearly defined referral pathways. Nevertheless, many of its core principles—such as the integration of person-centered care aligning with the PC concept and structured cross-sectoral communication—are likely transferable to other HCS. However, adaptation to local organizational structures, professional roles, and financing mechanisms would be necessary.

Conclusions

Following the MRC framework for complex interventions, the Cross-PALL model was developed as a pre-implementation intervention to support early and integrated basic PC for patients with incurable cancer. A PD approach was used to enhance the model’s clinical relevance, feasibility, and potential for long-term sustainability.

Following the MRC framework, we designed the Cross-PALL model to facilitate early and integrated palliative care for patients with incurable cancer. Employing a participatory design approach ensured the intervention’s clinical relevance, practicality, and long-term sustainability.
