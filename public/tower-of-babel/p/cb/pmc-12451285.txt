Virtual reality perimetry facilitates visual field evaluation in a previously non-assessable eye with severe glaucoma
Authors: Grant T Slagle, Sylvia L Groth, Sean P Donahue, William E Sponsel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVirtual reality perimetry (VRP) is rapidly growing in popularity. Advantages of these devices have been noted in the literature: they require less capital investment than table-mounted perimeters, have a smaller footprint, and improve patient comfort. Unlike traditional perimeters, virtual reality perimeters can assess both eyes simultaneously, which it does by default on most of its testing algorithms. This novel function leads to an advantage of VRP over traditional perimetry that has not yet been reported: the ability to assess the visual field of an eye with poor fixation. We report a patient with advanced glaucoma who could not perform reliable standard perimetry due to a central scotoma in one eye but could perform VRP testing of each eye simultaneously.ObservationsA 23-year-old male with advanced congenital glaucoma in the left eye presented for follow-up perimetry testing. In prior visits, his right eye consistently demonstrated a normal visual field, but left eye showed a progressively worsening global defect with poor reliability indices. At the present visit the patient performed VRP, which tested both eyes simultaneously. Good fixation with the right eye facilitated a reliable visual field of the left eye.Conclusions and importanceThe virtual reality perimeters tests both eyes simultaneously, which traditional perimeters cannot do. In this case, fellow eye fixation facilitated the acquisition of a clinically meaningful visual field from a poorly fixing eye due to advanced glaucoma.Keywords: Central scotoma, Fellow eye fixation, Glaucoma, Non-fixating eye, Perimetry, Virtual reality perimetry

Introduction

The popularity of virtual reality perimetry (VRP) is rapidly expanding, and many studies have demonstrated their clinical utility across a spectrum of patient ages and ophthalmic conditions, including glaucoma.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 While standard automated perimetry (SAP) with the Humphrey Field Analyzer (HFA) remains the gold standard for the evaluation of glaucoma, several advantages of VRP have been noted in the literature. Virtual reality perimeters require a smaller capital investment compared to SAP, are mobile, do not assume a large office footprint, are not reliant on patient head positioning, and do not require trial lenses (patients can wear their own corrective lenses).6,7 In addition, some patients prefer VRP to SAP.8 These characteristics make VRP useful in evaluating children, inpatients, or those who cannot be accommodated to a table-mounted perimeter.

Traditional table-mounted perimeters have been the only option for visual field evaluation for decades, but the emergence of virtual reality perimeters in recent years may signal a shift in the visual field testing paradigm. There is evidence that VRP is reliable and has good agreement with SAP in glaucoma, especially in milder stages.9, 10, 11 Perimetry becomes especially important in late stage glaucoma, when optical coherence tomography (OCT) becomes less reliable.12

Herein we report a patient with severe monocular central visual field loss in one eye, and who for five years had been unable to provide meaningful visual field results in that eye using SAP; the unique characteristics of VRP allowed reliable testing of the visual field in this eye. This is an unreported feature of VRP that may be beneficial to many patients where one eye has eccentric or variable fixation but who maintains good central vision in the fellow eye.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old male with a history of congenital glaucoma in both eyes presented for follow up and visual field testing. He underwent placement of Ahmed model S2 tube shunts (New World Medical, Rancho Cucamonga, CA) bilaterally as a child. His best corrected visual acuity was 20/60 in the right eye (OD) and count fingers at 1 m in the left eye (OS). Intraocular pressures (IOP) were 19 and 25 mmHg, respectively. Clinical exam revealed mild infantile nystagmus and no obvious strabismus. Slit lamp exam demonstrated mild band keratopathy and iris colobomata bilaterally. Both tube shunts were in good position with blebs elevated to 4+ according to the Moorfields Bleb Grading System.13 Bilateral band keratopathy precluded any view of the retina or optic nerve.

The patient underwent simultaneous bilateral testing of OD and OS visual fields using the 24-2 standard VisuALL (Olleyes, Summit, NJ, USA) VRP algorithm with stimulus size III. The results OD showed high fixation losses and a small superior defect with an otherwise full field. OS demonstrated good reliability with generalized depression along with a superimposed nasal-sparing superior altitudinal defect, and an inferior central-involving scotoma extending from the blind spot with notable inferior-temporal sparing.

The same test was repeated one week later to evaluate for reproducibility. The second test had no fixation losses and low false positives in both eyes (OU). OD redemonstrated a small superior defect with an otherwise full visual field. OS again showed generalized depression with a superimposed nasal-sparing superior altitudinal defect and an inferior altitudinal defect with temporal sparing. The reproducibility of the spared portions of the visual field OS (superonasal and inferotemporal) was noted. The results of the first and second tests OU are displayed in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. The VisuALL VRP was able to test OU independently and simultaneously, allowing the fixation OD to stabilize gaze in the fellow eye.

The test was repeated one week later (b) to confirm the results. Findings are notable for consistent good reliability in the left eye (OS) (which cannot fixate), a small reproducible superior defect in an otherwise full field in the right eye (OD), and large center-involving scotomata with reproducible superonasal and inferotemporal sparing OS.

Although the patient had previously been able to perform consistent perimetry in each eye, for the most recent five years he had only been able to complete SAP (Humphrey Visual Field Analyzer, Carl Zeiss Meditech, Inc. Dublin, CA) in his better seeing eye and had the often-observed “clover leaf” field OS (Fig. 2). The classic cloverleaf field is indicative of either inadequate attention, or in his case, inadequate fixation.14 In this case, the patient lacked fixation OS, so while he may have perceived the seed points early in the test, without the ability to fixate, his gaze quickly drifted beyond the 24–30° cone evaluated in the 24-2 perimetry test. This simulated inattention and resulted in a cloverleaf pattern on the printout. The new reliable perimetry results OS were used as a new baseline for continued glaucoma evaluation and management.

The most current three Humphrey perimetry tests with stimulus size V for the left eye in 5-month intervals (from top to bottom) in a 23-year-old male patient with bilateral congenital glaucoma, done five years prior to the introduction of the virtual reality perimetry (Fig. 1). Poor reliability and a cloverleaf pattern with near complete field loss is seen in all three visual fields and is worse in the final field (bottom visual field). At the time when the final cloverleaf pattern was observed, it was determined further testing with Humphrey perimetry would no longer be clinically useful in this eye.

Discussion

Traditional table-mounted perimeters test one eye at a time, and require the eye being tested to also have reliable fixation, as the fellow eye is occluded during the examination. The virtual reality perimeter, by contrast, performs the test with both eyes open, and each eye isolated in front of a separate dedicated 125.4mm × 70.56mm (1920 × 2160 pixel) display, each with a 100-degree field of view and refresh rate of 75 Hz.6 During bilateral simultaneous perimetry with VRP, each display maintains a fixation point at the center of the field of view, and the testing algorithm selectively alternates on which display (OD or OS) to shine a stimulus. Thus, in an orthotropic patient capable of central fixation in only one eye, the non-fixating eye is assessed while the “good” eye maintains fixation. The reliability indices employed by the VisuALL perimeter include assessment of fixation losses by the Heijl-Krakau strategy, false negatives, and false positives.6

The implications of the above case are vital to a subset of patients who have lost the ability to fixate in one eye, but maintain the ability to fixate in the fellow eye. Many of these non-fixating eyes may have been written off as “unable to assess.” Too often, such results are interpreted as subtotal blindness, and prevent appropriate monitoring of progression of visual loss, but these eyes may be assessed by VRP. In this case, band keratopathy prevented any view of the posterior segment. A central scotoma OS had also prevented any meaningful perimetry results for the previous five years despite use of a size V test object on HFA. Therefore, the only data available to the ophthalmologist in management of this patient's glaucoma during this extended period was IOP and OCT. By using VRP, this patient was again able to reliably perform perimetry, even with stimulus size III, which added valuable information that informed further management of his advanced glaucoma. It is unlikely that the change in perimetric testing devices (HFA to VRP) accounted for the observed visual field differences (cloverleaf pattern with HFA and clinically meaningful visual field with virtual reality perimeter) in this case. Multiple studies indicate good agreement between the VisuALL virtual reality perimeter and HFA in the setting of glaucoma.9,11

While previous studies have demonstrated many favorable characteristics of virtual reality perimeters, the benefit of using fixation in one eye when testing the fellow eye has not been reported. This is a unique feature of VRP, and directly results from the device's ability to display a separate screen to each eye.

Our patient had infantile nystagmus but it was mild and did not interfere with field testing. Patients with infantile nystagmus with a latent component (or fusion maldevelopment nystagmus) usually are precluded from performing automated perimetry monocularly, but should also be able to be tested using VRP. We hypothesize similarly that monocular vision loss from non-organic (i.e. functional) etiology can also be disproven using VRP for the same reason.

Other types of perimetry that test both eyes simultaneously (binocular field testing for driver's license, disability determination, or diplopia evaluations) evaluate the overall visual function and preclude testing only one eye. VRP testing has a separate display for each eye. Therefore, a stimulus can be shown on both displays simultaneously, or on either the right or left display individually. This allows for monocular evaluation while both eyes are open.

Patients need to be orthotropic to allow for binocular testing with the VRP. When a patient has any strabismus or both eyes cannot fixate on the same point, the manufacturer recommends using monocular mode, which tests each eye individually by only illuminating the screen for the eye being tested. If a patient has minor convergence insufficiency during the test, it is corrected using a proprietary Dynamic Matrix algorithm. Other potential limitations of virtual reality perimeter VRP include a limited range of stimulus and background luminance compared to HFA.15 The majority of visual field research since the advent of automated perimetry was done on table-mounted perimeters, and while there is evidence of parity between VRP and traditional perimetry, results between devices may not be perfectly transferrable.

In this case report, we demonstrate a unique and novel use-case for VRP in the setting of performing automated perimetry in a patient with one poorly seeing eye, and a fellow eye with good fixation, utilizing fellow eye fixation. Simultaneous bilateral visual field testing with the VisuALL virtual reality perimeter provided new capacity for monitoring visual field progression and informing therapeutic management for the non-seeing eye. We believe the transition to binocular testing is an advantage that should be leveraged in patients who have lost unilateral fixation.
