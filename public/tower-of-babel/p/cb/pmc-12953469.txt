Maternal–fetal attachment trajectories in IVF mothers during the first trimester: a prospective observational study
Authors: Xuzhen Cheng, Fangfang Chen, Huiling Huang, Fang Zhang, Xinfen Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify trajectories of maternal–fetal attachment (MFA) in IVF mothers during the first trimester of pregnancy, and to explore categorical predictors of subgroups demonstrating MFA trajectories.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted on 495 IVF mothers. Self-report questionnaires assessing MFA, anxiety, depression, social support, and marital satisfaction were completed at three time points: 3–4 weeks (T1), 7–8 weeks (T2), and 12–14 weeks (T3) of gestation. Repeated measurement data were analyzed and growth trajectories identified.ResultsThree MFA trajectories were delineated in IVF mothers during the first trimester: low-attachment/slow-elevation group (30.9%), moderate-attachment/steady-elevation group (56.2%), and high-attachment/rapid-elevation group (12.9%). Secondary infertility (OR = 0.575, 95% CI: 0.368–0.901, p = 0.013) and higher marital satisfaction (OR = 0.941, 95% CI: 0.915–0.968, p < 0.001) were predictors for not being in the low-attachment/slow-elevation group.ConclusionAmong IVF mothers with ongoing pregnancies, a subset exhibited suboptimal development of MFA during the first trimester. Marital satisfaction and infertility type may help identify those at potential risk.Keywords: Maternal–fetal relations, Prenatal attachment, Pregnancy, Infertility, Mental health

What does this study add to the clinical work

This study identifies heterogeneous trajectories of MFA in IVF mothers during the first trimester, with marital satisfaction and infertility type as early indicators of risk.

Introduction

Maternal–fetal attachment (MFA) denotes the relationship defined by the extent of emotional communication and interaction between pregnant women and their unborn fetuses [1]. MFA is intimately connected with the health of both mothers and infants. High levels of MFA are accompanied by positive health-related behaviors during pregnancy [2–4], which are linked to superior neonatal physiological outcomes (e.g., a healthy birth weight) [5] as well as more favorable delivery outcomes (e.g., reduced rates of obstetric complications and cesarean sections) [6]. Conversely, low levels of MFA are correlated to negative perinatal emotions, such as depression and anxiety [7, 8], potentially having an adverse impact on the socioemotional development in their future infants [9, 10].

Pregnancy is the period marked by the most complex physiological and psychological changes for women. In most cases, MFA generally develops spontaneously to prepare for the infant’s arrival, yet a minority of pregnant women experience a slight rise or no alteration [11]. In recent years, with the maturation of in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET), the number of IVF mothers (conceived via IVF-ET) has risen. Earlier studies have reported that the psychological state of IVF mothers does not significantly differ from those of women with natural conceptions regarding MFA [12–14]. But recently, some researchers have argued that negative emotions during IVF treatment, such as anxiety and depression [15, 16], and vulnerability [17], persist into pregnancy and can influence the optimal development of MFA [18–20]. Maehara noted that infertile women face a unique contradictory psychology during the transition to “pregnant mothers”, characterized by uncertainty regarding pregnancy outcomes and a delay in MFA formation [21].

The group characteristics of MFA in IVF mothers remain elusive. A longitudinal study investigated the trend of MFA in IVF mothers during 9–20 weeks of gestation [22], but without case grouping. Ersanli found no link between prenatal attachment and affective disorders in pregnant women past 20 weeks, suggesting cohort studies tracking emotions from early pregnancy for clearer insights [23]. The MFA generally originates in the first trimester and peaks or stabilizes by the third trimester [24, 25], demonstrating an evolving trend across gestational weeks. As MFA during each stage of pregnancy has been proven to be closely interconnected [9], it is reasonable to hypothesize that the trajectory of MFA in early pregnancy may predict its subsequent development. Assuming the MFA development in IVF mothers exhibits heterogeneous group characteristics, identifying high-risk groups with impaired early pregnancy attachment holds significant clinical importance. This not only pinpoints the target population, but also offers sufficient time for intervention, which minimizes the adverse impacts of the levels of MFA at the earliest opportunity.

The study aims to explore the developmental trajectory of MFA in IVF mothers during the first trimester and examine its influencing factors. This endeavor will contribute to the identification of high-risk groups with attachment impairment and the formulation of intervention strategies in the future.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective observational study which was conducted from September 2023 to May 2024 at a tertiary obstetrics and gynecology hospital in Zhejiang Province, China. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the hospitals (No. IRB-20230303-R).

Participants and sample size

Participants who returned for pregnancy confirmation 10–15 days post-embryo transfer during routine clinical follow-up were screened for eligibility. Those with serum beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) concentration > 5.3 IU/L and who signed informed consent were included. Those with active or severe mental and psychological disorders were excluded. Participants who: (1) experienced pregnancy loss (biochemical pregnancy loss, miscarriage, or ectopic pregnancy, etc.); (2) withdrew from the study midway; (3) were lost to follow-up despite repeated attempts were removed during the follow-up period.

Sample size calculations considered that previous studies, which employed the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) as the primary metric for model selection, reported a minimum requirement of 200 valid cases [26]. Under the assumptions of a 24% pregnancy loss rate in the first trimester [27, 28], 2% ectopic pregnancy rate [29], and 10% loss of follow-up, at least 313 participants were required.

Measurements

The Maternal Antenatal Attachment Scale (MAAS) was developed by Condon in 1993 [30]. We used the Chinese version translated by Nie Ge [31]. The 19-item questionnaire consists of two scales (intensity and quality), assessing the pregnant women’s emotional attachment to their fetuses. Each item is coded with points (1–5) that are added up to a total sum, with higher scores indicating stronger attachment. Reliability for the MAAS in the current study was considered adequate (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.774, 0.811, and 0.827 for each wave, respectively).

The Pregnancy-specific Anxiety Questionnaire (PAQ) was used to detect pregnancy-related anxiety among Chinese pregnant women [32]. The PAQ consists of 13 items scored from 13 to 52, with higher scores reflecting greater anxiety severity. The PAQ showed acceptable to good reliability across all waves, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.804, 0.778, and 0.811.

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) is the most widely validated screening instrument for perinatal depression [33]. The EPDS consists of ten items scored from 0 to 3 assessing depressive symptoms during the past 7 days, with higher scores indicating more serious depression. The EPDS maintained good internal consistency throughout the study, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.793, 0.831, and 0.848, respectively.

The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) assesses individuals’ subjective perceptions of social support [34], consisting of 12 seven-point items. Higher scores reflect stronger perceived social support. The PSSS exhibited excellent internal consistency in this study, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.931, 0.953, and 0.960 across three waves.

The ENRICH Marital Satisfaction is a subscale derived from the Olson ENRICH Marital Scale [35], which is a ten-item questionnaire used for evaluating marital relationship satisfaction. Scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating better marital quality. In this study, its internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.844).

The sociodemographic details and medical histories were self-reported by the participants.

Data collection

An electronic questionnaire encompassing the MAAS, PAQ, EPDS, PSSS, and marital satisfaction was distributed in the form of QR codes. Baseline assessment (T1) occurred on the day of enrollment after informed consent. Repeated measurements were carried out for all scales except marital satisfaction, as it is theoretically stable. The study time points were determined based on the number of days post-embryo transfer (T1: 10–15 days, 3–4 weeks of gestation; T2: 30–35 days, 7–8 weeks of gestation; T3: 70–75 days, 12–14 weeks of gestation), which aligns with key clinical checkpoints in IVF pregnancies and coincides with scheduled hospital visits, thereby maximizing follow-up rates and minimizing participant burden. A dedicated WeChat work account was used to facilitate follow-up, solely to send reminders and links (via non-traceable QR codes) to anonymized surveys. No personally identifiable information, sensitive medical data, or private communications were exchanged. The electronic questionnaire can only be submitted if all questions are answered to ensure response completeness.

Statistical methods

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 signifying a significant difference.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and qualitative data as frequencies (%). A one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare non-normally distributed data with external norms. For repeated-measures comparisons, repeated-measures ANOVA was applied, with Mauchly’s test of sphericity and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections when appropriate; otherwise, the Friedman rank sum test was employed for data violating normality.

Latent class growth models (LCGM) were established using the Mplus version 8.3 to analyze the trajectories, which were estimated with robust maximum likelihood (MLR). For intergroup comparisons of trajectories, the Chi-square test was used for qualitative variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for quantitative variables. Logistic regression analysis was finally conducted to explore the influencing factors of heterogeneous trajectories.

Results

All 495 individuals who were screened met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled at baseline; of these, 425 (85.86%) completed all three assessments. Participant flow and attrition reasons (pregnancy loss and non-response) are presented in Fig. 1. The mean age of the 425 participants was 32.07 ± 3.94 years, of whom 52.7% were between 30 and 35 years old. A majority (66.1%) held a bachelor’s degree or higher. Primary and secondary infertility were nearly equally prevalent (52.9% versus 47.1%), with 48.7% reporting infertility for less than 3 years. There was no statistically significant difference in the baseline characteristics between completers and attritors. Detailed information is provided in Table 1. Three assessments of the study, which were conducted per the prespecified protocol, occurred at 12.31 ± 2.02 (T1), 33.54 ± 3.50 (T2), and 75.12 ± 4.58 (T3) days post-embryo transfer.

Figure 3 shows the longitudinal patterns of three potential category groups. Group 1 had 131 cases (30.9%) and had the lowest level of attachment (intercept = 58.649 points). Though showing an upward trend in subsequent assessments, the growth rate was the least pronounced among the three groups (slope = 1.097, p < 0.001), so it was named as ‘low-attachment/slow-elevation group’. There were 239 cases (56.2%) in group 2, which showed a moderate level of attachment (intercept = 68.881 points) with a stable upward trajectory (slope = 1.286, p < 0.001), so it was named as ‘moderate-attachment/stable-elevation group’. 55 cases (12.9%) in group 3 presented the highest attachment level (intercept = 78.106 points) and the most rapid growth trajectory (slope = 1.406, p < 0.001), so it was named as ‘high-attachment/rapid-elevation group’.

Predictors of maternal–fetal attachment trajectories in IVF mothers during the first trimester

Group 1 demonstrated the lowest level of MFA among the subgroups studied, identifying it as a priority population for attention and screening. To identify predictors associated with this at-risk trajectory, groups 2 and 3 were combined into a single reference category labeled the ‘moderate–high attachment/elevation group’, which served as the control group in subsequent analyses. Since this study documented stable changes in PAQ, EPDS, and PSSS during the first trimester, baseline data collected at T1 were used as independent variables in subsequent statistical analyses.

As shown in Table 6, infertility type and marital satisfaction were independent predictors (p < 0.05). Secondary infertility demonstrated a significant protective effect against membership in the low-attachment/slow-elevation group (OR = 0.575, 95% CI: 0.372–0.889, p = 0.013). Each 1-point increase in marital satisfaction score was associated with a 5.9% reduction in the likelihood of belonging to the low-attachment/slow-elevation group (OR = 0.941, 95% CI: 0.913–0.969, p < 0.001).

To verify the above findings and enhance clinical utility, we supplemented the analysis with an endpoint analysis focused on the T3 time point (Table 7). Participants with the bottom tertile of the MAAS at T3 (≤ 67) were classified into the low endpoint group (n = 147, 34.6%). Results indicated that secondary infertility (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.34–0.78, p = 0.002) and lower marital satisfaction (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.98, p < 0.001) were independent predictors of low attachment at T3, which was highly consistent with the main analysis predicting low attachment trajectories.

Discussion

In this study, we prospectively observed the temporal changes in maternal–fetal attachment (MFA) in IVF mothers during the first trimester of pregnancy and identified distinct trajectories of MFA. On the whole, there was a progressive elevation in MFA in IVF mothers throughout the first trimester, which is consistent with our clinical observations. Gestational age is the most significant predictor of MFA [36].

Next, our study delineated three distinct heterogeneous trajectories of MFA. IVF mothers in groups 2 and 3 exhibited relatively high levels of MFA during the first trimester. They demonstrated rapid establishment of MFA, with gradual strengthening of bonding over time, and showed strong positive anticipatory attitudes toward the perinatal process. IVF mothers in group 1 exhibited the lowest initial MFA levels and the smallest growth magnitude compared to the other two groups. Even at the trajectory peak, this subgroup remained at lower attachment levels relative to the other subgroups, struggling to establish strong attachment bonds with the fetus rapidly. These findings identify them as a high-risk group warranting targeted clinical interventions. Our finding is consistent with a mixed-methods survey conducted by Toshinori Kitamura et al. [37]. MFA encompasses various types, including positive bonding and ambivalent bonding. However, their study was cross-sectional and only investigated MFA topics at one point. In contrast, our study is novel because it is a prospective observational study and provides insights into the development trends of MFA. Although IVF mothers anticipate pregnancy, some of them suppress their joy during the gestation process and exhibit a hypervigilant attitude toward pregnancy. Even minor or trivial symptoms can trigger an exaggerated state of fear. Sanli pointed out that maternal anxiety about losing the fetus and the resulting stress can lead to delayed or impaired MFA [38].

Our findings echo previous studies demonstrating marital satisfaction exerts an influence on MFA [39–42]. High levels of marital satisfaction indicate harmonious spousal relationships, which enable individuals to perceive a profound sense of intimacy with their partners. This, in turn, allows them to better cope with pregnancy-related physiological changes and psychological fluctuations. IVF mothers face greater challenges during the reproductive process. Elevated marital satisfaction accelerates their psychological adjustment, maintains fertility intentions [43], promotes rapid recovery from fertility setbacks, and stimulates positive pregnancy emotions.

While prior studies linked MFA of IVF mothers to depression and social support [44, 45], our analysis revealed no independent effects of these factors. Mediator interaction may weaken their impact on MFA. Marital satisfaction emerged as a concise independent predictor. These findings support early pregnancy screening for marital satisfaction in IVF mothers. Husbands’ involvement during pregnancy should be encouraged. Elieh Abasi et al. [46] showed that couples-based attachment training significantly enhances MFA, supporting its development in early gestation.

Furthermore, we found that infertility type was associated with the development of MFA during the first trimester. Specifically, compared to primary infertility, women with secondary infertility exhibited a significantly lower likelihood of developing a high-risk group. A cross-sectional study indicated that primigravidas and women with no children have higher levels of MFA [47]. Beauquier-Maccotta et al. [48] found that pregnant women with a history of miscarriage had lower attachment in early pregnancy. These previous research findings are inconsistent with our findings, and there is no discussion regarding infertility type. IVF women with primary infertility lack confidence in their own fertility. Assisted reproductive technologies lead them to focus more on external medical treatments rather than emotional communication with the fetus. In addition, maternal role development inherently involves significant stress adaptation processes. IVF women with secondary infertility who have pregnancy experiences can adapt to stressors more actively, while those with primary infertility require a longer adjustment period during the transition to motherhood. In this regard, psychoeducation and fetal ultrasound examination are beneficial [37]. Psychoeducation provides counseling to support adaptation to the maternal role. Fetal ultrasound, by displaying fetal images, redirects pregnant women’s focus toward the fetus, effectively alleviating pregnancy-related anxiety and enhancing MFA [49]. A systematic review reported that combining fetal ultrasound with interactive care that meets emotional and cognitive needs further enhances attachment relationships [50]. Although infertility type represents an unchangeable factor, our results offer actionable insights for enhancing healthcare efficiency. These findings underscore the importance of focusing on MFA of IVF mothers with primary infertility, optimizing resource allocation for at-risk groups during pregnancy management.

This study cross-validated the key risk factors by combining LCGM with endpoint analysis. Both methods showed that lower marital satisfaction and secondary infertility predicted both a low attachment trajectory and low endpoint scores at T3. Notably, “embryo transfer cycle type” was only marginally significant for trajectory prediction (OR = 0.642, p = 0.057), not the endpoint, suggesting its influence pertains more to the process than to a single time point. Furthermore, we explored an indicative threshold (MAAS ≤ 67 at 12–14 weeks of gestation) for identifying individuals who may be at greater risk of lower MFA. With further validation, this threshold could potentially inform early identification efforts.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths lie in its longitudinal design across three first-trimester time points, which captured the dynamic development of MFA and revealed group heterogeneity through LCGM. Although requiring further validation, this work is the first to propose a first-trimester reference value for low MFA, which may help guide future research on early identification.

This study has limitations. The single-center design and exclusion of pregnancy loss before 12–14 weeks limit generalizability to broader IVF populations. All data were self-reported, and without testing for longitudinal measurement invariance, subtle shifts in measurement cannot be ruled out. Baseline variables were treated as static (though stable here), neglecting their potential dynamics. Further, the focus on identifying high-risk groups over exploring all subgroup predictors may have lost nuanced subgroup information, and the short study period precluded linking trajectories to long-term maternal and infant outcomes. Finally, while the model identified key correlates, its predictive performance could be improved. Future research requires larger samples, more predictors, and a focus on psychological trajectories after pregnancy loss.

Conclusion

Among IVF mothers whose pregnancies continued to 12–14 weeks of gestation, we identified heterogeneous trajectories of MFA during the first trimester. Despite the overall favorable development of MFA, a minority face a risk of impaired early attachment formation, which links to low marital satisfaction and primary infertility. We strongly encourage the pursuit of in-depth investigations into MFA among special pregnancy cohorts, as such efforts are crucial for optimizing maternal and infant health outcomes.
