Injectable, oxygen-releasing, thermosensitive hydrogel promotes vascularized bone formation with prolonged oxygen delivery and improved osteoinductivity
Authors: Yixin Xu, Shaowei Zheng, Zinan Tang, Qiang Zhong, Rong Chen, Pinkai Wang, Jinlang Fu, Jiajun Xie, Yanhong Ning, Mingyuan Lei, Ding Wang, Huaming Mai, Hao Li, Chunhan Sun, Zhanjun Shi, Hao Cheng, Zhe Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe failure or delay in healing of critical bone defects is primarily due to early local anoxic conditions and reduced osteogenic activity. In this research, we integrated calcium peroxide (CPO) embedded polycaprolactone (PCL) microspheres and osteoinductive nanoparticles (Hydroxyapatite/Laponite) into a thermosensitive hydrogel (Pluronic F127), thereby formulating an injectable oxygen-releasing osteogenic thermosensitive hydrogel. Notably, the oxygen-releasing microspheres (ORMs) within the composite hydrogel provide stable oxygen release for up to 21 days, ensuring the survival, migration, and bioactivity of both mesenchymal stem cells and endothelial cells under anoxic conditions. Additionally, the composite hydrogel significantly augments the osteogenic potential of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells by providing a biomimetic microenvironment with the incorporation of nano-hydroxyapatite/laponite. Ultimately, the injectable composite hydrogel successfully stimulated bone regeneration within a cranial defect in a rat model after 8 weeks, with enhanced vascularization and bone quality. The engineered hydrogel provides a minimally invasive approach to stimulate bone regeneration with a sustained oxygen supply and osteogenic microenvironment provision, underlining its potential for treating critical bone defects.Keywords: Oxygen-generating materials, Osteoinductivity, Thermosensitive hydrogel, Osteogenesis, Angiogenesis

Introduction

Critical-sized bone defects, often resulting from fractures, infections, and bone tumors, have become increasingly prevalent [1]. The treatment of bone defects primarily relies on autologous or allogeneic bone grafting, which has been long used in clinics and yields satisfactory results for treating bone defects [2]. However, challenges arise from the autograft including the limited supply and potential risks such as local infection, bleeding, and sensory abnormalities during the procurement process [3]. Allogeneic bone grafting is economically burdensome and carries the risk of immune rejection as well as the potential for disease transmission [4]. Consequently, the challenges promote the development of artificial bone substitutes. Artificial bone substitutes offer several advantages such as ready availability, no need for additional surgery to harvest bone, customizable materials, and modifiable properties [4]. However, they also have disadvantages like potential infection risk, possible biocompatibility issues, difficulty in matching natural bone's mechanical properties, high cost, and lack of biological activity which might slow down healing [4]. To this end, there is still a need to develop innovative regenerative biomaterials that can efficiently stimulate local bone regeneration.

The failure or delay in the healing of critical bone defects is primarily engendered by early local anoxic conditions [5]. Bone defects are typically accompanied by local microvascular rupture, significantly diminishing the supply of oxygen and related factors [6,7]. While anoxia can stimulate angiogenesis in the body [8], it requires not only an increase in quantity but also the assurance that the formed vessels are functional and capable of effectively transporting oxygen and nutrients. Under acute anoxic conditions (O2 <0.02 %), endothelial cells die rapidly, preventing the formation of new blood vessels [9,10]; pathologically abnormal neovascularization formed under chronic anoxic exposure lacks physiological function due to its abnormal twisting, branching, or leaking [11]. This ultimately leads to a vicious cycle of ischemia and anoxia. Reduced oxygen levels not only affect the proliferation and vitality of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) but also inhibit their metabolic conversion and osteogenic effects [12,13]. Consequently, anoxia weakens the therapeutic efficacy of bone regeneration and repair. In this regard, oxygen-generating biomaterials have been developed and used to meet metabolic demands before efficient perfusion is rebuilt [14]. Common materials capable of producing oxygen include liquid peroxides [15], fluorides as oxygen carriers [16,17], and solid peroxides [[18], [19], [20]]. Of these, solid peroxides, such as calcium peroxide (CPO), are preferred due to their high oxygen generation capacity and low toxicity [21]. The oxygen release mechanism of CPO involves the hydrolysis-driven formation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), followed by its decomposition to generate molecular oxygen [14]. However, the use of solid peroxides confronts challenges as cells could potentially be exposed to swiftly produced high oxygen concentrations during the hydrolysis process. This could induce cellular death via the generation of free radicals [22]. Hence, the regulation of oxygen release kinetics is required to control oxygen delivery. Previous strategies predominantly involved the direct amalgamation of oxygen-generating materials with other substances, yet this technique proved insufficient in extending the duration of oxygen release. The latest strategy in recent years involves introducing hydrophobic barriers into these oxygen-generating materials to reduce the direct contact between solid peroxides and the aqueous environment [7]. The greatest advantage of this method is its ability to regulate the release of oxygen from solid peroxides in a more enduring and precise manner. polycaprolactone (PCL), a multifunctional synthetic polymer approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for drug delivery [23], is widely used in the development of sustained-release systems due to its ease of molding and manufacturing, excellent biocompatibility, and slow degradation rate [24,25]. Consequently, employing PCL to encapsulate CPO can effectively achieve a controlled release of oxygen.

While oxygen-regenerating biomaterials can ensure the survival of cells around critical bone defects through continuous oxygen release, there persists a problem of reduced local osteogenic activity [26]. Hence, the implanted bone regenerative biomaterials also need to be endowed with osteogenic potential. Compared to the application of growth factors (high cost and uncontrollable stability) [27,28], using bioactive and osteogenic nanoparticles in the scaffold has offered more feasibility and economic viability. For example, Nano-hydroxyapatite (HAP) and Nano-silicate (SN) have become particularly favored materials in the field of bone defect treatment [29]. HAP is a primary inorganic component of natural bone and teeth and is widely used as an additive in the manufacture of bone-mimicking substitutes and bone tissue engineering polymers [[30], [31], [32]]. After the addition of HAP, matrix materials exhibited significant improvements in mechanical performance, structure, and compositional similarity to natural bone extracellular matrix (ECM), thereby promoting biocompatibility by maintaining cell viability and enhancing cell proliferation [[30], [31], [32]]. SN [Laponite: Na+0.7[(Si8Mg5.5:Li0.3)O20(OH)4]−0.7] have garnered considerable attention due to their superior osteoinductive potential [33,34]. Compared to other bone-promoting particles like Whitlockite, nano-silicate materials offer multiple advantages. They enable controlled release of beneficial ions, such as silicon, lithium, and magnesium [35], which enhance bone cell activity and stimulate angiogenesis, aiding bone repair and regeneration. Their nanoscale size and high surface area make them versatile for biomedical applications, including drug delivery and tissue engineering scaffolds, and their synthesis is often more cost-effective, making them suitable for large-scale use. Additionally, degradation products of SN, for example, silicic acid (Si(OH)4) and lithium [36,37], distinctly facilitate the synthesis of vital bone extracellular matrix constituent, type I collagen, and modulate the expression of runt-related transcription factor-2 (RUNX-2), further promoting bone formation [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. Although there are concerns about the potential toxicity of lithium released from nano-silicates [40], our study shows that the released lithium is minimal and controllable. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed the biosafety and biofunction of the nano-silicate-embedded hydrogels.

In summary, SN can effectively promote osteogenic differentiation without the need for any exogenous bone-inductive factors. In addition, it is confirmed HAP and SN could exhibit synergistic effects in promoting bone formation in a biomimetic manner [41]. Thus, delivering HAP and SN into the bone defect region will significantly enhance local osteogenic activity and promote ossification.

From the perspective of clinical application, another factor that renders the repair of critical bone defects challenging is their common irregular shape and deep location [42]. Irregularly shaped defects and limitations of operating space in deep areas limit the usage of many modular bone regenerative biomaterials and effective osseointegration [42]. Thus. materials could be delivered to the bone defect site by a minimally invasive approach and shaped after application into the body is highly welcome [41,43]. Pluronic F127 (PF-127), is a nonionic triblock copolymer surfactant that is made up of poly(ethylene oxide)-poly(propylene oxide)-poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO-PPO-PEO) [44], possesses the ability to transform into a sol-gel state in situ at physiological temperature (37 °C) at certain concentrations [45]. Thus, the thermosensitive hydrogel possesses the advantage of filling any irregular bone defects with a local puncture injection method [46]. Meanwhile, PF-127 is considered biocompatible and non-toxic and has been employed as a delivery vehicle in various realms of tissue engineering [44], such as chondrogenesis [47], neurogenesis [48], angiogenesis [49], osteogenesis [50], and wound healing [51].

In this study, CPO-PCL microspheres and HAP/SN were introduced in PF-127 to fabricate an injectable thermosensitive composite hydrogel to treat bone defects. The introduction of CPO-PCL microspheres provided the sustained and slow release of oxygen locally over an extended period. The incorporation of nanoparticles helped rebuild the essential osteogenic microenvironment. The PF-127 hydrogels acted as an injectable delivery vehicle to distribute and fill CPO-PCL microspheres and HAP/SN into the bone defect area, finally fostering vascularized bone regeneration (Scheme 1). The injectability, oxygen-generating properties, bone-inductive capabilities, and biocompatibility of the composite hydrogel were comprehensively evaluated in both in vitro and in vivo studies. The injectable oxygen-releasing osteogenic thermosensitive hydrogel was expected to offer a novel option for the treatment of critical-sized bone defects.

Materials and methods

Materials

[Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 was provided by RuiTaibio (Beijing, China). Hoechst and Crystal violet staining were provided by Solarbio (Beijing, China). trypsin-Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (trypsin-EDTA), phalloidin, α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM), Alexa Fluor 594/488, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), phalloidin, α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM), L-glutamine, and antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin) were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Anti-OCN antibody (MABD123) and Anti-OPN antibody (AB1870) were provided by Millipore (Burlington, MA, USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Amplex Red, CCK-8 cell viability reagent, Calcein-AM/PI, ALP staining kits, Arlizarin red staining (ARS) kit, Anti-CD31 antibody (AG2849), Anti-Ki67 antibody (AG2646) and monoclonal antibodies for macrophages (CD86,(AG1453); CD206, (AG2664)) were provided by Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Anti-Flk-1 antibody (10407-RP01-400) was provided by Sino Biological (Beijing, China). Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and endothelial cells (ECs) were provided by Southern Medical University. Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were provided by the Experimental Animal Center at Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China).

Pluronic F127 preparation

Pluronic F127 was meticulously blended into deionized water (DI water), maintained at a chilled temperature of 4 °C, to achieve a solution with a 20 % weight/volume concentration. This solution was then stationed on a magnetic stirrer and permitted to dissolve completely over a span of 24 h. Following complete dissolution, the Pluronic F127 solution was subject to sterile filtration utilizing a bottle-top filter with an aperture of 0.22 μm. The sterile, filtered solution was thereafter preserved at a refrigerated temperature of 4 °C in readiness for future application.

Construction of polycaprolactone microparticles laden with calcium peroxide (CPO -PCL microparticles, ORMs)

The microparticles entrusted with oxygen production consisted of CPO emulsified within a PCL framework, which respectively functioned as the oxygen-providing agent and the hydrophobic barrier. To prepare the hydrophobic phase, PCL was dissolved in chloroform to create a 13.5 % weight/volume solution, which was subsequently agitated continuously with a magnetic stirrer. CPO was then incorporated into this PCL solution at a substantial concentration of 60 mg/mL to initiate the formation of the microparticles. In the preparation of the aqueous phase, a low molecular weight polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution at 0.5 % weight/volume served as the stabilizing agent. The thickened PCL-CPO blend was incrementally introduced into the PVA solution, whilst being stirred magnetically at a vigorous speed of 1000 revolutions per minute, resulting in the formation of the microparticles. These microparticles were then centrifuged at a gentle speed of 800 revolutions per minute to discern the disparate phases and to recover the microparticle pellet. After the removal of the surplus aqueous and viscous phases, the microparticle pellet was left intact. To adequately eliminate the residual chloroform, the concentrated microparticles were sequestered within a vacuum desiccator for an extensive period of 4 h. The microparticles were stored under dry, dark conditions at 25 °C. To maintain the composite hydrogels' optimal functionality, attributed to the reactivity of CPO, all composite hydrogels are synthesized when needed.

Fabrication of composite thermosensitive hydrogels

In the creation of the assortment of composite thermosensitive hydrogel specimens, a confluence of HAP and SN, together with ORMs, were meticulously integrated within a 20 % solution of Pluronic F127 in DI water. This amalgamation occurred independently, forming samples like PF/HAP/SN or PF/ORMs, or in unison, as in PF/HAP/SN/ORMs. Following this, a cross-linking process was enforced, utilizing bespoke polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold at a controlled temperature of 37 °C for a precise duration of 300 s, heralding the successful fabrication of the composite thermosensitive hydrogel specimens. Variegated concentrations of HAP, SN, and ORMs were introduced into the hydrogel compositions in isolation to identify the most propitious concentration for these particles. In pursuit of the optimal concentration, a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay was executed: BMSCs (1 × 104) were cultured with various PF-based hydrogel groups at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 3, 6, and 12 h. The absorbance of the final supernatant was measured using a 96-well microplate spectrometer (SpectraMax i3; Molecular Devices) at 450 nm. The formula for calculating cell viability in the CCK-8 assay is as follows: Cell viability (%) = (Measured Value - Blank Value)/(Control Value - Blank Value) × 100 %.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

The morphological characteristics of HAP, SN, CPO, and ORMs were inspected employing a Supra55 model SEM by Zeiss. In parallel, the lyophilized samples of the hydrogel were meticulously scanned with SEM to elucidate their intricate internal microstructures. The freeze-dried samples were fixed to the surface of the aluminum sample stage using a conductive adhesive and then transferred to a sputtering device (ETD-2000C, BOYUANWEINA, China). After evacuating the chamber, gold sputtering was performed at a current of 10 mA for 120 s. Subsequently, the sample was moved into the SEM chamber and after applying a vacuum, imaging was performed using an Inlens detector at an operating voltage of 15 kV with an aperture of 30 μm and a working distance of 8.5 mm to observe the sample surface at various magnifications form.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis of composite thermosensitive hydrogels

The hydrogel samples was meticulously examined through FT-IR, utilizing the sophisticated Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10 Infrared FT-IR. For this analysis, the hydrogel samples were initially ground into a fine mixture in unison with potassium bromide. The resulting homogenous powder mixture was then coalesced into dense tablets by the exertions of a hydraulic press (Anatomic Instruments NLFY-60, based in Tianjin, China), and these compacted specimens were thereafter probed by the aforementioned FT-IR equipment.

Rheology testing

The rheological attributes of the specimen were quantified employing the ARES-G2 rheometer from TA Instruments. The shear thinning property was discerned by assessing the shear viscosity under a continuous flow regime at a gamut of shear rates extending from 0.01 to 1000 s−1, precisely maintained at 37 °C. Concurrently, the dynamic evolutions of the storage modulus (G′) and the loss modulus (G″) were meticulously evaluated over a thermal spectrum spanning from 20 °C to 40 °C.

Swelling ratio assays of composite thermosensitive hydrogels

For the execution of the swelling examination, the hydrogel specimens were crafted into precise cylindrical shapes, adhering to the previously established protocol. The crafted specimens were then submersed in PBS and allowed a period of 1 h to reach a state of swell equilibrium. The initial phase involved the determination of the mass of the hydrogel after swelling, denoted as M (s). This was followed by a lyophilization process to desiccate the hydrogel, upon completion of which, its desiccated mass was recorded, indicated as M (l). The swelling ratio was subsequently deduced using the formula: Swelling ratio = (M (s) – M (l))/M (l).

Oxygen release kinetics

The experimental methods were organized as: 5 mL of PF/HAP/SN/ORMs was prepared in a 50 mL Falcon tube (LABSELECT), then 20 mL of deionized water was added and maintained at 37 °C in each test tube. A sensitive oxygen probe (JPBJ-609L, LeiCi, Shanghai, China) was used to regularly record the dissolved oxygen concentration in the test Falcon tube for 21 days.

Quantification of H2O2 release kinetics

The generation of H2O2 was discerned through the utilization of the Amplex Red hydrogen peroxide assay kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher), following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer [52]. Each hydrogel specimen was ensconced within its tube along with 1 mL of PBS. To obviate the likelihood of evaporation, the aperture of each tube was tightly sealed with parafilm, and the samples were then preserved at room temperature. On a daily basis, a 10 μL aliquot of the supernatant was collected from every tube, with the removed volume promptly replenished by an equivalent measure of PBS to maintain equilibrium. This harvested supernatant was subsequently diluted in PBS to proceed with the hydrogen peroxide analysis in a comprehensive volume of 100 μL for each microplate well. The conclusive readings of the assay were registered using a 96-well microplate spectrometer (SpectraMax i3; Molecular Devices) at 570 nm, facilitating the precise quantification of the outcomes.

In vitro cell culture

BMSCs and ECs cultures were meticulously maintained within an incubator set at an optimal temperature of 37 °C, furnished with a precisely calibrated atmosphere containing 5 % CO2, and sustaining a high relative humidity level of 95 %. The culture media were diligently refreshed at regular intervals, specifically every third day. Once the cell monolayers reached a confluence of 80 %, both BMSCs and ECs were gently disassociated from their substrate employing the trypsinization technique to prepare for further passages. In the subsequent co-culture experiments involving interaction with biomaterials, the cells were strategically positioned within the inferior chamber of a transwell insert (CORNING, China), characterized by a pore size of 0.4 μm. Meanwhile, the biomaterials were placed atop, the superior chamber of the same apparatus. Following assembly, the entire setup was subjected to incubation conditions with normoxic or anoxic environments, using as a substrate either the standard nutrient formula (α-DMEM with 10 % fetal bovine serum for BMSCs and ECM with 5 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % ECGS for ECs) or an osteoinductive culture medium tailored for enhanced differentiation (α-DMEM with 10 % fetal bovine serum, 1 % β-Glycerophosphate, 0.2 % Ascorbic acid, 0.01 % Dexamethasone).

Intracellular O2 level determination

The quantification of intracellular oxygen levels was facilitated by [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 [53]. Initially, BMSCs (3 × 104) were cultured in 12-well plates and subjected to either normoxic or anoxic conditions overnight to acclimate them to the desired oxygen concentrations. Subsequently, a dosage of 10 μg mL−1 [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 was introduced to the environment for an additional 12-h incubation period. Following this incubation, cells were cocultured with or without ORMs. After a 24-h interval, the cells underwent a double rinse with PBS to ensure the removal of extraneous substances. The resulting fluorescence emitted by [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 was then measured, deploying a fluorescence microscope calibrated with a 450 nm excitation wavelength (λex) and a 610 nm emission wavelength (λem), to accurately gauge the intracellular oxygen concentration.

Scratch test

Cellular migration was assessed employing the Scratch test. To initiate the process, a cell suspension comprising a density of 2 × 105/mL BMSCs and ECs was prepared respectively, and 1 mL of this suspension was introduced to the wells of a 6-well plate, further complemented by the addition of 1 mL of growth medium. Upon achieving 80 % confluence, a sterile 1 mL micropipette tip was utilized to inflict a precise scratch, effectively simulating a wound across the confluent cell monolayer. Any cells dislodged during this procedure were carefully removed to prevent interference with subsequent observations. Subsequently, biomaterials were placed within the upper compartment of a Transwell insert, orchestrating a controlled co-incubation with the wounded cell layer. Imaging sessions were scheduled at 12 and 24-h intervals post-injury using an inverted optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). The images thus obtained were meticulously analyzed by applying ImageJ software (version 1.53) to measure the extent and rate of wound closure as an indicator of cell migratory capacity.

Cell migration

BMSCs and ECs were seeded into the upper compartment of a Transwell insert at a density of 5 × 104 cells per chamber respectively. Hydrogels were then strategically placed into the lower segment of a 24-well culture plate. Following co-culture over intervals of 12 and 24 h, the cells were preserved in situ by fixing them with a 4 % solution of paraformaldehyde (PFA) before undergoing staining with crystal violet to highlight their morphology. The non-migratory cells on the upper membrane surface were meticulously removed with a gentle sweep of a cotton swab, ensuring that only those cells that had successfully migrated and adhered to the underside of the membrane remained. The migrated cells were then visualized and scrutinized under an inverted optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). Detailed analyses of the captured images were conducted using ImageJ software (version 1.53) to ascertain the migration efficiency of the cells through the membrane.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and Alizarin Red S staining

The BMSCs cultured in a 6-well plate were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min. The cells were then stained using an ALP staining kit and a 2 % (weight/volume) solution of Alizarin Red S. The stained cells were subsequently visualized using an inverted optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). Quantitative analysis of ALP activity was performed using a colorimetric assay following an established protocol. This assay exploits ALP's ability to enzymatically convert colorless p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) into yellow p-nitrophenol (pNP). At specified time points, cells cultured on hydrogels were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in ALP buffer to release the enzyme for detection. The lysates were then thoroughly homogenized. For quantitative assessment, aliquots of the cell lysates were combined with a 5 mM pNPP solution in a 96-well plate under light-protected conditions. After incubating at room temperature (25 °C) for 60 min, the absorbance at 405 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3, Molecular Devices).To quantitatively assess calcium accumulation, Alizarin Red-stained specimens were placed in a culture chamber containing a 10 % acetic acid solution overnight. The next day, the solution was centrifuged at a high speed of 20,000 g for 15 min to separate, and then collect the clear supernatant in new tubes. To neutralize the acidity of the solution, an equal volume of 10 % ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH) was introduced. The neutralized samples were aliquoted in 100 μL portions per well in a 96-well assay plate for quantification. The absorbance of each sample was carefully measured at a wavelength of 405 nm to accurately measure the level of calcium deposition using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3, Molecular Devices).

Immunostaining

BMSCs in a 6-well plate containing PF, HAP/SN, ORMs, and HAP/SN/ORMs, following the co-cultivation within a designated timeframe, initially, the plates were rinsed with PBS, followed by fixation in 4 % PFA for 20 min at room temperature. Post-fixation, the cells were subjected to permeabilization using 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS, which lasted for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were treated with a 10 % goat serum blocking solution, also at room temperature, for 1 h to suppress non-specific binding. The cells were then immersed in a primary antibody concoction at 4 °C overnight, which included Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated antibodies and rabbit monoclonal antibodies directed against mouse RUNX2, ALP, OCN, OPN, and Ki-67 (all diluted at 1:200) in 10 % goat serum as the antibody diluent. On the succeeding day, the constructs were subjected to incubation with secondary antibodies by Alexa Fluor 594 or 488 goat anti-rabbit (both meticulously diluted at a ratio of 1:500) for a duration of 1 h at a standard room temperature. The final step involved labeling the cell nuclei with DAPI for 5 min to facilitate nuclear visualization. After each step, thorough washes using PBS were conducted three times, lasting 10 min per wash to remove any unbound reagents. Ultimately, the stained hydrogel constructs were analyzed under a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM880, Germany) to evaluate the fluorescence signal representing the specific protein expression. The area of protein labeling was quantified utilizing ImageJ software (version 1.53), providing a robust measure of target protein abundance within the samples.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was employed to quantify the quantities of RUNX2, ALP, OPN, and OCN mRNA after the coincubation of BMSCs and diverse hydrogel groups with an osteoinductive medium for a duration of 7 and 21 days. The subsequent primer sequences, synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (USA), were employed for qPCR (Table S1). Vascularization

To monitor the formation of capillary-like structures, 200 μL of ECs at a density of 3 × 104 cells mL−1 were seeded into each well of a 48-well plate, coated with Matrigel and kept at 37 °C, along with the corresponding biomaterials from each experimental group. After 12 h of incubation, the cells were labeled using Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated phalloidin to highlight the cellular cytoskeleton. The visualization of these structures was performed utilizing an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73-A21PH, Japan), and the acquired data were meticulously analyzed utilizing ImageJ software (version 1.53) to evaluate the extent and pattern of tube formation by the endothelial progenitor cells within the microenvironment provided by the biomaterials.

Biocompatibility of composite thermosensitive hydrogels in vivo

The evaluation of the hydrogels' biocompatibility was conducted via subcutaneous implantation in SD rats, following ethical clearance from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanfang Hospital, affiliated with Southern Medical University (Approval No. R202009.05, based in Guangzhou, China). Compliance with animal welfare protocols was ensured by adhering firmly to the standards prescribed in the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 8023, revised in 1978). For this investigation, twenty-four 8 weeks male SD rats, each weighing on average 200 g, were employed and systematically allocated to one of four research cohorts: the PF group, the PF/HAP/SN group, the PF/ORMs group, and the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group. These animals were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center at Southern Medical University. Before implantation, each test hydrogel, fashioned into cylinders measuring 3 mm by 8 mm. Under anesthesia induced by isoflurane (2–3% for induce anesthesia and 1.5–2% for sustain anesthesia), a dorsal incision was made to create subcutaneous pockets in the rats, where the hydrogel specimens were meticulously inserted. Carprofen (5 mg/kg BW/day, subcutaneously) was administered postoperatively for three days to mitigate post-surgery pain in the animals. One and three weeks thereafter, tissues surrounding the implant sites were excised for immunological assessment. The tissues collected were preserved in a 4 % paraformaldehyde solution and subsequently processed through paraffin embedding. To analyze the local immune reactions, H&E staining was applied, complemented by immunohistochemical examination with CD86 and CD206 antibodies to discern the character of the immune response elicited by the hydrogel implants.

Construction of cranial defect model and composite thermosensitive hydrogel implantation

In the study mentioned, twenty-four 8-week-old male SD rats which were provided from the Experimental Animal Center at Southern Medical University were utilized and assigned to various experimental cohorts. Each SD rat was individually housed under controlled conditions (25 °C, 50 % humidity), with adequate ventilation, natural light, and access to food and water. Rats were acclimatized for one week before experimental treatment. The rats were anesthetized through the administration of an intraperitoneal injection of 3 % pentobarbital sodium (0.01 mL/kg). A sagittal incision around 1.5–2.0 cm long was then made on the scalp. A uniform, full-thickness cranial defect measuring 8 mm in diameter was meticulously crafted at the crown of the skull using a high-speed electric trephine. Into this defect, predefined cylindrical hydrogel constructs (measuring 3 mm by 8 mm) were delicately positioned. After the implantation, soft tissues were realigned and securely sutured with 4-0 silk stitches to ensure a clean, primary wound closure. To combat infection, an intraperitoneal injection of antibiotics was administered postoperatively. Meantime, carprofen (5 mg/kg BW/day, subcutaneously) was administered postoperatively for three days to mitigate post-surgery pain in the animals. Groups of 12 rats were euthanized at four- and eight-week intervals to collect skull samples for analysis. These specimens were scanned using a Skyscanner 1176 micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system to obtain high-resolution images. These images were then processed using 3D Creator software to generate detailed tomographic reconstructions. Data retrieved from the micro-CT images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.53). Concurrently, the excised specimens were preserved in 4 % paraformaldehyde, decalcified, and then embedded in paraffin. Subsequent to embedding, sections were stained using H&E, Masson's trichrome stains, and OCN immunohistochemical to evaluate tissue morphology. Furthermore, to probe the angiogenic, the samples also underwent immunohistochemical staining using antibodies against CD31 and Flk-1.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Initially, RNA was isolated and purified from BMSCs samples that underwent either anoxia treatment or treatment with ORMs, with each treatment group comprising three samples. The RNA extraction and purification procedures were conducted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's guidelines. The concentration and purity of the total RNA were then evaluated using a Nano Drop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nano Drop, Wilmington, DE, USA). The integrity of the RNA was assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA) and confirmed through agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples meeting the criteria of a concentration exceeding 50 ng/μL, RIN value above 7.0, OD260/280 ratio surpassing 1.8, and total RNA amount exceeding 1 μg were deemed suitable for subsequent experiments. Subsequently, mRNA with a PolyA tail was selectively captured through two rounds of purification using Dynabeads Oligo (dT) magnetic beads (Catalog #25–61005, Thermo Fisher, USA). The captured mRNA was then fragmented at high temperatures using the NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (Catalog #E6150S, USA) for 5–7 min at 94 °C. The fragmented RNA was converted to cDNA using Invitrogen SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Catalog #1896649, CA, USA). E. coli DNA polymerase I (NEB, Catalog #m0209, USA) and RNase H (NEB, Catalog #m0297, USA) were employed for second-strand DNA synthesis, and dA-tailing Solution (Thermo Fisher, Catalog #R0133, CA, USA) was used to smooth the double-stranded DNA ends. Adenine bases were added to the blunt ends of the DNA strands, followed by the selection and purification of specific-sized fragments using bead technology. After processing the double-stranded DNA with UDG enzyme (NEB, Catalog #m0280, MA, US), PCR amplification was carried out with the following program: 95 °C for 3 min pre-denaturation, followed by 8 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. This process aimed to generate a library with fragment lengths of approximately 300 bp ± 50 bp. Subsequently, paired-end sequencing was performed using the Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 sequencer (LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) with a sequencing mode set to PE150. The raw sequencing data in fastq format underwent initial quality control using the fastp software (available at https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp), involving the removal of adapter sequences, duplicate sequences, and low-quality sequences based on the software's default settings. The data were then aligned to the human genome GRCh38 using the HISAT2 tool (details at https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2), and the aligned data were saved in bam format. Gene and transcript assembly were conducted using StringTie software, and quantification analysis was performed using the FPKM method, calculated as FPKM = total exon fragments/mapped reads (in millions) × exon length (in kilobases). Differential expression analysis was carried out using the edgeR package (refer to https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html), with the criteria for differentially expressed genes set as a fold change greater than 2 or less than 0.5 and a p-value less than 0.05. Finally, the identified genes underwent Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis using the DAVID tool (available at https://david.ncifcrf.gov/).

Statistical analyses

In this experiment, each experiment was replicated at least three times, with the resultant data articulated as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical interpretation and analysis were conducted employing GraphPad Prism 8.0 Software and ImageJ 1.53 Software, with the Student t-test deployed to compare the two different groups of experimental data. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to draw comparisons between the experimental data across multiple groups. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: ∗P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Composite thermosensitive hydrogels’ fabrication and characterization

In order to evaluate the impact of each component on the hydrogel, three different composite hydrogels were fabricated with PF-127 (Henceforth referred to as PF) containing ORMs, HAP/SN, or both ORMs and HAP/SN. ORMs were synthesized utilizing a dual-emulsion method as previously expounded in our experimental procedure. Scanning electron microscopy illustrated that the average particle sizes of HAP, SN, CPO, and ORMs are about 35 nm, 28 nm, 40 nm, and 375 μm in diameter respectively. We chose these specific sizes because previously reported studies provided a foundation of validated results, allowing us to build upon established research in a reliable and reproducible manner [41,[54], [55], [56]]. However, given the low melting point of PF, it might be less typical that the porous structure was in the SEM image (Fig. 1a–e). FT-IR outcomes and scrutiny corroborated that no novel enhanced or displaced peaks of the amide functional groups surfaced post nanoparticle loading, insinuating that no chemical bond genesis between the nanoparticles and PF backbone (Fig. 1g). Besides, the swelling ratio result showcases that there were no significant differences in the four sample groups (Fig. 1k). The addition of HAP/SN and ORMs, which are stiffer materials than PF, might increase the stiffness of the composite hydrogels as compared to pristine PF. The test results validate that the strength of the composite material PF/HAP/SN/ORMs is superior to that of PF alone (Fig. S1). The shear-thinning effect of SN enhances the injectability of the PF/HAP/SN and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs groups compared to the pristine PF [41]. However, the solid particle nature of ORMs influences the injectability of the hydrogel, leading to a reduced injectability for the PF/ORM group in comparison to the pristine PF (Fig. 1h). At 37 °C, within an angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s, the storage modulus (G′) of 20 % PF consistently surpasses the loss modulus (G″), indicating that the PF gel network possesses commendable stability (Fig. 1i). As the temperature increases from 20 °C to 32 °C, the PF undergoes a transition from a sol state to a gel state (Fig. 1j). This indicates that when injected into the human body (reaching a physiological temperature of 37 °C), the hydrogel can successfully undergo a sol-gel transition (Fig. 1f). To investigate the oxygen release ability of the composite hydrogel, the examination of dissolved oxygen, conducted at a temperature of 37 °C in DI water with the caps unsealed, revealed that the method of encapsulation using PCL markedly extended the duration of Chlorine Peroxide's oxygen-release to a period of 21 days. (Fig. 1l). Concurrently, we measured the concentration of hydrogen peroxide in DI water, and the results aligned with the oxygen release curve (Fig. 1m). This affirmation reinforces the notion that the production of oxygen can be ascribed to the presence of CPO. The rate of oxygen liberation is a paramount consideration in the domain of chemical oxygen-generating biomaterials, exerting a pivotal influence on their biocompatibility and biological efficacy [57]. An uncontrolled burst release can result in excessive oxygen accumulation, a surge in reactive oxygen species, and an elevation in local pH, thereby augmenting the toxicity of the substrate [22]. Similarly, an insufficiently brief duration of oxygen emission falls short of sustaining cellular survival and fails to ensure the comprehensive vascularization of an implant [6]. In light of these challenges, recent advancements have reported an array of chemical oxygen-generating biological materials such as particulate forms [52], fibrous structures [58], films [59], three-dimensional scaffolds [60], and hydrogels [61]. They aim to deliver oxygen to sizeable tissue constructs or to address the oxygen deficiency in poorly vascularized tissues, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of anoxia. These innovative biomaterials are typically created by incorporating peroxides into their matrix, which unfortunately tends to yield a transient oxygen release period. For instance, Caterine Yesenia Carrasco Montesdeoca innovatively integrated CPO with gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) [62], culminating in the production of four-dimensional biomaterials capable of an extended oxygen release duration of around six days. Meanwhile, in another exploratory study, Parvaiz A. Shiekh skillfully combined CPO with an antioxidant polyurethane (PUAO) to fabricate a PUAO-CPO cryogel scaffold [63], which commendably maintained a consistent oxygen release for a period of approximately ten days. Evidently, factors beyond the mere variety of peroxides, including the hydrophilic or hydrophobic characteristics [58], the degradation rate [20], the integrity of the coating [64], the pH of the carrier medium, as well as the material's microarchitecture, play an essential role in moderating the rate of oxygen diffusion. It is clear that the rudimentary technique of mixing is insufficient in extending the oxygen release timeline, highlighting the need for more nuanced and sophisticated methodologies. In our research, the meticulous encapsulation of CPO within PCL, characterized by the gradual degradation kinetics of PCL and the complete encapsulation of microspheres, imparts the resulting ORM with the ability to sustain oxygen release for approximately 21 days. Meanwhile, oxygen levels gradually declined before the completion of the reaction. The prolonged oxygen release serves to alleviate acute anoxia at the injury site, ensuring the maintenance of cellular viability. This underscores encapsulation as a promising method for the fabrication of biocompatible materials designed for oxygen generation.

Biocompatibility of composite thermosensitive hydrogels

We conducted an investigation into the impact of the hydrogels on BMSCs and ECs viability under two distinct oxygen concentrations (normoxia (21 % O2); anoxia (5 % O2) [65]) within 1 and 3 days. The categorization primarily includes PF, PF/HAP/SN, PF/ORMs, and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs. Initially, we utilized the CCK-8 assay to identify optimal concentrations of the various components within the composite hydrogel. The result demonstrates that sustained exposure to elevated levels of ORMs culminates in cytotoxicity. The cytotoxicity primarily results from the explosive oxygen release from high-concentration ORMs, inducing oxidative stress [66], cell membrane and mitochondrial damage [67], and DNA damage [68]. These cumulative effects over time lead to apoptosis. Ultimately, we selected concentrations of 20 % PF, 100 μg mL−1 HAP, 2 % SN, and 2 mg mL−1 ORMs (Fig. 2a–d). In the normoxic environment, all groups showed great biocompatibility. In the anoxic cultivation environment, cell viability in PF group does not exhibit a significant decline. It was because short-term hypoxia does not substantially compromise cell survival, it primarily affects the corresponding biological functions of the cells [69]. Surprisingly, the PF/HAP/SN group exhibited a markedly increased incidence of cell death compared to the PF group. This observation suggested that HAP or SN might possess an unforeseen cytotoxic nature when employed in anoxic conditions. HAP/SN exhibits a detrimental impact on cellular proliferation and functionality under anoxic conditions, thereby diminishing the osteogenic efficacy of HAP/SN. It could be explained by anoxia-induced cell membrane alterations [70] increased susceptibility to nanomaterial damage, enhanced permeability, and intracellular disruption, culminating in cell death [71]. Simultaneously, reduced energy production owing to oxygen deficiency initiates stress responses [72], impeding metabolic activities. This diminishes the cell's capacity to manage nanomaterial metabolic byproducts and stress responses, disrupts redox homeostasis, and heightens oxidative stress [73], ultimately leading to cell death and functional impairment. The two groups containing ORMs maintained their viability at approximately 90 % for up to 3 days under anoxic conditions. This finding implies that the presence of ORMs effectively counteracted the cytotoxic effects associated with both anoxia and HAP/SN (Fig. 2e–g, i; Figure S2 a-c, e). Cell spreading area, as a biophysical signal, plays a regulatory role in cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Existing studies have demonstrated that larger mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) promote osteogenic differentiation more effectively than smaller ones [74]; similarly, endothelial cells with greater spreading areas exhibit stronger migratory capabilities and enhanced angiogenic potential [75]. Consequently, we proceeded to measure cell area to substantiate the impact of oxygen on cellular functions. Compared to the non-ORMs group, cells in the ORMs group displayed a significantly larger spreading area (Fig. 2e–f, h, j; Figure S2 a-b, d, f), suggesting that the oxygen supplied by the ORMs not only sustains BMSCs and ECs' survival but also stimulates their expansion, thereby greatly enhancing their osteogenic and angiogenic functions.

Subsequently, we assessed the oxygen supply capability of ORMs at the cellular level under two different conditions. Commercially available anoxia probes [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 were employed to monitor intracellular O2 levels [53]. The red fluorescence of [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 can be quenched by intracellular O2. The results indicated that under normoxic conditions, ORMs could provide additional oxygen to cells, whereas under anoxic conditions, ORMs could significantly alleviate cellular anoxia (Fig. 3a–b). It is worth noting that, while a certain level of hyperoxia environment contributes to cell survival and development, excessively high oxygen concentrations can also be detrimental to cells [26]. The CCK-8 assay reflects cellular activity based on the total viable cell count rather than elucidating the mechanisms of proliferation. Ki-67, on the other hand, is a nuclear antigen that serves as a marker for proliferative status, predominantly expressed in cells during G1, S, G2, and mitotic phases but absent in quiescent, G0 phase cells [76]. Its function is closely associated with mitosis and it offers high sensitivity. Hence, we employed immunofluorescence to detect intracellular Ki-67 activity to assess the impact of the supplementary oxygen provided by ORMs on cellular vitality. The results indicate that the oxygen from ORMs promotes cell proliferation under both anoxic and normoxic conditions, particularly, notable in anoxic environments (Fig. 3c–e).

In vitro chemotaxis and vascularization evaluation of the composite thermosensitive hydrogels

Insufficient angiogenesis within a short period is commonly an impediment to the healing of large bone defects [7]. In bone repair, the capacity for migration and proliferation of BMSCs and ECs is fundamental to the process of angiogenesis as it ensures an adequate supply of oxygen and nutrients [77]. Therefore, to verify the angiogenic capability of the composite thermosensitive hydrogel, we utilized scratch assays, migration experiments, and tube formation assays. Results from scratch assays and migration experiments revealed that under normoxic conditions, the PF/HAP/SN, PF/ORMs, and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs groups exhibited enhanced migratory activity compared to the PF group alone, suggesting that HAP/SN and oxygen can promote cell migration. In the PF and PF/HAP/SN groups, the growth and proliferation of BMSCs and ECs were compromised due to anoxic conditions and the potential cytotoxic effects of HAP/SN in low oxygen environments, adversely affecting bone healing. In contrast, the PF/ORMs and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs groups exhibited stronger migratory activity due to effective oxygen release, mitigating anoxia and cytotoxicity, surpassing the other groups (Fig. 4a–h, Fig. S3). Notably, the composite thermosensitive hydrogel must meet this condition to effectively stimulate vascular formation.

Similarly, tube formation assay results indicated that while additional oxygen supplementation in conditions of sufficient oxygen could enhance vascular formation, its impact was limited. However, under anoxic conditions, the PF and PF/HAP/SN groups displayed scant effective tubular development at 12 h, primarily observing punctate nodules or dendritic projections, mainly due to low oxygen levels affecting the vitality and functionality of ECs. In the PF/ORMs and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs groups, the release of oxygen curtailed the anoxic conditions and provided the potential for early vascularization and bone formation. It is noteworthy that the lumens in the PF/ORMs and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs groups were fully closed, and the degree of vascularization was higher than in the other groups (Fig. 4i–l). In summary, ORMs can alleviate cellular anoxia, significantly promoting cell proliferation and migration, thereby enhancing angiogenesis.

Assessment of the composite thermosensitive Hydrogel's capacity to facilitate osteogenesis in vitro

The concentration of oxygen within the environment plays a pivotal role in bone repair. Prolonged anoxic conditions can negatively impact the survival and proliferation of BMSCs, impede their metabolic transformation and osteogenic processes, and significantly reduce the expression of mineralization and osteogenic markers [12,13], which is detrimental to bone healing. Thus, the experiment in this segment aims to ascertain the osteogenic potential of the composite thermosensitive hydrogel. Primarily, to confirm the synergistic effect of HAP and SN in bone repair, we divided the experiment into four groups (Control, SN, HAP, HAP/SN), employing qPCR to detect the expression of early osteogenesis-related genes (RUNX2 and ALP) under both anoxic and normoxic conditions after 7 days. The research findings illustrate that the HAP/SN group manifested the most potent osteogenic potential under normoxic conditions, while its efficacy was diminished under anoxic conditions (Fig. S4). Having substantiated the synergistic effect of HAP and SN, we segregated the experimental groups into four categories: PF, PF/HAP/SN, PF/ORMs, and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs. Initially, under both normoxic and anoxic culture conditions, as well as under the induction of osteogenic culture medium, the BMSCs co-cultured with the composite hydrogel were stained using Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) on days 7 and 21 (Fig. 5a–b, e-f). ALP staining revealed that the staining intensity in the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group was significantly higher than that in the PF, PF/HAP/SN, and PF/ORMs groups. Furthermore, the BMSCs cultivated within the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group exhibited the greatest efficiency in calcium deposition, as determined by the Alizarin Red S staining of calcium-binding proteins.

Biocompatibility assay of the composite thermosensitive hydrogels in vivo

To empirically validate in vivo biocompatibility, acellular pristine PF hydrogels (Group 1), PF/HAP/SN (Group 2), PF/ORMs (Group 3), and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs (Group 4) with a volume of 150 mm3 (8 mm in width and 3 mm in thickness) were implanted into the dorsal region of six weeks Sprague-Dawley rats. At 1- and 3-weeks post-implantation (representative of short-term and long-term immune responses), the hydrogels and surrounding tissues were extracted for biocompatibility analysis. To investigate the host-graft interactions, histological and immunological evaluations were conducted on midsagittal sections of the implants. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, along with CD86/206 staining, were utilized to assess the infiltration of host immune cells. H&E staining results demonstrated a substantial increase in immune cell infiltration in the PF/HAP/SN group at one week, which was attenuated by the integration of ORMs, as noted in the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group. By the three-week mark, there were no significant discrepancies, with CD86 immunofluorescence staining corroborating similar outcomes, indicating that the immune response instigated by HAP/SN was transient. Furthermore, no observable significant differences were noted in CD206+ cells for any engineered formulation (Fig. S5). Synthesizing these findings suggests that the integration of the composite hydrogels does not lead to an enhanced immune response upon implantation.

In vivo evaluation of bone regeneration and vascularization of the composite thermosensitive hydrogels

It is widely recognized that the oxygen concentration within the microenvironment is critical for the success of bone repair and can effectively enhance vasculature-driven osteogenesis within extensive bone defects. It has been ascertained that composite hydrogels are capable of maintaining cell viability through the sustained, gradual release of oxygen for up to 21 days, concomitantly augmenting osteogenic activity to facilitate bone healing. To evaluate the efficacy of composite hydrogels in treating extensive bone defects in vivo, a rat model with critical-sized (8 mm) cranial defects which had been used typically in previously published studies was treated with hydrogels [26,78,79] (Fig. 7a). The composite hydrogels implanted during the surgical procedure intimately sealed around the deficits near the cranium. Moreover, the rats endured the empirical investigation without succumbing to illness or death. The subgroups included PF, PF/HAP/SN, PF/ORMs, and PF/HAP/SN/ORMs. At 4- and 8 weeks post-surgery, the rats were euthanized and the cranial bone defects in the rats were reconstructed using 3D micro-CT scanning (Fig. 7b). Our findings indicate that the PF group exhibited negligible new bone formation throughout the healing process. Among the experimental groups, the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group manifested the most favorable healing at all time points. Notably, by the 8th week, the new osseous tissue nearly filled the defect area, with new bone development visible from the periphery of the lesion to its center as the implantation duration extended. The results unequivocally showed that the reparative outcomes in the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group were superior to those of the other groups. This analysis postulates that the composite hydrogels, functioning as an excellent natural extracellular matrix, assist cellular proliferation and adhesion, thereby promoting vascularized bone repair. Bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), trabecular separation/spacing (Tb. Sp), and trabecular number (Tb. N) were measured and analyzed for each group (Fig. 7c–f). The PF/HAP/SN/ORMs groups showed significantly higher BV/TV, Tb. N, and Tb. Th, compared to other groups, with the inverse being true for Tb.Sp. Therefore, this suggests that the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group can facilitate vascularized osteogenesis by reconstructing the osteogenic microenvironment through sustained oxygen release and enhanced osteogenic activity.

To further assess the osteogenic efficacy of the composite hydrogels in vivo, the specimens were subjected to H&E, Masson's trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical detection (Fig. 8, Fig. S6). It was observed that in the PF group, without the incorporation of ORMs and HAP/SN, a scant growth of bone tissue was present only near the edges of the defect area, the remainder being fibrous tissue. This condition did not improve until the eighth week, suggesting that at an 8 mm cranial defect, a critical-sized defect is reached, with almost no new bone formation. The outcome of the treatment was an abundance of new bone growth at week eight in the treated groups, extending from the edges to the center of the defect, nearly filling the void (Fig. 8a). Immunohistochemical results for OCN also demonstrated the highest expression in the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group (Fig. 8b). The angiogenesis of the newly regenerated bone was also evaluated. The new blood vessel formation plays a crucial role in bone regeneration. CD31, a membrane receptor expressed on vascular endothelial cells, was used as an indicative marker for the identification of vascular endothelium within the tissue. Here, we employ CD31 immunohistochemistry to scrutinize the emergence of newly formed blood vessels within the area of osseous deficiency (Fig. 8c–f). The results demonstrated that the oxygen-releasing groups showed a significantly higher number of newly formed blood vessels compared to the other groups. Notably, the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group had a much higher mean number of blood vessels compared to the PF/ORMs group. To validate these results, Flk-1 immunofluorescence staining was also performed on the samples. Flk-1, alternatively referred to as vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2, assumes a cardinal role in the orchestration of vascular genesis [80,81]. The immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the PF/HAP/SN/ORMs group achieved the best outcome in terms of promoting angiogenesis (Fig. 8d–e). These findings suggest that oxygen supply at the site of bone defects can effectively promote angiogenesis, and prolonged oxygen release leads to the stimulation of even more blood vessel formation, which is essential for successful bone regeneration [82,83].

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Transcriptomic analysis through RNA-sequencing was employed to examine the molecular responses in BMSCs following exposure to interventions from both the anoxia + ORMs and anoxia groups. Fig. 9a displays a heatmap illustrating the most significantly up-regulated and down-regulated Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs). To elucidate the functional implications of these DEGs, analyses were performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Fig. 9b showcases the top 20 enriched pathways identified in the KEGG analysis, with the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway emerging as the most enriched pathway. Concurrently, the GSEA analysis results indicated an up-regulation of the PI3K/Akt signal in the anoxia + ORMs group (Fig. 9c). The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation. Notably, RUNX2, serving as an osteogenesis-specific transcription factor, is among the downstream targets of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [84]. Upon the up-regulation of key molecules in the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and RUNX2, the expression of osteogenic differentiation-related markers such as ALP, type I collagen, and OCN is also enhanced [85]. In conclusion, the release of oxygen by ORMs may facilitate bone regeneration by orchestrating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway.

Conclusion

In this study, an innovative bioactive hydrogel with controlled oxygen release and osteogenic activity induction was developed. The gradual and sustained release of oxygen maintained a balanced microenvironment, fostering the proliferation and vitality of osteoblasts and endothelial cells; osteoinductive nanoparticles constituted an osteogenic niche, augmenting the osteogenic activity of osteoblasts and thereby significantly promoting vascularized osteogenesis. In vitro oxygen release experiments demonstrated the potent and enduring oxygen release capabilities of the bioactive hydrogel, with a duration of up to 21 days. In vivo experiments utilizing a rat cranial defect model revealed that the oxygen-releasing bioactive hydrogel significantly enhanced angiogenesis and osteogenic functions, thereby markedly expediting the bone healing process. This offers a novel perspective for the identification and treatment of clinical bone ailments.
