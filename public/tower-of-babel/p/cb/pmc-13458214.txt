Evaluation of the ability of hemostatic agents to prevent hemoptysis during ultrasound-guided lung biopsy
Authors: Dongjun Wei, Yuxin Zhang, Jiaxin Tang, Liantu He, Wuxi Chen, Shiyu Zhang, Qing Tang, Haixing Liao, Xinghua Zhou, Hongwei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow-dose computed tomography screening has substantially improved the detection of peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs). Ultrasound-guided lung biopsy is widely used for diagnosis; however, postoperative hemoptysis is a critical complication, and the value of routine prophylactic hemostasis remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the risk factors for hemoptysis following ultrasound-guided percutaneous lung biopsy and evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of prophylactic hemostatic agents in high- and low-risk patients.MethodsA total of 697 patients who underwent ultrasound-guided peripheral lung biopsy were retrospectively enrolled in this study. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for postoperative hemoptysis, and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cutoff value for lesion thickness. Subsequently, 126 patients were prospectively recruited and randomly divided into four subgroups: high-risk with hemostatic agents, high-risk without hemostatic agents, low-risk with hemostatic agents, and low-risk without hemostatic agents. Quantitative perioperative indicators, including hemoptysis incidence and volume, blood oxygen saturation, pulse rate, hemoglobin, platelet count, and coagulation function indices, were statistically compared among groups, and hemostatic agent-related adverse complications were recorded.ResultsThe overall post-biopsy hemoptysis rate was 9.03% (63/697). Multivariate regression analysis confirmed that lesion thickness <15 mm and grade 2 air bronchial signs were independent risk factors for hemoptysis (all P<0.05). The optimal cutoff value of lesion thickness was 15 mm, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.713, a sensitivity of 92.4%, and a specificity of 52.4%. For high-risk patients, prophylactic hemostatic agents significantly reduced the hemoptysis rate (15.15% vs. 39.29%, P=0.034) and hemoptysis volume (P=0.014), and effectively stabilized postoperative blood oxygen saturation, compared with the non-hemostatic group. In low-risk patients, no significant differences in hemoptysis incidence, hemoptysis volume, or blood oxygen saturation were observed between patients who received hemostatic agents and those who did not (all P>0.05). Additionally, no significant differences between pre- and postoperative measurements were observed in pulse rate, hemoglobin level, platelet count, or any coagulation index across all subgroups (all P>0.05). The incidence of hemostatic agent-related adverse reactions was 7.58% (5/66), and all adverse symptoms were relieved after symptomatic treatment.ConclusionsLesion thickness <15 mm and grade 2 air bronchial signs were identified as independent risk factors for hemoptysis following ultrasound-guided biopsy of PPLs. Prophylactic hemostatic agents may help reduce the hemoptysis rate and volume in patients with at least one high-risk factor. However, given the moderate predictive performance of these risk factors (AUC =0.713), individualized clinical judgment remains essential.Keywords: Ultrasonic guidance, puncture biopsy, pulmonary peripheral lesions, hemoptysis, hemostatic agent

Introduction

With the widespread implementation of low-dose chest computed tomography (CT) for lung cancer screening, the detection rate of peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs) has increased steadily, necessitating precise diagnostic stratification. Image-guided percutaneous biopsy remains the cornerstone for obtaining tissue samples and establishing pathological diagnoses of these increasingly identified lung masses (1). Among these techniques, CT-guided percutaneous biopsy is widely used; however, challenges such as cumulative radiation exposure and reliance on static imaging limit its real-time applicability (2-4). Conversely, ultrasound-guided percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy (US-PTNB) allows for real-time monitoring of the needle trajectory, providing diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of CT-guided biopsy, with reported rates as high as 90.4% for subpleural lesions (5), while offering a radiation-free approach and enabling the visualization of the perilesional vasculature. Real-time imaging of PPLs enables precise positioning during respiratory motion and facilitates the avoidance of blood vessels and bronchioles during puncture (6,7). Additionally, the cost-effectiveness of ultrasound makes it an excellent method for guiding needle biopsies of PPLs (8).

Despite these advantages, US-PTNB is not without complications. Hemoptysis remains one of the most common adverse events. While earlier studies reported rates as high as 22%, more recent analyses have demonstrated significant variability, with reported incidences of hemoptysis following ultrasound-guided procedures ranging from approximately 1.2% to 9.6% depending on lesion characteristics and operator experience (9). In severe cases, patients may experience significant blood loss, shock, or airway obstruction due to blood clots (10), which may even lead to asphyxia and other serious clinical outcomes (11,12).

Hemoptysis may be secondary to iatrogenic vascular and parenchymal injury resulting in subsequent intrapulmonary bleeding. Prophylactic administration of hemostatic agents has been proposed as a strategy to attenuate this risk. However, the safety profile of these agents requires careful evaluation. Beyond immediate hypersensitivity reactions, recent studies have highlighted the risk of acquired hypofibrinogenemia associated with agents such as hemocoagulase batroxobin, which can paradoxically increase mortality and transfusion requirements in patients with hemoptysis (13,14). Thus, the widespread prophylactic use of these agents presents a challenge, and their clinical value in US-PTNB has yet to be confirmed. The clinical value of prepuncture hemostasis in US-PTNB remains unclear. Determining the appropriate selection and application of prophylactic hemostatic agents for patients undergoing biopsy warrants further investigation.

This study aimed to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of hemostatic agents in the management of puncture-related hemoptysis in patients at low or high risk of hemoptysis during US-PTNB. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0324/rc).

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (ethics number: ES-2024-K121-01). All patients signed informed consent forms prior to enrollment.

The first part of the study was retrospective, while the second was prospective. The retrospective phase identified the high-risk factors for hemoptysis associated with US-PTNB. Data from all patients who underwent US-PTNB at the Department of Ultrasound between January 2019 and December 2020 were retrospectively analyzed. Only the first biopsy for each patient was included in the analysis. These high-risk factors were subsequently used to implement a protocol for administering hemostatic agents to randomly selected patients (simple randomization). This approach aimed to prospectively evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of preoperative hemostatic injection for the management of postoperative hemoptysis related to US-PTNB in high- and low-risk patients.

This study included data from patients who underwent US-PTNB at the Department of Ultrasound between January 2019 and September 2021. Part I covered the period from January 2019 to December 2020, and Part II from January 2021 to September 2021. For Part I, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age ≥16 years; and (II) platelet count >50×109/L and international normalized ratio (INR) <1.5. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) incomplete clinical data; and/or (II) extrapulmonary lesions not detected by ultrasound. For Part II, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age ≥16 years; (II) platelet count >50×109/L and INR <1.5; and (III) no use of anticoagulant drugs within one week prior to the procedure. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) non-cooperation of patients; (II) lack of clinician approval for hemostatic agents; and/or (III) allergy to hemostatic agents.

Study variables and definitions

Definition and records of hemoptysis: For part I of the study (which aimed to identify high-risk factors for hemoptysis), patient data were reviewed to identify the occurrence and extent of hemoptysis on the day of puncture; cases in which hemoptysis occurred on the day after puncture were excluded. For the second part (which aimed to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of preoperative hemostatic injection), hemoptysis was recorded if it occurred within a half hour of the puncture, with indications ranging from the collection of sputum with blood to active hemoptysis. The hemoptysis volume was calculated as 1.05 g of sterile blood containing gauze equivalent to 1 mL of blood (15). Hemoptysis was classified as non-US-PTNB-related if patients had preexisting disease-associated hemoptysis, if no new hemoptysis occurred on the day after the biopsy, and if no increase in hemoptysis was observed during the monitoring period.

The variables were defined as follows.

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg.

Hepatitis B was defined as positivity for hepatitis B surface antigen and hepatitis B core antibody, with either hepatitis B e antibody positivity or hepatitis B e antigen positivity.

Renal insufficiency was defined as a serum creatinine level >133 µmol/L.

Lesion thickness was defined as the vertical distance from the deepest point of the lesion to the point of contact between the lesion and the pleura. This measurement was obtained by ultrasound along the planned puncture path.

The color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) Adler classification was divided into four levels: level 0 indicated no blood flow signal; level 1 indicated 1–2 dot-like or short rod-like blood flow signals; level 2 indicated 3–4 dot-like or short rod-like blood flow signals, or 1 long blood flow signal; and level 3 indicated more than 5 dot-like or short rod-like blood flow signals, or at least 2 long blood flow signals.

Air bronchial signs were classified into three grades: grade 0 indicated no air bronchial signs; grade 1 indicated a small number of air bronchial signs that could be avoided during puncture; and grade 2 indicated a large number of air bronchial signs that could not be avoided during puncture (Figure 1) (16).

Classification of the air bronchial sign. (A) Grade 0: No air bronchial sign. (B) Grade 1: A small number of air bronchial signs that can be avoided during puncture. (C) Grade 2: A large number of air bronchial signs that cannot be avoided during puncture.

The necrotic area within the lesion was defined as a non-enhancing region on contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS).

Blood oxygen saturation referred to the instantaneous value measured using an oximeter before and after puncture.

Pulse referred to the instantaneous value measured using an oximeter before and after puncture.

Hemoglobin and platelet counts referred to the values obtained from routine blood examinations performed before puncture and one day after puncture.

The coagulation index included multiple parameters derived from coagulation function tests performed before puncture and one day after puncture, including the INR, prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), activated partial thrombin time (APTT), fibrinogen (FIB), and D-dimer.

Because the surgeries were performed by the chief physician or associate chief physician, each with more than 15 years of experience in ultrasound-guided interventional surgeries, the effect of operator variability on outcomes was not analyzed.

Risk factors for hemoptysis were identified through univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses of all variables. Patients exhibiting at least one high-risk factor for hemoptysis were categorized as high risk, while others were classified as low risk. Patients in both risk groups were further divided into hemostatic and non-hemostatic agent subgroups based on the use of hemostatic agents before the procedure using the random number table method. Simple random grouping was performed using Microsoft Excel. Specifically, each patient in the high-risk group was assigned a number, which was entered in the first column of a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The function “=RAND()” was entered in the second column and pulled down to generate random numbers (random number 1). The numerical values in this column were then copied to obtain random number 2 in the third column. Random number 2 was arranged in ascending order, and the first half was selected as the control group and the last half was selected as the observation group to form the final random grouping. Simple random grouping was performed for the low-risk group using the same procedure. Hemoptysis occurrence and related indicators were then compared and analyzed within and between the high- and low-risk groups (Figure 2).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) software. The quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while the categorical variables are expressed as frequencies or percentages.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify high-risk factors for hemoptysis. Variables with P<0.05 were considered statistically significant and independent risk factors for hemoptysis. The optimal cutoff value for lesion thickness was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The sensitivity and specificity for postoperative hemoptysis were calculated, and the Youden Index (Youden Index = sensitivity + specificity – 1) was used to identify the maximum cutoff value as the optimal threshold.

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality in intergroup analyses of the hemoptysis and drug administration groups. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (minimum, maximum). Normally distributed intragroup and intergroup measurement data were compared using the paired t test. Non-normally distributed measurement data were compared using the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. Qualitative data were analyzed using the chi-square test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

To identify the risk factors for hemoptysis following US-PTNB, 715 patients were initially evaluated. Of these patients, 18 were excluded for the following reasons: (I) age <16 years (n=5); (II) incomplete information (n=4); and (III) extrapulmonary lesions (n=9). Ultimately, 697 patients, comprising 477 males and 220 females, aged 16–91 years, with a mean age of 57.1±15.15 years, were included in the analysis. Among these patients, 63 developed hemoptysis, corresponding to a hemoptysis rate of 9.03%, as shown in Figure 2 and Table 1.

Further analysis of lesion thickness revealed that the median lesion thickness was 15 mm in patients with hemoptysis compared with 30 mm in those without hemoptysis, with a significant difference between the two groups (P<0.001). The optimal diagnostic cutoff value for lesion thickness was 15 mm, yielding a diagnostic sensitivity of 92.4%, a specificity of 52.4%, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.713. The hemoptysis rate was significantly higher in patients with a lesion thickness <15 mm than in those with a lesion thickness ≥15 mm (P<0.05). However, no significant difference in hemoptysis rates was observed between patients with lesion thicknesses <20 mm and those with lesion thicknesses ≥20 mm.

The hemoptysis rates for patients with grade 0, grade 1, and grade 2 air bronchial signs were 5.39%, 14.06%, and 28.00%, respectively. Hemoptysis rates were significantly higher in patients with air bronchial signs than in those without air bronchial signs (P<0.001). The hemoptysis rate in patients with grade 2 air bronchial signs was significantly higher than that in patients with grade 0 air bronchial signs (no air bronchial sign) (P<0.001). Conversely, no significant difference was observed in the hemoptysis rates between patients with grade 1 and grade 0 air bronchial signs (P=0.288).

Based on these findings, patients with lesion thicknesses <15 mm or grade 2 air bronchial signs were classified as high risk. Patients without either of these characteristics were classified as low risk.

To evaluate the preventive effects and adverse reaction of hemostatic agents on postoperative hemoptysis in high- and low-risk patients undergoing US-PTNB, 126 patients were assessed from January 2021 to September 2021. The cohort comprised 69 males and 57 females aged 23–78 years, with a mean age of 47.62±21.59 years. Among the 126 patients, 61 were classified as high risk, including 33 in the high-risk hemostatic agent group and 28 in the high-risk non-hemostatic agent group. The remaining 65 patients were classified as low risk, including 33 in the low-risk hemostatic agent group and 32 in the low-risk non-hemostatic agent group. The hemoptysis rates were as follows: high-risk hemostatic group: 15.15% (5/33); high-risk non-hemostatic group: 39.29% (11/28); low-risk hemostatic group: 3.03% (1/33); and low-risk non-hemostatic group: 6.25% (2/32) (Table 3).

Adverse reactions to hemostatic agents

Among the 66 patients who received hemostatic agents, five (7.58%, 5/66) experienced complications, including one case of dizziness, two cases of tachypnoea, and two cases of dyspnea. The first three patients improved after discontinuation of the procedure and oxygen therapy, while the latter two showed significant improvement after oxygen therapy and intravenous glucocorticoids. All five patients returned to the ward safely and underwent biopsy the following day. Given the 2.5-hour half-life of the hemostatic agent (17), these five patients did not receive additional hemostatic agent administration before the subsequent biopsy and were allocated to the non-hemostatic agent group for analysis.

Discussion

US-PTNB is a safe and effective diagnostic method, especially for small PPLs affected by respiratory motion (18). Ultrasound-guided biopsy is considered relatively safe and is associated with fewer complications than CT-guided biopsy (19). However, serious complications, such as shock from massive hemoptysis, may still occur (11,12). In this study, two cases of massive hemoptysis occurred in patients who did not receive prophylactic treatment. Both patients improved following oxygen therapy, fluid resuscitation, and epinephrine administration. Prophylactic use of hemostatic agents is recommended to lower hemoptysis rates and reduce anxiety in patients undergoing ultrasound-guided biopsy; however, adverse reactions may occur that can sometimes necessitate immediate termination of the procedure. Despite its benefits, the risks of prophylactic hemostatic therapy must be carefully considered. This study identified a lesion thickness <15 mm and abundant air bronchial signs as high-risk factors for hemoptysis after ultrasound-guided biopsy. Preoperative use of hemostatic agents was beneficial in high-risk patients but had no significant effect in low-risk patients.

Various hemostatic agents are used in clinical practice, with reported adverse reaction rates ranging from 0.01% to 0.5% (20). In this study, sulin, a relatively safe hemostatic drug derived from Agkistrodon antikistrodon venom, a snake native to China, was used. Sulin is a single-component thrombin purified from this snake venom. It hydrolyses the A peptide fragment of FIB, converting it into fibrin monomer (αBβγ)2, which forms stable fibrin clots via covalent cross-linking to achieve hemostasis (21). Unlike other agents, sulin does not activate factor XIII or contain activators of factor X and prothrombin, thereby preventing coagulation cascade amplification. These characteristics reduce the risk of thrombosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and other complications, making sulin an effective and relatively safe hemostatic agent (22). Although relatively safe, sulin is a xenoserum protein that is antigenic to humans and may cause allergic reactions, most commonly anaphylaxis.

In this study, five of 66 patients (7.58%) injected with hemostatic agents experienced complications, including dizziness in one patient, tachypnea in two patients, and dyspnea in two patients. The first three patients improved after discontinuation of the procedure and oxygen therapy, while the remaining two patients improved significantly following oxygen therapy and intravenous glucocorticoids. All five patients successfully underwent repeat procedures the next day. These findings highlight that hemostatic agents can cause adverse reaction, emphasizing the need for caution and rigorous evaluation when used in patients without clear indications.

This study evaluated the benefits of prophylactic hemostatic agents before puncture, focusing on patients with varying hemoptysis risk levels. It also examined the necessity and safety of preoperative hemostatic agents in high-risk patients. The findings demonstrated that preoperative hemostatic agents significantly reduced the incidence rate and volume of hemoptysis in high-risk patients (P<0.05). In non-high-risk patients, there was no significant difference in the hemoptysis rate or volume. This is likely because these patients already have a low risk of hemoptysis after puncture, and when hemoptysis occurs, the volume is typically small. The clinical benefit of preoperative hemostatic agents was not evident in these patients. In high-risk hemoptysis patients, the incidence rate and volume of postoperative hemoptysis were relatively high, as shown in the first part of this study. The preoperative use of hemostatic agents significantly reduced both the incidence rate and volume of postoperative hemoptysis. In contrast, this advantage was not observed in non-high-risk patients, who typically exhibit less hemoptysis.

Although hemoptysis is self-limiting in most patients, its most immediate impact is the termination of the puncture procedure, preventing the biopsy from achieving its intended diagnostic purpose. Additionally, patients undergoing peripheral pulmonary disease biopsy often have preexisting respiratory conditions of varying severity. In high-risk patients, significant hemoptysis or reduced blood oxygen saturation following puncture can exacerbate respiratory damage and potentially lead to respiratory failure. While preoperative hemostatic agents may offer benefits in mitigating these outcomes for high-risk patients, the moderate predictive value of our identified risk factors (AUC =0.713) suggests that routine prophylaxis should be considered cautiously and tailored to individual patient profiles. This study also revealed that the use of hemostatic agents before and after puncture had no significant effect on pulse rate, regardless of hemoptysis risk. This finding may reflect the low complication rates of US-PTNB. Even when hemoptysis occurs, vital signs such as pulse rate remain largely unaffected.

Many researchers have investigated the risk factors for hemoptysis following ultrasound-guided biopsy of PPLs (23). Numerous studies, including this one, have identified the air bronchial sign as a high-risk factor for hemoptysis (16,24). This study further evaluated air bronchial signs and determined that abundant air bronchial signs, which cannot be avoided during puncture, are high-risk factors for hemoptysis. In contrast, a small number of air bronchial signs, which can be avoided during puncture, are not high-risk factors for hemoptysis. This study identified 15 mm as the optimal cutoff value for lesion thickness associated with hemoptysis, with a diagnostic sensitivity of 92.4%, a specificity of 52.4%, and an AUC of 0.713. These findings align with those of Elshafee et al. (25), who also identified lesion thickness as a high-risk factor for hemoptysis following lung puncture biopsy. In contrast, previous studies on CT-guided lung puncture biopsy reported a threshold of <20 mm as a high-risk cutoff (26). Our study revealed that a lesion thickness <15 mm had greater diagnostic value for predicting hemoptysis after puncture. This may be due to the 20 mm excitation length of the puncture needle used. No significant association was found between lesion thickness <20 mm and hemoptysis, highlighting the advantages of real-time ultrasound-guided lung biopsy, particularly in terms of safety. Ultrasound guidance enables real-time monitoring of lesion blood supply, characteristics, and respiratory movement. It also enables observation and calculation of the puncture needle position and its interaction with surrounding tissues, facilitating the selection of the optimal puncture path. These capabilities expand the safety range of ultrasound-guided lung biopsy.

Lin et al. (27) identified abundant blood flow as a high-risk factor for hemoptysis; however, the findings of this study differ, likely because most patients undergo CEUS before puncture. Compared with CDFI, CEUS provides a more detailed evaluation of the blood supply within and around lesions, enabling safer puncture path selection and reducing hemoptysis risk, even in lesions with abundant blood flow. These findings support the recommendation that CEUS should be performed before each ultrasound-guided lung puncture biopsy whenever feasible. CEUS avoids necrotic areas, targets active regions for biopsy, facilitates blood vessel avoidance, reduces complications, and improves procedural safety.

The study identified a lesion thickness <15 mm and abundant air bronchial signs as high-risk factors for hemoptysis. Based on these findings, patients with at least one high-risk factor were classified as high risk, whereas those without any risk factors were classified as low risk. This study is the first to define high-risk and low-risk patients in the context of US-PTNB. Identifying high-risk patients via ultrasonography before puncture enables operators to anticipate hemoptysis and prepare appropriate treatments.

The results of this study should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. Some cases of hemoptysis may have been unrecorded during the retrospective review of patient data for US-PTNB risk factors, leading to misclassification as non-hemoptysis cases. This misclassification may have influenced the results. Although the operations were performed by the chief physician or associate chief physician, differences in operator experience and technique may still have affected the results. Additionally, hemoptysis was recorded only if it occurred within a half hour of puncture. This restricted timeframe may have led to an underestimation of clinically relevant delayed hemoptysis events, representing a notable limitation of our study design. Finally, this was a single-center study, and the sample size of patients receiving hemostatic agents was relatively small. Future studies should address these limitations through larger, multicenter prospective designs.

Conclusions

A lesion thickness <15 mm and grade 2 air bronchial signs were identified as independent risk factors for hemoptysis after ultrasound-guided biopsy of PPLs. In high-risk patients, preoperative hemostatic agents effectively reduce the incidence rate and volume of hemoptysis. Given the moderate predictive performance of these risk factors (AUC =0.713), individualized clinical judgment remains essential. However, in non-high-risk patients, these agents offered no significant benefits.
