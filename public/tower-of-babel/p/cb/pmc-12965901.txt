Astragalus Polysaccharides Can Effectively Alleviate Cognitive Impairment in Mice With Alzheimer's Disease by Regulating the Gut Microbiota
Authors: Xilian Wang, Xinyun Ge, Xin Di, Zhiyan Zou, Dan Lei, Zihao Wang, Jiaqiang Hou, Yan Yang, Wanxue Zhang, Yuan Yin, Xinnuo Lei, Yu Long, Xuemin Jian, Xiaoan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGut microbiota disruption has been implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis. Although Astragalus polysaccharides (APS) exert neuroprotective effects and modulate gut microbial composition, the precise mechanisms underlying these actions remain unknown. This research sought to clarify how APS modulates the gut microbiota during AD progression. Triple‐transgenic (3xTg) AD mice received daily oral APS for 4 weeks. Cognitive performance was evaluated with the Morris water maze (MWM). Immunofluorescence (IF), immunohistochemistry (IHC), and western blotting measured β‐amyloid (Aβ) deposition and microglial and astrocyte markers. IHC also evaluated intestinal tight‐junction proteins (zonula occludens‐1 and occludin). Inflammatory markers in the brain, blood, and intestine were quantified using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Gut microbiota was analyzed through 16S rRNA sequencing. Treatment with APS significantly improved learning and memory performance in 3xTg mice. APS administration reduced cerebral Aβ deposition, decreased phosphorylated tau, presenilin‐1, and β‐secretase 1 levels, and elevated ADAM10 expression. APS significantly altered gut microbiota, notably increasing Akkermansia and decreasing Alistipes. At the intestinal level, APS enhanced expression of tight‐junction proteins ZO‐1 and occludin and reversed AD‐associated structural alterations in the intestinal lining. Furthermore, APS reduced inflammatory cytokine levels in intestinal tissue, peripheral blood, and brain tissue, as reflected by modulated IL‐4, IL‐10, TGF‐β, TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 expressions. The attenuation of neuroinflammation may be attributed to the inhibitory effect of APS on microglial and astrocyte activation. APS reduces neuroinflammation in AD by modulating gut microbiota, contributing to cognitive and pathological improvements, thus indicating its therapeutic potential for AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, Astragalus polysaccharide, gut microbiota, neuroinflammation

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a degenerative neurodegenerative condition that has emerged as the primary cause of cognitive decline in the elderly globally, with women representing two‐thirds of all AD cases (Kaur et al. 2025). The etiology of AD is still under extensive investigation; it primarily involves the accumulation of amyloid‐beta (Aβ), hyperphosphorylation of tau protein, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, as well as synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss (Li et al. 2022; Wimo et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2022). Despite advances in pathological understanding, current pharmacotherapies, including aducanumab, lecanemab, galantamine, and donepezil, provide only transient symptomatic relief without fundamentally altering disease trajectory (Hampel et al. 2018). This therapeutic gap highlights the urgent need for novel disease‐modifying strategies.

Recent evidence positions the microbiota–gut–brain axis as a critical regulator of neurodegeneration (Cao et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2025). Studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosis is a consequence as well as a potential contributor to AD pathogenesis, potentially preceding overt neuropathology (Chen et al. 2020). This dysbiosis can facilitate neuroinflammation, impair Aβ clearance, and disrupt gut–brain signaling (Cryan and Dinan 2012; Noguchi‐Shinohara et al. 2025). Consequently, modulating the gut microbiota presents a promising yet mechanistically incompletely understood avenue for AD intervention.

Astragalus membranaceus, a traditional herb, contains Astragalus polysaccharides (APS) as its primary bioactive component. APS is known for its anti‐inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and neuroprotective properties (Stolzer et al. 2023). Recent studies have reported that APS can modulate gut microbiota composition, restore microbial equilibrium, and enhance intestinal barrier integrity (Wang et al. 2024; Wei et al. 2023). Furthermore, preliminary experimental evidence has shown that APS can alleviate cognitive deficits and Aβ pathology in AD mouse models (Kostiuchenko et al. 2024). However, a critical gap persists between these observational findings and a mechanistic understanding. It is plausible that the neuroprotective effects of APS are facilitated via the microbiota–gut–brain axis; however, this causal relationship remains largely hypothetical and unsystematically explored. Notably, it is still unclear how microbial changes caused by APS affect cognitive improvement and brain pathology in AD. Furthermore, the dose–response relationship of APS on the gut–brain axis and the molecular mechanisms involved are elusive.

In this study, we hypothesized that APS ameliorates AD cognitive impairment by reconfiguring the gut microbiota and modulating the homeostasis of the microbiota–gut–brain axis. Therefore, we conducted a 16S rRNA sequencing and multidimensional analysis strategy to integrate gut microbial changes with brain function and further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms using triple‐transgenic AD mice treated with APS at different doses. Our research seeks to explore how APS influences the mechanism of AD pathogenesis, thereby providing a novel microbiota‐targeted therapeutic strategy for AD.

Materials and Methods

Drugs and Reagents

APS was obtained from Chengdu Ruiya Technology Co. Ltd. (Sichuan, China) and was dissolved in sterile 0.9% NaCl solution to create a working solution with a concentration of 20 g/L. Physiological saline (NaCl, 74%) and paraformaldehyde (PFA; 4%) were obtained from Chengdu Ruiya.

Animals

In this study, 48 female triple‐transgenic (3xTg) mice with AD (20 weeks old) and 12 female wild‐type (WT) C57BL/6J littermates were obtained from Jiangsu Jinzhihe Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). All animals were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions at the Animal Experiment Center of Mianyang Central Hospital. Environmental parameters were strictly maintained at 25°C ± 2°C, with humidity 50% ± 5%, and a 12‐h light/dark cycle for 4 weeks. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Mianyang Central Hospital (Ethics Approval number: S20250305‐01), with all procedures following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Throughout the study, animals had ad libitum access to standard chow and autoclaved water, and their health status was regularly monitored by trained technicians.

Female 3xTg mice with AD were randomly divided into four experimental groups (n = 12 per group): model group (AD, 20 mg/kg body weight physiological saline) and APS low‐dose (APSL, 50 mg/kg body weight), APS medium‐dose (APSM, 100 mg/kg body weight), and APS high‐dose (APSH, 200 mg/kg body weight) groups. Doses and sample size were selected based on previous studies showing neuroprotective efficacy (Qin et al. 2020; Zou et al. 2025). A post hoc power analysis with G*Power 3.1 was conducted on our primary outcome (escape latency in the MWM test) to verify the sample size, showing a statistical power of over 80%. This indicates that our sample size of 12 mice per group provided a high probability (> 80%) of detecting true, biologically meaningful effects induced by APS. An additional control cohort consisted of 12 WT C57BL/6J littermates (WT group) treated with 20 mg/kg body weight physiological saline. All groups underwent daily oral gavage for 4 weeks, consecutively. Within 24 h of treatment, fresh fecal samples were collected aseptically, followed by the Morris water maze (MWM) behavioral testing. The entire experimental protocol followed strict adherence to the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), with appropriate anesthesia during invasive procedures.

The MWM Test

The MWM is commonly employed to evaluate spatial learning and memory in rodents. The MWM featured a circular pool measuring 120 cm in diameter and 50 cm in height, filled with water to a depth of 30 cm and maintained at 25°C. The water was made opaque with non‐toxic white dye. The pool was divided into four quadrants, with a visible platform positioned 1.5 cm below the water surface in a randomly chosen quadrant. The MWM was used to assess spatial learning and memory in mice over 6 days. On Day 1, the animals were acclimated to the pool, water temperature, and platform location. Hidden platform training was performed on Days 2–5, during which escape success within 60 s was recorded. A probe trial on Day 6, with the platform removed, measured the crossings at the former platform site and the time spent in the target quadrant. VisuTrack captured swim paths, escape latency, average speed, quadrant dwell time, and platform crossings. Animals were randomly assigned, and all water maze tests were conducted blind to ensure objectivity and reliability.

Metagenomic Analysis

Following the last gavage, fresh fecal pellets were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and shipped to Novogene (Beijing, China) for DNA analysis. PCR reactions were performed using 15 μL of Phusion High‐Fidelity PCR Master Mix, 0.2 μM of each primer, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycle included an initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 50°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. PCR products were mixed with an equal volume of 1X loading buffer containing SYBR Green and run on a 2% agarose gel. The products were then pooled in equal amounts, purified, and used to prepare sequencing libraries with added indexes. Library quality and quantity were assessed using Qubit, real‐time PCR, and bioanalyzer. Libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina platforms based on their concentration and required data output. Species annotation was performed using QIIME2.

Sample Collection

The tissues were harvested using two protocols. For routine dissection, mice were placed in an induction chamber and exposed to 3%–4% isoflurane in 100% O2 at 0.8–1 L min−1 until the righting reflex disappeared (2–3 min); on ice, brain and intestine were excised, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. For perfusion fixation, deeply anesthetized mice were transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% PFA; brain and intestinal tissues were then collected into 4% PFA and kept at 4°C. All perfusion procedures were performed in a Class II biosafety cabinet to maintain sterility.

Immunostaining

Fixed brains and intestines were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 4 μm sections. Brain slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or processed for IF, whereas intestinal sections were subjected to H&E and IHC. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were incubated with H&E, dehydrated, and coverslipped. For IHC, intestinal sections were rinsed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), quenched with 3% H2O2 for 25 min, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, GC305010; Servicebio) for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ZO‐1 (21773‐1‐AP; Invitrogen) and occludin (GB111401; Servicebio). After washing, the appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 50 min at room temperature and signals were developed using a DAB chromogen kit. IF staining of brain sections followed the same blocking step (3% BSA for 30 min), and GFAP (GB12096; Servicebio) and anti‐Iba‐1 (GB113502; Servicebio) were used as primary antibodies and incubated overnight at 4°C. Sections were then labeled with fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies, counterstained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, treated with an autofluorescence quencher for 5 min, rinsed, and mounted with anti‐fade medium. H&E and IHC images were captured using a Nikon ECLIPSE E100 microscope, and IF images were acquired using a Nikon ECLIPSE C1 confocal microscope. Semi‐quantitative analysis of GFAP, Iba‐1, ZO‐1, and occludin was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Samples were randomly assigned, and all histological analyses were performed blind.

Western Blotting

Mouse brain tissue was homogenized on ice in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (PC101; Epizyme) with protease (GRF101; Epizyme) and phosphatase inhibitors (GRF102; Epizyme). The total protein concentration was then measured using a BCA assay kit (ZJ102; Epizyme). Primary antibodies used included Tau (ET1603‐2; HUBAIO), P‐Tau (HA721666; HUBAIO), PS‐1 (HA723301; HUBAIO), ADAM10 (ET1703‐60; HUBAIO), BACE1 (HA722244; HUBAIO), IL‐6 (EM1701‐45; HUBAIO), and GAPDH (ET1601‐4; HUBAIO). Secondary antibodies were goat anti‐rabbit (HA1001; HUBAIO) and goat anti‐mouse (HA1006; HUBAIO). Equal protein amounts were loaded for SDS‐PAGE and western blot analysis. The relative expression levels of target proteins, normalized to GAPDH, were quantified using ImageJ.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Serum, brain, and jejunal homogenates were tested in duplicate for TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐4, IL‐10, and TGF‐β using commercial ELISA kits (Beyotime, Sichuan, China; Bioswamp, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturers' instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader, standard curves were created from serial dilutions of provided calibrators, and the biomarker concentrations were determined.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For multiple group comparisons, either one‐way analyses of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by post hoc analyses (Tukey's or Dunn's tests, respectively). A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

APS Enhances Learning Ability in 3xTg Mice

The experimental procedure is illustrated in Figure 1A. The Morris water maze (MWM) test was performed to evaluate the impact of APS on spatial learning and memory in 3xTg mice. During the 5‐day hidden‐platform training phase, WT mice progressively adopted direct paths to the platform, whereas AD mice predominantly swam along the wall or in random patterns. Mice administered low‐ and medium‐dose APS (APSL and APSM groups) showed partial goal‐directed navigation, with the APSM group locating the platform more rapidly. In contrast, mice receiving high‐dose APS (APSH group) exhibited a combination of goal‐directed and direct trajectories, which is indicative of the most pronounced improvement in spatial learning (Figure 1B). The escape latency on Day 1 was markedly longer in AD group than in all other groups and gradually decreased across training days. On Day 5, the escape latencies of WT group mice were notably shorter than those in the AD group (p < 0.01), and the APSH group exhibited the greatest reduction among treatments, followed by the APSM and APSL groups (Figure 1C). The average speed of the five groups of mice showed no significance (Figure 1D). Moreover, spatial memory was assessed on Day 6 of the probe trial. Compared with the AD group mice, WT and APSH group spent significantly more time in the target quadrant (p < 0.01) (Figure 1E). Platform crossings followed the same pattern: AD group less than WT group (p < 0.01); crossing numbers increased significantly in the APSH group compared with AD group (p < 0.05), but were not significantly increased in the APSM or APSL groups (Figure 1F). Collectively, these behavioral results indicate that APS ameliorates learning and memory deficits of 3xTg mice in a dose‐dependent manner.

APS Attenuates Neuropathological Hallmarks in 3xTg Mice

To clarify the impact of APS on disease progression, we examined canonical AD‐type pathology, focusing on neuronal morphology and Aβ deposition. H&E staining showed no notable inflammatory cells in the brains of WT group mice, whereas mice in AD and the APS groups exhibited varying degrees of neuronal damage (Figure 2A). IHC analysis was used to assess Aβ deposition in the CA1 region of each group. Representative images show that no plaques were present in the hippocampal CA1 region of WT group mice, whereas notable plaques were visible in both the AD group and the APS treatment groups (Figure 2B,C). Subsequently, these findings were validated using western blot analysis. Compared to the AD group, the levels of Tau, P‐tau, BACE1, and PS‐1 showed a decreasing trend after APS treatment and were positively correlated with concentration, although the differences did not reach statistical significance. Notably, the expression level of ADAM10 was significantly elevated in the APSH group (Figure 2D–I). In conclusion, APS intervention effectively alleviates the neuropathological features of 3xTg mice, providing insights into the potential mechanisms of APS in treating AD.

APS Attenuates Neuroinflammation in 3xTg Mice

Neuroinflammation is an initial and ongoing characteristic of AD, mediated by microglia and astrocytes that produce cytokines, chemokines, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Qin et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2023). IF analysis of the hippocampal DG and cerebral cortex revealed marked activation of both glial populations in 3xTg mice (Figure 3A,D); GFAP and Iba‐1 fluorescence signal of AD group mice was highest in the DG and cortex (Figure 3B,C,E,F). Both markers of the APSL and APSM groups were reduced relative to that of the AD group, although the differences were not significantly. Notably, APSH significantly downregulated GFAP in the DG (p < 0.01) and diminished Iba‐1 in the DG and cortex (p < 0.05). These results indicate that APS reduces activation of microglia and astrocytes, thus inhibiting neuroinflammation in the 3xTg mice.

To further assess whether APS mitigates neuroinflammation in 3xTg mice, we quantified six cytokines in brain homogenates. As shown in Figure 3G–L, the WT group exhibited the highest levels of anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐10, and TGF‐β) and the lowest levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) than the AD group. In the APS‐treated groups, an upward trend was observed for anti‐inflammatory factors, whereas pro‐inflammatory factors showed a decreasing trend. Notably, IL‐6 is the most significant: its expression was highest in the AD group and decreased in a dose‐dependent manner following APS treatment. To further verify this result, western blot analysis was conducted. Consistent with the ELISA results, IL‐6 expression was significantly lower in the WT group (***p < 0.001), whereas all APS intervention groups showed a marked increase in IL‐6 levels (Figure 3M). These results suggest that IL‐6 could act as a key inflammatory mediator playing a role in regulating the neuroinflammatory response in 3xTg mice.

APS Intervention Can Reduce Intestinal Tissue and Peripheral Inflammation Levels in 3xTg Mice

Dysregulation of the gut–brain axis closely correlated with inflammatory response is increasingly recognized as a critical mechanism driving AD pathogenesis. Therefore, we detected the inflammatory factors of jejunum and serum. As illustrated in Figure 4A–F, the WT group showed a marked reduction in pro‐inflammatory cytokines and an increase in anti‐inflammatory cytokines. APS intervention groups demonstrated a tendency toward reduced pro‐inflammatory and elevated anti‐inflammatory cytokines, with the most notable change also observed in IL‐6 expression.

Compared to AD mice, WT mice exhibited a significant inflammatory response of peripheral, with serum TNF‐α (p < 0.05), IL‐1β (p < 0.01), and IL‐6 (p < 0.01) significantly elevated (Figure 4G–I). APS reversed this pro‐inflammatory profile in a dose‐dependent manner. High‐dose APS treatment restored TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels. Conversely, anti‐inflammatory cytokine levels were the lowest in AD group and the highest in WT controls (Figure 4J–L). Collectively, these findings indicate that APS effectively mitigated both central and systemic inflammation in the 3xTg mice, and APS may exert its anti‐inflammatory role depending on the gut–brain axis.

APS Strengthens Intestinal Barrier Integrity

To evaluate the impact of APS on barrier integrity, we analyzed jejunal mucosal structure with H&E staining and measured tight‐junction proteins through IHC. WT mice displayed tall, densely packed, and regularly arranged club‐shaped villi with an intact epithelial surface and well‐preserved cellular architecture. In contrast, the AD group showed irregular shortened villi with widened intervillous spaces, deepened crypts, and thinned basal laminae. APS treatment rescued these abnormalities including villous spacing narrowed, crypt depth decreased, and basal lamina thickened, with the greatest improvement seen in the APSH group (Figure 5A). These findings indicate that APS effectively mitigated mucosal injury and reinforced intestinal barrier function in 3xTg mice with AD.

Effects of APS treatment on intestinal tight‐junction proteins in 3xTg mice. (A) Jejunal H&E staining. (B) IHC staining of jejunal occludin and ZO‐1. (C) Quantitative analysis of jejunal occludin. (D) Quantitative analysis of jejunal ZO‐1. Data were analyzed using ANOVA and followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. *p < 0.05; ns, no statistical difference.

IHC staining of jejunal sections revealed a marked disruption of tight‐junction integrity in the AD group, with occludin and ZO‐1 displaying the lowest immunoreactivity (Figure 5B). Quantitative analysis confirmed that occludin levels were significantly higher in the WT and APSH (p < 0.05) groups than in the AD group, whereas the APSL and APSM groups showed an increase, although the differences were not significant (Figure 5C). A parallel pattern was observed for ZO‐1 expression, which was minimal in AD group mice but was significantly restored in the WT and APSH (p < 0.05) groups (Figure 5D). Overall, these findings indicate that APS, especially with high concentration, strengthens the intestinal barrier by increasing the expression of tight‐junction proteins.

APS Restores Gut Microbial Abundance in Mice With AD

To assess how APS restructures the gut microbiota of 3xTg mice with AD, we compared the pre‐ and post‐gavage sequence counts (Figure 6A,B) and identified 10 bacterial phyla across all fecal samples (Figure 6C). Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobiota, and Patescibacteria constituted the main phyla in all groups. At the phylum level (Figure 6D), WT mice showed non‐significant decreases in Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Patescibacteria, and a modest increase in Verrucomicrobiota relative to the AD controls. The APSL and APSM groups exhibited similar, though non‐significant, decreases in Bacteroidetes, Verrucomicrobiota, and Patescibacteria, accompanied by a modest reciprocal increase in Firmicutes. In contrast, the richness of Bacteroidetes was significantly increased in the APSH group (p < 0.05), whereas Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobiota, and Patescibacteria trended downward. These results indicate that APS, particularly at higher doses, selectively elevated Bacteroidetes and tended to suppress Firmicutes, potentially altering the phylum‐level makeup of the gut microbiota in patients with AD. At the genus level, 10 taxa with higher abundances were analyzed, among which Alistipes, Ligilactobacillus, Akkermansia, and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group showed more noticeable changes in abundance across the groups (Figure 6E,F). WT mice exhibited markedly higher levels of Ligilactobacillus and Akkermansia (p < 0.01) and lower levels of Alistipes (p < 0.0001) than the AD group, whereas Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was modestly increased, without reaching significance. APSL significantly depleted Alistipes (p < 0.0001) and reduced Ligilactobacillus and Akkermansia; APSM reduced Alistipes (p < 0.01), while also inducing a non‐significant increase in Akkermansia; similarly, APSH also significantly reduced the genus Alistipes (p < 0.0001). Thus, APS robustly suppressed Alistipes abundance, with APSL and APSH showing the strongest effects, whereas APSM uniquely fostered a modest, non‐significant expansion of Akkermansia, revealing the potential role of Alistipes and Akkermansia in restoring gut microbiota richness in APS‐treated mice with AD.

Effects of APS on Gut Microbial Diversity

α‐diversity indices (observed species, Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1) were calculated to evaluate the diversity and distribution of gut microbiota. Compared with the AD group, the WT group displayed lower observed species, Shannon (p < 0.05), Simpson, and Chao1 indices, whereas APS administration elevated all four metrics in a dose‐dependent manner, although the differences were not statistically significant (Figure 7A–D). The modestly reduced α‐diversity in WT group mice is likely attributable to age, housing environment, and individual resilience instead of treatment. Collectively, these data indicate that APS tends to increase intestinal microbial α‐diversity in 3xTg mice.

Effects of APS on the α diversity of gut microbiota in 3xTg mice. (A) Observed_species index. (B) Shannon index. (C) Simpson index. (D) Chao1 index. Data were analyzed using ANOVA and followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. *p < 0.05; ns, no statistical difference.

β‐diversity analysis was used to quantify compositional dissimilarity among fecal microbiomes. Principal component analysis (PCA) using Bray–Curtis distances demonstrated that samples with closer interpoint distances possessed more similar taxonomic compositions. The first two principal coordinates (PC1 = 6.81%, PC2 = 4.2%) separated WT mice from all other groups, whereas APS‐treated clusters remained partially overlapping (Figure 8A). To enhance this analysis, we conducted a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) at the genus level employing the same distance metric. The dominant axes (PCo1 = 24.63%, PCo2 = 11.38%) produced a clear segregation between WT and APS cohorts without overlap and revealed pronounced dispersion of a subset of high‐dose APS samples, indicating individual variability within this group (Figure 8B). Collectively, these findings suggest that APS has the potential to modify the gut microbiota composition in 3xTg mice.

(C, D) The LEfSe identified the taxa with the greatest differences in abundance between the WT and AD groups. (E, F) The LEfSe identified the taxa with the greatest differences in abundance between the AD and APSL groups.

To elucidate the microbial signatures underlying APS activity, we applied a linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a preset LDA score > 4 to identify taxa that distinguished the treatment groups. WT and AD group revealed pronounced segregation: WT was enriched in Akkermansiaceae, Verrucomicrobiales, and Verrucomicrobiae, whereas AD harbored Prevotellaceae, Rikenellaceae, and Saccharimonadaceae (Figure 8C,D). A comparison of the AD and APSL groups showed that Rikenellaceae was the sole discriminant in AD, with no robust markers in the APSL group (Figure 8E,F). Similarly, comparison of the AD and APSM groups identified Oscillospiraceae and Clostridia UCG‐014 as AD‐specific taxa without strong indicators of the APSM treatment (Figure 9A,B). In contrast, the APSH treatment produced the most extensive taxonomic shift; the AD group remained associated with Rikenellaceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Clostridia UCG‐014, whereas the APSH group was distinctly enriched in Muribaculaceae, Bacteroidales/Bacteroidia, and Lachnospiraceae/Lachnospirales (Figure 9C,D). Collectively, LEfSe analysis showed that APS remodeled the gut microbiota in a dose‐dependent manner, with the high‐dose profile converging most closely to that of WT mice.

Discussion

The global incidence of AD continues to rise, yet the development of effective disease‐modifying therapies remains a significant challenge. The microbiota–gut–brain axis has emerged as a critical frontier in research on AD, providing novel insights into the disease's pathogenesis and potential intervention strategies. APS, among the most extensively studied and widely used active components of Traditional Chinese Medicine, represent a key bioactive constituent derived from Astragalus membranaceus. Renowned for their potent antioxidant, free‐radical‐scavenging, anti‐inflammatory, and bidirectional immunomodulatory properties, APS has become a focal point of recent research (Qin et al. 2020). This study revealed that APS ameliorates cognitive deficits and core neuropathological changes in 3xTg mice. Notably, our findings provide integrated evidence that these benefits are achieved through modulation of the gut microbiota and reinforcement of the intestinal barrier, thereby mitigating systemic and neuroinflammatory responses.

Previous research has shown that ADAM10, functioning as an α‐secretase in the non‐amyloidogenic pathway, promotes the non‐amyloidogenic processing of APP, thereby reducing Aβ generation (Cochet et al. 2013). In contrast, BACE1 acts as the β‐secretase that facilitates Aβ production, and its upregulated activity is closely associated with the pathogenesis of AD. In the present study, APS increased the expression of ADAM10 and suppressed BACE1 levels, potentially influencing APP processing through the regulation of relevant signaling pathways.

APS was found to strengthen the intestinal barrier (as indicated by upregulated expression of ZO‐1 and occludin) and improve gut structure. Published reports have shown that pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF can activate neural pathways involving p38 mitogen‐activated protein kinase, c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase, and glycogen synthase kinase‐3β, which drive pathological tau phosphorylation (Kitazawa et al. 2005). Changes in these signaling pathways may indirectly influence the expression of APP‐related enzymes. Our findings demonstrated that APS attenuated the heightened levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines in the intestinal tissue, serum, and brain tissue of AD model mice, while simultaneously promoting the expression of anti‐inflammatory factors. Therefore, APS may improve cognitive impairment and pathological phenotypes in 3xTg mice by modulating the gut microbiota, reinforcing the intestinal barrier, lowering pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels, and mitigating inflammatory neurotoxicity along with Aβ/tau pathology. However, future studies should incorporate mechanistic experiments, such as cell culture or gene knockout models, to validate the specific signaling pathways through which gut microbiota modulation affects APP‐processing enzymes.

Notably, gut dysbiosis serves as a significant source of elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines in both peripheral and central systems. Reduced gut microbiota diversity or an increase in harmful bacteria can compromise the intestinal barrier. The integrity of this barrier is crucial for restricting the entry of inflammatory factors (such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α) into the systemic circulation. Damage to the intestinal barrier allows microbial products (such as endotoxins) to leak into the bloodstream, activating immune responses, increasing blood–brain barrier permeability, stimulating central immune cells, and ultimately exacerbating neuroinflammation (Mustafa et al. 2025). Mice with neuroinflammation often exhibit characteristic behavioral alterations in the MWM, including prolonged escape latency, decreased percentage of time and distance in the target quadrant, and fewer platform crossings (Husn et al. 2023). Neuroinflammation, particularly chronic inflammation mediated by overactivated microglia and astrocytes, elevates levels of cytokines such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6. These cytokines can directly interfere with synaptic plasticity and disrupt the neural circuits essential for water maze performance (Quintanilla et al. 2004), thereby contributing to spatial learning and memory deficits observed in the test. Therefore, an intact intestinal barrier helps prevent bacterial endotoxins and other pro‐inflammatory microbial products from entering systemic circulation, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation. Changes in gut microbiota diversity, along with the levels of beneficial and harmful bacteria, may significantly contribute to the development of AD. Studies have shown that patients with AD exhibit a marked reduction in gut microbiota diversity, decreased abundance of beneficial bacteria such as butyrate‐producing strains, and an increase in pro‐inflammatory genera. This imbalance in the microbial community can stimulate microglia and promote the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (including IL‐1β and TNF‐α), thereby intensifying neuroinflammation in the brain (Liang et al. 2024). The present study found that treatment with APS significantly reduced the abundance of Alistipes, while enriching probiotic genera such as Akkermansia and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group in an AD mouse model. In research on AD, Alistipes has been clearly identified as one of the six risk genera significantly associated with disease diagnosis, and its abundance is notably elevated in the gut of patients with AD (Parker et al. 2020). Multiple studies classify Alistipes as a pro‐inflammatory bacterium that may drive disease progression by aggravating neuroinflammation and disrupting neurotransmitter balance (Chelakkot et al. 2018). Akkermansia has been shown to alleviate neuroinflammation by producing short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that inhibit the NF‐κB pathway and to strengthen intestinal barrier function (Rindler et al. 2021). In this study, Akkermansia exhibited a particularly notable dose‐dependent response to APS: the medium dose enhanced its abundance, whereas both high and low doses suppressed its relative abundance. This bifurcated effect aligns with observations reported by Zhang et al. (Rindler et al. 2021). Several members of the Lachnospiraceae family are key producers of SCFAs, particularly butyrate (Lin et al. 2024). SCFAs function as a primary energy source for colonic epithelial cells, playing a crucial role in maintaining gut epithelial integrity and supporting the regulation of the intestinal barrier (Marć et al. 2022). A decreased Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio has been associated with systemic inflammation and neuropathology in AD models (Vogt et al. 2017).

Although these findings are compelling, this study has certain limitations. First, the 4‐week treatment period, although sufficient to show efficacy, may not fully reflect the long‐term effects and sustainability of APS intervention in a chronic progressive disease such as AD. Second, despite establishing a strong correlation between gut microbiota remodeling and clinical improvement, definitive causal evidence requires validation through fecal microbiota transplantation studies. A crucial next step is to confirm whether fecal matter from APS‐treated AD mice can confer therapeutic benefits to untreated recipients with SD. Although our inferences regarding gut microbiota metabolites are grounded in existing literature, direct measurement of these metabolites in the host circulation and brain is still lacking. Future research should incorporate metabolomic analyses to solidify these mechanistic links. Finally, as with any preclinical model, the translational relevance of these findings for human AD awaits confirmation through clinical trials. In summary, this study revealed that APS modulates the gut microbiota and enhances intestinal barrier integrity, thereby alleviating systemic inflammation and suppressing neuroinflammation, which in turn ameliorates Aβ and tau pathology. Our work establishes the potential of APS as an intervention for AD and highlights the considerable promise of targeting the gut–brain axis for therapeutic development.

Conclusion

In this study, APS alleviated learning deficits, reduced Aβ deposition, and attenuated neuroinflammation in 3xTg mice with AD by reshaping gut microbial diversity and abundance‐most notably by modulating the relative abundances of Akkermansia and Alistipes, restoring intestinal barrier integrity through the up‐regulation of tight‐junction proteins, and dampening systemic and central inflammatory responses. Building on these findings, future studies should integrate metagenomics, metabolomics, and fecal microbiota transplantation to dissect the molecular networks underlying the APS–gut–brain axis and proceed to multicenter clinical trials to validate its translational potential.
