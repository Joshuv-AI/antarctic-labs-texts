Evaluation of the Current ATTR‐CM Treatment Landscape via a Mathematical Model of TTR Dissociation and Amyloid Formation
Authors: Stevan Methven Jeknić, Travis A. Ahn‐Horst, Alan X. Ji, Jin Hyun Ju, Morgan Paull, Sun‐Gou Ji, Uma Sinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTransthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR‐CM) is a progressive, often fatal disease arising from the dissociation of circulating transthyretin (TTR) tetramers into monomers that misfold and form amyloid fibrils that deposit in the myocardium and other tissues. Approved treatment paradigms involve tetramer stabilization with small molecules or TTR knockdown via RNA interference. Despite the clinical success of these drugs across several metrics (including all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular hospitalization), gaps remain in understanding how measurable biological changes, such as differences in serum TTR concentration, translate into reductions in amyloid deposition. To address this, we built a mechanistic mathematical model of the TTR system that integrates heterogeneous data sources, including in vitro assays and clinical data, and recapitulates key features of ATTR‐CM disease biology. The model predicts “monomer efflux,” the rate at which unfolded monomers could potentially form amyloid, as a proxy for the pathogenic mechanism, which is difficult to measure. Model‐predicted monomer efflux linearly correlated with reported rates of change in clinical measures, supporting its validity as a comparative metric. The model showed that monomer efflux is nearly twice as high in hereditary ATTR‐CM (ATTRv) as in wild‐type ATTR‐CM (ATTRwt). Comparison of treatment modalities showed that acoramidis yields the greatest reduction in monomer efflux (96% in ATTRwt, 95% in ATTRv) due to rapid, near‐complete stabilization of TTR. This framework enables quantitative interrogation of the disease system and principled comparisons across modalities that may help inform treatment selection.

Introduction

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR‐CM) is a progressive, often fatal disease in which misfolded transthyretin (TTR) monomers deposit in the myocardium and impair cardiac function [1]. TTR is a tetrameric transport protein that is primarily produced in the liver and maintained at high serum concentrations. TTR tetramers can dissociate into monomers, which can unfold and ultimately assemble into amyloid fibrils [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Extensive biochemical and genetic evidence indicates that tetramer dissociation is rate‐limiting for amyloidogenesis [5, 7].

Genetically, ATTR‐CM manifests either as a hereditary disease (variant ATTR‐CM, ATTRv) driven by pathogenic, destabilizing TTR variants that accelerate tetramer dissociation, or without a TTR mutation as wild‐type disease (ATTRwt). Over 100 TTR variants have been identified [8]. One clinically important variant is p.Val142Ile, which is enriched in individuals of African ancestry [9] and has been shown to increase the rate at which tetramers dissociate into monomers, providing a mechanistic basis for the genotype–phenotype relationship [6, 7]. The increased dissociation rate caused by the p.Val142Ile variant leads to lower serum TTR levels, faster disease progression, and a worse prognosis [10]. By contrast, variants such as p.Thr139Met stabilize the tetramer [7, 11] and possibly offer protective effects [12, 13].

Two therapeutic mechanisms have become central to the treatment of ATTR‐CM. Stabilizers such as tafamidis and acoramidis are small molecules that bind within the two thyroxine‐binding pockets of the TTR tetramer and prevent dissociation into monomers. Knockdown agents such as vutrisiran reduce hepatic production of TTR tetramers, thereby lowering the circulating concentration of tetramers available to dissociate into monomers. All three of these drugs demonstrated efficacy in pivotal phase III trials in ATTR‐CM (ATTR‐ACT for tafamidis; ATTRibute‐CM for acoramidis; HELIOS‐B for vutrisiran) and have been approved by the FDA and other regulatory agencies [14, 15, 16].

Despite these successes, differences in baseline trial populations and remaining unknowns in the disease pathway complicate therapeutic interpretation and comparison [17]. Specifically, stabilizers increase TTR, while knockdown agents decrease TTR, thereby creating therapeutic benefits through opposite effects on the central protein in the pathway, with both modalities showing a correlation between serum TTR concentrations and observed outcomes [18, 19]. However, how the magnitude of the difference in TTR concentration correlates with the disease‐causing deposition of amyloid fibrils is unknown.

This context motivates a complementary approach. Quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) integrates biophysical, translational, and clinical data into a unified mathematical framework and can be used to elucidate details of systems that are difficult to measure experimentally. These approaches have recently been applied to ATTR‐CM [20, 21, 22, 23]; however, no single model has considered the effects of stabilizers and knockdown agents on TTR stability and loss rates, and how these changes affect amyloid formation and deposition.

Given the rapidly evolving treatment landscape for ATTR‐CM, we applied QSP and built a model that represents the core disease process of ATTR‐CM, as well as the two mechanisms of approved therapeutic interventions. We show that TTR monomer efflux from the model, which represents the production of amyloidogenic species, correlates with clinical outcomes. Perturbing parameters in the system provides insights into processes that impact how circulating serum TTR translates to pathogenic amyloid deposition. Additionally, using monomer efflux, we systematically compare available therapeutics under common assumptions. Our objective is to provide a coherent, testable framework to better describe and quantify disease processes in ATTR‐CM that may serve as an additional piece of evidence in therapeutic decision‐making.

Materials and Methods

Model Structure and Construction

Two models were constructed: the main model of the ATTR‐CM disease pathway (Figure 1) and a fluorescent probe exclusion (FPE) model for fitting in vitro assay data (Figure S1). The FPE model was used to constrain drug binding parameters, while the physiological model was used for analysis of the disease pathway. All models were systems of ordinary differential equations and assumed mass action kinetics.

The mechanistic model structure representing TTR dissociation, amyloid formation, and pharmaceutical intervention. A system of ordinary differential equations, assuming mass action kinetics, was used to build the model. TTR was assumed to be produced as a tetramer at a constant, genotype‐independent rate. Clearance occurs via a first‐order mechanism of intact tetramers or monomers. Monomer efflux is the model prediction of disease‐formation potential. Stabilizing drugs are explicitly modeled, while knockdown interventions are captured via modulation of the TTR production rate. Note that a fixed pool of stabilizing drug was assumed at the C min concentration for acoramidis (10 μM) and C max concentration for tafamidis (26 μM). TTR, transthyretin.

Python (3.11.9) was used to implement the models. Additional details on model structure, implementation, and assumptions are available in the supplement. The FPE model equations for fitting in vitro assay data are provided in Supplemental Equations 1–7, and the initial conditions are in Table S1. The model equations for the ATTR‐CM model are provided in Supplemental Equations 8–16, and the initial conditions are in Table S2.

Parameter Optimization

Parameter optimization was performed using the LMFIT Python package (v.1.3.2) [24], Powell's algorithm, and custom, multi‐component objective functions. Parameter optimization began with the FPE model to determine relevant drug binding parameters in serum. In addition to the published fluorescence curves, we used published protein binding data to constrain the fit (Table S3). One‐dimensional profiling of the objective using different fixed parameter values was used to estimate confidence intervals. Due to the temperature difference between the FPE assay and the physiological model, we applied the van't Hoff equation to estimate the K ds at 37°C. Using the K ds from the FPE fit, we next constrained the physiological model using an objective function composed of several experimental and clinical data sets (Table S4).

After the physiological parameters were fit, knockdown by vutrisiran was modeled by adjusting the production rate of TTR to reproduce the data from phase III clinical trials [16, 25]. We fit a single exponential curve to the knockdown observed in each trial. Additional details for parameter optimization can be found in the Supplemental Methods.

The ATTRv population comprises many different variants, which may have different thermodynamic and kinetic properties. Since data for specific variants are limited, we parameterized thermodynamic and kinetic parameters based on the p.Val142Ile variant, which comprised a majority of the ATTRv population in ATTRibute‐CM, ATTR‐ACT, and HELIOS‐B [15, 16, 26]. Other common variants included p.Thr80Ala and p.Val50Met.

Monomer Efflux Analysis

The model prediction of disease severity is the rate at which amyloid accumulates in the model, which we term “monomer efflux.” TTR monomer efflux is a representation of the flux that could potentially deposit as amyloid. It is used as a measure relative to the predicted monomer efflux of ATTRwt patients in the same cohort, rather than as a prediction of the absolute amount of potential amyloid deposition.

Monomer efflux was validated by comparing with publicly available phase III clinical trial results [26, 27]. Specifically, the rate of change in the 6‐min walk distance (6MWD), the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Overall Summary (KCCQ‐OS) score, and serum N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) levels were calculated for placebo and treated groups in both ATTRwt and ATTRv. These values were then correlated with model predictions of monomer efflux.

Sensitivity Analysis

A global parameter sensitivity analysis of monomer efflux was performed using the Sobol method from the SALib python package [28]. Sensitivity of drug comparisons to the selected parameter set was performed by sampling 500 unique sets of parameters and excluding parameter sets that did not achieve physiologically observed serum TTR concentrations. Additional details can be found in the Supplemental Methods.

Results

Model Structures

We built a system of ordinary differential equations to represent the key steps in the ATTR‐CM pathway (Figure 1). TTR is produced as tetramers with a zero‐order, genotype‐independent rate. TTR tetramers reversibly dissociate into monomers, which can reversibly assume an unfolded state [5]. We assumed rapid, irreversible formation of amyloid fibrils from unfolded monomers [5]. Stabilizers bind tetramers at two sites with negative cooperativity [29, 30], and albumin at four equivalent sites [20, 31]. The model allows drug‐bound tetramers to dissociate. Knockdown is represented by scaling tetramer production to reproduce levels achieved in phase III clinical trials.

We additionally implemented an FPE model to fit drug binding parameters (Figure S1) [32]. The FPE model represents TTR tetramers, albumin, the fluorescent probe, and stabilizers. Stabilizers compete for TTR binding pockets with the probe, which becomes fluorescent upon covalent modification of TTR. We assumed that a single probe or drug binding event was sufficient to exclude the probe from the second pocket based on the known negative cooperativity of the thyroxine‐binding pockets.

Tafamidis and acoramidis both had nanomolar binding constants for the two binding sites available on TTR tetramers (acoramidis: K d1 = 6.00 nM, K d2 = 190 nM; tafamidis: K d1 = 4.33 nM, K d2 = 248 nM) and exhibited negative cooperativity, as previously reported [20, 29, 30]. By contrast, the predicted albumin binding site affinity differed markedly between the drugs (acoramidis: K dA = 250 μM; tafamidis: K dA = 3.05 μM), consistent with reported in vitro plasma protein binding and gel filtration/dialysis and FPE experiments [33, 34, 35].

A range of values has previously been reported for the Kd of albumin binding for tafamidis and acoramidis [20, 33, 36]. The reported differences between publications can be attributed to differences in experimental methods, assumptions regarding albumin binding sites, and which data were used as high‐confidence measures. In particular, the acoramidis K dA we estimate is higher than previous estimates. One key reason is that our values utilize the measured free fraction of acoramidis in the presence of plasma proteins to constrain albumin binding [34]. Previous estimates significantly underestimate the reported fraction free drug (see Supplemental Methods Section 1.2.4 for more details).

With drug binding constrained, physiological parameters were then optimized to fit the following clinical data and literature metrics (Figure S4): serum tetramer concentration for ATTRwt, ATTRv, and p.Thr139Met cohorts [12, 20, 27, 37, 38]; tetramer half‐life [39, 40]; kinetic and thermodynamic properties of the system [3, 5, 41, 42]; and intervention‐induced changes in serum tetramer concentrations from phase III trials [15, 16, 20]. Serum monomer concentrations were excluded from the objective function as they were only available for polyneuropathy patient cohorts [43, 44]. The full list of fitting targets is provided in Table S4.

Monomer Efflux Prediction

The pathway leading from unfolded monomers to amyloid formation, deposition in cardiac tissue, and ultimately clinical decline has been reviewed in detail [1, 45]. We take the model prediction of the rate of formation of amyloid fibrils from unfolded monomers (“monomer efflux”) as a proxy for this pathway and disease progression. Importantly, we used monomer efflux relative to untreated ATTRwt patients in the same cohort, as the correlation between monomer efflux and deposition of amyloid fibrils is not well characterized due to the challenges in directly measuring amyloid deposition and clearance.

We validated this metric by correlating genotype‐specific clinical endpoints from the phase III ATTR‐ACT and ATTRibute‐CM clinical trials, which had publicly available data for ATTRwt and ATTRv subgroups, including KCCQ‐OS (a quality‐of‐life questionnaire), 6MWD (a standard measure of cardiovascular fitness), and NT‐proBNP levels (a biomarker of heart muscle damage) [26, 27]. The HELIOS‐B clinical trial was excluded from this analysis because, to our knowledge, genotype‐specific outcomes over time have not been reported. For each metric, in both trials, we found linear correlations between monomer efflux and the change in the specific metric (Figure 2). This suggests that the model prediction of disease severity is related to the rate of change over time in markers of cardiovascular health.

Insights Into Disease Biology

To quantify the effect that model parameters representing different aspects of disease biology have on monomer efflux, we performed a global sensitivity analysis. For simulations across variant status, most variation in monomer efflux was explained by variations in rates of monomer loss, tetramer loss, monomer unfolding, tetramer production, and tetramer dissociation (Figure 3a). Parameters associated with current therapeutic approaches such as the tetramer dissociation rate (stabilizers) and tetramer production rate (knockdown) were expected to contribute to the model prediction of disease severity. However, the monomer loss rate was the most sensitive parameter in wild‐type simulations, with a strong dependence on TTR stability demonstrated by increased sensitivity in ATTRv simulations versus minimal sensitivity in stable p.Thr139Met simulations (Figure 3a). When including pharmaceutical interventions, monomer efflux rates showed higher sensitivity to tetramer loss for stabilizers than knockdown, while monomer loss showed higher sensitivity for knockdown than stabilizers (Figure S5a,b). As monomers are expected to be removed predominantly via renal clearance [46], the model predicts that kidney function is a key aspect of ATTR‐CM disease biology [47, 48]. This is consistent with clinical literature where renal impairment and fluid status track ATTR‐CM disease progression [19, 47].

We next wanted to explore aspects of disease biology that are difficult to measure in vivo, such as quantifying the rates of TTR leaving the system in its tetrameric, monomeric, or amyloid form. Tetramers can be removed from the body via hepatic clearance [49], while monomers are predominantly cleared in the kidneys [46]. The model predicted that the proportion cleared by different pathways is genotype‐dependent, with monomer‐mediated clearance accounting for 15% of the total clearance in ATTRwt, 41% in ATTRv, and only 1% in p.Thr139Met (Figure 3b). This suggests greater dependence on renal clearance in the variant population and explains why monomer efflux shows greater sensitivity to the monomer loss rate in ATTRv. In all genotypes, treatment with the TTR stabilizer acoramidis is predicted to shift most of the clearance of TTR to clearance as tetramers (Figure 3b).

We also sought to recapitulate clinical results that show a correlation between greater increases in tetramer levels at day 28 upon dosing with acoramidis and more favorable disease outcomes at month 30 [19], to understand the heterogeneity that might be driving these results. Using monomer efflux AUC as a proxy for the reported all‐cause mortality at month 30, we sampled key parameters individually to represent potential patient variability and to stratify by changes from baseline TTR. Only changes to the monomer loss rate could reproduce the finding that greater increases in serum TTR with acoramidis treatment lead to a greater risk reduction (Figure 3c), while modifying other parameters alone showed the opposite trend (Figure S5c–f). Specifically, increases in the rate of monomer clearance were associated with a greater change in TTR levels upon stabilization (Figure S5g), allowing more monomer to leave through clearance mechanisms than through the amyloid formation pathway. This aligns with previous reports showing that higher kidney function leads to more favorable ATTR‐CM outcomes [47, 48, 50].

Comparison of Approved Stabilizers

We next investigated how the two approved stabilizers, acoramidis and tafamidis, alter disease biology to create a therapeutic effect. To account for differences in pharmacokinetics, we assumed a constant drug concentration at the trough concentration for acoramidis (10 μM) and at the peak concentration for tafamidis (26 μM), due to their short and long serum half‐lives, respectively [34, 35].

Consistent with prior reports, the model predicted that, at trough concentrations, acoramidis achieves near‐complete stabilization of tetramers (96% of tetramers bound; Figure 4a). At peak serum concentrations, tafamidis stabilized tetramers to a lesser extent (82% of tetramers bound; Figure 4a). The model predicted that this difference is primarily due to the marked difference in binding to albumin (acoramidis: 27% of drug bound to albumin; tafamidis: 82% of drug bound to albumin; Figure 4a), consistent with in vitro measurements [20, 33, 34, 35]. Results were consistent across simulations with ATTRv (Figure S6).

Next, we looked at the downstream effects of this difference in fundamental binding properties. Both stabilizers were effectively able to meaningfully shift the flux balance of tetramer clearance, resulting in less overall flux cleared as monomers (Figure 4a). Consistent with previous reports, we found larger increases in tetramer concentration with acoramidis than tafamidis (ATTRwt: acoramidis 19% increase, tafamidis 15% increase; ATTRv: acoramidis 72% increase, tafamidis 55% increase), reflective of the greater degree of stabilization (Figure 4b). This effect translates to significantly reduced monomer efflux with acoramidis compared with tafamidis (Figure 4c). Acoramidis reduced ATTRwt and ATTRv monomer efflux by 96% and 94%, respectively, versus 79% and 72% with tafamidis.

We then investigated how robust these findings were to changes in parameter values. First, to account for potential pharmacokinetic variation, we ran all analyses using lower concentrations of acoramidis and higher concentrations of tafamidis and found qualitatively similar results in both cases (Figures S7 and S8). Looking across other parameters with our sensitivity analysis, variance in the monomer efflux comparison between acoramidis and tafamidis was largely explained by tetramer loss and, to a lesser extent, by tetramer production (Figure S9). Loss of monomer and monomer unfolding rates, which were relatively unconstrained parameters during our fitting process and showed high contributions to the variance of monomer efflux for a single drug, showed limited contributions to the monomer efflux comparison, providing confidence that comparisons across this key model output are supported by the extensive datasets used to identify the tetramer‐related parameters.

Finally, we performed a systematic sampling of parameters in the model and found that, even with a range of parameter values, 97.9% of parameter sets in ATTRwt simulations and 98.7% of parameter sets in ATTRv simulations showed a greater monomer efflux reduction with acoramidis than tafamidis (Figure S10a,b). In these simulations, the increased stabilizing effect of acoramidis led to small increases in serum tetramer concentration over tafamidis (Figure 4d), mainly due to a higher fraction of bound tetramer. However, this small difference in serum tetramer increase was associated with a much larger decrease in monomer efflux (Figure S10c), highlighting the nonlinear nature of amyloid formation and the benefits of near‐complete stabilization of TTR. Quantifying the effect with a linear fit to the results from all sampled ATTRwt simulations, we found that on average, a 1% greater increase in serum tetramer from baseline can lead to an additional 4.6% decrease in monomer efflux from baseline (Figure 4e).

Taken together, these data are consistent with acoramidis at trough serum concentrations being a more effective stabilizer of TTR tetramers than tafamidis at peak serum concentrations. Our results further expand on this finding by quantifying the differences in terms of a metric of disease progression and a measurable biomarker.

Comparison of Stabilization and Knockdown Modalities

To compare treatment modalities, we compared acoramidis to vutrisiran, a knockdown agent approved for the treatment of ATTR‐CM. We first ensured that the model recapitulated the serum TTR reductions found in the HELIOS‐B monotherapy population [16]. We fit an exponential decay function to the serum TTR levels to model the time‐dependent reduction in the tetramer production rate. In HELIOS‐B, the first time point measured was 6 weeks after the initial dose. To model the maximal therapeutic effect, we applied 6‐week knockdown from the start of the simulation. This provided an accurate fit to the data for both HELIOS‐B (Figure 5a) and HELIOS‐A (Figure S11), a phase III trial of vutrisiran in polyneuropathy [25].

Across 30‐month simulations, acoramidis reduced the total monomer efflux AUC more than vutrisiran (ATTRwt: acoramidis 96% reduction, vutrisiran 75% reduction; ATTRv: acoramidis 94% reduction, vutrisiran 75% reduction) (Figure 5b). Given clinical separation as early as month 3 in ATTRibute‐CM [15], we examined early time points to quantify the effect of the pharmacodynamics of vutrisiran. At month 3, the relative difference was more pronounced, with acoramidis reducing the monomer efflux AUC by 96% in ATTRwt, versus a 61% reduction with vutrisiran (Figure 5c). Similar results were obtained when using knockdown levels observed in HELIOS‐A (Figure S12).

As for our comparison of stabilizers, we investigated the robustness of our monomer efflux findings by running a sensitivity analysis with systematically sampled parameter values. Results showed that, in 97.2% of the parameter sets in ATTRwt and 93.6% of the parameter sets in ATTRv, acoramidis showed favorable reductions in the monomer efflux AUC versus vutrisiran (Figure S13).

Discussion

Treatment options for ATTR‐CM have expanded drastically in recent years and are poised to continue growing [51]. In this work, we created a mathematical framework describing amyloid formation and therapeutic intervention in ATTR‐CM to elucidate quantitative aspects of disease biology. By integrating multiple therapeutic options in a cohesive model of amyloid formation, we enabled systematic comparison and removed the confounding effect of baseline trial cohort differences that can arise when comparing therapies across different clinical trials. Our results consistently indicated that acoramidis is the most effective approved treatment for reducing TTR monomer efflux.

Beyond therapeutic comparisons, a QSP model provides a powerful approach to elucidate quantitative details about disease systems and provide insights into perturbations or outputs that may be experimentally intractable. For example, our model predicted that ATTRv patients on average incur nearly twice as much monomer efflux as ATTRwt patients, consistent with their worse outcomes [10]. Moreover, we quantified the relative burden on the two primary pathways of clearance required to match the constitutive hepatic production of TTR tetramers. Consistent with the increased dissociation to monomers, the model indicated that ATTRv patients have a greater burden of renal clearance of monomers than ATTRwt patients.

The model also generates hypotheses to explain clinical data. We explored recent work by Maurer and colleagues showing that the degree of the early increase in serum TTR with acoramidis treatment is associated with the degree of the reduction in all‐cause mortality in ATTR‐CM [19]. Consistent with this finding, our model showed that decreased monomer efflux produced greater increases in serum TTR after treating with stabilizers. Additionally, by systematically adjusting various aspects of physiology, we showed that representing variability among individuals by adjusting monomer clearance rates was able to recapitulate clinical findings [19]. As monomer clearance is predominantly mediated by the kidney, these results further suggest that kidney function is an important aspect of disease progression in ATTR‐CM [47, 48].

A similar modeling approach was used by Tess and colleagues to characterize the clinical response to tafamidis [20]. As with our work, they first constrained drug binding and then built a population TTR binding and clinical outcome model based on differences in monomers. Despite using disparate data sources for drug binding parameters, we found similar K d values for tafamidis (all within reported 95% confidence intervals from Tess and colleagues). Tess and colleagues used their model to interrogate the possible therapeutic benefits of stabilization, positing that tafamidis achieves benefits near the maximal theoretical benefit of stabilization within their framework. For example, they suggested that the increase in TTR concentration in ATTRv with tafamidis (55%) is near a theoretical upper bound predicted by their model (59%). In our framework, stabilization‐induced tetramer concentration increases can exceed that theoretical upper bound, as acoramidis increased tetramer concentration in ATTRv by 71%, consistent with reported clinical observations [27]. Importantly, our model demonstrated that even small increases in tetramer concentration due to more complete stabilization can translate to much larger decreases in monomer efflux.

Taken together, these results support the utility of a mechanistic modeling framework, while underscoring the role of key assumptions in shaping model‐based inferences. Our model was calibrated to mean clinical and biomarker trajectories rather than individual patient data. We also assumed that TTR production and other parameters are constant over time. This limited our ability to capture the full heterogeneity observed in ATTR‐CM but also suggests a natural extension: estimating individual‐level parameters to predict patient‐specific treatment benefit and to explore how factors such as genotype, comorbidities, or baseline disease stage shape response to treatment. An individualized version of the model could also provide a more stringent test of monomer efflux as a metric by asking whether patient‐specific changes in efflux consistently track with individual outcomes. A natural framework for this extension could be fitting a nonlinear mixed‐effects model to longitudinal patient‐level data for clinical biomarkers and endpoints. Parameter variability could be structured against likely covariates, and identifiability analysis could determine which parameters can be identified from clinical measures.

Additionally, we did not consider TTR binding partners, such as retinol‐binding protein and thyroxine, and how they might impact the amyloidogenic cascade or be impacted by changing TTR levels [37, 52]. Finally, we modeled genotype differences through differences in TTR stability. Future work could expand to more clinically important and kinetically distinct variants, provided the required kinetic and thermodynamic data are available. In addition, conformational changes due to variants, stabilizers, or binding partners could also impact clearance mechanisms such as cellular uptake and kidney clearance and may be another avenue for future analysis directions. In particular, our parameter fit underestimates the increase in tetramer concentration with stabilizer treatment in ATTRwt, possibly indicating that stabilizer treatment may affect parameters besides tetramer dissociation, such as tetramer clearance, as described in recent modeling approaches [22, 23].

In addition to the suggestions above, other clear extensions of our model are those that would enable modeling of therapies currently in clinical development once late‐stage data is available for such treatments. Gene‐editing strategies could be represented in our model similarly to RNA interference. Antibody‐based therapies are designed to promote amyloid clearance, something our model does not capture. However, recent work has laid a foundation for modeling amyloid clearance in ATTR even though certain aspects of amyloid formation such as the location (serum or tissue) and exact process may remain difficult to characterize [21]. Modeling multiple modalities in the same framework also provides the ability to explore combination therapies (e.g., stabilizers and knockdown agents) and how that compares to monotherapies alone.

In conclusion, this work expands previous modeling work in ATTR‐CM by integrating recent clinical data across multiple approved therapeutic modalities. By linking stabilization, hepatic knockdown, and disease biology through a common readout of monomer efflux, the model generates testable hypotheses, quantifies the role of renal monomer clearance, and enables modality‐level comparisons without confounding contributions from baseline differences.
