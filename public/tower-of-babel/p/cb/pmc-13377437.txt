Alarm configuration and adherence to prescribed mean arterial pressure targets in the intensive care unit: A multicentre pre–post evaluation
Authors: Thomas K. MacLaverty, Paul J. Young, Ary Serpa Neto, Kyle C. White, Alessandro Caroli, Glenn Eastwood, Helen Young, Leah Peck, Yukiko Hikasa, Nuanprae Kitisin, Jonathan Nübel, Rahul Costa-Pinto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether structured education combined with target-aligned arterial pressure alarm limits improved alignment between documented achieved and clinician-prescribed mean arterial pressure (MAP) during vasopressor therapy.DesignMulticentre pre-post quality-improvement evaluation.SettingThree tertiary intensive care units in Australia and New Zealand.ParticipantsAdult intensive care unit patients receiving continuous vasopressor infusion with invasive arterial pressure monitoring, a documented clinician-prescribed MAP target, and available MAP and alarm-limit data during 24 h of vasopressor therapy.InterventionA centre-level bundled implementation strategy comprising structured education, target-aligned arterial pressure alarm-limit configuration, and bedside implementation support, without mandating MAP targets or altering prescribing practice.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was MAP gap, defined as documented achieved MAP minus prescribed MAP at four-hourly observation points during vasopressor therapy. Secondary outcomes included alarm window configuration, inter-site variation in MAP gap, exposure to MAP <60 mmHg, and exploratory short-term clinical outcomes.ResultsAfter the intervention, alarm window width was narrower and more consistently aligned with prescribed MAP targets (median difference in width −40 mmHg, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −45 to −35). The post-intervention phase was associated with a lower documented MAP gap (median difference: −4 mmHg, 95% CI: −5.0 to −3.0; p < 0.001), reflecting reduced overshoot above target. The proportion of observations with MAP <60 mmHg did not increase. Clinical outcome data were exploratory and incompletely captured.ConclusionsStructured education combined with target-aligned arterial pressure alarm limits and bedside implementation support was associated with improved alignment between documented achieved and clinician-prescribed MAP. Further evaluation should include continuous pressure-time exposure, vasopressor dose, alarm burden, and longitudinal adherence to target-aligned alarm limits.

Introduction

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is a central haemodynamic variable in the management of shock and is routinely targeted using vasopressor therapy in critically ill patients. International guidelines recommend an initial MAP of 65 mmHg in septic shock, with subsequent individualisation based on clinical context and comorbidity.1 The optimal MAP target, however, remains uncertain. Randomised trials comparing lower and higher MAP targets in septic shock, vasodilatory hypotension, and post–cardiac arrest populations have not shown consistent improvements in mortality or neurological outcomes with routinely targeting higher pressures.[2], [3], [4], [5] Recent metanalyses in critically ill and surgical populations reach similar conclusions, although certainty is limited by heterogeneity in patient populations and target ranges.6,7 Potential risks of both inadequate perfusion and excessive vasoconstriction have been recognised in retrospective analyses showing association between prolonged hypotension and higher mortality and organ injury,[8], [9], [10] and studies associating higher vasopressor dose and cumulative exposure with potential harm.11,12 These findings suggest that optimal MAP management sits within a relatively narrow therapeutic range, consistent with the proposed “Goldilocks zone” between hypotension and vasopressor excess described in recent commentary.13

Despite guideline recommendations and trial evidence suggesting a narrow optimal range, achieved MAP in routine practice frequently exceeds prescribed targets and varies widely among institutions, even among patients with similar clinical characteristics.[14], [15], [16], [17] Whether this gap can be reliably narrowed in routine intensive care unit (ICU) practice remains uncertain. Bedside implementation of haemodynamic monitoring systems may contribute to this discrepancy. Arterial pressure alarms provide continuous feedback to clinicians and can shape vasopressor titration independently of the documented MAP prescription, with small trials showing that tightly aligning alarms improves compliance.18,19 Consistent with this, nursing survey data20 indicate that MAP alarm thresholds are commonly set above prescribed targets, that clinicians express greater concern for hypotension than hypertension during vasopressor therapy, and that there is support for education and systematic alarm-limit optimisation to improve target adherence. Whether narrowing of MAP alarm limits can reduce the gap between prescription and practice is feasible in clinical practice is uncertain.

The present study was designed to address this gap by evaluating a multicentre, centre-level implementation strategy comprising structured education, target-aligned arterial pressure alarm limits, and bedside implementation support. The primary objective was to assess whether this approach improved alignment between documented achieved MAP and intended MAP without increasing exposure to MAP <60 mmHg. Secondary objectives included examining effects on practice variation across sites and exploring the feasibility of alarm-limit alignment as a scalable process-of-care intervention relevant to future haemodynamic trials.

Methods

Design and setting

Multicentre pre-post observational evaluation of a quality-improvement intervention in three tertiary ICUs in Australia and New Zealand (The Princess Alexandra Hospital (PAH), Queensland, Australia; The Austin Hospital, Victoria, Australia; Wellington Regional Hospital (WRH), New Zealand). Participating ICUs used continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring and electronic bedside systems capable of programmable alarm limits at 5 mmHg intervals and storage of physiological and alarm-limit data. The arterial monitoring systems and software used within units were not standardised. Philips IntelliVue (Amsterdam, Netherlands), Dräger Infinity C700 (Lübeck, Germany), and Mindray N1 (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) monitors were used across sites. WRH used paper charts with manually recorded values, whilst the other two sites used electronic information systems (Alarta, Incarta, Victoria, Australia at The Austin; MetaVision, iMDsoft, Tel Aviv, Israel at PAH) with automatically recorded and manually verified values.

The study was conducted across pre-intervention (PRE; during April–May 2025) and post-intervention (POST; during October–November 2025) phases, with no major concurrent changes to vasopressor protocols or staffing models reported by participating sites. The three participating ICUs were tertiary adult ICUs using continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring and programmable bedside alarm limits, although monitor platforms and local data systems differed between sites.

Participants

We included a convenience sample of consecutive adult ICU patients who received a continuous infusion of any vasopressor and had a documented clinician-prescribed MAP target, continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring, and documented MAP values and alarm-limit settings over 24 h of vasopressor therapy. Vasopressor therapy was recorded as present or absent during each observation period. Vasopressor selection, dose, and cumulative exposure were not captured in a harmonised form across sites. Inclusion also required clinician willingness for the prescribed MAP target and alarm limits to be applied.

Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, lacked invasive arterial monitoring, lacked a documented MAP target, had an alternative perfusion target (such as cerebral perfusion pressure), or had insufficient monitoring data to compute MAP gap or alarm-window parameters.

During each study period, participating sites enrolled a convenience sample of consecutive eligible patients. Complete screening logs were not available across all sites.

Intervention

The intervention was a centre-level, bundled implementation strategy comprising structured education, target-aligned arterial pressure alarm-limit configuration, and bedside feedback during the POST phase. A focused educational package consisting of posters, an online educational presentation, and bedside teaching was provided at each site. The key message was to “use the minimum effective dose of vasopressor required to maintain the MAP at the prescribed target, avoiding unnecessary elevation above this target”. During the POST phase, participating sites implemented a structured change to arterial pressure alarm-limit configuration intended to narrow alarm windows around prescribed MAP targets. MAP alarms were set 5 mmHg above and below the clinician-prescribed target. The study did not mandate a specific MAP target and did not alter prescribing practice. The intended downstream mechanism was a change in bedside vasopressor titration behaviour while preserving clinician discretion over the prescribed MAP target. Direct feedback to nurses caring for patients in the POST phase was provided by study investigators to support use of the specified MAP alarm limits.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome was MAP gap, defined as the difference between documented-achieved MAP and prescribed MAP during periods where vasopressors were administered.

Achieved MAP was defined as the MAP value recorded at prespecified four-hourly clock timepoints during the first 24 h of vasopressor therapy. Scheduled observations were recorded at 12:00, 16:00, 20:00, 00:00, 04:00, and 08:00. These values represented discrete documented observations rather than continuously sampled arterial pressure data. Under this fixed-interval sampling framework, each observation carried equal temporal weight within the 24-h observation period. MAP gap was calculated as achieved MAP minus the contemporaneous clinician-prescribed MAP target for each four-hourly observation obtained during vasopressor therapy. Presence or absence of vasopressor therapy was derived from medication administration records. Prescribed MAP was obtained from clinician documentation. Alarm window width was defined as the difference between upper and lower MAP alarm limits. Observed MAP values were classified relative to the clinician-prescribed target as below, on, or above target (MAP <, =, or > prescribed). For below-target observations, deviation magnitude was calculated as prescribed minus observed MAP (mmHg). Positive MAP gap values indicate overshoot above target, whereas negative values indicate under-target MAP. Clinically meaningful hypotension was defined a priori as MAP <60 mmHg. This threshold was selected as a pragmatic safety measure below commonly prescribed ICU MAP targets, rather than as an individualised lower limit of safe perfusion for all patients. Patient characteristics and outcomes were obtained from local data sources.

Bias considerations

Potential sources of bias included secular trends between the pre- and POST periods, differential data completeness, intermittent rather than continuous MAP sampling, potential documentation bias in an open-label POST phase, and unmeasured confounding related to illness severity or clinician behaviour. To mitigate clustering and repeated measurements, we used mixed-effects models with random intercepts for the patient and site. Clinician-level clustering was not captured.

Study size

Sample size was determined pragmatically by the number of eligible patients during the predefined study periods; no formal power calculation was performed.

Statistical analysis

Process measures are summarised as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) unless otherwise stated. Between-phase differences are reported as Hodges–Lehmann median differences with 95% confidence intervals. The primary model estimated the association between study phase and repeated MAP-gap observations, rather than a single patient-level summary outcome. To account for repeated MAP observations within patients and clustering within sites, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with MAP gap as the dependent variable, study phase (pre vs post) as a fixed effect, and random intercepts for the patient and site. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Only observations where a vasopressor was being administered and MAP was recorded were included in analysis. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made. No prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Vienna, Austria) (v4.5.1; R Core Team 2021, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and used available data; no imputation was performed.

Ethics

In New Zealand, the study was approved as a quality-improvement project using routinely collected healthcare information to address a mismatch between prescribed and delivered treatment. It was conducted with a waiver of consent and was outside the scope of Health and Disability Ethics Committee (HDEC) review. Approval was gained in Australia from the Austin Health Human Research Ethics Committee (reference number VicTRI-23360).

Across the first 24 h following vasopressor initiation, alarm window width remained wide in the PRE phase but was persistently narrower in the POST phase (Supplemental Fig. 2). Median alarm-window width was unchanged over time in the PRE phase, from 45 mmHg (IQR: 35–75) to 45 mmHg (IQR: 40–76). In the POST phase, alarm-window width remained substantially lower, although it widened from 10 mmHg (IQR: 10–10) to 14 mmHg (IQR: 10–38). Widening of alarm limits occurred during ongoing care after initial POST standardisation, suggesting partial loss of intervention fidelity over time. Site-level summaries demonstrated heterogeneity in alarm-limit trajectories over time, including differences in whether limits were maintained or widened during ongoing care (Supplemental Table 3).

Site-level changes are presented descriptively (Supplemental Fig. 4). Median MAP gap decreased from 10.0 to 5.0 mmHg at Site 1 and from 8.0 to 1.0 mmHg at Site 2, while Site 3 showed a smaller change from 7.0 to 6.0 mmHg. Absolute gaps decreased at all sites but between-site variability remained.

Across both study phases, narrower alarm windows were associated with smaller deviations from prescribed MAP. In the POST phase, closer alignment of alarm limits with prescribed MAP corresponded with reduced overshooting above target (Supplemental Table 2).

While the proportion of observations below the prescribed MAP target increased from 5.9% to 15.8% (odds ratio: 3.35, 95% CI: 1.57–7.16; p < 0.01; Supplemental Table 3), these deviations were small in magnitude (median: 2 mmHg below target, with 63% within 1–2 mmHg). The proportion of observations with MAP <60 mmHg was unchanged (0.8% vs 1.0%), consistent with centring of documented achieved MAP around the prescribed target following the intervention.

Clinical outcomes

Short-term clinical outcomes are summarised in Supplemental Table 4. No clear differences between PRE and POST phases were observed in hospital mortality, intensive care unit length of stay, hospital length of stay, or use of organ support therapies. Clinical outcome data were extracted from local study datasets rather than through a harmonised registry linkage. In 11 of 45 patients in the POST phase, mortality and length of stay outcomes were not captured.

Discussion

In this multicentre pre-post quality-improvement evaluation, a bundled intervention comprising structured education, target-aligned arterial pressure alarm limits, and bedside implementation support was associated with closer alignment between documented achieved MAP and clinician-prescribed MAP targets. The intervention was also associated with reduced overshooting above target and lower inter-site variation. These effects persisted after adjustment for repeated observations and clustering at patient and site levels. The dominant observed change was a reduction in upward deviation, without an observed increase in MAP values < 60 mmHg.

These findings underscore the role of implementation factors in shaping haemodynamic delivery. Alarm configuration and alarm burden are known to influence clinician behaviour, responsiveness, and adaptation in ICU environments.[21], [22], [23], [24] Observational studies demonstrate that alarm frequency is affected by both patient characteristics and monitor settings, reinforcing the notion that alarms function as behavioural cues rather than passive safety nets.23,24 In the present study, narrowing alarm limits following education and bedside feedback was associated with closer adherence to clinician intent. These findings suggest that monitoring configuration and implementation processes may contribute to the delivery of prescribed haemodynamic targets.

The predominant observed effect was a reduction in MAP overshoot. Although this may reduce unnecessary vasopressor exposure, vasopressor dose was not measured and this mechanism cannot be confirmed in the present dataset. Excessive vasopressor exposure has been consistently associated with adverse outcomes, including increased mortality and organ dysfunction, in observational analyses.25,26 Dose–response relationships between noradrenaline exposure and mortality further support the biological plausibility of harm from unnecessary escalation.26 The present findings are therefore consistent with efforts to minimise pressor burden where clinically feasible, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of reduced vasopressor exposure.

A further important finding was the reduction in inter-site variability following the intervention. Prior to implementation, documented achieved MAP and alarm configuration differed substantially across centres, consistent with previously described practice variation in vasopressor management.[13], [14], [15] The smaller observed change at one site should be interpreted cautiously. The study was not powered to identify site-specific determinants of response, and site-level differences may reflect case mix, local monitoring practice, staffing patterns, or implementation fidelity.

Short-term clinical outcomes did not differ clearly between study phases, but these data were exploratory and incompletely captured. This study was not designed or powered to detect modest differences in mortality or other patient-centred outcomes and results should be interpreted as evidence of a process-of-care effect rather than proof of clinical benefit. Nonetheless, the ability to systematically alter documented haemodynamic delivery has important implications for future trials. Many MAP target trials specify distinct haemodynamic targets; however, achieved pressures often overlap in practice, reducing physiological contrast between trial arms and potentially contributing to neutral results.6,7,27

Future haemodynamic trials may therefore benefit from incorporating implementation safeguards such as structured education, standardised alarm-limit configuration, bedside feedback, and continuous monitoring of achieved exposure. Metrics including time-weighted MAP gap, time in range, duration outside target ranges, pressure-time dose, and cumulative vasopressor dose may better characterise delivered therapy than nominal target assignment alone.[26], [27], [28], [29] Target-aligned alarm limits may be a pragmatic way to enhance intervention fidelity but their sustainability and alarm-burden consequences require direct measurement.

Finally, these findings support broader consideration of precision-oriented haemodynamic strategies. Growing evidence suggests heterogeneity in patient response to MAP targets, with subgroup and meta-analytic data indicating that a single universal threshold may not be optimal for all patients.2,3,6,7 Differential effects may be particularly relevant in older patients, patients with chronic hypertension, vasodilatory shock, cardiac dysfunction, or post-cardiac arrest syndromes. These groups may differ in their tolerance of lower MAP values and in the potential harms of excess vasopressor exposure. The present study was not designed to assess subgroup-specific effects.

The widening of some POST alarm windows over time may suggest partial loss of fidelity after initial implementation. This finding has implications for future haemodynamic trials, where incomplete implementation fidelity may reduce separation between prescribed targets and achieved haemodynamic exposure. Alarm-limit standardisation may therefore need to be paired with repeated feedback, automated audit, or real-time reporting of alarm-limit changes.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The pre-post observational design limits causal inference, and secular trends or unmeasured co-interventions may have contributed to observed differences. Although baseline characteristics appeared broadly similar between phases, incomplete capture of some covariates restricts the ability to exclude residual confounding. Complete screening logs were not available across all sites, so we cannot describe the number or characteristics of patients screened but not enrolled. This limits assessment of generalisability. Although alarm windows were standardised at initiation, some widening occurred during ongoing care, indicating partial decay of intervention fidelity.

The intervention was a bundled implementation strategy and was not designed to isolate the independent effect of alarm configuration from education, bedside feedback, or investigator presence. Alarm frequency, actionable alarm burden, alarm silencing, staff workload, and user experience were not measured. We therefore cannot determine whether widening of alarm windows reflected clinically appropriate adaptation, alarm fatigue, or loss of intervention fidelity. Narrower alarm windows may increase alarm burden or contribute to alarm fatigue, a recognised risk in ICU monitoring environments.18,21,22

Achieved MAP was based on intermittent four-hourly documented observations rather than continuously sampled arterial pressure data. The fixed sampling schedule gave each observation equal temporal weight. Intermittent sampling may not capture short-lived or recurrent deviations above or below the target, particularly for an alarm-based intervention intended to influence brief haemodynamic excursions. Continuous waveform-derived pressure-time exposure was not available, limiting comparison with analyses based on continuous haemodynamic exposure.

Although the presence or absence of vasopressor therapy was recorded, time-resolved vasopressor dose and cumulative vasopressor exposure were not captured as infusion-rate documentation, drug concentrations, dose units, and local electronic medication systems differed across sites. We therefore cannot determine whether reduced MAP overshoot translated into lower noradrenaline-equivalent dose, lower cumulative vasopressor exposure, or more vasopressor-free time.

The absence of increased MAP <60 mmHg does not exclude harm from smaller below-target deviations, particularly in patients with chronic hypertension, impaired autoregulation, or higher individual perfusion requirements. Clinician-level clustering was not captured and may have influenced observed practice patterns. In addition, with only three participating ICUs, estimates of site-level variance are imprecise and should not be overinterpreted.

Data completeness varied across outcomes and monitoring-derived variables, and the analytic cohort represents a subset of ICU patients with available invasive arterial pressure and alarm-limit data. Registry linkage to Centre for Outcome and Resource Evaluation (CORE) was not undertaken for this quality-improvement analysis. Outcome ascertainment was therefore incomplete for 11 POST patients (24% of the total cohort) whose local study dataset had not captured ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, or mortality at the time of analysis. These outcomes were unadjusted, exploratory, and not part of the primary process-of-care endpoint and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Findings may not be generalisable to settings with different monitoring systems, staffing models, or vasopressor practices.

Conclusion

In this multicentre pre-post quality-improvement evaluation, structured education combined with target-aligned arterial pressure alarm limits and bedside implementation support was associated with closer alignment between documented achieved MAP and clinician-prescribed MAP targets. The intervention was associated predominantly with reduced overshoot above target, without an observed increase in MAP values below 60 mmHg. These findings support target-aligned alarm configuration as part of a systems-level strategy to improve haemodynamic target fidelity. Further evaluation should include continuous pressure-time exposure, vasopressor dose, alarm burden, and longitudinal adherence to target-aligned alarm limits.
