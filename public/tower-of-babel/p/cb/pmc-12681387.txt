Effect of Statins and PCSK9 Inhibition on Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease
Authors: Eleni Theocharidou, Thomas Gossios
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDyslipidemia is common in patients with metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and, along with other metabolic comorbidities, accounts for an increased cardiovascular risk. Effective treatment of dyslipidemia not only reduces such risk but may have a beneficial effect on MASLD as well. Here we reviewed published data on the efficacy and safety of available hypolipidemic treatments for MASLD. Statins are the mainstay of hypolipidemic therapy for MASLD. In patients with compensated cirrhosis, statins are safe and can improve the risks of decompensation and hepatocellular carcinoma as well as mortality. However, in those with decompensated cirrhosis, statins should not be used unless there are strong indications that outweigh the risk of adverse events. Studies on proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors acting as the second‐line therapy in MASLD remain scarce. Preliminary clinical data support their protective roles in MASLD; however, preclinical data raise concerns regarding safety, in particular with complete PCSK9 inhibition due to hepatocyte lipid accumulation. While more evidence is required to elucidate the role of PCSK9 inhibitors, statins should be used for the treatment of hyperlipidemia in patients with MASLD according to their cardiovascular risk stratification.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common liver disease worldwide, with a prevalence of approximately 25% (range 14%–32%) [1]. It is regarded as the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and is strongly associated with obesity and diabetes mellitus (DM) [2]. The spectrum of MASLD ranges from hepatic steatosis with no or mild inflammation (70%–75% of the patients) to steatohepatitis with fibrosis of variable stages (20%–25%) [2]. Only a small proportion of patients eventually develop cirrhosis, and fewer than 10% develop complications of end‐stage liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) over 10–20 years [3]. Cardiovascular (CV) disease is one of the leading causes of death in patients with MASLD [4], which reflects a high burden of metabolic risk factors in this population. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines recommend a more holistic approach for MASLD, which includes not only the management of liver disease per se but, more importantly, effective management of metabolic comorbidities, particularly central obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia [5, 6].

Dyslipidemia is not uncommon in patients with MASLD. The risk of plasma lipid abnormalities is significantly higher in MASLD patients than in non‐MASLD individuals [7]. Circulating atherogenic lipoprotein subparticles, including low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), contribute to the increased CV risk in these patients [8]. As MASLD progresses to more advanced stages, lipid levels tend to normalize as a result of impaired hepatic cholesterol synthesis. Management of dyslipidemia in MASLD patients follows the same principles as in the general population. Statins, acting as the first‐line pharmacological therapy for hypercholesterolemia, can be used in combination with ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, fibrates, or omega‐3 fatty acids in cases with suboptimal response to statins and/or concomitant hypertriglyceridemia. Pharmacological therapies for dyslipidemia not only reduce CV risk in patients with MASLD, but may exert an effect on other liver disease as well. This review mainly focuses on the roles of statins and PCSK9 inhibitors in MASLD management.

Statin Therapy

Safety of Statins

There has been increasing evidence that statins, also known as hydroxymethylglutaryl (HMG)‐CoA reductase inhibitors, are generally safe for patients with chronic liver disease. However, there are concerns regarding the hepatotoxicity of statins, which frequently leads to treatment discontinuation or non‐initiation among patients with MASLD. Long‐term use of the seven types of currently available statins, namely atorvastatin, fluvastatin, lovastatin, pitavastatin, pravastatin, rosuvastatin, and simvastatin, has been reported to be associated with mild‐to‐moderate liver enzyme elevation. Nonetheless, this is typically transient, mostly asymptomatic, and may even resolve with no requirement for dose adjustment, according to LiverTox data (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548720/). However, clinically apparent acute liver injury, although rare, has been reported in all types of these statins, which is most frequently found in atorvastatin and simvastatin and is less common with pravastatin and pitavastatin, as shown in LiverTox.

A recent meta‐analysis included 21 placebo‐controlled randomized trials with 1900 patients with MASLD receiving hypolipidemic therapy for a median of 26 weeks [9]. Of them, 304, 520, and 61 patients received statins, other lipid‐lowering therapies, or both, respectively. The results showed no increased risk of hepatotoxicity in MASLD patients treated with statins, even in those with impaired liver chemistry at baseline. This meta‐analysis reveals that although treatment with statin may cause hepatotoxicity, it should not be prohibited in MASLD patients who are indicated for and expected to benefit from such therapy.

Effects of Statins on MASLD

Statins reduce hepatic cholesterol synthesis and consequently lower LDL‐C level in the peripheral circulation (Figure 1). Meanwhile, they also improve certain parameters of MASLD via the decrease in hepatic cholesterol content. One of the first studies to assess the effect of statins on MASLD was the Greek Atorvastatin and Coronary Heart Disease Evaluation (GREACE) study, which included 1600 patients with previously diagnosed coronary heart disease who were randomized to receive statins or usual care (lifestyle interventions and necessary drug treatment except statins) [10]. Among them, 437 had mild‐to‐moderate abnormal liver enzyme levels at baseline, of whom 227 received statins. The results showed that the statin group exhibited significant improvement in liver function tests and a lower risk of CV events compared to the control group (10% vs. 30%).

The effect of statins on liver histology has also been assessed in 68 patients with MASLD who underwent repeat liver biopsies [11], which included 17 patients initiated on statin therapy during the follow‐up period and 51 who received no statin. In the statin group, there was significant improvement in hepatic steatosis, while the prevalence of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and fibrosis remained unchanged. For those who did not receive statins, quantitative hepatic steatosis remained unchanged, whereas the prevalence of MASH and fibrosis increased significantly. In addition, in a study of 107 individuals with MASLD who underwent liver biopsy, statins were associated with a lower risk of hepatic steatosis, steatohepatitis, and advanced fibrosis [12]. Moreover, this effect was more evident in patients not carrying the PNPLA3 I148M risk variant.

In summary, although statin use does not meet the stringent criterion for MASLD‐specific therapy—that is, improvement in MASH and/or fibrosis—it improves hepatic steatosis and may exert a protective role against steatohepatitis and fibrosis. Patients with MASLD should be prescribed statins based on their CV risk stratification.

Statins in Patients With Cirrhosis

A large retrospective cohort included 51 023 patients with newly diagnosed cirrhosis over an 8‐year period and no prior exposure to statins, of whom 8794 subsequently initiated statin therapy [13]. Subgroup analysis revealed that in patients with cirrhosis of Child–Pugh classifications A and B, statin exposure was associated with an 8.0%–8.7% annual decrease in all‐cause mortality. Moreover, those with Child–Pugh classification A cirrhosis who exposed to statins also presented with reduced risks of major adverse cardiac events (MACEs), decompensation, and HCC.

The effect of statin use on the risk of decompensated cirrhosis was further assessed in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of 158 patients with cirrhosis and a previous episode of variceal bleeding [14]. All patients underwent endoscopic variceal band ligation and received secondary prophylaxis with nonselective β‐blockers. In addition to standard care, 75 patients received simvastatin and 83 had placebo. The authors found that simvastatin was not associated with an improvement in the variceal rebleeding rate but improved patient survival in those with Child–Pugh classifications A and B cirrhosis; however, this was not observed in those with Child–Pugh classification C cirrhosis.

Statins have been shown to improve endothelial dysfunction and hepatic vascular resistance. Whether they exert such an effect on portal vein hemodynamics has been investigated in two studies, which reported controversial results. Vijayaraghavan et al. assessed the effect of simvastatin on hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) reduction in 220 cirrhotic patients with esophageal varices [15]. All patients were given primary prophylaxis with carvedilol alone for variceal bleeding, and 110 of them received additional simvastatin. The authors demonstrated that 3‐month treatment with simvastatin plus carvedilol was not superior to carvedilol alone on the reduction in HVPG by over 20%. A more recent study including 82 patients with cirrhosis and high‐risk varices who were deemed suboptimal responders to intravenous propranolol (HVPG reduction < 20%) [16] assessed the efficacy of carvedilol plus simvastatin versus carvedilol plus placebo. After 4–6 weeks, patients given simvastatin achieved a more significant decrease in HVPG, with more patients exhibiting HVPG reduction ≥ 20% (37% vs. 15%). The effect of statins on portal hypertension might be more evident in the subgroup of patients with more refractory portal hypertension who only partially respond to β‐blockers.

In summary, in patients with compensated cirrhosis, statin therapy is associated with a lower mortality and a decreased risk of MACE. However, its effects on portal hypertension and decompensated cirrhosis require further verification. Therefore, statin use should be considered in patients with compensated cirrhosis according to their CV risks. In those with decompensated cirrhosis, however, whether statin therapy is associated with a survival benefit needs to be confirmed in future studies and is generally not recommended at present as it could lead to increased risks of adverse events [5]. A possible exception could be that patients with decompensated cirrhosis and a high CV risk may be candidates for liver transplantation, in whom statins should be used under careful monitoring.

PCSK9 Inhibitors

PCSK9 plays an important role in LDL homeostasis by binding to the LDL receptor (LDL‐R) on the hepatocyte membrane and inducing its degradation, which results in a reduced LDL uptake by the hepatocytes and increased LDL in the peripheral circulation [17]. PCSK9 can also bind to the very‐low‐density lipoprotein receptor (VLDL‐R) and CD36, which also induces their degradation. CD36 is a scavenger receptor for fatty acids and is implicated in the pathogenesis of MASLD. The effect of PCSK9 on different receptors on the hepatocyte membrane is mediated by its different domains [17], which may explain the variable effects of complete PCSK9 inhibition, for example, in knockout rodent models or with small interfering RNA (siRNA), when compared to partial inhibition such as with neutralizing antibodies.

PCSK9 inhibition leads to reduced LDL‐R degradation and increased LDL‐R expression on the hepatocyte membrane, thus increasing cholesterol uptake by the hepatocytes and decreased circulating LDL (Figure 1). PCSK9 inhibitors are therefore considered as the second‐line therapy for dyslipidemia in patients who achieve partial response or are intolerant to statins. Currently available PCSK9 inhibitors mainly include monoclonal antibodies (such as alirocumab and evolocumab) and siRNAs (inclisiran). Effects of these inhibitors need to be further confirmed, as the existing pre‐clinical and clinical data are controversial.

Genetic studies have investigated the role of PCSK9 in the pathogenesis of MASLD. Different gene variants have been reported to be associated with a higher or lower risk of hepatic steatosis. As reported previously, the PCSK9 rs505151 variant may be associated with moderate and severe steatosis in liver transplant recipients [18], whereas the PCSK9 rs11591147 loss‐of‐function (LOF) variant may protect against hepatic steatosis [19].

Circulating level of PCSK9 correlates with the severity of liver damage in MASLD. A previous study including 201 patients with histologically confirmed MASLD showed that higher circulating PCSK9 levels were associated with more severe hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis [20]. These findings are consistent with another study including 698 patients in whom the presence of steatosis was assessed via ultrasonography, the hepatic steatosis index, and the fatty liver index, which also showed a smilar association between corculating PCSK9 circulating levels and hepatic steatosis [21]. There are two potential explanations for this observation. PCSK9 is involved in the pathogenesis of MASLD either directly through its involvement in de novo hepatic lipogenesis, which is triggered by reduced hepatic cholesterol uptake, or indirectly via a compensatory mechanism by which accumulating hepatic fat may cause an increased PCSK9 production in an effort to limit cholesterol uptake by the already overloaded hepatocytes. The potential direct association is supported by the findings of a multicenter European study of 1874 patients who underwent liver biopsy for suspected MASLD [19]. Among these, patients with a PCSK9 rs11591147 LOF variant had a lower risk of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, irrespective of the established metabolic risk factors as well as the PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 gene variants. The authors also showed that PCSK9 overexpression in male mice enhanced the development of MASLD and liver fibrosis upon a dietary challenge [19].

A hypothesis has been proposed for the potential protective effect of PCSK9 inhibition in MASLD. In MASLD, in particular MASH, liver parenchyma is infiltrated by neutrophils. Neutrophils excrete miRNA‐223‐enriched extracellular vesicles, which are taken up by the hepatocytes and inhibit inflammatory and fibrogenic gene expression. Their uptake is mediated by LDL‐R on the hepatocyte membrane. PCSK9 inhibition leads to increased LDL‐R and miRNA‐223‐enriched extracellular vesicle uptake by the hepatocytes, where they exert their anti‐inflammatory and antifibrotic effects [22].

Clinical Data of PCSK9 Inhibitors

Clinical data on the role of pharmacological PCSK9 inhibition are scarce. In a previous study, 40 patients with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) received either evolocumab 140 mg or alirocumab 150 mg every 14 days for 1 year [23]. Among them, 13 patients had MASLD confirmed via ultrasound scan (USS), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or transient elastography. At the end of the treatment, a significant improvement in features of MASLD, mainly steatosis, was observed.

Another small‐scale study included 26 patients with FH and MASLD who had elevated LDL‐C despite statin and ezetimibe therapy [24]. The patients were given evolocumab 140 mg or alirocumab 75 or 150 mg every 14 days for 6 months. The presence of MASLD was assessed by using USS, the triglyceride‐glucose index (TyG), and the hepatic steatosis index. Both TyG and the hepatic steatosis index improved in patients who achieved a low triglycerides/high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio. A recent study included 29 patients treated with PCSK9 inhibitors for a mean duration of 24 months [25]. MASLD was assessed by radiological modalities. At the end of the study period, 73% of patients achieved radiological resolution of hepatic steatosis and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) improvement.

In summary, preliminary clinical data support a protective role of PCSK9 inhibitors in MASLD, although there are important limitations to be taken into account, including the small number of patients included in most of these studies, variability of methods used to assess MASLD, and insufficient histological data, as liver biopsy was not performed in any of the abovementioned studies.

Safety of PCSK9 Inhibitors in MASLD

Concerns have been raised regarding a potential detrimental role of increased hepatic cholesterol uptake resulting from PCSK9 inhibition. Notably, a meta‐analysis of 25 RCTs that included 12 200 patients showed no increased risk of liver injury with alirocumab and evolocumab [26]. However, preclinical data support the hypothesis that accumulating lipids within the hepatocytes might have a deleterious effect on the liver. A study by Lebeau et al. revealed that Pcsk9‐knockout mice that were fed a high‐fat diet developed more severe hepatic steatosis, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, inflammation, and fibrosis compared to the control mice [27]. Pcsk9‐knockout mice also exhibited elevated expressions of hepatic PCSK9‐regulated receptors, that is, LDL‐R, VLDL‐R, and CD36. CD36 overexpression may precipitate triglyceride accumulation, lipid‐induced ER stress, and progression to MASH. Similarly, another study showed that Pcsk9‐knockout mice developed increased cholesterol crystal–containing lipid droplets and hepatocytes, more severe hepatic inflammation, and fibrosis when they were fed a high‐fat diet [28].

Although clinical data do not raise concerns regarding the safety of PCSK9 inhibitors in MASLD, the controversial results of preclinical studies may be attributed to the differential effects of complete and partial PCSK9 inhibition (Figure 2). In Pcsk9‐knockout mice, complete PCSK9 inhibition affects not only LDL‐R but also other PCSK9‐dependent receptors such as VLDL‐R and CD36. Whether inclisiran, an siRNA that mediates its hypolipidemic effect via complete PCSK9 inhibition, has the same effect remains unclear [29]. On the other hand, partial PCSK9 inhibition, such as via inhibitory antibodies, only affects LDL‐R. Moreover, PCSK9 inhibitors are known to increase circulating PCSK9 levels [30], which may further inhibit CD36 expression and lead to MASLD improvement.

In summary, preliminary data support the role of PCSK9 in the pathogenesis of MASLD. Circulating PCSK9 level correlates with the severity of hepatic steatosis. With regard to the effect of PCSK9 inhibition on MASLD, preclinical results remain controversial. Preliminary clinical studies have reported a protective effect of PCSK9 inhibitors against MASLD; however, their findings are limited by small sample size and a lack of histological evidence. Further studies should focus on the effects of both inhibitory antibodies and siRNAs in MASLD.

Conclusions

Dyslipidemia is a key component of MetS and one of the major CV risk factors. It is commonly observed in MASLD and should be treated aggressively. Statins are the first‐line pharmacological therapy for dyslipidemia and should not be withheld because of safety concerns, with the exception of patients with decompensated cirrhosis. Apart from reducing CV risk, they also improve hepatic steatosis and mortality, and may prevent disease progression. Their effects on portal pressure need to be further investigated. In addition, PCSK9 inhibitors are potential second‐line lipid‐lowering agents that may also have a role in MASLD. It is hoped that future research will elucidate their safety and effects on MASLD.
