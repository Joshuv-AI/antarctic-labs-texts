Dataset on phytochemical profiling of Moringa oleifera leaves ethanolic extract using spectrophotometry UV-Vis, TLC, FTIR, and GCMS: Neuroregenerative potential, IC₅₀ determination, and proliferation assay evaluation
Authors: Wilda Bunga Tina Sanjaya, Rini Widayanti, Kazutoshi Nishijima, Hevi Wihadmadyatami, Ulayatul Kustiati, Dinda Aliffia, Anisa Putri Aviana, Dwi Liliek Kusindarta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Moringa oleifera, also referred to as the drumstick tree, is a rapidly growing, drought-tolerant species originally found in South Asia. It is highly regarded for its leaves, which are rich in nutrients, and for its diverse therapeutic applications. Moringa oleifera possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, antidiabetic, and antimicrobial qualities that render it a viable choice for therapeutic and functional food applications. This dataset article presents a comprehensive screening of phytochemical constituents and secondary metabolites in the ethanolic extract of Moringa oleifera. The analysis was conducted using a combination of spectrophotometry, Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS). The ethanolic extract of Moringa oleifera was further evaluated for its cytotoxic and neuroregenerative potential, with IC₅₀ values determined and cell proliferation assessed through an MTT assay using PC-12 neuronal cells. Both qualitative and quantitative methods were employed, revealing the presence of various phytochemicals, including flavonoids, phenols, tannins, saponins, alkaloids, steroids, terpenoids, and 46 active phytoconstituents. Strong appearance displays in many functional groups including Alcohol and hydroxy compound, Ether-oxy compound, and Common inorganic ions. The ethanolic extract of Moringa oleifera exhibited a maximal proliferative effect on PC-12 neuronal cells at a concentration of 62.5 µg/mL, while its half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated to be 984.9 µg/mL, indicating a favorable therapeutic window for neuroregenerative applications

Background

Moringa oleifera, frequently called the horseradish tree or drumstick tree, this versatile shrub is indigenous to the Indian subcontinent that thrives in arid conditions and grows quickly. This deciduous species is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical zones and typically rises to a height of five to twelve meters. It has a soft, corky, and greyish bark with a whitish wood that is lightweight and porous. The leaves are tripinnately compound, 20–70 cm long, with small oval-shaped leaflets arranged in pairs and a terminal leaflet at the end. The tree produces fragrant, bisexual, zygomorphic flowers, typically white to creamy-white in color, with yellow stamens, blooming in loose, drooping clusters. The fruit is a long, slender, three-sided capsule (commonly called a ``drumstick''), which can grow up to 45 cm in length and contains dark brown, trigonous seeds with papery wings to aid dispersal [2]. It is highly valued for its exceptional nutritional profile and diverse pharmacological properties. Traditionally used as a food and in folk medicine, Moringa oleifera reveals broad pharmacological potential, such as antioxidant, antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and neuroprotective properties [3,4]. Owing to its medicinal potential, comprehensive analysis of its phytochemical and secondary metabolite content is crucial.

Data Description

In this study, leaves of Moringa oleifera were extracted using ethanol through the maceration technique. The resulting extract was subjected to phytochemical screening via UV–Vis spectrophotometry and Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC), with quantitative analysis performed for spectrophotometric data and qualitative assessment for TLC results. Further identification of functional groups and secondary metabolites was performed with the utilization of Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)..(Fig. 1; Fig. 2; Fig. 3; Table 1; Table 2; Table 3). The extract’s biological activity was also evaluated through IC₅₀ determination and an MTT-based proliferation assay on PC-12 neuronal cells, revealing promising neuroregenerative potential (Fig. 4; Fig. 5). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Preparation of moringa oleifera ethanolic extract

The plant samples used in the study were collected in August 2024 from the plantation of CV Merapi Farma Herbal, Sleman, Yogyakarta, Indonesia. During this period, the regional climate was characterized by average daytime temperatures of approximately 29–30 °C and nighttime temperatures around 21–22 °C, with relative humidity ranging from 70 % to 82 %. August represents the drier season in Yogyakarta, with mean precipitation of about 30–35 mm over 4–5 rainy days. The soil in the plantation of CV Merapi Farma Herbal is believed to be derived from volcanic materials, with a predominantly sandy to loamy sand texture, low to moderate organic carbon content, and low cation exchange capacity (CEC). Soil pH under H₂O is inferred to be slightly acidic to neutral (6–7), and major macro‐nutrients are present but may be less available due to the soil’s coarse texture and low CEC. The samples were dried in the shade and stored under appropriate conditions before being prepared for analysis.

The dried leaves simplicia of Moringa oleifera was sourced from CV Merapi Farma Herbal, Yogyakarta, Indonesia. For extraction, a maceration technique was employed using 96 % ethanol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) as the solvent. A portion of 300 g from a total of 1 kg of dried material was immersed in 4 liters of ethanol. After the maceration process, under lower pressure, in order to concentrate the resulting filtrate a rotary vacuum evaporator (Heidolph, Schwabach, Germany) was used, yielding a semisolid extract in paste form.

Ethanolic extract of moringa oleifera identification

The identification of the ethanolic extract of Moringa oleifera was performed through a series of analytical techniques, including Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV 1800–1700, Tokyo, Japan), Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC) using a CAMAG TLC Scanner 4 (Muttenz, Switzerland), Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA), and Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Shimadzu 8201, Tokyo, Japan).

Spectrophotometry UV–Vis

Flavonoid

A total of 50 mg of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was weighed and placed into a clean test tube. To initiate the colorimetric assay, a value of 0.3 mL of sodium nitrite (NaNO₂) at 5 % solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was included. After five minutes, Ten percent aluminum chloride (AlCl₃) over 0.6 mL solution was introduced. The mixture was left to react for another 5 min, after which two milliliters of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) concentrated at 1 M (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was included. The ultimate amount of 10 mL was achieved with deionized water. The concoction was well combined, transferred into a cuvette, and absorbance was recorded at 510 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer [5].

Fenol

A total of 50 mg of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was precisely measured. A quantity of zero point five milliliters of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) along with a volume of 7.5 mL of purified water was included to the test tube containing the sample. To facilitate the initial reaction, the solution was kept at ambient temperature for ten minutes. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of a twenty percent sodium carbonate solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was included, and the final volume was completed to ten milliliters with water from distillation. The prepared mixture was blended altogether thoroughly, after that, the absorption will being recorded at 760 nm via a UV–Visible spectrophotometer [5].

Tanin

A total of 50 mg of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was immersed in 10 mL of diethyl ether (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and left to stand for 20 h. After evaporation of the residual diethyl ether, ten milliliters of deionized water were included to the remaining sample. Subsequently, 10 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was then incorporated, and the sample was vigorously shaken before being incubated for five minutes. Following this, two milliliters of sodium carbonate solution (20 %) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was subsequently introduced. The final solution was maintained at ambient temperature and thirty minutes were spent incubating before recording the absorbance at 760 nm through a UV–Visible spectrophotometer [5].

Alkaloid

A total of 50 mg of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was mixed with 2 N hydrochloric acid (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The resulting mixture was rinsed a total 3 times with ten milliliters portions of chloroform. To neutralize the acidic medium, 0.1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was added. Upon achieving neutral pH, five milliliters of phosphate buffer and five milliliters of Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) reagent (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) were included to the mixture, followed by five milliliters of chloroform. The solution was then agitated at 500 rpm for 15 min using a magnetic stirrer. The chloroform layer was separated and dried under nitrogen flow. The remaining content was redissolved in chloroform to a final total of five milliliters. An ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer was used to measure the absorbance of the resultant solution at 470 nm [5].

Saponin

A total of 50 mg of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was combined with 2 mL of 25 % sulfuric acid (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) and subjected to autoclaving at 110 °C for 120 min. Ether was subsequently employed to extract the mixture, and the resulting filtrate was evaporated to dryness. Upon addition of 1 mL of distilled water, the preparation was vortexed for 5 min. This was followed by homogenization and incorporation of 50 mL of 4-anisaldehyde (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA), after which the mixture was allowed to rest for 10 min. Then, 2 mL of 50 % sulfuric acid (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was introduced, and the solution was heated in a water bath at 60 °C for 10 min. Finally, the volume was adjusted to 10 mL with distilled water using a volumetric flask, diluted fivefold, and its absorbance was determined at 435 nm with a UV–visible spectrophotometer [5].

Thin layer chromatography

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was utilized to analyze the presence of steroids and terpenoids in the Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract. A 50 mg portion was dissolved in 1 mL of ethanol and put through sonication for one hour. The solution was then vortexed and centrifuged, followed by maceration for 24 h. A 100 µL aliquot of the prepared extract was spotted onto a silica gel 60 F254 TLC plate alongside β-sitosterol standard solution (60 % purity) (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The plate was developed in a pre-saturated chamber containing a mobile phase consisting of a mixture of ethyl acetate and toluene at a volume ratio of 93:7 (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). After development, the plate was sprayed with Liebermann–Burchard reagent (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) and heated at 110 °C for two minutes. Absorbance was determined at 340 nanometres in wavelength [5].

Fourier transform infrared spectroschopy (FTIR)

For FTIR spectroscopy, a powdered sample of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was evenly blended with potassium bromide (KBr) at a 1:100 ratio by weight. The blend was subsequently pressed into pellets with uniform thickness. Using a Shimadzu 8201 spectrometer (Tokyo, Japan), FTIR spectra were acquired, covering a wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm⁻¹ [5].

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GCMS)

A total of 0.5 g of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract was diluted in one milliliter of ethanol and then put through centrifuge at 9500 rpm approximately three minutes at ambient temperature. Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was carried out on a one milliliter quantity of the resultant supernatant using a Thermo Fisher Scientific (Massachusetts, USA) apparatus. The splitless mode was used and the injector was kept at 230 °C. With the transfer line set at 250 °C, the ion source temperature at 200 °C, and a carrier gas purge rate of 3mL/min, the analysis made use of a split injection flow rate of fifty mL/min. The separation was carried out on an Agilent HP-5MS UI capillary column, with helium gas as the mobile phase and a maximum column oven temperature of 350 °C. The flame ionization detector (FID) was used for detection, and subsequent mass spectra will be compared to several list in the MAINLIB spectral library to characterize the chemicals [5].

IC50 and proliferation assay

To determine the IC₅₀ value of Moringa oleifera ethanolic extract, PC12 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells for each well in a 96-well plate. Following a one-hour pre-treatment with 50 µM trimethyltin (TMT) (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) to induce neurotoxicity, cells were exposed to varying concentrations of M. oleifera extract (4000, 2000, 1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.25, 15.625, and 7.8125 µg/mL) as well as 1 µM donepezil hydrochloride (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) as a positive control. Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) was being used to gently wash the wells after the culture media was removed after an incubation period of twenty-four hours. Cell viability was assessed by adding 0.5 mg/mL of MTT reagent (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) to every single well, and then being incubated at a period of four hours. Subsequently, the MTT solution was discarded, and 100 µL of DMSO was included to every single well, dissolving the resulting formazan purple crystals. The absorbance was read at 595 nm using a microplate reader.

For the proliferation assay on the in vitro Alzheimer’s disease (AD) model induced by TMT, a similar protocol was followed. PC12 cells were seeded at the same density and divided into seven experimental groups: (1) untreated negative control, (2) TMT-treated positive AD model control (50 µM), (3) TMT-treated group receiving donepezil (1 µM), and (4–7) TMT-treated groups receiving M. oleifera extracts at graded concentrations of 7.81, 15.63, 31.25, and 62.5 µg/mL, respectively. These concentrations were selected based on the determined IC₅₀ value. Cell proliferation was calculated using the standard formula for proliferation rate assessment:Proliferationrate(%)=(TreatedCellAbsorbance−ControlMediaAbsorbance)(ControlCellsAbsorbance−ControlMediaAbsorbance)X100%[5]

Limitations

Variability in the plant's growing conditions, including soil composition, climate, and geographic location, can influence the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Additionally, seasonal differences at the time of collection may alter the concentration of certain phytochemicals. Furthermore, differences in the extraction techniques and analytical protocols used for identification and quantification may result in slight discrepancies when compared to other studies. These factors should be considered when interpreting and comparing the findings.
