Design of a card-based operational framework for the implementation of inclusive representation in clinical trials
Authors: Marisa Minetti, Ylenia Paleari, Chiara Mazzoli, Giulia Pierini, Marcos Puebla, Valeria Cavatorta, Chiara Fontani, Noemi Muzzopappa, Eduardo Perez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite growing recognition that trial populations should reflect the broad range of people who will receive therapies, implementation of representative enrollment remains inconsistent. Existing frameworks often lack role-specific guidance, phase-specific actions, and actionable tools to support sponsors—referring specifically to pharmaceutical companies—in embedding inclusive representation (IR) across the trial life cycle. To address these gaps, we developed a card-based operational framework (CBOF) that assigns clear responsibilities, aligns with regulatory expectations, and supports implementation at both the trial and organizational levels.MethodsWe conducted a four-phase methodological study to design the CBOF. First, a structured scoping review identified existing frameworks tackling diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion, as well as guidance documents. Second, we performed a comparative matrix-based analysis to assess operational completeness, surface gaps across 15 frameworks, and to develop the design brief of the developed framework. Third, we held a 120-min expert advisory board consultation with representatives from regulatory, academic, patient advocacy, and pharmaceutical domains to validate the design brief. Fourth, we developed the CBOF using a 5W1H (What, Why, Who, When, Where, How) structure to inform framework content and format.ResultsThe resulting CBOF consists of a modular, card-based tool set organized across nine inclusive representation “IR” categories: seven focused on trial-level design and two on organizational readiness. It includes editable Core Cards to prompt IR planning, Support Cards to provide guidance and role clarification, a library of 349 implementation tactics, and a KPI catalogue to track progress. The framework specifies who is responsible, when and where actions should occur across the product life cycle (discovery through life cycle management) and the clinical trial stages (planning to read out), and how each activity can be executed. It assigns IR-related responsibilities to 15 functional teams and includes step-by-step workflows and decision-question pathways. Expert feedback affirmed the framework’s usability, relevance, and completeness, while also informing improvements such as adaptability, integration of digital tools, and expanded guidance on performance metrics.ConclusionsThis framework has been designed to support companies and sponsors, specifically referring to pharmaceutical companies interested in strengthening the representation of real-world patients in clinical trials. The CBOF translates diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion principles into structured, actionable guidance for clinical trial sponsors. It addresses longstanding operational gaps by combining decision-support tools, role-specific delegation, and measurable KPIs across development phases and trial stages. While further validation in real-world settings is needed, the framework represents a practical advancement in operationalizing inclusive trial design and supporting sponsors in meeting regulatory and ethical expectations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-026-09566-x.

Introduction

Clinical trials play a critical role in advancing medical knowledge and improving patient care. They are designed to assess the clinical efficacy and safety impact of medical, surgical, and behavioral interventions, and serve as the primary evidence base for clinical decision-making, regulatory approval, and health policy development [1–3]. For findings to meaningfully inform real-world practice, trial populations must reflect the demographic and social representation of those who will ultimately receive the interventions.

The generalizability of trial outcomes is therefore contingent on adequate representation across relevant demographic and social groups [4]. This need for representativeness has brought greater attention to ensuring that trial populations are reflective of the broader communities affected by the health conditions under study.

Relevance of inclusive representation in clinical trials

Inclusive representation has gained increasing prominence in clinical research [4], and in this context, it refers to the intentional design and execution of practices that seek a broad and fair participation of eligible individuals from underrepresented communities and backgrounds [5]. Inclusive representation encompasses the characteristics and lived experiences that distinguish individuals and groups; health equity involves ensuring fair access to participation opportunities; and inclusion relates to creating environments in which every participant feels respected, supported, and valued [6, 7]. Together, they seek to reduce barriers and expand access to clinical trial participation for all populations [8]. Viewed through the lens of Clinical Research As A Care Option (CRAACO), inclusive representation (IR) also widens therapeutic choices for patients who have limited or ineffective standard treatments [9].

Inclusive representation spans many dimensions that map to intrinsic factors, such as age, biological sex, metabolism, or genetics, and extrinsic factors, such as socioeconomic status or environmental exposures [10]. These dimensions shape disease risk, presentation, and treatment response. For instance, men appear more likely to respond to tricyclic antidepressants, whereas women show greater responsiveness to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors in depression treatment [4]. Individuals of African ancestry experience substantially higher cancer mortality, with African American men and women being 111% and 39% more likely, respectively, to die from prostate and breast cancer than White counterparts [11]. Therefore, enhancing diverse representation across varied population groups not only upholds ethical principles and fairness but also allows accurate evaluation of safety and efficacy across populations, thereby strengthening scientific validity and external generalizability by ensuring findings reflect the real-world patients who will use the therapy if approved [12]. Failure to do so may risk exacerbating existing health disparities and limit the translational value of clinical research [13].

Evidence on lack of inclusive representation implementation in clinical trials

Despite longstanding recognition of disparities in clinical research, individuals from minority and underrepresented populations, including those defined by race, ethnicity, gender, age, disability status, geography, and socioeconomic background, remain insufficiently represented in clinical trials [14]. A multi-country review of trials conducted between 1997 and 2014 found that 86% of participants were White, raising concerns about the generalizability of findings. In a separate review of 83 Alzheimer’s disease trials over a 10-year period, only 2% of participants were Black, despite a higher disease burden in this group [14]. A recent 2024 systematic review of clinical trials in Food Allergen Immunotherapy titled “Racial and Ethnic Representation in Food Allergen Immunotherapy Trial Participants: A Systematic Review” showed that 72% of the participants were White, while only 8% were Black and 3% Hispanic/Latino, underscoring stark minority under-representation despite a higher food allergy burden among Black and Hispanic/Latino children [15]. Such disparities undermine the external validity of research and may contribute to inequities in efficacy, safety, and pharmacological response across populations [6].

Challenges in operationalization of inclusive representation in clinical trials

Despite increasing recognition of the importance of IR in clinical research, the adequate representation of patients in clinical trials remains a significant challenge.

Diverse enrollment and inclusion in clinical trials are hindered by barriers that vary across under-represented groups but consistently reflect broader issues in trial design and delivery. Key challenges include language and literacy gaps, mistrust and cultural concerns, limited understanding of research, restrictive eligibility criteria, and logistical constraints. Addressing these requires culturally responsive communication, participant and community engagement, flexible inclusion standards, and practical support to ensure inclusive participation [16].

In addition, organizational capacity within sponsoring organizations—referring specifically to pharmaceutical companies—is a critical determinant of IR in clinical trials. Evidence and guidance show that inclusive recruitment stalls when no personnel are explicitly tasked with leading inclusion efforts, cross-functional coordination is weak, accountability and metrics are absent, and budgets for outreach and community engagement are inadequate. Forming dedicated personnel at all levels of the organization, tasked with designing and implementing clinical trial strategies that promote inclusive representation by embedding objectives across departments, and ensuring these roles are properly resourced with clear leadership oversight, constitutes a critical foundation for achieving inclusive and representative enrollment [17].

Regulatory requirements and pharmaceutical companies’ initiatives

Recognizing these persistent barriers, a growing number of efforts both regulatory and non-regulatory have emerged to promote more inclusive representation in clinical research. Regulatory authorities have played a central role in this evolution; for example, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) has emphasized the need for representative populations in clinical trials through the EMA’s ACT EU (Accelerating Clinical Trials in the EU) initiative, specifically promoting diverse participant inclusion to produce robust clinical data and improve health outcomes [18].

In the UK, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) and the Health Research Authority (HRA) have jointly introduced an Inclusion and Diversity Plan framework. While not mandatory, sponsors and researchers are strongly encouraged to submit a plan alongside clinical trial applications. The plan guides researchers to include under-represented groups, set inclusion goals, and plan engagement strategies so trials reflect target populations and reveal any differences in outcomes across demographics [19]. Pharmaceutical companies are also seizing this moment to advance inclusive representation in their own research practices. For example, Pfizer, BioMarin, Bristol Myers Squibb, and Genentech, among others, have launched initiatives aimed at broadening representation in clinical trials. These efforts range from major investments, such as Bristol Myers Squibb’s $100 million commitment to train new clinical investigators from a wide spectrum of clinical and ethnic backgrounds to Pfizer’s $1.75 million award to support the Communities forward solutions with one of the main aims to increase diverse representation in clinical trials through addressing racial biases embedded into algorithms and promoting data disaggregation to gain greater insight into the health needs of underrepresented populations [20, 21]. These initiatives demonstrate a growing industry-wide recognition of the need to embed inclusive representation at the core of clinical research.

Beyond regulatory measures and pharmaceutical companies’ initiatives, broader collaborative efforts are also underway. One example is the Research in Europe and Diversity Inclusion (READI) project, a 6-year public–private partnership launched under the Horizon Europe Innovative Health Initiative (IHI). It brings together a wide range of organizations and stakeholders to build a cohesive, patient-centered ecosystem for clinical research that enhances inclusion. READI is actively developing practical tools, training programs, and frameworks to design and implement inclusive representation in clinical trials [22]. This type of initiative provides a natural link to the growing body of structured frameworks designed to translate inclusion principles into trial practice as it complements and connects with such principles. Further collaborative efforts are described in the following section.

Frameworks for the implementation of inclusive representation in clinical trials

In parallel with regulatory developments, several research organizations and multi-stakeholder collaborations have released frameworks to translate diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion principles into trial practice. Notable examples include the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR)-INCLUDE Ethnicity Framework [23], the Achieving Diversity, Inclusion, and Equity In Clinical Research Guidance and Toolkit from the Multi-Regional Clinical Trials (MRCT) [24, 25], the REP-EQUITY Toolkit [26], TransCelerate’s Collaboration Hub Sponsor Toolkit: Portfolio and Program-Level Considerations for Diversity, Equity and Inclusion of Participants in Clinical Trials (DEICT) [27], and initiatives by the Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI) [28]. These frameworks provide a good overview of steps to implement IR, often providing a wide range of aspects and tactics that impact IR in trials across the different trial stages (i.e., planning, start-up, maintenance, read out) or development cycle phases (i.e., discovery and preclinical research, early development, late development, life cycle management) and sometimes use decision engines to ease its use. However, taken individually, each present gaps. For example, MRCT is content rich and provides progress metrics, but its breadth and limited workflow intuitiveness can make day-to-day use more challenging, and guidance on team-level responsibilities is limited. INCLUDE offers a clear decision engine and case examples but provides less detail on role assignment or measurement. TransCelerate supplies an accessible decision pathway and high-level-role descriptions yet gives scant detail on trial stage-specific actions, metrics, and practical examples of use.

A common area for further development across many frameworks is the clear assignment of precise roles and responsibilities within sponsor teams, as evidence shows that the internal organization, accountability, and resource allocation are pivotal to successful implementation [17]. While some frameworks do reference roles (for example, TransCelerate) or emphasize the importance of organizational structure (for example, CTTI), additional detail on aspects such as who carries out specific actions, at what phase in the product life cycle, and with what deliverables could further enhance their applicability when in the conduction of a clinical trial. Likewise, practical tools such as checklists and execution guides drive accountable implementation and may help translate these principles into consistent, accountable practices. Consequently, there remains a need for a single, practical framework that (1) spans core IR content areas, (2) uses an intuitive decision engine, (3) is aligned to development phases and trial stages, (4) embeds measurable indicators of progress, and (5) assigns clear responsibilities across sponsor functions.

In response to these gaps, we developed a card-based operational framework (CBOF) for the implementation of IR in clinical trials that is practical and process-oriented. It has been designed to support sponsors interested in implementing IR in clinical trials. The framework employs an actionable, card-based format structured around a question-flow decision system, guiding trial teams through key considerations across the clinical trial stages. It is designed to promote accountability, clarify decision-making, and embed inclusivity principles in a systematic and actionable manner. In this study, we describe the development of the framework and examine its potential to address persistent barriers to inclusion in clinical research. Although the framework spans the entire development cycle, including discovery and preclinical research, we refer to “clinical trials” throughout for simplicity and because they represent the primary focus of the framework.

Methods

We conducted a multi-phase, qualitative study to develop a practical, team-oriented inclusive representation framework tailored to sponsor organizations, particularly pharmaceutical companies conducting clinical trials. Our aim was to improve upon existing high-level IR guidance by creating an operational tool that offers clear, actionable direction across the clinical trial life cycle. The development approach was grounded in principles from implementation science and methodological framework design, emphasizing an iterative, evidence-informed, and stakeholder-aligned process.

The author team includes researchers working at Chiesi Farmaceutici (pharmaceutical company; Parma, Italy) and Alira Health (healthcare consulting firm; Bologna, Italy and Barcelona, Spain), with expertise spanning clinical development, implementation science, regulatory affairs, and patient engagement. We acknowledge that these institutional positions may predispose emphasis toward sponsor-facing, operational solutions for inclusive representation. To reduce the risk of bias and broaden perspectives, we used purposive external validation via a multidisciplinary expert advisory board (EAB) including the following profiles: (1) patient engagement advocate, (2) senior regulatory official, (3) academic IR scholar, (4) inclusive trial methodologist, and (5) industry ethics leader. This step increased transparency and helped balance author perspectives in framework refinement.

We followed a four-phase methodology, in which first we conducted a scoping review of existing guidance tackling inclusive representation; second, we executed a comparative framework analysis and gap identification; third, we conducted the EAB consultation and concept validation; fourth, we designed the framework and related tools.

This structure was adapted from methodological recommendations by McMeekin et al. [29], who proposed a multi-step framework development approach incorporating literature synthesis, conceptual development, and external validation. Although our process followed four phases, rather than McMeekin’s three, the intent was consistent: to produce a tool that is contextually grounded, practically applicable, and responsive to the real-world needs of clinical trial teams. The following sub-sections describe each phase in detail, outlining how the framework was constructed and refined. Each phase built upon the preceding one to ensure that the final tool was not only conceptually robust but also operationally relevant for clinical trial teams.

Phase 1: scoping review

We conducted a structured scoping review to identify existing and relevant frameworks, toolkits, regulatory guidance, and both peer-reviewed and grey literature to capture the breadth of available resources, identify gaps, and define the requirements to inform the design, structure, and content of a practical, operational framework in subsequent phases.

Given that the concepts are emerging, and the topic is complex and multidisciplinary, we selected a scoping review rather than a systematic review. The review followed methodological guidance outlined by Peters et al. [30] and Hill et al. [31], which recommends scoping as an appropriate approach when the aim is to explore the extent, range, and nature of available evidence. Searches were conducted in two electronic databases, PubMed® and Embase©, with additional grey literature sourced from regulatory agencies, including FDA, EMA, consortia, for example, TransCelerate, MRCT, and published frameworks/toolkits focused on diverse representation and inclusion, such as NIHR INCLUDE [23], REP-EQUITY [26]. The search strategy targeted English-language documents published between 2020 and 2025.

“The start date of 2020 was selected to focus the review on the most recent wave of implementation-oriented frameworks and guidance that reflect the current regulatory and operational environment for sponsors. Given the multidisciplinary scope of the topic and the rapid evolution of relevant tools, restricting the time window also ensured the search remained feasible while prioritizing actionable resources.” Reference lists from materials included were also reviewed to identify supplementary sources. The full list of frameworks and guidelines reviewed for this scoping review can be found in Additional file 1.

Documents were included if they provided frameworks, toolkits, or formal guidance specifically addressing diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion in the context of clinical trial design, conduct, or reporting. At the time of writing this article, publications focused solely on diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion in clinical trials were considered. Key content was reviewed and thematically organized. From this process, we identified a consolidated set of IR-enabling factors, which served as a foundational output of Phase 1 and was used to construct the comparison matrix that guided the comparative assessment in Section “Phase 2: comparative framework analysis and gap identification.”

Phase 2: comparative framework analysis and gap identification

Leveraging the consolidated set of IR-enabling factors identified during the scoping review in Section “Phase 1: scoping review,” we assessed the operational completeness of existing frameworks and guidelines in clinical research. This assessment was a structured qualitative approach commonly applied in implementation research to surface thematic and structural gaps across policy or tool-based documents [32].

Using a matrix constructed from the IR-enabling factors, we evaluated a range of frameworks, toolkits, and regulatory guidance materials. The matrix developed and populated using Microsoft® Excel® for Microsoft 365 MSO (Version 2502) was categorized in five comparative dimensions: scope, content components, implementation feasibility, alignment with regulatory or policy directives, and validation. Each document was reviewed to determine the presence, depth, and operational clarity of IR-related guidance across these dimensions. This analysis revealed key areas of improvement and gaps between high-level IR commitments and practical, trial-level actionability, described in Section “Comparative analysis outputs.”

Insights from this structured analysis were consolidated into a design brief that outlined core functional requirements for a novel operationalized IR implementation framework. This brief served as the blueprint for developing the framework described in Section “Design brief development.”

Phase 3: expert advisory board consultation

Following the development of the design brief described in Section “Design brief development,” we sought to validate the concept of the proposed IR framework through structured consultation with an expert advisory board (EAB).

We convened a multidisciplinary EAB selected by purposive sampling. Meaningful evaluation required balanced representation from key stakeholder domains and therefore sought input from patient advocacy, regulatory oversight, academic IR scholarship, inclusive trial methodology, and pharmaceutical implementation.

The board was composed of five members: one patient engagement advocate, one senior regulatory official, one academic with extensive experience in representation and inclusion in clinical trials, one inclusive trial methodologist, and one industry leader with research ethics experience, collectively spanning the UK, the USA, and Europe.

The consultation was conducted as a virtual, 120-min moderated session using a structured discussion guide in January 2025 (Additional file 2). Members of the EAB played a consultative role, offering insight into three key blocks: (1) Sources of information and regulatory bodies’ perspective; (2) Design brief and (3) Emerging Trends. Special emphasis was placed on collecting their insight on the conceptual model outlined in the design brief, mainly the selection of trial-related areas where IR can be applied (enrollment goal setting and knowledge base, robust data practices, participant and community engagement, study design, access and logistics, site and investigator selection, recruitment and retention strategies), the use of a card-based format, the use of guided categorical questions, the emphasis on team-specific execution across trial phases, and the use of KPIs. Additional aspects of the design brief were reviewed, such as the consideration of nuances throughout the drug development life cycle, and the consideration of subpopulation-specific barriers and solutions at each trial stage (planning, start-up, maintenance, readout).

Participants were encouraged to think aloud and share experiences relevant to IR initiatives in trial execution, as well as to reflect on the relevance and barriers to adoption in real-world sponsor settings.

We collected qualitative feedback through session notes and reviewed it through a thematic analysis by the study team. Input from the advisory panel directly informed refinements to the framework’s structure, terminology, and logic model.

Phase 4: framework development and tool construction

Informed by the validated design brief and advisory board feedback, the fourth phase focused on the development of the CBOF. This phase translated conceptual priorities into a tangible, operational toolset designed for use by sponsor organizations across the clinical trial life cycle.

To design the CBOF we followed the 5W1H Inquiry approach [33]. This allowed us to categorize the information needed to implement IR in clinical trials based on What actions need to be conducted; Why needs to be conducted; Who needs to conduct them; When needs to be conducted in the development cycle phases; Where in the development cycle needs to be executed, and How can be executed and measured.

Comparative analysis outputs

Most commonly met factors

Across the 15 frameworks and guidelines analyzed [14, 23–28, 34–43], several IR implementation factors emerged as consistently addressed, reflecting a shared understanding of foundational requirements for inclusive clinical research. Most frameworks demonstrated strong global and pan-therapeutic applicability, indicating that they are designed to be relevant across geographic regions and therapeutic areas. There was also widespread attention to trial stage specificity, with many frameworks distinguishing between phases such as pre-trial design, recruitment, and retention. Core operational areas, such as study design, recruitment and retention strategies, and access and logistics, were frequently included, suggesting a broad consensus on the importance of addressing structural and procedural barriers to participation. Participant and community engagement was another commonly met factor, underscoring the recognition that trust-building and stakeholder involvement are essential for sustainable inclusion. The full assessment of the 15 frameworks and guidelines can be found in Additional file 3.

Additionally, many frameworks aligned with regulatory guidance (e.g., FDA, EMA) and had been validated through expert consultation, lending credibility and reinforcing their practical relevance. Data collection and analysis practices, particularly those disaggregated by demographic variables, were also well represented, reflecting the growing emphasis on evidence-based IR monitoring.

Least commonly met factors

Some key implementation elements appeared less prominently across the frameworks, pointing to valuable opportunities to enhance operational depth and strengthen accountability. Few frameworks included concrete business use cases, limiting their ability to demonstrate real-world applicability or return on investment. Role-specific guidance delving into responsibilities and accountabilities to specific pharmaceutical company functions was limited across most models. Similarly, structured decision-making tools such as question flows or decision trees, step-by-step implementation guides, and resource-enabled support such as templates or initiative libraries and scalability mechanisms, and organizational adaptivity were addressed only in a few cases; notable examples were the MRCT toolkit and guidance as well as the CTTI framework. While enrollment goal setting is a cornerstone of inclusive planning, only a handful of frameworks offered structured methods for defining and operationalizing such targets. Organizational readiness, encompassing leadership commitment, workforce development, and governance, was often overlooked, despite its critical role in sustaining IR efforts. Finally, although many frameworks acknowledged the importance of evaluation, few provided detailed KPIs or mechanisms for tracking progress across categories. These gaps underscore the need for more comprehensive, operationally grounded tools like the CBOF.

Design brief development

As a result of the comparative analysis outputs, we developed the following design brief:

Design a globally applicable, condition-agnostic framework for pharmaceutical companies to enable representative enrollment in clinical trials by embedding inclusive representation objectives across all departments and accounting for nuances throughout the development lifecycle, from discovery and early development to late development and life cycle management. It should address subpopulation-specific barriers and solutions at each trial stage (planning, start-up, maintenance, readout), and incorporate various trial-related domains including the structured enrollment goal setting and knowledge base, robust data practices, participant and community engagement, study design, access and logistics, site and investigator selection, and recruitment and retention strategies, all of which are commonly addressed in existing frameworks.

To address persistent gaps, the framework must also encompass the assignment of accountable personnel at all organizational levels and integrate measurable KPIs with leadership oversight. The framework format would be mainly based on the use of cards, and should provide step-by-step tools, role-specific guidance, and decision-making engines such as tailored questions. It should also include supporting materials such as real-world use cases and be adaptable to different resource levels. Finally, it should be aligned with regulatory standards (e.g., FDA, EMA, PhRMA).

Expert advisory board consultation and concept validation

During the consultation, experts highlighted several strengths of the framework. They valued its comprehensive coverage of various IR categories such as participant and community engagement, recruitment and retention, site selection and resourcing, data collection, analysis, and reporting, and affirmed the importance of approaching the topic holistically. Experts also positively viewed the integration of trial stages, emphasizing that the framework should not be static or limited to a single stage in the clinical trial process.

Another strength identified was the inclusion of organizational readiness and the assignment of accountable personnel across all levels within pharmaceutical companies. Experts stressed the importance of leadership commitment, dedicated staff, appropriate budgets, and clear accountability mechanisms to sustain inclusive representation over time. One EAB member noted that “having a dedicated focal person to guide inclusive representation efforts within the organization is essential.”

They also underscored the need for participant and community engagement and partnerships across all trial stages, highlighting that such engagement should be continuous rather than episodic. Another EAB member said that “you can’t just go to a community when you’re designing a trial and say, ‘we need you now’. Relationships need to be built over time.” In addition, the use of decision question flows and a card-based architecture was highly regarded and welcomed as it made the framework navigable and practical.

Alongside these strengths, experts offered constructive suggestions for improvement. While the card-based structure was well received, they recommended ensuring flexibility so that it could be adapted to the specific context of each trial, rather than being seen as a one-use tool. An EAB member mentioned that “tools like these help ensure inclusive representation factors are considered, but they shouldn’t become rigid checklists that overlook unique trial needs.” Question-driven decision-making was endorsed, but experts requested more concrete guidance on next steps, consultation pathways, and ongoing monitoring, to encourage deeper exploration beyond predefined questions. One EAB member noted that “decision-triggering questions are valuable, but teams need guidance on where to go if they don’t know the answers.” They also noted that KPIs were essential but should be adaptable to trial design and phase, with mechanisms for continuous tracking and closing of gaps. Another EAB member mentioned that “metrics are helpful, but they must be linked to action plans to ensure progress is monitored and gaps are addressed in real-time.” Furthermore, experts encouraged greater attention to technology, digital tools, artificial intelligence, real-world data, and hybrid trial designs, while emphasizing the importance of safeguards against bias, privacy breaches, and misuse.

In response to these suggestions, the design brief was adapted and refined. Open questions were incorporated into some of the cards, and editable panels were added to allow teams to tailor solutions to each study, avoiding a one-size-fits-all application. An implementation library was developed to provide practical guidance, activity menus, and examples that help teams translate questions into actionable steps. KPIs are now presented as adaptable examples, with explicit guidance on tailoring them to organizational and protocol-specific needs. Digital and AI-enabled approaches, hybrid designs, and real-world data applications were expanded and embedded into the Implementation Library, with particular attention to bias and trust.

Finally, the organizational readiness section was strengthened to more clearly reflect leadership engagement, appropriate budget allocation, dedicated staffing, and sustained accountability mechanisms. In addition, continuous participant and community engagement is now explicitly recommended throughout the entire trial life cycle, reinforcing the importance of partnerships across all stages of the process.

Framework development and structure

Considering the revised design brief, we proceeded to develop the concept of the framework, related tools, and workflow for users. As mentioned in Section “Phase 4: framework development and tool construction,” we followed the 5W1H enquiry approach to categorize the information.

The framework is organized around nine IR Categories that cover the critical factors influencing trial diversity representation, health equity, and inclusion in clinical trials. Two of those categories address the organizational readiness of the pharmaceutical company planning and executing the trial (Table 2), while seven of those categories focus on the design and execution of a given clinical trial (Table 3). Cards-Based System

To operationalize the framework, we created a system of cards that categorizes information around the 5W1H approach, using two components: Core Cards and Support Cards. Together, these components form a question card system that helps identify IR issues in clinical trials and provides guiding information to craft appropriate responses and approaches. Support cards

Support Cards provide detailed, category-specific information that orients teams and guides their responses to the questions of the Trial-Specific Core Cards, and underpins development of IR strategies for each trial-specific category. They also cover the two Organizational Readiness Categories, enabling the company planning or conducting the trial to assess their organizational capabilities. These cards, taken together, clarify what should be done, why it should be done, when it should be done, where it should be done, who should be involved, and how it should be done and measured.

What refers to the specific implementation initiatives and core elements of action within each IR category. It captures tangible activities and strategies that bring diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion principles into practice, serving as the actionable foundation for embedding IR across clinical trials.

Why captures the objectives and intended outcomes of implementing each IR category. It connects each set of initiatives to tangible benefits such as recruiting diverse participants, enhancing retention, generating representative and actionable data, or strengthening internal organization and accountability.

Who focuses on the roles and responsibilities of each team involved in implementing IR across the product development cycle. It details the specific contributions, accountabilities, and interactions of several functions as they relate to the IR categories, and how these roles evolve across the four trial stages and the development cycle, ensuring that every team knows what actions they own, when to act, and how their work supports IR goals.

When has two complementary meanings. First, it captures on a high level the evolution of IR implementation across the seven trial-specific categories, showing how approaches naturally progress across the four development cycle phases. Second, it reflects the presence and role of each team across the four development phases, highlighting on a high level the most relevant contribution of each team to advancing IR objectives.

Where has two complementary meanings. First, it captures the applicability of IR actions across the four-trial stage, indicating when each activity can best be executed within the operational timeline of a trial. Second, it reflects the presence and role of each team across these stages, highlighting how their contributions advance IR objectives by linking teams, categories, and trial stages in a coherent, stage-specific manner.

How has two meanings. First, it refers to the deeper layer of implementation initiatives for each IR category, providing practical tactics, activities, and examples that enable teams to move from high-level IR intentions to actionable execution. It focuses on the specific methods and tools that guide teams in operationalizing IR strategies within trials. Second, it encompasses the mechanisms for measuring and tracking execution, ensuring progress can be monitored and accelerated.

Completion of the Trial-Specific Core Cards should draw not only on these Support Cards but also on community input, lessons from earlier studies in the same development cycle, and relevant literature; however, the Support Cards provide a thorough understanding of key activities that could be conducted in the implementation of IR in clinical trials.

Discussion

Overview and contextualization

Efforts to improve IR in clinical trials have gained momentum, driven by persistent disparities in trial representation and evolving global regulatory expectations [45]. In the United Kingdom, the MHRA, aligned with the NIHR’s INCLUDE initiative, has similarly emphasized inclusive recruitment strategies and transparent demographic reporting [46]. These efforts are further reinforced by the EMA Accelerating Clinical Trials in the EU 2022–2026 work plan [19], which underscores the importance of ensuring participant diversity across trials.

Complementing these regulatory initiatives, the IHI READI project, which is a 6-year public–private partnership that aims to build an inclusive study ecosystem by connecting stakeholders, creating tools, and training to improve inclusive representation and access to underserved populations in European clinical research.

Together, these initiatives are part of a broader, international shift toward embedding health equity into the scientific, regulatory, and operational foundations of clinical research. They reflect a growing global consensus that diverse representation is essential not only for fairness but also for scientific rigor and real-world relevance.

Despite notable progress, the implementation of diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion principles in clinical trials remains inconsistent. While existing frameworks offer valuable conceptual guidance, they often fall short in providing the operational specificity, accountability structures, and organizational alignment necessary for sustained execution. In particular, evidence indicates that inclusive recruitment efforts frequently stagnate when sponsoring organizations lack dedicated personnel for leading diversity strategies, exhibit weak cross-functional coordination, fail to establish accountability mechanisms and metrics, and allocate insufficient resources for community outreach and engagement [12–14, 47]. To address these systemic gaps, we developed a card-based operational framework (CBOF) for the implementation of IR in clinical trials. This structured, decision-driven tool is designed for sponsors, referring specifically to pharmaceutical companies interested in implementing IR in clinical trials, specifically supporting teams in embedding IR considerations across the full trial life cycle, while simultaneously reinforcing organizational capacity by promoting cross-departmental diverse representation and inclusion objectives, clarifying roles and responsibilities, and ensuring leadership oversight and resource allocation.

Design description

The results of this study yielded a comprehensive, card-based operational framework for implementing IR in clinical trials, grounded in a multi-phase methodology that included a scoping review, comparative analysis, expert validation, and iterative design. The framework integrates five core comparative dimensions, including scope, content components, implementation feasibility, regulation, and validation, derived from a thematic synthesis of existing guidance.

A matrix-driven analysis revealed critical gaps in current frameworks, including a lack of role-specific accountability, limited implementation tools, and insufficient logistical planning. These insights informed a design brief that guided the development of a modular, decision-engine-based toolset, structured around nine IR categories spanning trial-specific and organizational readiness domains. The framework was positively validated by a multidisciplinary expert advisory board, who endorsed its anticipatory design, emphasis on organizational readiness, and integration of community engagement. Key innovations include editable Core Cards for trial planning, Support Cards for guidance and training, a library of 349 implementation tactics, and a KPI catalogue for performance tracking. Together, these components operationalize diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion principles across the clinical trial life cycle, offering a scalable, actionable, and context-sensitive solution to longstanding implementation challenges. In addition, although the framework is primarily designed for application at the level of individual clinical trials, it also incorporates considerations and insights relevant to each phase of the product development cycle. This broader perspective enables its use not only within single trials but also at the program level, supporting organizations in embedding inclusive representation principles across their entire portfolio.

Comparative summary and contribution

The CBOF distinguishes itself from existing models through its operational depth, structured decision-making engine, and emphasis on cross-functional accountability. A comparative analysis of 15 frameworks tackling diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion, including INCLUDE, MRCT, TransCelerate, REP-EQUITY, and CTTI, revealed that while many offer strong conceptual guidance, they often fall short in implementation feasibility, role-specific accountability, and integration of measurable outcomes. The CBOF addresses these gaps by scoring consistently high across all five comparative dimensions: scope, content components, implementation feasibility, regulatory alignment, and expert validation. Notably, a distinctive aspect of this framework is the incorporation of a decision engine, step-by-step tools, and a comprehensive KPI catalogue, while also assigning responsibilities across 15 functional teams.

Despite usual frameworks, which tend to focus on either trial-level or organizational-level interventions, the CBOF model integrates both through a dual-card system, Core Cards and Support Cards, covering nine IR categories. This structure enables perspective integration, proactive planning, mid-study adjustments, and post-hoc evaluations. Furthermore, the framework’s modular design, editable components, and extensive implementation library (349 tactics) make it adaptable across therapeutic areas, geographies, and resource levels. As such, this model does not seek to replace existing tools (e.g., MRCT, TransCelerate) but rather to complement and extend them, serving as a bridge between high-level IR strategy and day-to-day clinical trial execution. Its contribution lies in transforming IR from a conceptual imperative into a practical, measurable, and sustainable operational standard.

Limitations

While the CBOF offers a comprehensive and operationally detailed approach to embedding multifactorial elements of inclusive representation in clinical trials, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this framework needs to be systematically piloted in live clinical trial settings. As such, its real-world usability, adaptability across therapeutic areas, and measurable impact on participant diversity remain to be validated. Second, although the framework includes a robust KPI catalogue, some IR categories, particularly those related to organizational readiness and community engagement, pose measurement challenges that require further refinement to ensure consistent and meaningful tracking.

Additionally, while the framework is designed to be globally applicable, certain tools (e.g., the enrollment goal-setting framework) are currently tailored to U.S.-based data sources and may require adaptation for use in other regulatory or demographic contexts. The reliance on qualitative expert feedback, while valuable, also introduces the potential for bias, and broader stakeholder engagement, including site-level staff and patient representatives, will be essential in future iterations. Finally, the framework’s modular design and extensive documentation, while strengths in terms of flexibility and comprehensiveness, may present a learning curve for teams unfamiliar with IR operationalization, underscoring the need for training and change management support during rollout.

An additional practical consideration is resourcing. Many of the activities required to strengthen inclusive representation, such as sustained community engagement, site capacity building, translation and health literacy optimization, participant support, and enhanced data practices, require dedicated time and budget. While the framework was designed to be modular and adaptable to different resource levels, some organizations may face constraints that limit the pace or breadth of adoption without appropriate financial support. This highlights the importance of early budget planning and leadership commitment, which were also emphasized during expert validation. Future evaluation should therefore consider feasibility across a range of sponsor contexts and explore how implementation can be prioritized and scaled based on available resources.

Moreover, while the framework’s design is grounded in expert consensus, its practical performance in real-world contexts remains to be demonstrated. The absence of prospective validation creates uncertainties regarding its feasibility in routine trial operations and, most critically, its measurable impact on improving inclusive representation. Validation in practice will therefore be essential to confirm its utility and refine its components.

Future directions

To fully realize the potential of the CBOF, future work should focus on systematic piloting in active clinical trials across diverse therapeutic areas and geographic regions. These pilots will be essential to evaluate the framework’s usability, adaptability, and impact on recruitment diversity, team coordination, and trial efficiency. Quantitative and qualitative data should be collected to assess how well the framework supports inclusive planning, facilitates cross-functional collaboration, and improves representation outcomes.

While this study and the proposed framework are designed for sponsors, referring specifically to pharmaceutical companies, the underlying principles and decision questions may also be relevant to academic institutions running investigator-initiated or cooperative group trials. Exploring this translation is outside the scope of the current study, but we expect adaptation would primarily involve aligning the organizational readiness elements to academic governance and infrastructure, such as institutional research offices, clinical trials units, and ethics committee processes, and mapping the functional team responsibilities to academic equivalents, such as principal investigators, study coordinators, biostatistics and data management cores, and community engagement resources. In addition, academic settings may require a streamlined implementation library and a pragmatic approach to resourcing so that inclusion activities can be planned during grant development and protocol authoring and then maintained through study conduct. Future piloting in academic medical centers could refine these mappings and test feasibility across different levels of institutional capacity.

Further development is also needed to expand the KPI catalogue, particularly for categories where measurement remains challenging, such as community engagement and organizational readiness. Integrating real-world data sources and analytics, similar to the approach used in the REP-EQUITY Toolkit, could enhance the framework’s capacity to support data-driven enrollment goal setting and subgroup-specific planning. Additionally, tailoring tools and templates for use in non-U.S. regulatory environments will be critical to ensure global applicability.

To support adoption, future iterations should include training modules, onboarding guides, and digital versions of the card system that allow for interactive use and integration into sponsor workflows. While this study focuses on the design and methodological development of the Card Based Operational Framework, the creation and evaluation of a complete training and dissemination package is outside the scope of the present work. Nevertheless, several elements were included to support uptake and consistent use, including a structured user workflow, guidance cards, an implementation library, and a worked case example to illustrate application in practice. In future iterations, user support could be strengthened further through concise guidance materials, short instructional videos, facilitated training sessions, and additional case examples across therapeutic areas, trial phases, and geographic settings to help teams translate the questions and activities into routine trial planning and execution. Finally, broader stakeholder engagement, including patient representatives, site staff, and regulatory bodies, will be vital to refine the framework and ensure it remains responsive to the evolving landscape of inclusive clinical research.

Conclusion

The CBOF represents an important advancement in the operationalization of IR within clinical trials. By combining a structured decision-engine model with modular tools, role-specific guidance, and measurable performance indicators, the framework addresses longstanding gaps in implementation feasibility, accountability, and organizational alignment. Its design reflects a shift from aspirational IR goals to actionable, scalable strategies that can be embedded across the trial life cycle and sponsor functions. While further validation through real-world application is needed, the framework offers a practical and adaptable solution for sponsors seeking to improve representativeness, reduce barriers to participation, and meet evolving regulatory expectations. As global standards for inclusive research continue to rise, tools like this will be essential for translating diverse representation, health equity, and inclusion principles into consistent, impactful trial practices.

The card-based decision flow engine developed provides a practical approach to embedding IR considerations throughout the clinical trial life cycle. By guiding structured, cross-functional action at key trial stages, the framework promotes shared accountability and makes inclusion planning more transparent and consistent. While early application indicates improved alignment across teams, further evaluation is needed to assess its scalability, adaptability across trial contexts, and long-term impact on inclusive trial design.
