MRI-enhanced online adaptive radiotherapy by virtual integration of a standalone MRI simulator and CBCT-based treatment delivery
Authors: Silvia Fabiano, Klara Kefer, Michael Baumgartl, Hubert Gabrys, Riccardo Dal Bello, Elizabeth Denney, Michael Mayinger, Jens von der Grün, Lotte Wilke, Matthias Guckenberger, Stephanie Tanadini-Lang, Sebastian M Christ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeMR-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT) offers superior soft-tissue contrast and enables adaptive treatments which improve tumor targeting and organ-at-risk sparing. However, integrated MR-Linac systems remain financially and logistically inaccessible for many hospitals. To address this gap, we implemented a modular MRgRT workflow — termed the “Virtual MR-Linac”— which decouples MR imaging and treatment delivery, enabling daily adaptive radiotherapy (ART) using diagnostic-quality MRI.Materials and methodsBetween November 2023 and January 2025, 31 male patients with pelvic lymph node metastases were treated using this workflow. Daily MR scans were acquired in treatment position using a 1.5T MR scanner. Contours were adapted on 2D T2 turbo-spin-echo images, while synthetic CTs generated from T1 DIXON sequences were used for dose calculation. Adapted plans were delivered on a C-arm Linac using CBCT imaging for positioning. Quality assurance included independent Monte Carlo dose recalculations, automated plan parameters verification and a plan complexity check using in-house scripting.ResultsAcross 99 adapted fractions, plan adaptation improved dosimetric outcomes, with a median 14% increase in Planning Target Volume (PTV) coverage and a 7% reduction in bowel D1cc (dose to 1 cc). Median times for MR imaging, contouring, and planning were 12, 12, and 20 min, respectively.ConclusionsThe Virtual MR-Linac approach enables high-quality ART to compensate for inter-fractional motion without the costs and complexity of integrated systems. It improves resource utilization, maintains patient comfort, and offers a flexible, scalable solution for clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13014-026-02887-w.

Introduction

Magnetic Resonance (MR) has become a benchmark in radiation oncology treatment planning, as it provides high spatiotemporal resolution, superior soft-tissue contrast compared to computed tomography (CT), and functional as well as biological tissue characterization capabilities [1]. Building on these advantages, MR-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT) has emerged as a promising approach for adaptive radiotherapy. In regions affected by substantial inter-fractional anatomical variability, daily changes in patient anatomy can considerably alter target position relative to organs at risk (OARs). Compared with CT-based imaging, the superior soft-tissue contrast of MRI enables more accurate target and OARs delineation, thereby facilitating daily anatomy-driven treatment adaption with greater confidence. This may enable more precise tumor targeting, reduced safety margins, and minimized damage to surrounding healthy tissues [2–6]. In addition, real-time imaging during treatment delivery further enables intra-fractional motion monitoring [7–9], offering the potential to improve patient outcomes and reduce side effects [10, 11].

However, the implementation of MRgRT faces significant financial and logistical challenges that limit its widespread adoption [12, 13]. The high manufacturing costs of MR-guided linear accelerator (MR-Linac) systems, driven by the complexity of integrating linear accelerator technology with MR functionality, pose obstacles to their broader commercial viability. Construction, purchase, and maintenance costs for these systems are approximately two to three times higher than those of standard CT-based linear accelerators, posing a substantial financial barrier for many departments [14]. Moreover, online adaptive radiotherapy (oART) with MR-Linacs is both resource- and time-intensive [15], further limiting patient throughput and overall accessibility.

Recently, CT-guided oART systems, including the Varian Ethos and Elekta Evo radiotherapy platforms, have been introduced. These systems integrate semi-automated anatomy detection and plan generation, enabling streamlined and fast adaptive workflows [16–20]. However, CT imaging is inherently limited by its lower soft-tissue contrast compared with MRI, which may reduce confidence in target and OARs delineation during daily adaptation, particularly in anatomically complex regions affected by substantial inter-fractional variability. To address this limitation, previous studies proposed a workflow combining weekly MR imaging with Cone Beam CT (CBCT)-based ART through a shuttle-based approach, thereby incorporating offline MR guidance into the adaptive process [21–23]. However, because MR imaging was performed only intermittently, this approach could not fully support daily anatomy-driven adaptation.

Building upon this approach, our team has developed an innovative MRgRT system design, termed “Virtual MR-Linac”, as part of the ARTEMIS Program (Adaptive Radiation Therapy Enhanced by Magnetic Resonance Imaging Systems). The system features a dedicated MR scanner in close proximity to multiple C-arm linear accelerators. This multi-room configuration enables an adaptive radiotherapy workflow, in which patients undergo daily MR-imaging in treatment position using a diagnostic MR scanner specifically tailored to radiotherapy needs, allowing daily anatomy-driven re-contouring and plan adaption based on diagnostic-quality MR-images. Adapted plans are generated on synthetic CT (sCT) images derived from the daily MRI and subsequently delivered on a C-arm linear accelerator using in-room CBCT-based image guidance.

We hypothesize that this approach preserves the clinical advantages of MRgRT by enabling daily adaptive treatments based on high-quality MR-imaging, while providing a more flexible and cost-effective alternative to fully integrated MR-Linac systems. Compared with MR-Linac technology, the proposed approach has the potential to streamline resource utilization, increase patient throughput, and leverage the superior imaging performance of a diagnostic MR as an integral component of the ART workflow.

To support the clinical implementation of this workflow, our research group has recently conducted a Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA), with the goal of ensuring patient safety and operational efficiency [24].

In this study, we report the initial clinical experience with the treatment of 31 male patients with pelvic lymph node lesions using the Virtual MR-Linac approach. We evaluate its dosimetric benefits and the adaption time.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and prescriptions

This retrospective single-center analysis was approved by the Swiss Ethics Committee (BASEC: 2018 − 01794) prior to study initiation. All patients provided informed consent to undergo MR-guided treatment within the ARTEMIS program and to participate in the study.

The ART workflow using a virtual MR-linac was applied to 31 male patients with pelvic lymph node metastases who were eligible for MR-linac treatment. The median PTV volume was 4.51 cc (IQR: 3.16–13.23 cc).

The majority of patients (52%) were prescribed 10 Gy in 2 fractions, while 35% (11 patients) received 35 Gy in 5 fractions. The remaining patients were prescribed a single fraction of 5 Gy (2 patients; 6%), 4 fractions of 5 Gy (1 patient; 3%), or 6 fractions of 6 Gy (1 patient; 3%). An 80% prescription isodose line was consistently used.

OAR constraints primarily focused on the bowel (including sigmoid), with limits of D1cc < 30 Gy and D0.1cc < 33 Gy for fractionation schemes of 4–6 fractions. For treatments delivered in 1–2 fractions, avoidance of bowel hotspots was ensured.

ART workflow using the virtual MR-Linac

Base plan preparation

For treatment planning, patients undergo 2D T2 turbo-spin-echo and 3D T1-DIXON MRI scans in treatment position using the MAGNETOM SOLA 1.5T MR scanner (Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany), located in our radiation oncology department. Detailed sequence parameters are provided in Supplementary Table T1. A sCT is generated at the scanner console using the T1-DIXON data. The sCT is then sent to MIM Software® (v. 7.4.2, MIM Software Inc., Cleveland, OH, USA) for resampling to a 1 mm resolution and for auto-contouring of the main organs at risk (OARs), including the rectum, bladder, and bowel. MIM also automatically transfers the MR and sCT images into ARIA, with all images sharing the same frame of reference, eliminating the need for additional co-registrations. An experienced radiation oncologist reviews the quality of the intrinsic registrations and subsequently contours the target volumes on the sCT, using the T2-weighted image for guidance.

Next, a dosimetrist creates a stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) plan using the Eclipse (v. 16.1, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) treatment planning system, following a checklist to ensure compliance with internal quality standards [24]. Treatment plans are generated using institutional templates that define beam configuration (number of beams, directions, and energies) as well as optimization objectives and clinical goals. Dose calculation is performed on the sCT using the AcurosXB, v.16.1.0, algorithm (Varian Medical Systems). Plans are normalized to the Planning Target Volume (PTV), ensuring that 100% of the prescribed dose covers at least 98% of the PTV, with a maximum dose of 127% of the prescribed dose within the Gross Tumor Volume (GTV) (corresponding to a prescription isodose line of 80%).

Treatment adaption and delivery

As outlined in Fig. 1, the adaptive workflow includes the following steps and was applied to all 31 patients and 99 fractions. First, T2-weighted and T1-DIXON MRI sequences are acquired in treatment position using the 1.5T MAGNETOM SOLA MR scanner. A smaller field of view (FOV) in the superior-inferior direction minimizes imaging time to under 15 min. At the scanner, the system automatically generates a new sCT based on the daily anatomy. Afterwards, all images are manually sent to ARIA and automatically renamed according to institutional guidelines using an in-house developed Eclipse Scripting API (ESAPI) script. This script also resamples the sCT to a 1 mm resolution. Although all images share the same frame of reference, minor patient motion between MRI sequences may occur during acquisition. Therefore, the quality of the intrinsic registration is systematically verified by an experienced radiation oncologist. If clinically relevant misalignment between MRI sequences is observed, the image frame of reference can be reassigned and manual registration is performed prior to contour adaptation.

Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)-guided adaptive radiotherapy with a daily magnetic resonance (MR)-informed workflow [24]

Next, the daily sCT is registered to the original planning sCT. The original target contours are transferred onto the new sCT using rigid registration, whereas the OAR contours are transferred using deformable registration. A physician then adapts the GTV on the sCT using the T2-weighted image for guidance and generates the PTV. Additionally, any OARs within a 2 cm margin around the GTV, are also adapted as needed.

A physicist then uses the ESAPI script to automatically copy the original treatment plan onto the new sCT, attach the adapted structure set, and create auxiliary structures necessary for plan optimization. The physicist can either recalculate the base plan on the daily anatomy or perform a full optimization, informed by changes in target–OAR geometry and the extent of contour modifications. When re-optimization is required, the original optimization objectives are used as a starting point and may be manually adjusted until an acceptable plan that meets clinical goals is achieved. Finally, the physician approves the selected plan following a dosimetric assessment.

To ensure accurate and safe treatment delivery, multiple verification checks are performed prior to beam-on, as described in section “Plan-specific quality assurance”.

Patients are ambulatory between imaging and treatment delivery and are repositioned on the treatment couch prior to irradiation. After completing the adaptive procedure and before delivering radiation, a verification CBCT scan is acquired to aid patient positioning at a TrueBeam Linac equipped with either a High Definition Multileaf Collimator (HD-MLC) or a Millennium MLC (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Additionally, the ESAPI script automatically verifies plan normalization and conducts a comparative analysis of key parameters, including dose per fraction, target volumes (with an internal threshold of 20%), and monitor units (MUs) (with an internal threshold of 20%), against the base plan.

Data analysis

A dosimetric evaluation was performed for the 31 included patients with pelvic lymph node metastases. The dose distributions of the base plan, the adapted plan, and the base plan recalculated on the daily sCTs were compared to assess the impact of adaptation on target coverage and OAR sparing. This analysis was complemented by a time evaluation of the adaptive workflow, in which five phases were measured:

Additionally, the total session time was defined as the interval from the start of MRI acquisition to the completion of the radiation treatment, and notably included any delays such as waiting times, or workflow obstacles.

Portal dosimetry measurements, when performed, were acquired before treatment delivery and were included in phase V.

Finally, a comparative evaluation of resource utilization was conducted using a 0.35T MR-Linac workflow as benchmark.

Results

Our department began treating patients using this workflow in November 2023. By January 2025, 31 male patients with pelvic lymph node metastases had completed treatment. In total, 99 fractions were adapted for the 31 patients, and treatment was completed as planned in all cases. Portal dosimetry measurements were performed in 2 out of 99 fractions (2%) and were acquired before treatment.

The dosimetric benefits of the adaptive approach are highlighted in Fig. 2 (with expanded subgroup analysis by fractionation scheme available in the Supplementary Figures F1 and F2). For each patient, Figs. 2a-c show the average GTV and PTV volumes receiving the prescribed dose, as well as the average dose to 1 cc of the bowel, for the base plan, the adapted plans, and the base plan recalculated on the daily anatomy. Plan adaptation resulted in improved bowel sparing in most cases (Fig. 2c). For patients in whom the bowel already received low doses, adaptation primarily enhanced target coverage (Fig. 2a and b). Specifically, plan adaptation led to a median increase in PTV coverage of 14% and a reduction in bowel dose of 7%.

A representative case (Patient 4) is shown in Fig. 3, illustrating how the Virtual MR-Linac workflow can compensate for inter-fractional anatomical variations, maintaining adequate target coverage while improving bowel sparing. In the original plan, the bowel was located well away from the target volume (Fig. 3a and b). During all five treatment sessions, however, the bowel moved into close proximity to the target and partially overlapped with the PTV. When the original plan was recalculated on the daily sCTs, these anatomical changes resulted in both increased bowel irradiation and reduced dose conformality, leading to a decrease in PTV coverage from 98.2% in the original plan to 78% in the recalculated plans (Figs. 3c). By adapting the plan each day, mean PTV coverage was improved to 86% across all fractions. GTV coverage remained high, with the volume receiving the prescribed dose being 100% in the original plan, 99% in the recalculated plans, and 100% in the adapted plans. At the same time, plan adaptation reduced the average dose to 1 cc of the bowel from 30 Gy in the recalculated plans to 20 Gy in the adapted plans (Figs. 3d).

Discussion

Between November 2023 and January 2025, 31 male patients with pelvic lymph node metastases were successfully treated using a Virtual MR-Linac at our department. Most patients received either 10 Gy in 2 fractions or 35 Gy in 5 fractions (87%). Dose was prescribed to the 80% isodose line for all patients, and all fractions were adapted, with treatments completed as planned. While adaptation was performed for all fractions in this study, the modular design of the workflow would also allow adaptation decisions to be made at different stages of the process.

Dose re-optimization was required for all fractions, either to restore target coverage or to improve OAR sparing, due to the base plans’ lack of robustness in a population exhibiting considerable inter-fractional anatomical variability. Pelvic lymph node metastases are particularly susceptible to internal organ motion driven by day-to-day variations in bladder, bowel, and rectal filling. Patients were therefore instructed to attend treatment with an empty rectum and a comfortably full bladder, achieved by drinking approximately one glass of water 45 min prior to the session. No additional dietary restrictions or bowel preparation measures were implemented. In this setting, the use of diagnostic-quality daily MR imaging facilitated anatomy-driven contour adaptation with improved confidence in target and OAR delineation. Plan adaptation ultimately resulted in a median increase in target coverage of 14% and a median reduction in bowel dose of 7%, underscoring the potential clinical benefits of daily adaptation.

These findings are consistent with prior studies. For example, Winkel et al. [27] reported target coverage improvements of 10–15% with daily MR-guided adaptation for pelvic nodes, while Tetar et al. [28]. observed significant bowel dose reductions through adaptation, with potential implications for reducing gastrointestinal toxicity. Given the established association between suboptimal target dosing and inferior local control in nodal metastases, the observed increase in PTV coverage may translate to improved tumor control [29–31]. Similarly, minimizing bowel dose is critical, as late GI toxicity remains a dose-limiting factor in pelvic RT, particularly when treating nodal disease adjacent to mobile bowel loops. In this context, our results further support the growing consensus that daily adaptation is not merely dosimetrically superior, but also clinically meaningful in improving therapeutic ratios for patients receiving pelvic nodal radiotherapy.

The median adaptive session time was 104 min, which currently exceeds the session durations reported for oART with MR-Linacs across various indications and protocols [32–34]. We acknowledge that these session times reflect an early-phase implementation and that further workflow optimization is essential both for clinical scalability and for minimizing the potential impact of intrafraction anatomical variation [35]. Nevertheless, because MRI acquisition and treatment delivery are physically decoupled, the workflow limits the time patients spend immobilized on the treatment table to less than 30 min, similar to some optimized MR-Linac workflows [36]. However, in contrast to MR-Linacs, where continuous machine occupancy is required throughout the adaptive session, the modular design additionally enables parallel imaging and treatment, a unique feature of the Virtual MR-Linac.

In fact, our comparative evaluation of resource utilization revealed that, while the MR-Linac workflow offers a streamlined and integrated solution, the Virtual MR-Linac approach allows for more flexible use of clinical resources. By separating the imaging and treatment steps, this workflow has the potential to increase patient throughput—reserving the MR-Linac for cases requiring intra-fractional tumor monitoring, while treating patients who can tolerate longer adaptation times with the Virtual MR-Linac.

Because patients are ambulatory between imaging and treatment, we acknowledge that intrafraction anatomical changes may partially reduce the dosimetric benefit observed in the adapted plans. Therefore, the results presented in Fig. 2 should be interpreted as a best case estimate of the dosimetric benefit under the current workflow implementation. Assessment of intrafraction motion between planning MRI and pre-treatment CBCT imaging represents an important step for this technology. However, this was beyond the scope of the present feasibility study and is instead a prespecified endpoint of a future prospective clinical trial. Importantly, substantial day-to-day anatomical variability remains a major challenge in pelvic nodal radiotherapy and was the primary motivation for implementing daily adaptive replanning in this study. In addition, qualitative review of the verification CBCTs revealed no major anatomical changes that prevented treatment delivery using the adapted plans.

Several factors were identified as important contributors to the overall session duration. Although the minimal MRI acquisition protocol itself requires approximately 6 min and 19 s, the total imaging time additionally includes acquisition of supplementary slices when needed, and generation of the sCT used for planning. Currently, the sCT is generated from T1-weighted images, requiring verification of the T1–T2 registration before contouring. Generating the sCT directly from T2-weighted images could eliminate this step and further reduce imaging time. Additional delays resulted from coordinating MRI acquisition with treatment machine availability. Dynamic allocation of the C-arm Linac based on expected availability may further improve efficiency. At the Linac, image matching was performed between the sCT and CBCT, whereas direct T2–CBCT matching would be preferable. Nevertheless, lymph nodes were clearly visible on the CBCTs, increasing our confidence not only in contour adaptation and dose calculation, but also in patient positioning. An example of a sCT–CBCT match is provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure F4). Finally, an initial learning curve further contributed to delays as the team became familiar with the workflow.

The Supplementary Figure F5 summarizes the workflow steps that are currently being automated using Python- and ESAPI-based scripting tools to reduce manual workload, standardize planning procedures, improve workflow robustness, and shift staff effort from manual execution toward verification and approval tasks. Once fully optimized, we anticipate median total session times below 40 min.

One limitation of our workflow is the absence of real-time imaging during beam-on time, a feature available in the MR-Linac through cine-MRI for tumor tracking during radiotherapy, allowing ART to compensate for inter-fractional changes but not intra-fractional motion. Furthermore, the workflow relies on intrinsic registration between MRI sequences acquired over a period of up to 12 min, during which patient motion may occur. Although all images shared the same frame of reference, small inter-sequence shifts cannot be entirely excluded. To mitigate this, all registrations were systematically reviewed by an experienced radiation oncologist, with the option to perform manual re-registration if required. In this cohort, no clinically relevant misregistrations were observed that necessitated correction. In addition, neither a second MRI immediately prior to treatment nor post-treatment imaging for dose verification was acquired in this study; both approaches will be evaluated in future studies.

Despite these limitations, the modular design of our system offers distinct advantages: it could be more easily implemented in standard radiotherapy departments, as most already possess the necessary hardware, unlike MR-Linacs. The main requirement is a dedicated software, which, once available, could be rapidly deployed. In addition, the system provides greater flexibility through the independent operation of each unit and allows for high-quality MRI for plan adaptation. These features make such a modular system a promising approach for enhancing accessibility and optimizing resource utilization.

For ART to achieve broader clinical adoption, cost-effective solutions must demonstrate clear dosimetric and/or clinical benefits in prospective trials. At our institution, we have opened the MACULA trial, a phase 1 clinical study assessing the feasibility and efficacy of MRgRT for pelvic and para-aortic lymph node metastases using a Virtual MR-Linac, combined with an AirShuttle system for patient transfer between imaging and treatment rooms. The latter may even further enhance workflow efficiency in a future, more time-efficient implementation of the workflow by eliminating the need for patient repositioning.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that MRgRT using a Virtual MR-Linac is clinically feasible for the treatment of pelvic lymph node lesions. The approach may offer dosimetric benefits through daily anatomy-driven adaption based on diagnostic-quality MR-imaging, including improved target coverage and enhanced bowel sparing. Compared to integrated MR-Linac systems, the modular design provides a flexible and potentially more accessible solution for radiotherapy departments, optimizing resource utilization and improving patient comfort by minimizing time on the treatment table. Moving forward, we plan to expand this workflow to include tumor sites commonly treated on the MR-linac system at our institution, such as thoracic or abdominal tumors, to further assess its clinical versatility and extend its application across a wider range of disease sites.
