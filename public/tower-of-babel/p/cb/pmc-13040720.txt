Climate change and malaria control in Africa: country experiences and strategic responses
Authors: Anankpo Gildas Yahouedo, Corinne S Merle, Ibrahima Diallo, Médoune Ndiop, Andrew Muhire, Clarisse Mukashema, Alphonse Mutabazi, Emmanuel Hakizimana, Christel Muteba, André Ngombe Kaseba, Mea Antoine Tanoh, Alexis Serge Aimain, Kolotioloma Alama Coulibaly, Voahangy Razanakotomalala, Mahamat Saleh Issakha Diar, Mahamat Idriss Djaskano, Israël Kodindo, Diego Ayala, Cyril Caminade, Ibrahima Diouf, Thibaud de Chevigny, Olivia Ngou, Emilie Pothin, Florian Girond, Andre-Marie Tchouatieu, Eric Fleutelot, Veronica Noseda, Jane L Deuve
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is recognized as a critical determinant of malaria transmission in the coming decades. This manuscript synthesizes testimonies from six African countries (Chad, Côte d’Ivoire, Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Madagascar, Rwanda, and Senegal) highlighting how climate variability has recently influenced malaria epidemiology and how national malaria control programmes (NMCPs) are adapting. The correlation between changing patterns of rainfalls, floodings, and how they affect the expansion of mosquito vector habitats with malaria transmission has been reported. Adaptive strategies adopted by the NMCPs include integrating climate and health data, strengthening surveillance and establishing early warning systems, engaging and educating communities, promoting multisectoral collaboration, and leveraging technology and innovation. However, systemic barriers, such as limited data integration capacity, infrastructural deficits, behavioural factors, and financial constraints, continue to impede effective implementation of adaptive strategies. Addressing these barriers requires sustained political commitment, enhanced technical capacity, operational and implementation research, and long-term investment in climate-resilient health systems. The findings offer valuable insights for broader regional efforts to align malaria control strategies with climate change.Keywords: Malaria, Vector, Climate change, Africa

Background

Malaria remains a major public health challenge, with the African continent bearing the majority of the global burden [1–3]. While significant progress has been made in reducing malaria incidence and mortality over the past two decades [1], these gains are increasingly threatened by the impacts of climate change, alongside rising mosquito resistance to insecticides, emerging antimalarial drug resistance, and persistent socioeconomic instability [4–6]. The World Malaria Report 2025 [3] reinforces an alarming trend: malaria cases continue to rise in 2024, with 610,000 lives lost and 282 million reported cases of malaria worldwide, 9 million more than in 2023. Africa accounts for 94% of cases and 95% of deaths.

Climate change acts as a threat multiplier, altering malaria spatio-temporal transmission dynamics and placing unprecedented strain on already fragile public health systems across Africa [7]. Rising temperatures can accelerate parasite and mosquito development, while warmer nightly temperatures significantly influence transmission dynamics [8, 9]. Warming trends are expanding malaria into mid- and high-altitude regions, exposing populations and increasing epidemic risk [4, 10, 11]. Altered timing and duration of transmission seasons complicate intervention planning [4]. Changes in rainfall patterns and extreme weather create new breeding sites, and flooding heightens malaria risk while disrupting health infrastructures, and hindering access to care and control measures [4, 5, 12, 13]. Public health systems face mounting pressure as malaria expands geographically with unpredictable changes, requiring broader surveillance, prevention, and treatment with limited resources [1]. Core tools such as long-lasting insecticidal nets and indoor residual spraying are being undermined by the growing problem of mosquito resistance to insecticide [3]. However, climate change may also contribute to the reduction of their efficacy, as the immediate and residual effectiveness of certain insecticide classes is known to vary with temperature [14, 15] and humidity conditions [16, 17]. Climate change also intersects with poverty, food insecurity, and political instability, amplifying population vulnerability [1].

In response to the growing threat of climate change on malaria transmission, L’ Initiative (Expertise France) and the Optimizing Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention (OPT-SMC) project organized a regional workshop on malaria and climate change at “Institut d’Enseignement Supérieur” (INES) of Ruhengeri, Musanze, Rwanda, from the 2nd to the 6th of December 2024. The event gathered representatives from National Malaria Control Programs (NMCPs) across 19 sub-Saharan African countries (Benin, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Congo, Côte d’Ivoire, Djibouti, Gabon, Guinea, Equatorial Guinea, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda, Senegal, and Togo) [18].

The workshop aimed to raise awareness on the links between climate-driven changes and malaria transmission, strengthen collaboration between health and meteorological sectors, and promote integration of environmental data into health systems such as District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2). Participants explored context-specific modelling tools for responses to malaria outbreaks and the role of implementation research in developing cost-effective resilience strategies.

Training was delivered by a multidisciplinary team from leading institutions, including International Centre for Theoretical Physics (ICTP), Institut de Recherche pour le Développement (IRD), Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV), Institut Pasteur de Madagascar, Impact Santé Afrique (ISA), Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute (Swiss TPH), the Special Programme for Research and Training in Tropical Diseases (TDR), Rwanda Biomedical Center (RBC), University of Cheikh Anta Diop (UCAD), and WHO’s Global Malaria Programme. Sessions covered surveillance, climate forecasting, modelling, and vector control strategies, enabling participants to share experiences and best practices for climate-informed malaria control. This manuscript analyzes national experiences and adaptive strategies that were presented by NMCPs and discussed during the workshop, highlighting solutions to overcome barriers posed by climate change in malaria prevention and control.

Countries experiences

Chad

The malaria case incidence in the country has slightly decreased from 215 per 1000 population in 2015 to 206.1 in 2024 [3]. The estimated malaria cases increased from 3.1 m in 2015 to 3.9 m in 2023 [19]. Thirty percent (30%) of medical consultations and forty-three (43%) of deaths are attributed to malaria (NMCP). The typical rainy season extends from July to October, leading to increased Anopheles mosquito populations and consequently malaria transmission. Flood events can further increase risk by generating additional aquatic breeding habitats.

The reported case study during the workshop analysed the impact of climate fluctuations on the abundance of Anopheles mosquitoes and malaria cases between December 2022 and March 2023. The case study showed that 79% of Anopheles mosquitoes were captured in areas affected by flooding, with the major malaria vectors, Anopheles coluzzii (86%) and Anopheles arabiensis (14%) as the predominant species [20]. There was a significant correlation between increased rainfall, temperature, and the incidence of malaria. Flooding has extended the malaria transmission period from the usual 3–4 months to nearly 9 months each year. The residual water resulting from flooding creates ideal aquatic habitats for Anopheles stephensi, a highly invasive mosquito species that is already present in eight states in Sudan, including those bordering Chad [21]. Additionally, certain behavioural practices, such as sleeping outdoors without mosquito nets during the dry seasons due to high temperatures (40–45 °C), continue to increase the population's vulnerability to malaria.

Chad’s adaptation strategies were not reported but rather the way forward to overcome challenges, including improving collaboration with meteorological services to forecast and manage floods, maintaining consistent public awareness on insecticide treated nets (ITN) usage throughout the year, building local entomological and molecular diagnostic capacity, and securing donor funding to strengthen or scale up activities aimed at mitigating the impact of climate change on malaria transmission.

Côte d’Ivoire

The malaria case incidence in the country increased from 242.1 per 1000 population in 2015 to 267.9 in 2024 [3]. The estimated malaria cases increased from 6.1 m in 2015 to 7.8 m in 2023 [19]. Côte d'Ivoire encompasses diverse ecological zones, creating varied microclimates that affect disease transmission. Northern regions have experienced rising temperatures, variable rainfall, and extreme weather events such as droughts and floods since the 1970s. Rural agricultural communities, which comprise a significant portion of Côte d'Ivoire's population, may be particularly vulnerable due to their direct dependence on climate-sensitive livelihoods such as shea trees [22] and limited access to healthcare services. Climate data from 2004–2013 revealed: an incremental increase of 10 mm of monthly precipitation correlates with 1–1.2% more malaria cases, a 1 °C temperature rise associates with 3.5% fewer malaria cases, and a 0.1 NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) unit increase links to 7.3% more monthly malaria cases [22, 23].

Case studies reported during the workshop demonstrate relationships between rainfall and malaria incidence. In Abidjan-Niangon, 36% of monthly malaria prevalence is positively associated with rainfall (R = 0.66, p < 0.05, Pearson test) [24]. An increase of 58% in malaria cases in Grand-Bassam is correlated with higher rainfall (R = 0.53, p < 0.05, Pearson test) [25]. Granadoet al. observed that episodes of heavy rainfall in Abidjan lead to the accumulation of stagnant water, thereby creating favourable conditions for the expansion of mosquito breeding sites [26]. A strong correlation (R = 0.90, p < 0.001, Pearson test) was observed between rainfall and malaria prevalence in the region of Korhogo, Northern Côte d’Ivoire [27]. This correlation was identified through a sample survey involving 200 women conducted between 2018 and 2019.

Democratic Republic of the Congo

The malaria case incidence in the country increased from 303 per 1000 population in 2015 to 321.9 in 2024 [3]. The estimated malaria cases increased from 24.3 m in 2015 to 33.1 m in 2023 [19]. The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), characterized by its vast geography and environmental diversity, including the world's second-largest tropical forest, is particularly vulnerable to climate-related health impacts. Climate change, deforestation, and environmental degradation are acknowledged as significant factors influencing malaria transmission in DRC.

The reported adaptive strategies include:

The development of a national dashboard that combines epidemiological and climate data to support decision-making and guide interventions.

The adjustment of malaria epidemic response measures, including case management, mass distribution of ITNs, use of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), community sensitization for personal protection and malaria vector control, and the pre-positioning of supplies to ensure timely response.

Challenges reported included limited training on climate-health links, weak data analysis capacity at decentralized levels, administrative delays in fund disbursement, and insufficient climate sensors and Information and Technology (IT) equipment in many health zones. The essential requirements reported include real-time investigations, emergency funding, high-quality data, and more integrating climate data into health decision-making.

Madagascar

The malaria case incidence in the country increased from 107 per 1000 population in 2015 to 255.4 in 2024 [3]. The estimated malaria cases increased from 2.7 m in 2015 to 6.2 m in 2023 [19]. Madagascar faces persistent malaria transmission, exacerbated by climate change, which is expanding malaria risk into high-altitude areas. Despite progress, malaria remains a major public health challenge, with over 50% of cases concentrated in just 5 of the 23 regions. The country has observed over 100% year-round malaria cases since 2015 [3], attributed to socio-economic crisis, climate variability, health system weaknesses, insecticide resistance, and behavioural factors.

Rwanda

The malaria case incidence in the country decreased from 308 per 1000 population in 2015 to 77.1 in 2024 [3]. The estimated malaria cases decreased from 3.5 m in 2015 to 0.7 m in 2023 [19]. Rwanda, a high-altitude country in East Africa with a temperate climate and two rainy seasons, has established a robust association between climate variability and malaria transmission. Studies have confirmed strong correlations among rainfall, temperature, and malaria outbreaks, with El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events also influencing transmission patterns [30–32]. Rwanda classifies malaria transmission into four strata (ranging from low to very high) based on incidence rates, elevation, and climatic data.

To address climate change impact on malaria transmission, the country combines different strategies.

The challenges reported include limited health sector awareness of the value of climate data, the lack of a fully operational early warning system, fragmented initiatives reliant on individual networks, insufficient capacity and sustained funding for climate-health data integration.

Senegal

The malaria case incidence in the country decreased from 69.3 per 1000 population in 2015 to 36.8 in 2024 [3]. The estimated malaria cases increased from 0.9 m in 2015 to 1.1 m in 2023 [19]. Senegal’s NMCP has observed significant shifts in malaria transmission dynamics due to climate change, particularly since the 2010 s (NMCP). These changes include increased rainfall, rising groundwater levels, and flooding, which have led to a proliferation of malaria vectors and a rise in severe cases and deaths. The country is divided into three malaria transmission zones: the southern zone, which experiences high transmission; the central zone, with moderate transmission; and the northern zone, where transmission is low. The highest malaria burden is disproportionately concentrated in the south-eastern districts of Kédougou, Kolda, and Tambakounda.

The country faces persistent challenges despite the implementation of adaptive strategies. Limited healthcare access and uneven geographical coverage impede effective malaria case management. Low usage of insecticide-treated nets and inadequate distribution among vulnerable populations reduce the efficacy of vector control interventions. The limited reach of SMC requires expansion into new regions. There is a need to reconsider the target age groups for SMC to enhance effectiveness. There is uncertainty regarding the optimal number of treatment cycles for SMC. Challenges exist in scaling up mass drug administration across broader geographic areas and determining appropriate treatment frequencies. Predictive modelling for malaria control still needs the integration of a comprehensive set of parameters, including clinical data, climatic variables, socio-demographic and economic indicators, environmental factors, and the level of coverage of existing interventions.

Discussions and suggested way forward

The experiences of Chad, Côte d’Ivoire, Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Madagascar, Rwanda, and Senegal, collectively demonstrate the increasingly significant influence that climate change exerts on malaria transmission dynamics. Despite variations in geography, healthcare system development, and resource availability, common themes emerge highlighting progress and ongoing obstacles in adjusting malaria control approaches to shifting climatic conditions.

This study synthesizes the experiences of National Malaria Control or Elimination Programmes (NMC/EPs) across six African countries, revealing a continent grappling with the dual burdens of a persistent disease and the escalating impacts of climate change. The findings underscore a critical reality: climate change is no longer a future threat but a present-day amplifier of malaria transmission, actively complicating and, in some cases, reversing hard-won gains in disease control [5, 33]. The cross-cutting themes include altered transmission patterns, increased vulnerability of health systems, and the exacerbation of existing operational barriers, and are consistent with a growing body of literature that links climatic factors to the epidemiology of vector-borne diseases [34, 35].

Shifting epidemiological landscapes

A primary finding from the country NMC/EPs experiences is the observable shift in malaria's geographical and seasonal boundaries. Reports of malaria encroaching into higher altitude regions, previously considered malaria-free, align with former predictions from climate models. This expansion is biologically plausible, as rising temperatures create more favourable conditions for both the Anopheles vectors and the Plasmodium parasite to complete their life cycles in these new environments [9], while also increasing human vulnerability through socioeconomic disruption, population displacement, food insecurity, and constrained effective access to prevention and care measures. The influence of temperature is not linear; daily temperature fluctuations, not just mean temperatures, can significantly alter transmission dynamics, potentially accelerating parasite development in cooler regions and slowing it in warmer ones [8]. Temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity are key climate variables that influence malaria transmission, with humidity showing a positive effect on total malaria cases three months later in Western Ethiopia [36, 37]. This complexity highlights the challenge for NMC/EPs, which must now contend with a moving target, requiring surveillance in areas not previously prioritized.

Furthermore, countries reported changes in transmission seasonality, with prolonged transmission seasons or erratic, intense outbreaks following extreme weather events like floods. These experiences are echoed in recent studies from Kenya, which have documented the influence of climate variability on parasite prevalence [38]. Such unpredictability strains health systems, complicates the timing of interventions like SMC, and undermines logistical planning for commodities.

Exacerbation of systemic and operational challenges

Climate change does not create challenges in a vacuum; rather, it acts as a threat multiplier, exacerbating pre-existing vulnerabilities within health and governance systems [39]. The common barriers identified, such as inadequate funding, weak multisectoral collaboration, and data limitations, are long-standing issues in African public health [1]. Extreme weather events, for instance, directly impact infrastructure, washing away roads and clinics, which disrupts access to care and the delivery of essential supplies like antimalarial drugs and insecticide-treated nets (ITNs). This physical disruption is compounded by socio-economic impacts, as climate-related shocks can deepen poverty and food insecurity, making populations more susceptible to disease [40].

A critical barrier highlighted by NMC/EPs is the siloed nature of operations. Health ministries often work in isolation from meteorological agencies and the ministries of environment and agriculture. This lack of integration prevents the effective use of climate data for forecasting and early warning systems, a strategy that has been identified as important for proactive disease control [41]. The call for urgent, collaborative action from Africa's frontlines reflects a growing consensus that tackling the intersection of climate and health requires a fundamental shift away from sector-specific responses toward integrated, multi-disciplinary strategies [4].

Strategic responses and way forward

Despite the formidable challenges, the strategic responses initiated by several countries offer a blueprint for adaptation. The integration of climate variables into malaria surveillance systems and the development of early warning models represent a significant step towards building climate-resilient health systems. Predictive modelling that incorporates climate change scenarios alongside vector control interventions shows that while climate change is projected to increase the malaria burden, the consistent application of interventions like IRS and ITNs can substantially mitigate this risk [42]. This reinforces the fact that strengthening and adapting existing control programmes is as important as developing new climate-specific tools.

However, these efforts are often localized and pilot-based, hampered by a lack of sustainable funding and technical capacity. To scale up these adaptive strategies, a paradigm shift is needed in both national health planning and international development assistance. National Adaptation Plans (NAPs) and health financing mechanisms must explicitly budget for the health impacts of climate change. Furthermore, strengthening governance and institutional capacity is paramount [43]. This includes investing in human resources capable of working across disciplines, from epidemiology to climatology, and creating formal platforms for multisectoral collaboration [44]. Barriers and suggested way forward for African malaria control programmes

Data and infrastructure

Fragmented data systems, limited meteorological infrastructure, and poor integration of climate and health data

• Strengthen disease surveillance system even in areas not previously exposed to malaria but becoming affected because of climate change.

• Develop or strengthen integrated climate-health surveillance systems (early warning systems) that combine meteorological, environmental, and epidemiological data.

• Expand and modernize meteorological networks with more weather stations/climate sensors, especially in rural and malaria-prone areas.

• Leverage satellite data and remote sensing to supplement ground-based observations.

• Lessons learned from past studies linking climate data with malaria should be considered when emulating such research in other countries to help identify tools for developing robust early warning systems for outbreaks.

• The operationalization of the Early Warning and Response System for climate-sensitive diseases (EWARS-csd) promoted by TDR is ongoing in many countries [48].

• Ethiopia’s EPIDEMIA early-warning system integrates climate and case data to forecast outbreaks in highland districts, enabling proactive interventions [49].

• In Mexico, the national public health system successfully moved an Early Warning and Response System (EWARS) for dengue from a research concept to full operational implementation. This success story offers a powerful blueprint for malaria control programs [50].

• A weather-based malaria prediction model using weekly time-series data on temperature and precipitation from Limpopo, South Africa, demonstrated good predictive accuracy up to 16 weeks in advance. The study highlighted the potential of using operational seasonal climate forecasts to drive Malaria Early Warning Systems [51].

Capacity and training

Lack of skilled professionals in data science, epidemiology, and climate modelling

• Invest in training programmes for entomologists, data analysts, data scientists, modellers, climate scientists, and public health workers.

• Establish regional centres of excellence for climate and health research to help build local expertise.

• Promote South-South cooperation to share knowledge and best practices across African countries.

• Explore opportunities with existing structures in Africa such as Clim-HEALTH Africa.

• Conduct implementation research to better document best adaptive practices.

• Conduct more case studies to assess the impacts of climate change and identify the projected expansion of malaria transmission periods in order to inform evidence-based prevention strategies.

• Regional Centre for Vector-borne diseases in West Africa have trained PhD-level entomologists and data scientists [52].

• CLIMADE initiative led by the Centre for Epidemic Response and Innovation (CERI) at Stellenbosch University, South Africa [53].

• Regional hubs like Africa CDC’s high-performing NPHIs and ARCH climate-health research centres have emerged as centres of excellence [54, 55].

• South–South cooperation through SADC’s E8 initiative and peer-learning exchanges have improved cross-border malaria control [56].

• Clim-HEALTH Africa continues to lead climate-informed early warning systems and capacity-building efforts [57].

• In Rwanda, a case study projected a shift in the risk of malaria transmission to the highlands in the western and northern regions. Additionally, it suggested that the peak season for malaria transmission in the highlands could potentially occur one month earlier due to a seasonal shift in precipitation [58].

• Hennessee and collaborators found that a 1 °C rise in 1-month lagged maximum temperatures corresponded to a 23% surge in malaria incidence rates during a malaria resurgence in Eastern Province ( Rwanda) from 2011 to 2016 [59].

Operational and logistical

Inadequate coordination among sectors, restricted entry to remote communities, and ineffective health delivery systems

• Establish or leverage a multisectoral collaboration for improved coordination among relevant sectors.

• Use mobile health (mHealth) technologies for real-time data collection and malaria case tracking.

• Strengthen supply chains for distributing ITNs and antimalarial drugs in a timely manner as soon as a risk is identified.

• Guangzhou’s compelling success story of multisectoral collaboration in malaria control and elimination [60].

• Ethiopia’s mHealth surveillance system improved real-time outbreak forecasting and response [61].

• Malakit, the self-diagnosis/treatment kit for malaria used by illegal gold miners in French Guiana, Suriname and Brazil is a valuable case study for replication in remote communities [62].

• Uganda has successfully leveraged technology and innovative data collection methods to enhance the timeliness and efficiency of their malaria commodity supply chains [63].

Behavioural and social factors

Cultural practices such as sleeping outdoors, low ITN usage, and limited awareness of climate-malaria links

• Conduct community-based education campaigns to promote ITNs use and climate-resilient health behaviours.

• Engage community leaders and health workers to tailor messages to cultural contexts.

• Incorporate behavioural change communication (BCC) into malaria control programmes.

• Exposure to BCC messages demonstrates a statistically significant effect on women's use of ITNs in Zambia [64].

• Engaging community leaders, religious figures, and local health workers as trusted messengers led to successful organization and implementation of anti-malaria activities in Haïti [65].

Need of financial arsenal

African health systems have long contended with systemic financial challenges, including chronic underfunding, a high degree of fragmentation, and significant reliance on external donor aid and out-of-pocket expenditures by citizens [66, 67]. This financial landscape is exacerbated by the United States' retreat from major global health initiatives and a global decrease in multilateral funding [68–70], resulting in an anticipated resurgence and significant increase in malaria morbidity and mortality, particularly affecting the most vulnerable populations [71].

Strengthening domestic resource mobilization is an imperative to build the expected climate-resilience systems. Taxation remains the primary source of health financing, but limited fiscal space necessitates innovative approaches. Emerging mechanisms include levies on mobile services, airline tickets, financial transactions, and “sin taxes” on tobacco and alcohol, with earmarked revenues for health and climate adaptation [72]. Expanding national health insurance schemes supports universal health coverage and reduces reliance on out-of-pocket payments [73]. Performance-based financing (PBF) can improve service quality and incentivize climate-resilient malaria interventions [74, 75].

International financing complements domestic efforts. Global climate funds, such as the Green Climate Fund, provide opportunities to strengthen health system resilience [76]. Multilateral initiatives like the Global Fund remain central for malaria control, while the Pandemic Fund enhances preparedness [77]. Development banks, including the World Bank and African Development Bank, offer loans and grants for climate-integrated health projects [78–81]. Public–private partnerships mobilize resources for research and innovation [82], and African-led platforms like African Vaccine Acquisition Trust (AVAT) demonstrate regional leadership in health financing [77, 83].

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The findings are based on the experiences and perspectives of a selection of NMC/EPs and may not be generalizable to all 54 African countries. However, the diversity of environmental settings, socioeconomic contexts, and malaria transmission patterns represented in this study suggests that the key themes identified are likely to be relevant across a wide range of African settings. The quality and availability of data likely vary between countries, which could influence the reported trends and challenges. Furthermore, this analysis did not quantitatively disentangle the effects of climate change from other confounding factors driving malaria trends, such as land-use change, population movement, malaria vector resistance to insecticides, and changes in intervention coverage [84]. Future research should employ robust analytical methods to attribute changes in malaria burden more precisely to climatic and non-climatic drivers.

Conclusion

The experiences of the six African countries provide a stark warning: climate change is actively undermining decades of progress against malaria. The shifting epidemiology of the disease, coupled with the amplification of systemic weaknesses, poses an unprecedented threat to public health across the continent. Yet, this study also highlights a path forward. The proactive and innovative strategic responses being pioneered by NMC/EPs, though currently limited in scale, demonstrate a clear recognition of the problem and a will to adapt. To translate these efforts into continent-wide resilience, a concerted and collaborative response is urgently needed. This requires sustained investment in capacity building of NMC/EPs, integrated surveillance systems, climate-resilient health infrastructure, and multi-sectoral partnerships. Failure to act decisively will not only jeopardize the goal of malaria elimination but will also compromise the broader health and well-being of millions of people in Africa's most vulnerable communities.
