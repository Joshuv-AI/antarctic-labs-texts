Investigating topological alterations in procedural memory network across neuropsychiatric disorders using rs-fMRI and graph theory
Authors: Mahdi Mohammadkhanloo, Hamid Sharini, Mitra Yousefpour, Mohammad Pooyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive network dysfunction represents a core pathophysiological feature across major neuropsychiatric disorders, including Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), bipolar disorder (BD), and schizophrenia (SZ). The procedural memory network (PMN), involving cortico-striatal-cerebellar circuits, is vital for skill learning and automatic cognition. However, its topological changes and link to cognitive impairments have not been studied across major neuropsychiatric disorders.MethodsThis study analyzed resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data from 40 individuals with ADHD, 49 with BD, 50 with SZ, and 50 healthy controls (HCs). PMN was defined using 34 regions of interest (ROIs) from Harvard-Oxford Atlas, with graph theory measures calculated for all regions. Significant network disruptions emerged, showing altered local efficiency (LE), average path length (APL), and degree (P < 0.05) across groups.ResultsKey findings show that in ADHD, increased APL in left cerebellar lobule VII indicates disrupted information flow and emotional processing, while decreased connectivity in the right claustrum may impair integration and working memory. In BD, reduced LE in right cerebellar lobule II is linked to attention and motor control deficits; increased APL in lobules I and VIII suggests disrupted network communication and emotional processing; and decreased connectivity in the right subthalamic nucleus and lobule VIII may contribute to mood swings and attention problems. In SZ, decreased LE in right putamen and left cerebellar lobule VIII relates to working memory and emotional processing deficits; reduced APL in right caudate and cerebellar lobule II implies more effort for regional communication; and increased connectivity in the caudate and right cerebellar lobules I and II likely reflects compensatory or pathological hyperactivity. Comparisons indicate SZ shows increased connectivity in the claustrum and cerebellar lobule I, unlike ADHD which shows decreases in these areas; SZ has lower network efficiency but higher caudate connectivity than BD, which has more cerebellar and subthalamic disruptions; and BD shows decreased connectivity in the claustrum and subthalamic nucleus compared to ADHD, which has more cerebellar and attention network changes.ConclusionThese findings suggest that the PMN, particularly its segregation and integration properties, plays a key role in explaining cognitive deficits in ADHD, BD, and SZ.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12868-025-00979-z.Keywords: Procedural memory, Cognitive dysfunction, Functional connectivity, Resting-state fMRI, Graph theory, Brain mapping

Introduction

Neurological and psychiatric disorders represent a major global health burden, affecting approximately 970 million people worldwide [1]. Among these, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), bipolar disorder (BD), and schizophrenia (SZ) are highly prevalent, with estimated population prevalences of 5%, 2.5%, and 0.5%, respectively—cumulatively impacting nearly 8% of individuals [1–3]. These disorders are characterized by significant cognitive impairments, including deficits in attention, executive function, and memory processing [4, 5]. Notably, long-term memory dysfunction—particularly in implicit (non-declarative) forms such as procedural memory—has been increasingly recognized as a core feature across ADHD, BD, and SZ [6, 7].

Procedural memory, a subtype of implicit long-term memory, supports the acquisition and execution of motor skills, habits, and automatic behaviors without conscious awareness [8]. It relies on a distributed network known as the procedural memory network (PMN), which includes the cerebellum, basal ganglia, and supplementary motor area (SMA) [9]. The cerebellum contributes to motor learning and coordination, with distinct lobules implicated in motor (I–V), cognitive (VI–VIII), and emotional regulation (IX–X) functions [10]. The basal ganglia—including the caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, substantia nigra, subthalamic nucleus, and claustrum—play critical roles in habit formation, action selection, and integration of cognitive and emotional signals [11–13]. The SMA, in turn, is involved in motor planning, sequence learning, and the integration of cognitive-motor processes [14, 15]. Together, these regions form a tightly interconnected system essential for both motor and higher-order cognitive functions.

Recent advances in resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) have enabled the in vivo investigation of intrinsic brain network dynamics, offering unique insights into the functional architecture of the PMN in health and disease [16]. Graph theoretical analysis of rs-fMRI data provides a powerful framework to quantify the topological organization of brain networks, capturing both local segregation (e.g., clustering coefficient, local efficiency) and global integration (e.g., global efficiency, path length) [17, 18, 20–22]. These metrics have revealed widespread disruptions in network topology in neuropsychiatric disorders [19], including altered connectivity within motor and cognitive networks in SZ [20], BD [21], and ADHD [22]. For instance, studies have reported reduced global efficiency and disrupted hub organization in the basal ganglia-cerebellar circuits in SZ [23], while ADHD has been associated with decreased local efficiency in motor-related regions [24]. Similarly, BD patients show aberrant connectivity in cortico-striatal pathways during rest [25].

Despite these advances, the topological integrity of the PMN—specifically its functional organization during rest—remains underexplored in patients with ADHD, BD, and SZ. Given that procedural memory operates largely unconsciously, examining the PMN via rs-fMRI may circumvent challenges related to task compliance in clinical populations, providing a more ecologically valid window into cognitive network dysfunction [26, 27]. Moreover, as the PMN interfaces with broader cognitive control and emotional regulation networks, its topological properties may serve as a proxy for system-level impairments in these disorders.

Therefore, this study aims to: (1) investigate topological alterations within the PMN during rest in individuals with ADHD, BD, and SZ compared to healthy controls (HCs), while also conducting direct inter-group comparisons (SZ vs. ADHD, SZ vs. BD, and ADHD vs. BD) using rs-fMRI and graph theory; and (2) evaluate whether resting-state PMN connectivity patterns correlate with disruptions in large-scale cognitive networks. By integrating cutting-edge neuroimaging methods with a well-defined network model, this work seeks to advance our understanding of the neural basis of cognitive dysfunction in major neuropsychiatric disorders.

Materials and methods

This study utilized de-identified, publicly available data from the Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics (CNP) dataset. The original data collection was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the UCLA Institutional Review Board (IRB#10-001416). Ethical approval for this secondary analysis was obtained from the Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (IR.KUMS.REC.1402.036).

Methodology

Figure 1 summarizes the pipeline for extracting and analyzing the PMN using rs-fMRI and graph theory. Preprocessing of fMRI data included slice-timing correction, realignment, distortion correction, co-registration, segmentation-based normalization (MNI space), spatial smoothing (6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel), and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). These steps were implemented in CONN toolbox, with order and parameters optimized to minimize motion artifacts and physiological noise, as such choices critically influence graph metrics [28]. The PMN was parcellated into 34 ROIs using the CONN toolbox, encompassing key structures (basal ganglia, cerebellum, supplementary motor area). Time series were extracted by averaging voxel-wise signals per ROI. Functional connectivity was computed via Pearson’s cross-correlation between ROI pairs, generating a 34 × 34 correlation matrix. Graph theory metrics—global/local efficiency, betweenness centrality, clustering coefficient, average path length (APL), degree, and cost—were derived from connectivity matrices using CONN. Groups comparisons were conducted in SPSS (v19.0) via ANOVA, covarying for [age, sex]. Significance was thresholded at p < 0.05 (FDR-corrected), with post hoc tests for disorder-specific effects (ADHD, BD, SZ).

Participants

This study utilized a publicly available dataset from the UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics (CNP), accessible through OpenNeuro (accession: ds000030; 10.18112/openneuro.ds000030.v1.0.0) [29]. The dataset includes neuroimaging and phenotypic data from 265 participants, comprising 130 healthy controls (HCs), 50 individuals with schizophrenia (SZ), 49 with bipolar disorder (BD), and 43 with adult attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Participants were aged 21 to 50 years (mean age: 33.23; median age: 31.0), and all met strict inclusion criteria: right-handedness, at least 8 years of formal education, primary language of English or Spanish, absence of contraindications to MRI (e.g., metal implants, claustrophobia, pregnancy), and no history of head injury with loss of consciousness or significant medical illness. Visual acuity was required to be at least 20/60, and urinalysis confirmed absence of recent drug use (cocaine, methamphetamine, morphine, THC, benzodiazepines). Diagnoses were established using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID-I) for Axis I disorders. For ADHD, the Adult ADHD Interview—a structured instrument derived from the KSADS-PL—was used to assess lifetime symptoms. Healthy controls were excluded if they met criteria for any lifetime psychiatric diagnosis, including SZ, BD, ADHD, major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders (e.g., PTSD, OCD, GAD), or substance use disorders (excluding caffeine and nicotine). Individuals with subthreshold ADHD (≥ 4 symptoms) or those who had received ADHD medication within the past 12 months were also excluded from the HC group. Patient groups were excluded if they met diagnostic criteria for any of the other primary disorders under study, although comorbid conditions were permitted and common (present in 81% of patients; see Table 2 in Poldrack et al., 2016). The final sample of 50 healthy controls used in this study was selected from the original 130 HCs based on data quality and completeness, not random sampling. Specifically, participants were excluded if they had missing or poor-quality T1-weighted scans or failed preprocessing steps (e.g., due to excessive motion or artifacts). This selection ensured high-quality, artifact-free neuroimaging data suitable for advanced connectivity analyses. All preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep v0.4.4, which includes automated quality control metrics (e.g., framewise displacement, DVARS, tSNR) and visual inspection reports for every participant. Mean framewise displacement (FD) was calculated and included as a covariate in all group comparisons to control for motion-related confounds. No significant differences in mean FD were observed across diagnostic groups (p > 0.05), and sensitivity analyses confirmed that results remained robust after motion correction. Image acquisition

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and structural MRI (sMRI) data were obtained from the UCLA CNP dataset (OpenNeuro: 10.18112/openneuro.ds000030.v1.0.0) [29]. All scans were acquired using a 3 Tesla (3T) Siemens Trio scanner. For rs-fMRI, a T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence was performed during eyes-open fixation (duration: 304 s; 152 volumes, TR = 2 s). Imaging parameters included: 34 axial slices (4 mm thickness), FOV = 192 mm, matrix size = 64 × 64, TE = 30 ms, and flip angle = 90°. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence (1 mm isotropic voxels, 176 sagittal slices, TR = 1.9 s, TE = 2.26 ms, FOV = 250 mm, matrix = 256 × 256) for spatial normalization and tissue segmentation.

Image preprocessing

As highlighted in recent literature, global signal regression (GSR) can introduce spurious correlations and distort functional connectivity patterns, particularly in rs-fMRI studies [30–32]. To mitigate these potential issues, we deliberately excluded GSR from our analysis. For preprocessing both structural MRI (sMRI) and functional MRI (fMRI) data, we utilized the CONN v21.a [33] functional connectivity toolbox, which is built on SPM12. To preprocess the structural MRI (sMRI) data, the following steps were carried out: First, the image center was translated to the origin coordinates (0, 0, 0). Next, unified segmentation and normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space were applied. The unified segmentation approach combined segmentation, registration, and MNI normalization into a single, unified model, ensuring a streamlined and efficient preprocessing workflow [34]. The pre-processing of functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) data is a crucial step to ensure the accuracy, reliability, and interpretability of subsequent analyses. The following pre-processing steps were applied to the raw fMRI data to address various sources of noise, artifacts, and variability, while standardizing the data for group-level comparisons: (1) Slice Timing Correction: Temporal discrepancies in slice acquisition were corrected by aligning all slices to a common reference time point, ensuring consistent temporal dynamics across the brain volume [35]. (2) Motion Correction: Head motion artifacts were minimized by realigning all functional volumes to a reference volume, and motion parameters were extracted for later use as nuisance regressors [36]. In addition to motion correction, we implemented a stringent quality control protocol to identify and exclude participants with excessive head motion. Participants were excluded from the analysis if their data exhibited any of the following: (a) a maximum translational displacement greater than 2 mm in any direction (x, y, or z), (b) a maximum rotational displacement greater than 2 degrees, or (c) a mean Framewise Displacement (FD) value of 0.2 mm or greater. The mean FD was calculated using the formula from Power et al. [56]. This rigorous exclusion criterion was applied to ensure that our results were not confounded by motion-related artifacts. 3) Distortion Correction: Geometric distortions caused by magnetic field inhomogeneities were corrected using field maps or reverse phase-encoded scans, ensuring accurate spatial representation of the functional data [37]. 4) Co-Registration: Functional images were aligned with high-resolution structural (T1-weighted) images to ensure accurate anatomical localization of functional activity. 5) Segmentation: Structural images were segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid to facilitate normalization and create tissue-specific masks. 6) Normalization: To enable group-level comparisons, the functional and structural images were normalized to a standard stereotactic space, typically the MNI template. This step involves warping the individual brain images to match the template, ensuring that corresponding brain regions are aligned across subjects. Normalization also facilitates the comparison of results across different studies and datasets [38]. 7) Smoothing: Spatial smoothing was applied to the functional data using a Gaussian kernel with a 6 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM). The choice of 6 mm FWHM was based on established practices in neuroimaging, which aim to balance the trade-off between improving the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and preserving spatial resolution. This kernel size is commonly used to ensure that the data conforms to the assumptions of Gaussian random field theory, which underpins statistical inference in group-level analyses, while also accounting for inter-subject variability in brain anatomy [39]. 8) Band-Pass Filtering: To isolate the frequency range of interest and reduce the influence of low-frequency drifts and high-frequency noise, a band-pass filter (0.008–0.09 Hz) was applied to the time series data. The frequency range of 0.008–0.09 Hz was selected based on the characteristic frequency profile of the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal, which is typically dominated by slow oscillations within this range. This range effectively retains the frequencies associated with neural activity while filtering out unwanted physiological noise, such as respiratory (approximately 0.1–0.5 Hz) and cardiac (approximately 0.6–1.2 Hz) fluctuations, as well as scanner-related artifacts and very low-frequency drifts that are unlikely to reflect neural processes [40]. 9) Nuisance Regression: Following the above steps, we regressed out several nuisance variables from the fMRI time series to further mitigate noise and artifacts. These included the six head motion parameters (three translations and three rotations) and their first-order derivatives, as well as the principal components from the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signals, using the aCompCor method. This step ensures that the final functional connectivity estimates are less influenced by these physiological and motion-related sources of variance.

Procedural memory network

Procedural memory, a crucial component of the memory system, facilitates the retrieval and execution of motor and executive skills essential for various tasks. Operating primarily at an unconscious level, it guides activities seamlessly. When needed, procedural memories are automatically retrieved and employed in executing complex procedures involving motor and cognitive functions [41]. This network comprises key anatomical brain structures: the basal ganglia, cerebellum, and SMA [42]. The basal ganglia play a vital role in selecting and initiating motor actions, as well as in learning and refining motor and cognitive skills through practice [43]. The cerebellum contributes to motor coordination, precision, and timing, aiding in fine-tuning movements and error correction during skill acquisition [44]. However, recent research has recognized that these subcortical structures also contribute to a variety of functions beyond motor control, including affective processing. Their roles have expanded to encompass diverse limbic and cognitive processes, such as emotion recognition, reward- and error-based learning, language, decision-making, working memory, and spatial attention [45]. The supplementary motor area (SMA) is primarily responsible for planning and coordinating complex movements, particularly sequences or well-learned motor patterns. It plays a crucial role in initiating and executing motor programs [46]. Beyond its motor functions, the SMA has also been implicated in a variety of cognitive processes. Research has demonstrated its involvement in working memory, including auditory, visual, spatial, and object working memory, as well as in arithmetic operations. These findings highlight the SMA’s broader functional contributions, extending beyond motor control to encompass higher-order cognitive tasks [47]. In this study, we defined a PMN comprising 34 ROIs using the CONN toolbox. These ROIs were distributed across key brain regions, including the basal ganglia (12 ROIs), the cerebellum (encompassing lobules I–X; 20 ROIs), and the supplementary motor area (SMA; 2 ROIs) [42]. A 3D visualization of all ROIs within the PMN is presented in Fig. 2, with their corresponding MNI coordinates detailed in Table 1. The ROIs were identified using MNI coordinates derived from the default atlas in the CONN toolbox [33] and were further validated based on findings from the study by Song et al. [48]. Following the ROI definition, the time series for each region was extracted. Subsequently, a cross-correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the pairwise relationships between the time series of the procedural memory regions, thereby constructing the functional connectivity network, represented as a correlation matrix.

Graph theory analysis

Graph theory is a mathematical framework used to represent and analyze complex networks. In neuroscience, it serves as a powerful tool for modeling the brain as an intricate system of interconnected regions [49]. By quantifying network topology, graph theory provides valuable insights into the organizational principles and structural properties of brain networks [50]. This framework offers a comprehensive set of metrics to characterize brain network topology, which are widely used in cognitive science to understand the functional and structural organization of the brain [17]. These metrics are particularly useful for assessing how efficiently information is processed and transmitted across neural networks, as well as for identifying critical nodes and pathways that contribute to overall brain function [51]. Commonly used metrics in neuroscience for examining the topological organization of brain networks include global efficiency, LE, degree, APL, clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality, and cost [52]. Together, these metrics provide a robust toolkit for analyzing the complex architecture of brain networks and their implications for cognitive processes [53]. These metrics elucidate the dual principles of integration and segregation within brain networks, reflecting how information is distributed and processed across distinct regions [54]. LE quantifies the efficiency of information processing within local neighborhoods of the network. It reflects the ability of a node to effectively exchange information with its immediate neighbors [55]. Reduced LE indicates impaired information processing within specific brain regions [34]. Global efficiency measures the overall efficiency of information transfer across the entire network. It is defined as the average inverse shortest path length between all pairs of nodes, reflecting how efficiently information is integrated across distributed brain regions. Lower global efficiency suggests compromised integration and communication across the network [56]. Degree represents the number of connections (edges) a node has with other nodes. It provides information about the centrality of a node within the network. Altered degree distributions may indicate aberrant connectivity patterns, such as hub disruption or network fragmentation [57]. APL represents the average shortest path length between all pairs of nodes in the network. It reflects the overall efficiency of global information transfer. Increased APL suggests impaired communication between distant brain regions, potentially indicating reduced integration within the network [58]. Clustering coefficient quantifies the tendency of nodes to form tightly interconnected groups or clusters. It reflects the level of segregation within the network, indicating how well nodes are organized into functional modules. A higher clustering coefficient suggests robust local connectivity, while a lower value may indicate disrupted modular organization [59]. Betweenness centrality measures the extent to which a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes in the network. It identifies critical hubs that facilitate efficient communication between different regions. Nodes with high betweenness centrality play a pivotal role in maintaining network integration, and their disruption can significantly impact overall network function [60]. Cost represents the physical or metabolic expense associated with maintaining network connections. It is often quantified as the ratio of existing connections to the total possible connections in the network. Cost-efficiency trade-offs are critical in understanding how the brain balances resource expenditure with optimal network performance [61]. In this study, we employed graph-theoretic metrics to examine the topological organization of the procedural memory network (PMN) using the CONN toolbox (version 21a) [33]. Importantly, we analyzed weighted (non-binarized) connectivity matrices to preserve the continuous spectrum of functional connectivity strengths, avoiding the information loss associated with binarization. Negative correlations were retained in our analysis as they may reflect meaningful inhibitory interactions between networks, consistent with CONN’s standard pipeline and current methodological recommendations [33]. We computed several key metrics that capture complementary aspects of network organization: global efficiency, LE, degree, APL, clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality, and cost. These metrics were calculated directly from the weighted adjacency matrices without thresholding, circumventing the well-known limitations of sparsity-based approaches. All analyses used connectivity matrices derived from denoised BOLD time series, with global mean scaling applied to control for inter-subject variability. This comprehensive approach allowed us to characterize both local and global network properties while maintaining biological plausibility and methodological rigor. The results of these analyses, which reveal important alterations in PMN topology across the studied disorders, are presented in Sect. “Results”.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v19.0 [75]. Prior to analysis, the normality of data distribution was confirmed, with values concentrated around the mean [62]. A one-way ANOVA was performed to compare graph theory measures—including local efficiency (LE), global efficiency, clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality, average path length (APL), degree, and cost—between patient groups (ADHD, BD, SZ) and HCs. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s HSD test, with significance set at p < 0.05, and effect sizes (ƞ²) were calculated to assess the magnitude of differences [63]. To minimize Type I error risk in multiple comparisons, we applied Bonferroni correction, adjusting thresholds to p < 0.0019 for node-level analyses (26 PMN ROIs) and p < 0.0038 for edge-level connectivity. We also performed FDR correction (q < 0.05), which corroborated Bonferroni-corrected results, ensuring robustness. Given our hypothesis-driven focus on the Procedural Memory Network (PMN), Bonferroni’s conservative approach was deemed appropriate, aligning with established fMRI pipelines (e.g., CONN toolbox).

Results

The results revealed that patients with ADHD, BD, and SZ exhibited significant alterations in several graph theory measures, including LE, APL, and degree, within specific regions of the PMN (cerebellum and basal ganglia) compared to HCs. To ensure rigorous control of demographic variables, we conducted systematic analyses of potential confounders. Age distributions showed excellent balance across all groups (one-way ANOVA, p > 0.05). However, χ² tests identified significant gender disparities, with the schizophrenia group exhibiting both: (1) higher male representation (n = 38 male vs. n = 21–25 in other groups, p = 0.04) and (n = 12 female vs. n = 19–25 in other groups, p = 0.03). We implemented safeguards to address this: (a) gender inclusion as a covariate in all primary analyses, (b) careful interpretation of schizophrenia findings within this demographic context. Complete demographic data appear in Table 2, and all reported effects remained significant after these adjustments. The statistical analysis of the PMN regions that showed significant differences between patients and the healthy control group in terms of LE, APL, and degree is detailed in Table 3. Discussion

To examine cognitive dysfunctions including attention, emotion, and memory in patients with ADHD, BD, and SZ, we analyzed TAPMN using rs-fMRI and graph theory analysis, independent of any specific task. Given the robust insights provided by graph theory into the brain’s topological organization [18], we employed highly reproducible graph theory measures in neuroscience, including global efficiency, LE, betweenness centrality, clustering coefficient, APL, degree, and cost, to evaluate the segregation and integration properties of the PMN. Among these measures, three—LE, APL, and degree—exhibited significant differences in the cerebellum and basal ganglia when comparing the studied patient groups (ADHD, BD, and SZ) to HCs. In the following, we will discuss these potential changes and their effect on cognition in detail for each studied disorder.

ADHD

Our investigation of the PMN in ADHD patients revealed increased APL in the left cerebellar lobule VII compared to HCs. This finding suggests potential disruptions in information flow within this region and its connections with other network components [64]. Cerebellar lobules VI–VIII are known to be involved in emotional processing [65, 66], and disrupted information processing within lobule VII, a region implicated in these cognitive functions, may contribute to the observed cognitive deficits in ADHD patients, such as impaired emotional regulation and related behavioral challenges. These alterations highlight the potential role of cerebellar dysfunction in the pathophysiology of ADHD, particularly in the context of procedural memory and emotional processing networks. Furthermore, our analysis indicated a decreased degree in the right claustrum, suggesting a potential reduction in communication with other PMN regions. The claustrum, a complex and enigmatic brain structure, has emerged as a crucial hub for integrating information from different brain regions [67]. This reduction in connectivity could underlie impairments in information integration, contributing to the observed cognitive deficits in ADHD patients, such as difficulties focusing, filtering distractions, and holding information readily available for tasks, ultimately impacting working memory and executive function [68, 69]. These results are consistent with previous research indicating reduced functional connectivity in the cerebellar lobule VII and claustrum, associated with cognitive deficits in ADHD [70].

BD

Our investigation of the PMN in BD patients revealed decreased LE in the right cerebellar lobule II. This finding suggests impaired information processing within this region, which is known to play a critical role not only in motor coordination and procedural learning but also in cognitive functions, particularly attention [71]. Cerebellar lobules I–V, which include lobule II, are associated with learning, movement control, and attentional processes [72]. The observed disruption in lobule II may therefore contribute to both the motor impairments and attentional deficits frequently reported in BD patients [73]. Given the role of lobule II in supporting attention, its dysfunction could exacerbate difficulties in maintaining focus and executing routine tasks, which are common challenges in BD. These findings highlight the potential role of cerebellar dysfunction, particularly in lobule II, in the cognitive and motor deficits observed in BD [74]. Conversely, increased APL in the left cerebellar lobules I and right cerebellar lobule VIII suggests a need for greater effort to facilitate communication across these regions, which are implicated in working memory, executive function, and emotional processing [75]. Cerebellar lobules I–V, including lobule I, are associated with learning, movement control, and attention [72], while lobules VI–VIII, including lobule VIII, are involved in emotional processing [65, 66]. The observed increase in APL in these regions may reflect disrupted connectivity, potentially contributing to the difficulties with concentration, planning, decision-making, and emotional regulation frequently reported in BD patients [76]. These connectivity issues in the cerebellum could underlie the integration deficits between cognitive and emotional processes, further exacerbating the executive and affective symptoms characteristic of BD [77]. Furthermore, reduced degree centrality in the right subthalamic nucleus and right cerebellar lobule VIII points towards potential communication deficits between these regions [18]. The subthalamic nucleus, which is primarily involved in attention and emotional regulation [78], and the cerebellum, particularly lobule VIII, which is increasingly recognized for its role in emotional processing [65, 66], are both critical for integrating cognitive and affective functions. Disruptions in the connectivity between these regions could impair the coordination of attention, emotional regulation, and motor control, potentially contributing to the characteristic mood swings, attentional deficits, and motor impairments observed in BD. These findings highlight the importance of functional integration between the subthalamic nucleus and the cerebellum in maintaining emotional stability and cognitive-motor coordination [79].

SZ

Our investigation of the PMN in SZ patients revealed decreased LE in the right putamen and left cerebellar lobule VIII, indicating impaired information processing within these critical brain regions. The putamen, a key component of the basal ganglia, is implicated in motor control, procedural learning, and working memory [80, 81], while cerebellar lobule VIII is known to contribute to emotional processing [65, 66]. These findings suggest disrupted neural connectivity within the PMN, which is fundamental for cognition in SZ. Our results align with previous research demonstrating reduced functional connectivity in the putamen and cerebellar lobule VIII in SZ patients [82]. Additionally, our findings are consistent with studies linking cerebellar abnormalities to cognitive deficits in SZ, including impaired working memory and reduced emotional processing [83].

Furthermore, reduced APL in the right caudate nucleus and right cerebellar lobule II might reflect a need for increased effort in communication between these regions. Beyond its established involvement in motor execution planning, the caudate nucleus contributes to cognitive functions such as learning and memory, as well as affective processes including reward, motivation, emotion, and social interactions [84]. The functional implications of elevated degree in the right caudate nucleus and right cerebellar lobules I and II may be related to impairments in memory, emotion, and attention, respectively. These findings could represent compensatory mechanisms or contribute to other symptoms observed in SZ [85].

SZ vs. ADHD

The present findings elucidate distinctive alterations in brain network organization characterizing schizophrenia (SZ) compared to attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), emphasizing disorder-specific neural dysfunctions. Notably, SZ patients exhibited pronounced hyperconnectivity in key subcortical hubs, including the right claustrum and cerebellar lobule I, as evidenced by significantly elevated degree centrality metrics relative to ADHD counterparts. This enhanced nodal importance suggests aberrant integration within these regions, potentially reflecting compensatory mechanisms or pathological over-engagement in SZ (p = 0.007 and p < 0.001, respectively). The claustrum, recognized for its role in multisensory integration and interregional connectivity, alongside the cerebellum’s involvement in motor coordination and cognitive processes, may constitute pivotal nodes whose dysregulated connectivity contributes to SZ symptomatology.

Importantly, deficits in global network efficiency were revealed by APL in the right cerebellar lobule I and right caudate nucleus among SZ patients. Although a shorter APL typically indicates more efficient communication, in this pathological context, it likely reflects disrupted large-scale integration and network reorganization detrimental to optimal information flow. This interpretation aligns with prior literature demonstrating that altered APL can signify pathological rewiring or network fragmentation in psychiatric disorders [86].Complementing these global disruptions, local processing impairments were observed via decreased LE in the left cerebellar lobule I and right subthalamic nucleus. Reduced LE points to compromised regional information processing and diminished resilience of local networks, possibly underlying specific cognitive and motor deficits in SZ [87]. The subthalamic nucleus, part of the basal ganglia circuitry implicated in motor control and executive functions, when dysfunctional, may exacerbate SZ-related impairments across cognitive and affective domains.

The striatum also presented complex connectivity alterations, with the right caudate displaying simultaneous reduced APL and increased degree centrality. This pattern suggests a reorganization marked by denser local connectivity but less optimal global integration, reflecting striatal dysregulation that is consistent with models positing basal ganglia abnormalities as central to SZ pathology [88]. Such disruptions in striatal-cerebellar circuits are hypothesized to underlie the hallmark cognitive inflexibility, working memory deficits, and motor abnormalities frequently observed in SZ patients.

Overall, these results delineate a schizophrenia-specific network pathology typified by hyperconnectivity in cerebellar and subcortical hubs alongside impaired global and local network efficiencies. The identified disturbances particularly implicate striatal-cerebellar circuits as critical dysfunction loci, potentially contributing to the broad spectrum of cognitive and motor symptoms characteristic of SZ. Such mechanistic insights enhance understanding of SZ neuropathophysiology and may guide targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring network integration.

SZ vs. BD

The present findings reveal distinctive network dysfunction patterns in SZ compared to BD, highlighting both shared and disorder-specific neural disruptions that elucidate their divergent pathophysiologies. Notably, cerebellar network abnormalities in SZ appear characterized by impaired global integration alongside regional hyperconnectivity. The significant reduction in APL within the right cerebellar lobules I and II in SZ patients compared to BD suggests less efficient large-scale information transfer, indicative of disrupted cerebellar communication pathways and suboptimal network organization. This inefficiency in cerebellar integration aligns with prior literature emphasizing the cerebellum’s role not only in motor coordination but also in cognitive and affective regulation, domains profoundly affected in SZ [89].

Paradoxically, despite impaired global connectivity, an increased degree centrality in the same right cerebellar lobules points to hyperconnectivity and heightened hub status, which may reflect abnormal compensatory mechanisms or pathological over-engagement of these nodes within the SZ brain network. Such hyperconnectivity contrasts with the more distributed or less centralized network topology observed in BD, suggesting disorder-specific network reorganization. This phenomenon of localized hyperconnectivity amid broader network inefficiency has been documented in SZ, reflecting dysfunction in functional integration and segregation [90].

Further, striatal dysregulation is evidenced by bilateral caudate hyperconnectivity in SZ, demonstrating elevated degree centrality in both right and left caudate nuclei compared to BD. The caudate’s involvement in cognitive, motor, and emotional processes suggests that such dysregulation may contribute to the hallmark clinical symptoms of SZ, including cognitive impairments and affective disturbances [91, 92]. The bilateral nature of this striatal hyperconnectivity contrasts with BD and supports a model of widespread basal ganglia network dysfunction specific to SZ pathology.

Additionally, laterality effects in cerebellar dysfunction emerge, with elevated degree centrality in the left cerebellar lobule II indicating asymmetric but extensive cerebellar abnormalities. This lateralized pattern may correlate with functional specialization within the cerebellum and its differential involvement in SZ symptomatology [93].

Collectively, these findings underscore that SZ patients exhibit more inefficient and disconnected functional brain networks relative to BD patients, who demonstrate less centralized and less integrated network architectures. Such distinctions in neural network topology highlight distinct underlying mechanisms, reflecting varying patterns of brain connectivity disruption. Understanding these disorder-specific connectivity profiles is crucial for elucidating the divergent clinical presentations and cognitive deficits characteristic of SZ and BD, and may inform targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring network integrity (Table 5).

ADHD vs. BD

The present findings elucidate distinct neural connectivity profiles between patients with ADHD and those with BD, highlighting disorder-specific neural signatures that have significant implications for understanding the neurobiological underpinnings of these conditions. Specifically, the observed reductions in degree centrality within the right claustrum and right subthalamic nucleus in BD patients, relative to ADHD, suggest marked impairments in subcortical integration processes unique to BD. The claustrum, widely regarded as a crucial hub for multisensory integration and inter-regional communication, and the subthalamic nucleus, integral to motor and emotional regulation, both show diminished connectivity that may contribute to the characteristic mood dysregulation and cognitive control deficits in BD [94, 95]. In contrast, ADHD patients, while exhibiting different connectivity impairments, do not show the same degree of subcortical disruption, implicating divergent neuropathological mechanisms between the disorders.

Moreover, the reduction in degree centrality in cerebellar lobules IV&V and VIII in BD patients further underscores cerebellar involvement in the disorder, corroborating a growing body of literature that implicates cerebellar dysfunction in cognitive and affective disturbances characteristic of BD [96, 97]. The cerebellum’s emerging role beyond motor coordination—specifically in cognitive and emotional processing—is increasingly recognized, and our results are consistent with this conceptual expansion, indicating that disrupted cerebellar hub function may exacerbate BD symptomatology.

Interestingly, motor network alterations evidenced by increased local efficiency in the left SMA in BD may represent compensatory neural reorganization aimed at mitigating motor and executive function deficits. Such compensatory increases in connectivity have previously been observed in neuropsychiatric populations as a means to maintain functional output despite underlying pathology [98]. This pattern of altered motor network connectivity distinguishes BD from ADHD and implies that different network-level adaptations occur in response to disorder-specific neural disruptions.

Collectively, these findings affirm that ADHD and BD exhibit distinct disruptions across subcortical, cerebellar, and motor networks, supporting the hypothesis that they are underpinned by unique neurobiological substrates. This differentiation extends prior research indicating that ADHD primarily involves connectivity deficits in executive and attentional networks, whereas BD is characterized by abnormalities in affective and integrative circuits [27, 99]. The clear delineation of connectivity profiles furnishes critical insights for refining diagnostic criteria and tailoring intervention strategies to target disorder-specific neural circuit dysfunctions effectively (see Table 6 for detailed connectivity metrics).

Limitations

This study provides novel insights into PMN dysfunction across ADHD, BD, and SZ, but several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our graph theory approach revealed distinct topological alterations, the absence of standardized cognitive assessments (e.g., procedural memory tasks) limits direct clinical interpretation of these network-level findings. Second, the dataset’s inherent limitation of including fewer female schizophrenia patients (n = 12) compared to other groups (ADHD: n = 19; BD: n = 21; HCs: n = 25) and higher male schizophrenia patients (n = 38) compared to other groups (ADHD: n = 21; BD: n = 28; HCs: n = 25) may affect the precision of SZ-specific findings. This observed gender disparity may particularly influence the interpretation of network alterations, as schizophrenia manifests differently by gender in both symptom profiles and cognitive trajectories. While we adjusted for gender in all analyses, this imbalance could affect the generalizability of PMN dysfunction patterns to female patients, who typically show later onset and potentially distinct network pathophysiology. Third, the moderate sample sizes overall, though adequate for initial exploration, may constrain generalizability to broader populations or clinical subtypes. Fourth, our cross-sectional design cannot determine whether observed PMN abnormalities reflect trait-like vulnerabilities or state-dependent changes. Finally, our focus on static connectivity metrics, though methodologically rigorous, precludes examination of dynamic network reorganization during actual cognitive performance.

Future directions

To advance this line of research, we propose several key directions. First, future studies should incorporate standardized cognitive assessments (e.g., MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery, WAIS working memory indices) alongside neuroimaging to directly link procedural memory network alterations with specific cognitive deficits, with particular emphasis on gender-sensitive cognitive measures (e.g., verbal learning vs. visuospatial tasks) that account for known sex differences in procedural memory performance. Second, integrated behavioral-neuroimaging paradigms could combine resting-state fMRI with procedural memory tasks (e.g., serial reaction time tasks) to establish more robust brain-behavior relationships. Third, longitudinal designs tracking both network changes and cognitive performance across illness stages would help distinguish state versus trait characteristics of PMN dysfunction. Fourth, transdiagnostic approaches using dimensional cognitive measures may better disentangle disorder-specific effects from shared neurocognitive deficits. Finally, advanced computational modeling techniques (e.g., dynamic causal modeling) could characterize how temporal network dynamics relate to real-time cognitive performance. Future studies should explicitly examine gender-specific PMN organization in larger, balanced cohorts to determine whether observed network differences reflect true disorder effects or sampling biases. These methodological advancements would significantly enhance the clinical interpretability of network neuroscience findings while maintaining rigorous graph theory applications, ultimately bridging the current gap between observed network alterations and their functional cognitive consequences.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate distinct patterns of network dysfunction in each disorder, characterized by alterations in LE (a measure of local network integration), APL (a measure of global network integration), and degree (number of connections) within specific brain regions associated with the PMN. These disruptions within the PMN correlate with known cognitive deficits in these psychiatric conditions, suggesting a potential neural underpinning for these impairments. Our results highlight the cerebellum as a key region implicated in the cognitive dysfunction of all three disorders, emphasizing its role beyond motor control. Additionally, the claustrum, putamen, and subthalamic nucleus emerged as critical nodes within the PMN, with connectivity abnormalities contributing to cognitive impairments in these patient populations.
