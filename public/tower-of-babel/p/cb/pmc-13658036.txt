Psychedelic-induced hypomania and mania: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mickael Eskinazi, Rayan Nasserdine, Romane M. Cusin, Pantelis Baniotoupoulos, Luigi F. Saccaro, Marco De Pieri, Tamara Corino, Federico Seragnoli, Jean-Francois Briefer, Tatiana Aboulafia Brakha, Hélène Richard-Lepouriel, Louise Penzenstadler, Kerem Böge, Matthias Kirchner, Daniele Zullino, Mikkel Højlund, Jacopo Sapienza, Marta Bosia, Ana Catalan, Eduard Vieta, Marco Solmi, Michel Sabé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Serotonergic psychedelics are increasingly investigated as treatments for affective disorders. Concerns persist regarding their potential to induce hypomania or mania, particularly in individuals with bipolar spectrum vulnerability. Whether these substances precipitate transient mood switches or contribute to persistent bipolar illness or diagnostic transition remains unclear. We conducted a systematic review of human studies examining manic or hypomanic symptoms following exposure to serotonergic psychedelics (psilocybin, LSD, mescaline, DMT/ayahuasca) or MDMA (CRD420251160656). Databases and trial registries were searched through January 26, 2026. Eligible designs included randomized and non-randomized clinical studies, registry-based cohorts, cross-sectional surveys, and longitudinal observational studies. Outcomes included dysphoria/euphoria, manic or hypomanic symptoms and transition to bipolar disorder. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I, ROB2 or NIH tools. Twenty-three studies met inclusion criteria, four contributing to meta-analysis. Rates of psychedelic-associated dysphoria/euphoria, hypomania or mania ranged from 5.8% in controlled trials of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy for major depressive disorders to 30% in naturalistic studies of individuals with bipolar disorder. When present, manic symptoms were typically acute and self-limited. Observational studies identified higher risks among individuals with bipolar I disorder, familial vulnerability, polysubstance use, and unsupervised or illegal use. Registry-based cohorts examining diagnostic transitions showed a prevalence of subsequent transition to bipolar disorder of 4% (95% CI 2–8%; N = 7478; I² = 32.1%), with little evidence for a hallucinogen-specific signal. Overall, serotonergic psychedelics appear to pose a low but clinically meaningful relative risk of transient mood-related symptoms in susceptible individuals while remaining relatively safe in controlled clinical settings. Long-term outcomes and repeated exposure remain insufficiently studied, underscoring the need for rigorous longitudinal research.

Introduction

Over the past two decades a resurgence of clinical interest in serotonergic psychedelics including psilocybin, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), dimethyltryptamine (DMT)/ayahuasca, and mescaline as well as MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine), has prompted renewed investigation into their therapeutic potential for mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders [1]. Psilocybin and LSD has shown promising antidepressant effects in treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder, with several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrating rapid and durable symptom relief [2]. These developments, alongside growing public and legislative support [3], have accelerated the momentum toward regulatory approval and expanded clinical access.

However, while enthusiasm for psychedelic therapies has surged, concerns about adverse psychiatric reactions—especially psychotic exacerbation [4], and manic or hypomanic switches in individuals with underlying bipolar spectrum vulnerabilities—remain under-examined and poorly quantified [5]. Mania and hypomania are potential consequences of pharmacologically induced serotonergic activation, and serotonergic antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and monoamine oxidase inhibitors are well-known precipitants of treatment-emergent affective switches in bipolar disorder [6], especially in the context of antidepressant monotherapy [7]. Given that classic psychedelics act primarily as 5-HT2A receptor agonists, and with indirect effects on dopaminergic, and glutamatergic transmission, it is plausible that they may carry similar or even greater risks of inducing manic or hypomanic symptoms or episodes, particularly in susceptible individuals. Indeed, clinical and survey-based evidence suggests that psychedelics may precipitate new or worsened manic or hypomanic symptoms in some individuals with bipolar disorder or bipolar-spectrum vulnerability, although the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, raising questions about unidentified risk modifiers [8, 9]. Furthermore, retrospective chart reviews and registry-based data suggest that substance-induced manic episodes may evolve into primary bipolar diagnoses in a substantial subset of patients [10]. The phenomenology of psychedelic-induced mood symptoms is complex, ranging from transient symptoms (trip-related euphoria, persistent psychedelic effects) to psychedelic-induced hypomanic/manic episodes (or mood switch, or mixed states), that could ultimately lead to a transition to a bipolar disorder (Supplementary Table 1). These symptoms and episodes must also be distinguished in patients with an actual diagnosis and those with/or without personal of family history.

Modern clinical trials of psychedelics have generally excluded individuals with bipolar-spectrum disorders, particularly bipolar I disorder, as well as those with histories of mania or psychosis or a first-degree family history of bipolar disorder, although a few recent early-phase studies have begun to include carefully selected individuals with bipolar II disorder. While this approach has contributed to reassuring safety data in unipolar depression and anxiety populations, it has created a significant gap in our understanding of the risks posed to those with bipolar spectrum vulnerabilities. Consequently, existing RCTs cannot be used to infer the safety profile of psychedelics in patients with known or latent bipolar disorders. In contrast, large-scale online surveys—while subject to self-selection and recall biases—suggest that new or increased manic symptoms may be among the more frequently reported adverse effects in real-world settings. One international survey of individuals with bipolar disorder who had used psilocybin reported that approximately 14–32% experienced new or intensified manic symptoms the week following use [10]. These findings echo concerns voiced by clinicians that psychedelic substances may unmask or trigger manic episodes in a subset of users.

Given the increasing use of psychedelics outside clinical research—often in unregulated, unsupervised, or recreational contexts [11]—better characterization of bipolar-relevant adverse outcomes, particularly treatment-emergent dysphoria/euphoria, mania, and hypomania, is needed [9]. Clinical guidance remains limited [12, 13], and decision-making around the inclusion of bipolar patients in therapeutic protocols is often based on expert opinion or indirect evidence [14]. Current consensus favors avoiding psychedelic administration in individuals with bipolar I disorder or clinical high risk for psychosis, and exercising caution in those with chronic psychosis, bipolar II disorder, or a positive family history [15, 16].

Despite this caution, a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous synthesis of literature on psychedelic-associated dysphoria/euphoria, mania and hypomania is lacking. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review of retrospective, and prospective studies to evaluate the prevalence, temporal characteristics, and risk factors associated with psychedelic- (or MDMA)-induced manic and hypomanic symptoms. We aimed to distinguish short-term versus persistent outcomes, explore potential moderating variables such as psychiatric comorbidities or family history, and assess the strength and quality of evidence across study types. MDMA was included as a related but pharmacologically distinct compound because it is often discussed within the broader psychedelic treatment literature. By consolidating current knowledge, we seek to inform clinical risk assessment and guide future research on the safe application of psychedelic treatments in mood disorder populations.

Methods

This systematic review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guideline (CRD420251160656).

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic search in PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and PsycARTICLES from database inception to January 26, 2026, without restrictions on publication date or language. A comprehensive search terms combining (bipolar OR dysphoria OR euphoria OR hypomania OR manic) with psychedelics (psilocybin, LSD, ayahuasca, DMT, mescaline, MDMA) was used (Supplementary Information 1). ClinicalTrials.gov was also screened for ongoing studies, and we also manually screened the reference lists of all included articles and relevant reviews to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

We included human studies involving adults aged 18–65 years that examined exposure to classic psychedelics (including psilocybin, LSD, DMT, and ayahuasca) or MDMA, in either clinical, naturalistic, or recreational contexts. Eligible study designs comprised randomized and non-RCT, as well as observational studies, including cohort, case–control, cross-sectional, registry-based, and prospective or retrospective longitudinal designs. Studies were required to report manic or hypomanic symptoms, manic episodes, or diagnostic transition to bipolar disorder following psychedelic or MDMA exposure, assessed through standardized clinical scales, structured diagnostic interviews, registry diagnoses, or clearly defined self-reported outcomes. Studies enrolling mixed diagnostic samples (e.g., major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder) were included only if bipolar-specific outcomes were reported separately or if individuals with bipolar disorder constituted at least 75% of the sample. We included both acute/post-acute manic or hypomanic symptoms and longer-term persistent outcomes following psychedelic exposure. When temporal detail was available, symptoms occurring during the acute phase or persisting beyond 24 h after administration were considered treatment-emergent mood outcomes, while sustained syndromes over follow-up were examined separately as persistent outcomes or diagnostic transition.

We excluded case reports and small case series, given their high susceptibility to publication and selection bias, as well as conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, and reviews lacking original data. Studies focusing exclusively on classical psychedelic substances or MDMA were included with exclusion of cannabis, ketamine, phencyclidine, or other stimulants, as were preclinical or animal studies. Given the rarity of manic outcomes and the ethical constraints surrounding experimental exposure in bipolar populations, we adopted a broad inclusion strategy encompassing both clinical and observational designs to capture short-term affective destabilization and longer-term diagnostic transitions.

Study screening and data extraction

All records identified through the search strategy were imported into Rayyan, a web-based tool [17] for title/abstract and full-text screening. At each stage, screening was conducted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers selected from a pool of three reviewers (ME, MS, RC). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated. Formal inter-rater reliability was not assessed. Data extraction was also performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers selected from the same pool (ME, MS, RC), using a standardized Microsoft Excel spreadsheet developed a priori. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and, when needed, by consultation with the third reviewer. Given the focus of this review, the primary outcomes were the occurrence of manic or hypomanic symptoms, manic episodes, or diagnostic transition to bipolar disorder following psychedelic or MDMA exposure. Secondary outcomes included changes in depressive symptoms, persistence or resolution of manic symptoms over time, psychiatric hospitalization, and treatment discontinuation where applicable. Additional outcomes were extracted when relevant and consistently reported across studies. When outcomes were reported in both grey literature and peer-reviewed publications, the peer-reviewed version was prioritized.

For each included study, we extracted the following information: first author, year of publication, DOI, study design, setting, sample size, participant demographics (mean age, sex/gender when available), diagnostic characteristics (e.g., bipolar I disorder, bipolar II disorder, bipolar vulnerability or family history), type of psychedelic or MDMA exposure, context of use (clinical, supervised, naturalistic, or recreational), dosing and frequency of exposure when reported, follow-up duration, outcome measures used to assess manic, hypomanic, depressive, or psychotic symptoms, timing of outcome assessment, and reported adverse events. For interventional studies, we also extracted information on preparatory and integration procedures, monitoring protocols, and discontinuation due to adverse events. Where applicable, analyses based on intention-to-treat principles were prioritized.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (ME, MS), with disagreements resolved through consensus. Cross-sectional studies were appraised using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies [18].

For non-randomized and observational studies, risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool [19], focusing on bias due to confounding, participant selection, exposure classification, deviations from intended exposure, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. For RCTs, risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [20]. Given the predominance of naturalistic and uncontrolled designs in this literature, particular attention was paid to residual confounding, self-selection, and outcome misclassification.

Overall risk-of-bias judgments were incorporated into the interpretation of findings and informed the strength and certainty of conclusions drawn in the Discussion section. To contextualize the strength of inferences across heterogeneous study designs, the overall level of evidence supporting each results domain was graded using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) framework [21].

Statistical analysis

To estimate the pooled prevalence of transition to bipolar disorder following substance-related psychiatric presentations, a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions was performed. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.0). Proportions were analyzed on the logit scale using the metafor package [22] which provides appropriate variance stabilization for prevalence outcomes, and pooled estimates were back-transformed to proportions for interpretability.

Random-effects models were fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to quantify between-study variance (τ²). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using both τ² and the I² statistic, with I² values above 50% indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity. Given the small number of included studies, confidence intervals around pooled estimates were interpreted conservatively. Continuity corrections were not required, as all studies reported non-zero events. Funnel plots were generated for visual assessment of small-study effects; however, no formal tests for publication bias were performed due to the limited number of studies (k < 10).

Studies in the general population and individuals with bipolar vulnerability

Several large population-based studies examined associations between psychedelic use and manic or psychotic symptoms in the general population, with particular attention to the moderating role of bipolar or psychosis vulnerability. Overall, these studies did not support a uniform association between psychedelic exposure and manic symptoms at the population level. Rather, risk appeared to be concentrated among individuals with elevated genetic, familial, or personal vulnerability to bipolar or psychotic disorders.

Using a genetically informative Swedish twin cohort, Simonsson et al. [39] found no overall association between lifetime psychedelic use and manic symptoms at the population level. However, a significant interaction was observed whereby individuals with higher polygenic risk scores for schizophrenia or bipolar I disorder exhibited greater manic symptom severity following psychedelic exposure, whereas no such effect was observed in individuals with lower genetic liability.

Similarly, in large U.S. population samples, Simonsson et al. [39] reported that lifetime psychedelic use was associated with increased psychotic-like and unusual perceptual experiences among individuals with a family history of psychotic or bipolar disorders, whereas no such association, or even lower symptom burden, was observed in those without such familial vulnerability. Honk et al. [23] likewise found no overall association between psychedelic use and psychotic symptoms, but identified increased symptom counts among participants reporting a personal or family history of bipolar disorder.

Importantly, through these studies, the reported manic or psychotic symptoms were generally subsyndromal and did not provide evidence of sustained mood episodes or diagnostic transition. Taken together, these findings support a diathesis–stress interpretation, in which psychedelic exposure does not appear to increase manic risk uniformly in the general population, but may be associated with greater affective or psychotic symptom expression in individuals with underlying bipolar-spectrum or psychosis vulnerability.

Studies in individuals with diagnosed bipolar disorder or self-reported bipolar disorder

Multiple studies investigated psychedelic-associated manic symptoms among individuals with a diagnosed or self-reported bipolar disorder.

Morton et al. [10] conducted a large international web-based survey of 541 individuals with self-reported bipolar disorder who had used psilocybin. Approximately 32.2% reported new or worsened symptoms—including manic symptoms, sleep disturbance, or anxiety—during the week following psilocybin use. Emergency medical service utilization was rare (3.3%), and most respondents rated psilocybin as beneficial overall. No significant differences were observed between bipolar I and bipolar II subgroups.

Marrocu et al. [25] pooled data from three prospective naturalistic cohorts assessing mental health before and four weeks after psychedelic use. While most participants demonstrated improvement in well-being, 16.4% met criteria for clinically meaningful deterioration. Among participants with self-reported bipolar disorder, 12.5% were classified as “negative responders,” indicating worsening of psychiatric symptoms following psychedelic exposure.

In contrast, Meyer et al. [11] conducted a retrospective within-subject pre–post analysis in 105 individuals with bipolar disorder. Psychedelic use was associated with reductions in depressive symptoms, decreased cannabis use, and increased symptom-free days, with no statistically significant increase in manic, psychotic, or anxiety symptoms over a three-month follow-up period.

Longitudinal analyses by Simonsson et al. (2025) further demonstrated that naturalistic psychedelic use—particularly in illegal or unsupervised contexts—was associated with increases in manic and psychotic symptoms over two months, with stronger effects among individuals with bipolar I disorder. Symptom severity was moderated by frequency of use and intensity of challenging experiences.

Overall, studies in diagnosed bipolar populations suggest a heterogeneous risk profile, with transient manic or hypomanic symptoms occurring in a minority of individuals, particularly those with bipolar I disorder and exposure occurring in unsupervised or high-risk contexts.

Clinical studies of psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy

The few published controlled clinical studies evaluating psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy in bipolar depression have consistently reported a relatively favorable safety profile with respect to treatment-emergent manic or hypomanic symptoms.

Aaronson et al. [16] reported large antidepressant effects following a single 25 mg dose of psilocybin administered with psychotherapy in individuals with bipolar II depression, with no increase in Young Mania Rating Scale scores and no emergent suicidality.

Rosenblat et al. [30] conducted a clinical trial in treatment-resistant depression that included a subset of individuals with bipolar II disorder. Sustained antidepressant effects were observed across repeated dosing, with no systematic emergence of manic or hypomanic symptoms during follow-up. Similarly, Meshkat et al. [31] reported significant improvements in depressive symptoms following psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy, with stable YMRS scores and no manic or psychotic exacerbations.

Across all uncontrolled clinical trials, no persistent manic episodes were observed. However, sample sizes were small, follow-up durations were limited, and exclusion criteria were stringent, limiting generalizability to broader bipolar populations.

However, we identified two RCTs on individuals with major depressive disorders, and alcohol use disorders [32, 33] that systematically monitored participants’ mood for several weeks following psychedelic administration. In the study by Goodwin et al., participants were followed for up to 12 weeks post-treatment, during which 4 individuals (5%) experienced transient euphoric or dysphoric mood states occurring between 2 days and 3 weeks after administration. Other participants even experienced anxiety and depressive states (4(5%) and 3(4%) respectively). In contrast, Bogenschutz and colleagues monitored participants for up to 32 weeks following treatment and reported a single case of dysphoric mood during the follow-up period. Notably, many RCTs did not provide detailed longitudinal reporting of post-acute mood changes following psychedelic administration. Overall, as reported in one of our previous publications [5], the pooled proportion of participants experiencing ‘euphoric’ mood states in RCTs is approximately 5.8%.

Prevalence of transition to bipolar disorder following psychedelic exposure

Four registry-based cohort studies examined the prevalence of transition to bipolar disorder among individuals with substance-related psychopathology. Starzer et al. [36] reported that among individuals that received a diagnosis of hallucinogen-induced psychosis, 5.26%(6/114) transitioned to bipolar disorder over follow-up, with bipolar transitions typically occurring within five years follow-up window.

Rognli et al. [35] reported a six-year transition rate of 5.80% to bipolar disorder following hallucinogen-induced psychosis in a Norwegian national cohort occurring over six years. Wirowski et al. [34] examined transition from major depressive disorder to bipolar disorder among individuals with lifetime use of hallucinogens and found a 12.4% transition rate overall and a 10% transition rate among individuals who had a lifetime hallucinogen use, occurring over six years.

Myran et al. [40] reported a 3-year incident rate of mania requiring acute care among those who had emergency visits for hallucinogen use was approximately 1.43%, compared to 0.06% in the matched general population. Myran et al. [40] reported a five-year cumulative incidence of 3.13% for bipolar disorder among individuals who received acute care for hallucinogen-related conditions, compared with 0.86% in matched population controls, indicating an elevated prevalence of subsequent transition to bipolar disorder diagnosis following severe hallucinogen-associated presentations. This prevalence was even higher, at 8.55%, when considering acute care and outpatient setting. Authors found that the transition prevalence was highest within the first year following hospital presentation and attenuated over longer follow-up.

We conducted an exploratory meta-analysis on the prevalence of transition from hallucinogens-induced mood/psychosis disorders to a diagnosis of bipolar disorder within a 5–6-year time-window (Fig. 1). Ongoing and registered clinical trials

Four ongoing or registered clinical trials are currently evaluating psychedelic interventions in bipolar II disorder, including psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy (NCT05065294; NCT06506019; NCT06706232) and a phase 2 trial with GH001(5-MeO-DMT) (NCT05839509)(Table 2). All are open-label or single-arm studies primarily designed to assess feasibility, safety, and neurobiological mechanisms rather than long-term manic risk. However, NCT05839509 was terminated due to regulatory and development strategy considerations.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from clinical trials, observational studies, and registry-based cohorts to examine the prevalence of manic symptoms and diagnostic transition following psychedelic exposure in individuals with bipolar disorder or bipolar vulnerability. When interpreted in light of the evidence grading, a consistent domain-specific pattern emerges (Table 3). Moderate-quality evidence (Level 2b) from population-based and genetically informed cohorts indicates that psychedelic exposure is not associated with increased manic symptoms at the population level, with risk emerging primarily among individuals with elevated genetic or familial liability. In contrast, evidence regarding individuals with diagnosed bipolar disorder—particularly bipolar I disorder—is derived mainly from naturalistic and self-reported studies (Level 3b), which suggest a modest incidence of transient manic or hypomanic symptoms, especially in unsupervised or illegal contexts. Synthesis of the evidence.

Despite heterogeneity in study design and outcome assessment, the overall evidence base—graded predominantly at Levels 2b–3b—does not support a strong or generalizable causal relationship between psychedelic use and the induction of bipolar disorder. Rather, the data suggests that manic symptoms, when they occur, are typically transient, context-dependent, and concentrated in high-risk subgroups. This distinction between short-term affective destabilization and persistent bipolar illness is critical, particularly given diagnostic criteria requiring symptom persistence beyond substance effects. Taken together, and consistent with the evidence hierarchy presented in Table 3, the reviewed literature supports a nuanced interpretation in which psychedelics may confer situational risk in vulnerable individuals while appearing relatively safe with respect to manic outcomes in carefully screened and monitored clinical settings.

Short-term vs. persistent effects

Across the included studies, manic or hypomanic symptoms associated with psychedelic exposure were predominantly acute and time-limited, typically emerging within 24–72 h following use and resolving over days to weeks. Cross-sectional and longitudinal observational studies consistently described symptom exacerbations occurring in close temporal proximity to psychedelic ingestion, most often characterized by transient increases in mood elevation, reduced need for sleep, or behavioral activation (Morton et al., [10]). Importantly, in prospective and retrospective observational cohorts with follow-up extending from several weeks to months, no study demonstrated evidence of persistent or progressive manic syndromes attributable solely to psychedelic exposure [11, 25]. In controlled therapeutic contexts, including multiple uncontrolled clinical trials of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy in bipolar II disorder, systematic monitoring using standardized scales, showed transient euphoric, dysphoric symptoms for individuals with depressive disorders [32, 33], while for individuals with bipolar disorders potential transient activation resolved without pharmacological intervention [16, 31].

When manic symptoms persisted beyond the expected window of substance effects, the clinical course was more consistent with underlying bipolar disorder unmasked or precipitated by psychedelic exposure, rather than with a substance-induced bipolar disorder as defined by DSM-5 criteria. This interpretation is further supported by registry-based cohort studies of substance-induced psychosis, which demonstrated that diagnostic transitions to bipolar disorder typically occurred over years, not acutely, and were associated with established vulnerability factors rather than with hallucinogen exposure itself [35, 36]. Collectively, these findings suggest that psychedelics may precipitate short-term affective destabilization in vulnerable individuals, but do not appear to induce persistent bipolar illness in the absence of pre-existing liability.

Transition to bipolar diagnosis

Our systematic review proposes that the transition to a formal bipolar diagnosis following psychedelic-associated manic symptoms was difficult to establish, with long-term outcome data remaining limited. Observational and registry-based cohort studies examining diagnostic transitions after substance-related psychopathology indicate that conversion to bipolar disorder, when it occurs, typically unfolds over years rather than acutely, and is strongly influenced by pre-existing vulnerability factors rather than by hallucinogen exposure itself [35, 36]. In contrast, the only large hallucinogen-specific population cohort to date reported a low absolute prevalence of transition to bipolar disorder (3–6%), comparable to background rates and not exceeding prevalences observed with other substances [37, 40].

In the only longitudinal cohort to explicitly report hallucinogen exposure in patients with unipolar disorders, lifetime use was limited (17.8%) and only a single hallucinogen-exposed individual transitioned to bipolar disorder, precluding any inference of substance-specific risk [34]. For Starzer and Rognli studies, 4 and 3.8% for amphetamine-induced psychosis, 5 and 4.9% for cannabis-induced psychosis were found respectively. These risks are secondary to schizophrenia-spectrum transitions due to cannabis and amphetamines (around 30%) [41, 42], and suggest that the transition risk is not substance-specific, but rather reflect shared vulnerability factors (affective instability, bipolar-spectrum vulnerability, stress-diathesis mechanisms). Furthermore, Sabé et al. proposed a 13.1% of prevalence of transition from psychedelic-induced psychosis to schizophrenia, which advocate for a lower risk of transition when considering serotoninergic psychedelics [4].

Within cross-sectional and prospective observational studies of psychedelic use among individuals with self-reported bipolar disorder, some participants reported manic episodes temporally associated with psychedelic exposure; however, no study provided systematic evidence that these episodes resulted in sustained bipolar illness attributable to psychedelics [10, 25, 26, 43]. Notably, in therapeutic and research contexts, individuals with bipolar II disorder who experienced transient activation or hypomanic symptoms frequently remained episode-free during follow-up, with no progression to bipolar I disorder or recurrent mania [16, 31].

According to DSM-5 criteria, a manic episode that persists beyond the expected physiological effects of a substance is sufficient to warrant a diagnosis of bipolar I disorder. However, the available evidence suggests that such sustained episodes following psychedelic exposure are rare, and when diagnosed, are more plausibly interpreted as manifestations of previously unrecognized bipolar disorder unmasked by an acute affective stressor, rather than as true substance-induced bipolar disorder. Consequently, while isolated cases of diagnostic transition have been reported [38], the current literature does not support a robust causal link between psychedelic exposure and durable bipolar disorder onset in the absence of underlying susceptibility.

Risk factors and prominent moderators identified

Our findings suggest that the emergence of manic or hypomanic symptoms following psychedelic exposure appears to be strongly moderated by pre-existing vulnerability factors rather than by psychedelic use alone. An interpretive possibility is that psychedelic use may, in some cases, represent a behavioral manifestation of emerging hypomania or mania rather than a purely exogenous trigger. This is particularly relevant in bipolar disorder, where prodromal or early syndromal phases may include increased energy, impulsivity, risk-taking, reduced sleep, and behavioral disinhibition, potentially complicating causal attribution in naturalistic studies [44]. The most consistently identified moderator was a personal history of bipolar disorder, particularly bipolar I disorder, with multiple cross-sectional and longitudinal studies reporting greater manic symptom expression among individuals with established bipolar diagnoses following naturalistic psychedelic use [10, 24, 26]. Familial and genetic vulnerability further amplified risk: in a genetically informed twin study, psychedelic use was associated with increased manic symptoms only among individuals with higher polygenic risk scores for schizophrenia or bipolar I disorder, whereas no such association was observed in those with lower genetic liability [39].

Context of use emerged as another critical moderator. Longitudinal observational data indicated that psychedelic use occurring in illegal or unsupervised settings was associated with increases in manic and psychotic symptom severity, particularly among participants with bipolar I disorder or other severe psychiatric histories [23, 26]. In contrast, structured therapeutic contexts, including uncontrolled clinical trials of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy in bipolar II depression, consistently showed no evidence of emergent mania or hypomania, despite robust antidepressant effects [16, 31].

Co-exposures and clinical state factors were frequently implicated in adverse outcomes. Several observational studies highlighted the role of poly-substance use, particularly stimulants and antidepressants, as well as sleep deprivation and psychosocial stress, as proximal contributors to manic symptom emergence following psychedelic exposure [10, 25]. Importantly, large registry-based cohort studies examining diagnostic transitions following substance-induced psychosis found no hallucinogen-specific signal for conversion to bipolar disorder, with risk largely driven by younger age, self-harm, and non-psychedelic substances such as cannabis or stimulants [35, 36].

Taken together, the available evidence supports a diathesis–stress model, in which psychedelics may act as non-specific affective or neurobiological stressors capable of precipitating manic symptoms primarily in individuals with pre-existing bipolar liability, high genetic risk, or destabilizing contextual factors, rather than as independent causal agents of bipolar disorder.

Theoretical framework: psychedelics as precipitants, sensitizers, or inducers of bipolarity

A key unresolved question is whether repeated psychedelic exposure could contribute to a kindling- or sensitization-like process, whereby the threshold for affective episodes becomes progressively lower in vulnerable individuals. This possibility is conceptually related to the affective kindling model described in antidepressant-associated bipolarity (sometimes referred to as “Type III” bipolar disorder), in which repeated pharmacological perturbations have been hypothesized to increase affective instability over time rather than induce bipolar disorder de novo [45].

From a theoretical perspective, psychedelics could plausibly participate in such a process. Acute serotonergic and downstream glutamatergic perturbations, together with sleep disruption, emotional intensity, and stress-related arousal, may transiently destabilize affective regulation. In individuals with latent bipolar vulnerability, repeated exposures might hypothetically lower the threshold for subsequent manic or depressive episodes through sensitization mechanisms. This framework has been discussed in narrative and conceptual reviews of psychedelic-related adverse events, which note phenomenological similarities between psychedelic-associated manic reactions and antidepressant-induced mood switching [8, 46].

However, no empirical study included in this systematic review directly examined a kindling or sensitization trajectory. Available longitudinal observational studies did not show progressive worsening of bipolar course with repeated psychedelic use, nor did registry-based cohorts identify hallucinogens as clear drivers of diagnostic transition or recurrent affective cycling. Similarly, uncontrolled clinical trials involving one or two supervised psychedelic sessions did not provide evidence of cumulative manic risk or increasing affective instability over follow-up. When manic symptoms were reported, they were generally acute, temporally related to exposure, and non-recurrent, which does not support a clearly established kindling process.

Thus, while a kindling or sensitization model is well supported in antidepressant-induced bipolarity and with certain pro-dopaminergic agents [47], no comparable empirical evidence currently demonstrates progressive affective sensitization following psychedelic exposure. In the available literature, psychedelic-associated manic symptoms appear more often episodic and exposure-linked than cumulative. At present, this supports only a speculative sensitization hypothesis rather than a demonstrated kindling mechanism. This limitation is also consistent with the type and duration of the studies currently available. Longitudinal studies specifically designed to assess repeated exposure, episode recurrence, and temporal changes in affective threshold are needed to evaluate this hypothesis more directly.

Strengths and limitations

This review integrates evidence from diverse study designs, including large registry-based cohorts, observational studies, and clinical trials, allowing triangulation across clinical and population settings and providing insight into both short-term manic symptoms and long-term diagnostic transitions. The inclusion of national registry studies strengthens conclusions regarding rare outcomes, while therapeutic trials contribute systematically monitored safety data.

However, the overall evidence base is limited by substantial risk of bias. Most studies were observational, uncontrolled, and cross-sectional, limiting causal inference. Self-selection, recall bias, exposure misclassification, and residual confounding (e.g., co-substance use, antidepressants, sleep deprivation) were common. Follow-up durations were often short, and individuals with severe adverse outcomes may have been underrepresented. Only few studies were eligible for quantitative synthesis because longitudinal data specifically examining bipolar diagnostic transition after hallucinogen exposure remain scarce, and most transition studies do not report hallucinogen-specific outcomes in a form suitable for pooling. An additional limitation concerns the search strategy. Because searches did not include full-text records, some studies may have been missed if bipolar or manic outcomes were not reported in the title, abstract, keywords, or indexing terms. Publication bias is also likely. Therapeutic studies may under detect mood-related disturbances due to strict exclusion criteria and intensive monitoring, while survey and case-based reports may overemphasize adverse events. Psychedelic trials are generally short, although very carefully monitored [48]. Consequently, although the available evidence does not support a strong causal link between psychedelic use and persistent bipolar disorder, confidence in precise risk estimates remains limited, underscoring the need for prospective, well-controlled studies with systematic adverse-event reporting.

Conclusion

Across the available evidence, psychedelic use is associated with a low but clinically relevant risk of manic or hypomanic symptoms in individuals with bipolar vulnerability, particularly in naturalistic or unsupervised settings. In contrast, the few controlled clinical studies conducted in carefully screened bipolar II populations have not demonstrated treatment-emergent or persistent manic episodes, suggesting that structured therapeutic contexts substantially mitigate this risk. Nevertheless, transient mood disturbance, including euphoric or dysphoric state, have been reported in RCTs conducted in populations with major depressive disorder.

The existing literature does not support the induction of chronic mania or de novo bipolar disorder following psychedelic exposure. When manic symptoms do occur, they are typically acute and self-limited and are more consistent with affective destabilization or the unmasking of latent bipolar vulnerability than with DSM-5 substance-induced bipolar disorder. Importantly, the absence of evidence for long-term harm should not be interpreted as evidence of long-term safety.

While psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy shows short-term promise for treatment-resistant bipolar depression, long-term psychiatric outcomes—including cumulative effects, sensitization processes, and dose–response relationships—remain insufficiently characterized. Accordingly, screening procedures should emphasize dimensional assessments of affective instability, symptom trajectory, and longitudinal stability rather than categorical diagnostic exclusion alone, and should be embedded within rigorous monitoring frameworks and prospective longitudinal controlled studies prior to broader clinical implementation.
