Advanced intensity-modulated fiber sensors for scalable sensing
Authors: Filip Malinka, Ondrej Vachek, Daniel Krizan, Jiri Stipal, Carlos Marques, Petr Siska, Jan Nedoma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntensity-modulated fiber optic sensors (IM-FOSs) represent a cost-effective and structurally simple alternative to phase-based and wavelength-based optical sensors. Their operational principle enables the development of robust, scalable, and multiplexable systems suitable for a wide range of mechanical measurements. This review provides a structured overview of IM-FOS technologies, highlighting recent developments in architectures, interrogation methods, and application domains. An analysis with interferometric and Bragg-grating-based sensors is presented to clarify the trade-offs in sensitivity, complexity, and cost. Especially in industrial and civil monitoring, IM-FOSs offer unique advantages in terms of simplicity and adaptability. Finally, key research directions are outlined, including the integration of machine learning algorithms, the use of hybrid materials and smart structures, strategies to improve long-term stability, miniaturization and commercialization potential, and the pressing need for standardization. The article aims to provide a comprehensive reference for researchers and engineers seeking to develop or deploy intensity-based optical sensing systems.

Introduction

Optical fiber sensors (OFSs) have become a key technology in modern sensing systems, offering unique advantages that make them highly suitable for a wide range of industrial, structural, and environmental monitoring applications. Their immunity to electromagnetic interference, resistance to corrosion, chemical inertness, small size, and capability for remote or distributed sensing distinguish them from traditional electronic sensing systems.1,2 These properties are particularly advantageous in harsh environments, such as high-voltage substations, aerospace structures, or chemically aggressive industrial zones, where conventional sensors fail or require extensive shielding.

The deployment of OFSs has expanded considerably in recent years, driven by the rising demand for real-time monitoring of mechanical, thermal, chemical, and biological parameters. Fields such as civil engineering, energy production, robotics, process control, and medical diagnostics have embraced optical sensing technologies to improve safety, efficiency, and automation capabilities.2,3 In many of these applications, displacement, vibration, strain, or inclination measurements are crucial parameters, and fiber optic sensors offer reliable performance even in dynamic, high-vibration, or long-term load conditions.1

Among the various classes of fiber optic sensors, intensity-modulated fiber optic sensors (IM-FOSs) stand out due to their structural simplicity, low cost, and ease of implementation. These sensors detect variations in light intensity, either transmitted or reflected. These variations correlate with changes in external physical parameters. Unlike phase-modulated or wavelength-modulated sensors that require coherent sources and complex demodulation techniques, intensity-based systems rely on basic photodetectors and often incoherent or broadband light sources. This reduces the overall system complexity and cost, making IM-FOSs highly attractive for applications requiring large sensor networks, disposable sensors, or embedded sensing in compact systems.4,5,6

A key advantage of IM-FOSs is their potential for multiplexing. While wavelength-based sensors such as fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs) are limited by the spectral bandwidth and cross-sensitivity issues, intensity-based sensors allow for simpler multiplexing schemes using spatial separation, bundle topology, geometric encoding, or newer techniques like time division multiplexing, transmission and reflection analysis, and wavelength division multiplexing.3,7 This opens possibilities for dense multi-point sensing arrays, especially in applications like structural health monitoring (SHM) of bridges, pipelines, or large machinery, where monitoring dozens or hundreds of locations is desirable. Additionally, the simplicity of intensity sensors facilitates miniaturization, enabling their use in wearable systems, microfluidic devices, or soft robotics.2,8

Intensity-based sensors also have limitations. Their sensitivity is generally lower than that of interferometric or grating-based systems, and they can be more affected by light source fluctuations or environmental noise. However, with advances in materials, optomechanical design, and interrogation algorithms, including the application of machine learning, many of these drawbacks are being actively addressed.3,9 Moreover, the robustness, compactness, and ease of deployment of IM-FOSs make them ideal for applications where absolute precision is less critical than system reliability, cost-efficiency, or real-time responsiveness.

This article presents a structured overview of the current state and development of intensity-modulated fiber optic sensors. The article is organized as follows: first, it introduces the fundamental operating principles and categorization of IM-FOSs, then analyzes various interrogation methods and compares sensor configurations reported in recent literature. Emphasis is placed on practical applications, performance benchmarks, and fabrication considerations. At last, it identifies and discusses the major research challenges and future directions in the field, including the integration of intelligent signal processing, the use of advanced materials, and the push toward standardization and commercial scalability.

Contribution of this review

Many reviews on FOSs already exist; however, they generally take a look at various sensor types, with IM-FOSs representing only a small part. They also usually do not focus on a specific field of FOS application.10,11 Those who do focus on a specific field of application often concentrate on medicine.12,13 Reviews focused on sensors usable in industry usually limit themselves to the measurement of one specific physical quantity, like strain,14 bending,15 displacement,6 or gas concentration.16 Reviews that focus specifically on IM-FOSs also concentrate on different fields of applications, like health monitoring17 or biomechanics.18 One review points out the underreporting of IM-FOSs, but it is focused only on refractive index sensors.19 Other reviews focus only on specific types of sensors, like microbending20 and U-shaped sensors.21 One review focuses only on macrobending specifically for temperature measurement in harsh environments.22 The advantage of these reviews is that they thoroughly overview the specific area of interest; however, none represent the whole of intensity sensors.

Our review goes through many types of IM-FOSs and focuses on their application in various industry settings, thus covering types of FOSs that go underreported and areas of application not explored much.

Search methodology

For the compilation of this review, the following keywords and terms reflecting the type of sensors being searched were used: “intensity fiber optic sensors,” “intensity modulated fiber optic sensors,” “industrial,” and “industry.” To focus on specific subcategories of IM-FOS, other complementary terms were used in tandem with the main ones, such as “bending,” “microbending,” “macrobending,” “U-bent,” “twisted,” “evanescent field,” “fiber Bragg grating,” and “fused coupler.” The databases searched using these terms were primarily Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar.

The results were filtered beyond 2014 to create a review aimed primarily at papers published in the last decade or so, with a few exceptions we deemed interesting enough to be included. The discussed methodology yielded 152 relevant sources that were further filtered down to the final 123 by excluding sources without practical experiments, sources without possible industrial applications, and sources without intensity modulation.

Principles of intensity-based optical fiber sensing

Typically, two modulation types were used for data transmission through the fiber: the phase and the intensity modulation. Commercial optical fibers are effective phase modulators themselves, meaning that the transmitted signal phase can change randomly over longer distances and with the intervention of external effects.23 Intensity modulation is based on changing the transmitted power, and commercially available fibers are much more stable in this manner and thus more suitable for sensing.23,24 Variations in signal intensity do not occur only due to fiber attenuation; however, external effects and the surrounding environment also affect the transmitted signal.

Intensity-based sensors can be divided into extrinsic (hybrid) and intrinsic (internal effect).23 For extrinsic sensors, fiber is just a light-transporting medium ending with the sensor itself, often composed of miniaturized optical components.23,24,25 Intrinsic sensors rely on the change in modulation happening inside the fiber.23,24 The working principle of intensity sensors is based on several effects, which will be discussed in further detail. Macrobending

Macrobending losses in optical fibers occur when the fiber is bent with a radius of curvature that exceeds the critical threshold, causing part of the guided light to leak into the cladding and radiate out of the fiber. The critical bending radius Rc can be approximated by a Equation 1, where a is the core radius and Δ=n12−n222n12 is the relative index difference. This phenomenon results from the breakdown of total internal reflection conditions, particularly at high curvature levels, and is influenced by several factors, including the bending radius, wavelength, polarization, and fiber geometry.26,27(Equation 1)Rc≈2aΔ

Experiments have shown a steep increase in attenuation as the bending radius decreases below the critical limit. For instance, in single-mode fibers, a bending radius below 10 mm can cause significant losses at 1,550 nm, making macrobending a sensitive mechanism for sensing applications. At longer wavelengths, the evanescent field penetrates deeper into the cladding, making the guided mode more susceptible to bending-induced radiation losses. Bending-induced losses are also strongly dependent on the polarization state of the input light. In standard single-mode fibers, the two orthogonal polarization modes experience different effective refractive indices due to asymmetrical stress distribution during bending.27,28

Microbending

Microbending losses are also caused by the breakdown of total internal reflection conditions. These bending losses are localized and caused by longitudinal perturbations with a minimal bending radius.29

In the case of multi-mode optical fibers, the microbend sensitivity is given primarily by the separation of the highest order modes in phase constant space. The phase constant difference between two coupled modes Δβ for a step-index profile fiber can be expressed as29:(Equation 2)Δβ=2aΔmM=2·NAnamMwhere a represents a core radius, NA a numerical aperture, n the core refractive index, m mode order, and M the highest order mode number.

The power coupling coefficient of the highest order modes is of interest as the main power leakage to the cladding comes from them and can be expressed as29:(Equation 3)hxy=Cxy14(naNA)4F(Δβ)

C is a constant dependent on the fiber’s geometry and the coupled modes’ field distribution. Higher core radius and lower NA can thus increase the sensitivity to random perturbations. For the periodic perturbations, the sensitivity can be increased by increasing the coefficient ΔT/ΔA, which relates the change in transmission to the change in fiber deformation amplitude. For step-index fibers, higher-order modes can be coupled with small periodicity Λ, and lower-order modes are coupled with large periodicity Λ.29

Evanescent field

As electromagnetic waves cannot be reflected instantaneously at the interface, the energies of reflected and incident light are superposed into a standing electromagnetic wave known as an evanescent wave, with the wavefront perpendicular to the fiber. Total reflection can happen anywhere on the circumference of the fiber core, creating these standing waves and forming an evanescent field around the fiber. The intensity of this field decreases exponentially with distance from the interface. The penetration depth dp can be expressed via Equation 430,31,32:(Equation 4)dp=λ2πn1sin2θ−(n2n1)2

As seen in Equation 4 above, the penetration depth depends on the wavelength λ, impact angle θ, core n1, and cladding n2 refractive indices. With more propagating modes, different evanescent fields will form based on their order. Higher modes have more energy in their evanescent fields, thus penetrating further. Also, macrobends of the fiber can lead to an increase in leaked light into the evanescent zone. This bent fiber’s evanescent field is more prone to react to changes in the refractive index of the surrounding environment.30,31,33

Cladding modification can increase sensitivity in the evanescent field for certain external effects. Modifications range from polishing or tapering part of the cladding to applying special coatings or their combinations. This method facilitates improved interaction of the evanescent field with the outside environment.31,34

Side-coupling

Sensors based on the side-coupling design use the same principles already used with the macrobending and evanescent field sensors. The design uses at least two optical fibers twisted together, one active with a light source attached and one passive with a power meter attached, and measures the amount of light coupled from the active/illuminating fiber to the passive/receiving fiber.35,36 If the bending radius of the twisted pair below is changed (the critical bending radius Rc), more of the light gets decoupled not only from the optical fiber core but also from the cladding outside the fiber. This decoupled light then gets coupled into the passive fiber, and its intensity is controlled directly by the bending radius.37,38

Another way to affect the intensity of light coupled into the passive fiber is by changing the refractive index of the environment outside the fiber. The critical angle at the cladding-environment interface θclen can be expressed as39 follows:(Equation 5)θclen=arcsinnennclwhere the nen is the refractive index of the environment, and ncl is the refractive index of the cladding. As the refractive index of the environment increases, the critical angle θclen will decrease, thus increasing the amount of light decoupled from the active fiber and coupled to the passive fiber.39

Reflection-based

The operating principle is based on one light-transmitting fiber, a reflective surface, and one or more receiving fibers. The light is transmitted in a conical shape close to a Gaussian distribution. The same light is then reflected from the surface back into the receiving fiber or fibers, where it is coupled and further transmitted.40,41 In some cases, the same fiber can be used both for transmitting and receiving.42

The distance between the fibers and the surface is also crucial because of the emitted and reflected light power. According to the amount of light reflected, single or multi-fiber designs can be used as receivers. With fewer fibers, the sensor structure is simpler and compact, but less sensitive, and an improper positioning of the receiving fiber can lead to light being reflected in the opposite direction. Multifiber designs are more complex in this way, often used in a circle or a symmetrical shape, with the transmitting fiber in the middle, to ensure that most of the reflected light is coupled into the receiving fibers. Industrial applications of intensity sensors

Optical fiber intensity sensors have gained significant attention in industrial applications due to their inherent advantages, such as small form factor, immunity to electromagnetic interference (EMI), and sensitivity to mechanical and environmental changes. These sensors operate on the principle that external perturbations alter the light intensity transmitted through the fiber, allowing indirect measurement of various physical parameters.

The mechanisms that induce such intensity variations differ depending on the design and intended application. In this section, the main intensity-modulation principles used in optical fiber sensors and their industrial implementations are reviewed. Specifically, have taken into consideration five dominant approaches: macrobending, microbending, evanescent field interaction, side coupling, and cavity-based structures. Each subsection introduces a distinct mechanism and highlights representative sensing applications.

Applications of bending-based optical fiber sensors

Bending-based sensors represent a well-established and widely adopted configuration of intensity-modulated optical fiber sensing. Their operating principle is based on large-radius bending for macrobending and small and subtle deformations for microbending, which induce controlled attenuation as light leaks from the fiber core. Thanks to their robustness and simplicity, macrobending sensors have been successfully employed in diverse industrial settings for the detection of angular displacement, deformation, load, temperature, analyte, and surface profiles. Bending and angular sensing

Bending-induced intensity modulation in optical fibers is widely utilized for capturing angular deflection, rotation, and spatial orientation in both soft and rigid industrial mechanical systems. In soft robotics, a fiber loop embedded in a continuum robotic arm was used to measure both elongation and bending angle. This sensor demonstrated maximum errors of 7 mm (18%) for arm length and 14° (13%) for angular deflection, validated against an electromagnetic tracking system.28

Another approach employed a plastic optical fiber (POF) monitored via charge-coupled device (CCD) imaging, where angular displacement was detected by comparing images of the fiber’s end facet in reference and rotated states. The method achieved a rotational resolution of 0.01°, confirming its effectiveness for high-precision angular tracking.43

Finally, vectorial angular sensing was demonstrated using a multicore fiber with asymmetric air-hole distribution. Bending radius and orientation affected confinement losses differently in each core, enabling vector bending reconstruction. Schematics of the measurement setup can be seen in Figure 2. This configuration yielded a bending angle resolution of 20° and a tip displacement resolution of 1.5 mm across angular ranges from 0° to 345° and displacements from 0 to 1.6 cm.44Figure 2Schematic diagram of measurement setup for vectorial angular movement monitoring using multicore fiber with asymmetric air-hole distribution, where FI stands for fan-in, FO for fan-out, L is the length of the bend-sensitive fiber, and A is the distance between straight and maximum bend position44Figure reproduced from Budnicki, D. et al., Journal of Lightwave Technology 38, 23, licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Curvature-based structural sensing

Structural curvature sensing benefits significantly from the simplicity and sensitivity of macrobending configurations. Such systems can then be used as a baseline for multiple industrial settings. One implementation utilized a fiber sensor with intensity coding to capture angular displacement and curvature during elbow joint motion. The system demonstrated strong linearity, with correlation coefficients ranging from 94.6% to 98.4% on a workbench setup and from 93.1% to 98.7% during human joint measurements. Sensitivity values ranged from −0.25 to −0.477 dB/° for joint flexion and −0.552 to −1.052 dB/cm for curvature sensing.27

In profilometry, a multimode-coreless-multimode (MCM) fiber structure was attached to a stylus that tracked the surface of 3D-printed objects. The sensor demonstrated a displacement sensitivity of 89.43 μW/mm and a curvature sensitivity of 54.789 μW/m−1, resolving curvature down to 0.0033 m−1 and displacements as small as 2 μm.45

For torsional sensing, a helical fiber taper structure enabled bidirectional twist detection based on asymmetric macrobending loss. The sensor achieved a maximum torsion sensitivity of −0.484 dB/(rad/m) in the twist range from −10.67 to 0 rad/m, while maintaining negligible temperature sensitivity, thus minimizing cross-sensitivity effects.46

Displacement and deformation sensing

Linear motion tracking using macrobending sensors has proven effective for displacement quantification in industrial applications. A notable example incorporated a fiber into a cogwheel-driven configuration, where rotation induced controlled changes in bend radius. The sensor provided a high sensitivity of 0.1668 dB/mm over a displacement range of 0 to 201 mm, with a resolution of 0.06 mm and a maximum error below 10%. Additionally, long-term monitoring confirmed good repeatability and stability across 10 cycles of testing.26

A microbend sensor was also used for displacement monitoring. The sensor was constructed using a POF with multiple surface structural imperfections to improve sensitivity. A sensor with 6 cm in length and three imperfections achieved a sensitivity of 0.2401 W/mm and a resolution of 4.2 m.62

Force and pressure sensing

Mechanical load and pressure sensing via fiber macrobending are often achieved by translating force-induced deformations into changes in bend radius. In one study, a silicone elastomer structure with embedded fiber loops detected applied loads with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.12 kg, corresponding to 7.8% of maximum load. Performance was customizable through parameters like loop radius, encapsulation material, and fiber positioning, making the system adaptable to various mechanical environments.47

A multiplexed sensing platform designed for pressure localization and magnitude estimation showed an accuracy of 94.0% for determining location, with a mean absolute error of 0.31 kPa and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.45 kPa, less than 5.0% of the full-scale pressure range.48

In weight sensing, a deformable foam layer was paired with a fiber and rubber impactor mat, where applied loads altered the bend length of the fiber. A similar working principle can also be applied to microbending-based sensor designs. The major difference is the size of the perforators and the fiber deformation scale. This is proven by an optical fiber sandwiched between two deformed plates with a specific period of grooves. As the force is applied to the plates, they cause a periodic deformation of the fiber. This sensor achieved a sensitivity of 0.6 nW/N.56

Another study involved tooth-like structure deformers made of Al-7075 alloy, with a 3 to 1 mm pitch. The largest detection range, up to 13 N, was observed for 1.5 mm pitches. The sensor could be further improved by incorporating multilayered structures such as Glass/Ag/SU8/PDMS that showed even greater comprehensive performance with a range from 1,200 to 2,800 N.57

On a smaller scale, an optical fiber inserted into a silicon rubber with integrated sand grains acting as miniature deformers can also be utilized as a load sensor. For forces up to 30,000 N, a maximum sensitivity of 0.0239 mV/N was measured.63

Pressure measurement using a fiber embedded in a building material matrix is also possible. As the pressure increases, the fiber is deformed, causing a variation in output power. Three samples of fiber embedding were tested. Samples S1 with periodic induced microbending and S2 without were embedded in an Araldite matrix. Sample S3 without induced microbending was embedded in a cement matrix composite to emulate a real working environment. These samples achieved sensitivities of 2.304, 0.906, and 2.218 nW kPa−1, with reproducibilities of 96.61%, 97.95%, and 87.2%, respectively.58

Thermal and environmental sensing

Thermally responsive macrobending sensors exploit temperature-induced refractive index changes or material expansion. A U-shaped single-mode fiber coated with India ink and nickel exhibited a bend-loss sensitivity of 0.023 dB/°C and a resolution of 0.5°C, maintaining a linear response across the 0°C–80°C temperature range, even under harsh conditions.50

Another implementation employed a V-beam made of 6061-T6 aluminum alloy, where thermal expansion induced fiber bending. The system showed consistent linear behavior and a temperature measurement error below 5%, with a proportional displacement change of approximately 2.5 μm/°C. Results were confirmed through both experimental and finite element simulations.51

Environmental magnetic field sensing was demonstrated using a U-bend fiber coated with Fe3O4. The magnetic attraction caused deformation of the fiber and subsequent optical loss. Sensitivities were measured as −0.0013 nm/Gauss for wavelength shift and 0.00028 dB/Gauss for transmission loss, confirming the viability of the design for magnetic field detection.52

Material condition monitoring

Surface degradation and corrosion monitoring can be achieved by translating physical separation into bending deformation. A magnet-fiber configuration was used to detect changes in the distance between a metal surface and the sensor head caused by material loss. The system was responsive to a 5 mm gap and showed good repeatability when scanning fabricated corrosion samples with varying pit shapes. Vibration sensitivity of 2 Hz further enabled dynamic scanning of pipelines or tanks.53

Similar metal structure health monitoring can also be done using microbending. Optical fiber placed on bare metal is subjected to microbending as metal tends to corrode, and this random expansion of rust creates rustic and uneven surface anomalies, forcing pressure onto the fiber. Visualization of the sensor on bare and corroded metal can be seen in Figure 4. This situation is not limited to bare metal surfaces and occurs underneath corrosion-protective coatings. Multi-mode fibers showed even greater sensitivity in this field than single-mode fibers. The same sensing principle can also be used for distributed sensing via optical time domain reflectometry (OTDR). Vibration sensing

Dynamic vibration monitoring using macrobending sensors allows for high-resolution detection across multiple frequencies. A fiber loop configuration demonstrated strong agreement with accelerometer readings in the 30–133 Hz range, achieving a correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9999 and enabling frequency tracking with high accuracy.54

In another approach, a standard single-mode fiber loop was shown to be suitable for detecting multi-frequency vibrations. The sensor achieved a resolution of 0.04 Hz while maintaining immunity to ambient temperature variations and offered a low-cost and simple fabrication method.55

Tactile sensing

Especially in the robotics industry, tactile sensing is crucial for manipulating the surrounding environment. Microbending has shown great possibilities in this field, such as a knot-like tactile sensor for in-orbit applications. Implemented at the robotic arm end, it serves as a pressure, hardness, and texture sensor. When force is applied, an exterior button presses the optical fiber knot, which induces losses and is transformed into a measurand. Pressure sensing was achieved for 0–10 N with 0.12 N resolution. The hardness range was 1–30 on the Shore A scale with a resolution down to 0.03–0.4. Finally, texture can be measured for wavelengths down to 300 μm.59

For more complex 3D surfaces, a polymer-based waveguide tactile sensor can be used. The waveguides can be formed alongside the measured 3D structure, which induces microbending losses that grow with applied pressure. The best-obtained sensitivity was 1.42 · 10−5 kPa−1 with a maximum measurement range from 0–850 kPa.60

Analyte concentration sensing

As other concentration sensors tend to be expensive and quite sophisticated, microbending offers a simpler and more cost-effective alternative for commercial industrial applications. One such sensor was manufactured by creating permanent microbends on the fiber with a pair of corrugated plates. These deformations are much more sensitive to the properties of any medium in contact with the fiber. The sensor sensed changes in chlorine concentration on a minute basis, and the measured amount was comparable to the concentration used for testing.61

Industrial impact and deployment potential

The presented studies show that bending-based optical fiber sensors can be highly effective tools in multiple industrial domains. The sensors have the potential to be used in aerospace, robotics, power, heavy, and manufacturing industries, as well as civil engineering, which are all highly influential fields of the current era. Another key aspect of these sensors is their immense scalability using simple multiplexing techniques and a straightforward interrogation setup, as well as their ability to use distributed sensing technologies. All this allows for the deployment of dense sensor networks in large-scale infrastructure. With advances in both materials and signal processing, bending-based sensors are positioned to play a vital role in future industrial systems that demand real-time, robust, and scalable monitoring solutions.

Applications of evanescent-field-based optical fiber sensors

Evanescent-field-based optical fiber sensors are a distinct branch of intensity-based sensors. Unlike electronic sensors, the working principle is based on the interaction between the evanescent wave, created on a core and cladding interface, and the surrounding environment. This results in an ability to detect even subtle changes in the environmental parameters, such as refractive index, analyte concentration, humidity, or temperature. A tapered, U-bent, or polished structure design is often used to increase sensitivity, which is further improved by adding a specific coating. The industrial application of these sensors can be found in chemical manufacturing, environmental compliance, food processing, petroleum refining, and pharmaceutical quality control. Refractive index sensing

Evanescent-field-based sensors find vast applications in many industries. A prime example is refractive index sensing, which can be directly used to determine multiple parameters in an industrial environment, as further demonstrated by the presented studies. These sensors operate based on the principle of direct interaction between the evanescent wave and the outside environment, with changing refractive index creating variations in light intensity. A POF bend-side-polished sensor with multiple bending radii in the range of 2–8 mm, etching depths in the range 0–600 μm, and polishing angles in the range 30–130° was tested to fine-tune the sensitivity. In the end, the sensor with a bending radius of 5 mm, etching depth of 500 μm, and polishing angle of 60° reached a linear sensing trend and the highest sensitivity of 154 dB/RIU in the refractive index (RI) range of 1.33–1.44.34

An alternative batch of sensors was compared using different ratios of the fiber radius R and its bending radius ρ. This parameter was fine-tuned using a ray tracing simulation. Sensors with diameters of 20 μm, 40 μm, and bending radii ranging from 0.1 to 1 mm were experimentally tested. It was found that sensors with a parameter R/ρ of 20 achieved a linear sensing trend with a sensitivity of 500%/RIU.64

Another D-bend polished sensor was constructed using a thin gold film coating to create the transition between dielectric and metal needed for surface plasmon resonance. This ensures a dual sensing mechanism, with both intensity and spectrum change. The sensor with different bend radii (Figure 5) and polished depths was tested in liquids with a refractive index from 1.31 to 1.4. The highest sensitivity of 43 dB/RIU was achieved with a bend radius of 5 mm and a polished depth of 0.15 mm.33Figure 5A photograph of multiple manufactured side-polished gold-coated fiber optic sensors for refractive index sensing with different radii33Reprinted with permission from Jing, N. et al., IEEE Sensors Journal, 19(19), 5665–5669, ©2019 IEEE.

A decladded U-bend sensor with poly-methyl-methacrylate core was also tested. The sensors varied in diameter from 250 to 1,000 μm, and the bending radius varied from 1.25 to 3 mm. Increasing the refractive index of the surrounding environment from 1 to 1.38 decreased the normalized intensity of transmitted light to 0.8 and 0.2, for lower and higher source wavelengths.32

Analyte concentration sensing

Another application of evanescent-field-based sensors can be found in analyte concentration monitoring, which is crucial in multiple industrial fields. The working principle is very close to the refractive index sensing. The key difference is the direct transformation of intensity fluctuation into a measured quantity. The first example is ammonia concentration in water, which can be used for water quality monitoring to ensure safe drinking water levels of 0.5 ppm of ammonia. It uses a layer of copper-benzene tricarboxylic acid, which changes its RI when interacting with ammonia. A detection limit of 0.1 ppm was reached with a total measurement range of 0.1 to 10 ppm.66

Another ammonia concentration sensor was manufactured for possible application in fish farms to prevent fish mortality and improve production quality. Multiple configurations of the sensor were tested using Oxazine 170 perchlorate coating, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) protective layer, and a gold-palladium thin film at the fiber’s end. The sensor reached the detection limit of 0.1 ppm.67

For sodium chloride (NaCl) and ethanol solution monitoring, U-bent multimode fiber, depicted by Figure 6, with special tungsten disulfide (WS2) coating and in situ growth of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) proved to be a viable option. The sensor exhibited high sensitivity, reproducibility, stability, and rapid response time. Humidity and temperature monitoring

Special coatings are often used to create susceptibility to changes in humidity and temperature, for example, an agarose-coated U-bent sensor working at 2 μm achieved a maximum sensitivity of 0.314 nm/%RH in the range of 40%–95% for humidity monitoring and 5.37 nm/°C in the range of 20°C –50°C for temperature monitoring.74

Swellable polymer cladding (polyvinylpyrrolidone [PVP]) proved effective in humidity measurement. In the presence of water, the swelling of the polymer changes its refractive index, which changes the amount of light leaking from the fiber. The curvature of 0.2 cm−1 improved the sensitivity 30 times above the non-curved sensor.75

Au/SiO2 plasmonic nanocomposite optical fiber sensor that is based on sol-gel was used for thermal monitoring of Li-ion batteries. The working principle is based on the effect of temperature-dependent charge mobility and resistivity change due to electron-phonon scattering of the embedded Au nanoparticles. The whole sensor structure was then combined with wireless long-range (LoRa) technology, demonstrating the possibility of low-energy and low-cost IoT (Internet of Things [IoT]) deployment. The measured temperature response exhibited a high R2 value of 0.9962, and an average temperature sensitivity of 10% transmission change/°C was resolved.76

Food processing and pharmaceutics

The same principle as previously discussed can also be exploited for use in the food processing and pharmaceutical industries. An illustrative example is the detection of chlorogenic acid (CGA) in green tea products, achieved by a bent chitosan-coated sensor creating a membrane on the surface of the fiber sensitive to CGA. The sensor showed high sensitivity with a 0.018 μg/mL detection limit.8

For iron concentration measurement, a U-bent sensor with a removed cladding section was developed. The sensor performance was then experimentally evaluated on commercial iron supplements. The measured sensitivity was 0.0316 O.D/ppm (O.D stands for optical density), with a linear regression coefficient (R2) of 98.1%.77

A hand-held, highly sensitive U-bent POF sensor for non-destructive milk fat concentration monitoring was manufactured. Milk fat concentration and its variation are crucial parameters for assessing the quality of milk products in the dairy industry. The sensor reached an optical absorbance sensitivity of 0.15 ΔA/Δ% fat.78

Liquid level monitoring

An intriguing industrial field of application for the evanescent-wave-based sensor is liquid level monitoring. This can be achieved by wrapping an optical fiber around a square cylinder. Bent sections of the fiber then interact with the rising water level by an increase of light leakage into the medium, resulting in measurable light loss. The usable measuring range was 80 mm with a sensitivity of 0.088 dB/mm and a resolution of 1.03 mm.81

The same principle can be applied to the multi-S-bend sensor, which proved its effectiveness as well. S-bend sections were created using a thermal setting while rolling POF onto heated metal cylinders. In this case, the measuring range increased to 100 mm with the highest achieved sensitivity of 0.04 dB/mm.82

For the kerosene concentration range from 0% to 15% and 15%–50%, a sensitivity of 390 nW/% and 110 nW/% was achieved.83

Magnetic field sensing

The evanescent-field-based U-bend optical fiber sensor was also used for magnetic field sensing. This sensor was coated in a magnetic fluid, which affects the evanescent field leakage by interacting with the surrounding magnetic field. Industrial impact and deployment potential

The aforementioned sensors showcased a variety of possible industrial deployments with overlap into multiple different fields. Mainly the chemical, food processing, pharmaceutical, environmental, and petroleum industries, as they all demand high-sensitivity deployments. This is especially critical when it comes to quality control or safety in those industries, when any abnormalities could have a worldwide impact. In terms of scalability, a necessity for individual calibration and fluctuations in the surrounding environment could pose a challenge. But this can be overcome with the use of wavelength and time-division multiplexing. With this in mind, a scalable networked sensing system built on top of these sensors has the potential for adoption in domains where precision, adaptability, and real-time responsiveness will be required.

Applications of side-coupled optical fiber sensors

The working principle of these sensors is based on a twisted fiber structure. This structure consists of one active fiber and one or more passive fibers. The macrobending and the evanescent field effect are responsible for light leakage from the active fiber to the passive fibers. When introduced to external phenomena, the amount of leaked light changes. Based on this change, the measurement of this phenomenon can be concluded. This simple but effective and robust design ensures possible applications in a wide range of industry domains, often based on monitoring displacement, angle, rotation, refractive index, humidity, liquid level, pressure, and force. Angle and rotation sensing

A similar application of side-coupled sensors, based on the same principle of changes in structure’s bending radius, is used in angle and rotation monitoring. One such sensor was created by connecting one end of the twisted pair of fibers to a metallic strip, which is being displaced by an attached stepper motor. This displacement then changes the bending radius of the fibers. The sensor achieved a sensitivity of 2.2 nW/° and a resolution of 0.45°.38

This configuration was experimentally validated on a rotating robotic arm. The sensor could operate a full rotational cycle from 0° to 360° using different speeds of 2, 3, 5, and 10°/s with an approximate sensitivity of 8.69 nW/°.91

It was further advanced by multiplexing three of these sensors to achieve full spatial rotation monitoring. All three sensors could monitor rotation up to 360° and temperature up to 50°C.37

At last, the three multiplexed sensors were tested directly on a robot arm. The sensors operated simultaneously and monitored the robotic arm’s position in all three axes, achieving full 3D monitoring.92

Liquid level monitoring

Fiber-to-fiber light transmission susceptibility to the ambient environment can be exploited as a great sensor design candidate for liquid level measurement. The simplest sensor consisted of just two twisted POFs wrapped around a polyamide stick. The sensing principle is based on coupled energy variations that change with the surrounding environment. The sensor was tested with water, sugar, salt solutions, and multiple bending radii. With a liquid level from 0 to 350 mL, sensitivities of different solutions are 0.0014, 0.0024, and 0.0026 μW/mm, respectively.93

A similar sensor design—coiled around a helical structure—was tested with plain water, as well as 9.1% and 16.7% NaCl solutions. For a 150 mm range, sensitivities for bent sections were 12.97, 13.42, and 14.12 nW/mm and for straight sections, 3.01, 3.22, and 3.58 nW/mm.36

Further development of this sensor incorporated UV optical cement into the gap between the POFs to improve the side-coupling ratio. The range was increased to 100 cm with a resolution of 2 cm, and the measured sensitivity for water was 2.45 times higher than the previous design.39

Improvement in the form of a dual-point cascade sensor designed with twisted POF around a primary fiber and a support structure was demonstrated. In a 0–12 cm range, sensor parts achieved R2 values of 0.9924 and 0.9925.94

Further enhanced sensor introduces a multipoint liquid level by cascading three sensors. The sensors achieved measuring range up to 12 cm and sensitivities of about 0.2726, 0.1715, and 0.1281 μW/cm.95

Another approach used a twisted and a parallel helical macro-bend coupled structure (THMBCS and PHMBCS). These configurations were supported by a nylon rod, showcasing the ability to perform dynamic measurements with a continuously changing water level. The average sensitivity of both structures was lower than 0.3 mm. Experiments resulted in a bigger range of 350 mm for THMBCS and 70 mm for PHMBCS. THMBCS also exhibited superior linearity with a headroom for improvements.96

Humidity and refractive index

Refractive index and humidity sensing can also be done using a side-coupled sensor. Again, variations in the amount of transmitted light between the fibers are caused by interactions with the changing environment in the vicinity of the twisted structure. In the first case, a new type of sensor was manufactured using two fibers placed in a concentric semicircle manner. Due to the bending radius, this position allows light from the first active fiber to be leaked into the second passive fiber. Testing with glycerol solution showed sensitivities of 115.70, 63.29, and 33.12 dB/RIU for bending ratios 6, 12, and 17, respectively.97

A simple twisted fiber structure was used to measure relative humidity. Figure 11 shows a close-up photograph of the sensor probe. Agarose gel was applied to the sensing region. When in contact with moisture, it changes its RI and influences the light leakage from one fiber to another. Industrial impact and deployment potential

Sensors based on side-coupling present a compelling solution across a variety of industrial domains. These sensors have an impact on industries such as automotive, robotics, manufacturing, aerospace, and transportation, focusing on fluid management, displacement or rotation monitoring, and pressure. The flexible geometry typically used in these sensors offers possible integration even in complex surfaces or moving parts, demanded by the industry. Their natural suitability for multiplexing in cascaded or spatially distributed arrangements enables multi-point and multi-axis deployments. Another advantage is the minimal hardware needed for such scalability, and with the increasing demand for industrial sensing networks, side-coupled configurations represent a comprehensive competitor.

Applications of reflection-based optical fiber sensors

Reflection-based sensors introduce an alternative configuration to intensity-based sensing. These sensors are often formed by two or more optical fibers bundled to create a sensor structure. One of the fibers is typically an actively transmitting one, with the rest used to receive reflected light, whose intensity is modified by either a distance change between the sensor head and the reflective surface or via the influence of the solution in which the reflective surface is submerged. This provides a relatively cheap, robust, and easy-to-manufacture design with great use in industrial settings, such as displacement, vibrations, pressure, temperature, analyte concentration, and electrical quantities. Summary of studies of reflection-based optical fiber sensors

Displacement, angle, and vibration sensing

An angular displacement sensor was constructed using an optical fiber bundle, with one emitting and multiple receiving fibers perpendicular to the reflecting plane, as well as two more optical fiber bundles comprising only receiving fibers leaned at an angle from the first fiber bundle. This sensor has achieved a range of 74.4° and a sensitivity of 0.051 V/°.102

Another approach utilized single and bundle emitting and receiving fibers. The sensor was created to monitor small angles ranging from −100 to 100 arcmin. The setup using single fibers achieved a sensitivity of 9.5 ± 0.210 mV/arcmin at the negative angles and 9.76 ± 0.231 mV/arcmin at the positive angles, while the setup using fiber bundles achieved a sensitivity of 10.93 ± 0.200 mV/arcmin at negative angles and 10.74 ± 0.166 mV/arcmin at positive angles.4

A displacement sensor for microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) was also tested. This sensor consisted of one fiber, which acted as emitting and receiving fiber. For a mirror-reflecting surface, the sensor achieved a range of up to 400 μm, a resolution of 15 nm, and a sensitivity of 0.19 V/nm. Furthermore, the sensor enables the characterization of a Quartz Tuning Fork with a very rough surface as an example of MEMS with a vibration of 92 nm amplitude corresponding to the sensitivity of 0.017 mV/nm.103

Another displacement sensor created with one emitting and two receiving fibers was used for atomic force microscope tip approach monitoring. This sensor showed great positioning accuracy and repeatability and helped optimize the atomic force microscope tip approach, which can be finished in 20 s.104

A sensor based on a seven-core few-mode fiber was used to measure displacement. The working principle was based on the fiber placement in close proximity to a mirror surface. Two cores acted as transmitters, and five cores were receiving. The measurement range was about 320 μm, together with a high sensitivity of 53.87 nW/μm and a dead zone of 70 μm.105

A vibration using an aperture within a proof mass was tested. The sensor used one fiber as an emitting and receiving fiber and an aperture within a proof mass attached to four springs between the fiber’s end and the mirror. Three prototypes with natural frequencies of 91, 130, and 200 Hz were tested against a commercial accelerometer and achieved sensitivities of 163, 183, and 206 mV/g for acceleration, minimum detectable accelerations of 0.082, 0.086, and 0.082, and dynamic ranges of 18–45, 26–52, and 20–120 Hz.106

Another sensor for vibration sensing in machining operations to control their quality and stability was also tested. The sensor was applied for monitoring milling tool bar vibrations and was constructed using four optical fiber bundles with one emitting fiber surrounded by multiple receiving fibers. Two fiber bundles were used to monitor one axis of the milling tool bar, each from one side. The sensor achieved a range of more than 2 mm and a maximum linear error of 0.216%.40

Pressure and temperature monitoring

A fiber structure with one active and one or more receiving fibers proved effective for reflection-based pressure and temperature sensing in industrial settings. Movement or deformation of the reflective surface and the change in the optical parameters of the cavity between the fibers and the reflective surface affect the reflection of light and create a shift in light intensity. A novel sensor based on a silicon diaphragm with a SI3N4 film is crucial for reflecting light from an active fiber into two receiving fibers. Measurements were conducted up to 20 MPa with a −7.2 mV/MPa sensitivity. The construction of this sensor allows usage in flammable environments such as the petroleum and oil industries.107

A similar concept was explored using only two fibers, with microspherical ends fitted inside a quartz tube within a high borosilicate glass substrate (HBGS) casing and a silicon-sensitive diaphragm. The sensor was tested over a range of 1 MPa (the experimental setup is visible in Figure 13) and achieved a high sensitivity of 0.139 mV/kPa, a temperature coefficient of 0.87 mV/°C for temperatures of 20°C to 150°C, and a dynamic response of 1 kHz. Analyte concentration and refractive index sensing

Based on the working principle, reflection-based fiber optic sensors are a prime candidate for monitoring analyte concentration. The reflective surface is submerged in the examined solution, and the analyte concentration can be measured via variations in light loss. A sensor designed to monitor hydrocarbon levels in distilled water was manufactured from one emitting and receiving fiber and a flat mirror. Experimental testing with an average of 2.37% and 23.16% hydrocarbon concentrations concluded linearities of 1.18 and 0.965, respectively, and high repeatability.109

The same sensor structure was used for the measurement of the purity of ethanol. With an ethanol concentration of 95%–100%, the sensor sensitivity was 0.999 V/%, and the resolution was 0.5%. The sensor could also be used to monitor sugar parameters in the sugar industry.110

A more complex sensor, based on one transmitting fiber and a bundle of receiving fibers, was also used to examine hydrocarbon levels in water. An optimal displacement of the sensor was found to be 1.5 m. For 0% to 20% hydrocarbon concentration, the sensor exhibited a sensitivity of 0.12 V/wt%, repeatability of 96%, resolution of 0.027 V, and 0.23% detection limit. The average linearity index of 0.98 and 1.04 was measured for hydrocarbon concentrations of 6% and 14%, validating the sensor performance.111

A sensor manufactured with one transmit fiber and 10 receiving fibers divided into two groups based on their core radius, with a concave mirror, is a promising solution for saline concentration measurement using a refractive index, which is critical for protecting concrete structures in a marine environment. Measurement methods based on the varying sensor position and liquid level are tested with four multiplexed sensors. The sensor reached a sensitivity of 15.419 and 14,847.486 mm/RIU at RI = 1.3525 for each method, respectively.112

Electrical quantities measurement

An interesting field of application is electrical quantities. Here, voltage and current could be transformed into displacement via the Lorentz law and piezoelectric components with a reflective film. A sensor based on a single active fiber and six receiving fibers was tested for DC offset, low frequencies, and dynamic behavior. In the last-mentioned case, the voltage and current errors were 0.00059 and 0.0011 p.u., respectively, and the frequency error was 2 MHz. The sensor was also implemented in a universal grid analyzer platform and met the required parameters to comply with the IEEE phasor measurement unit C37.118 standard.3

The measurement of three-phase electrical systems with voltage up to 150 V was tested with a matching sensor design. Experiments concluded a flat response from 10 to 500 kHz with a sharp resonance peak at 2 kHz, although measurements imply a dynamic range of 60 dB for the band from 10 to 3,000 kHz. Similar behavior is also expected for high voltages, indicating further scalability.113

Industrial impact and deployment potential

A plethora of reflection-based sensors with industrial impact potential was described, with the main possible industrial fields of application being manufacturing, automotive, power distribution, environmental and chemical industries, or precise instruments. In these sectors, high resolution and fast response are of importance, with emphasis on achieving these results even in confined spaces. The point-based nature, which enables these results, also limits the possibilities of large-scale multiplexing compared to other configurations. The scalability can be further extended by integration with wavelength and time multiplexing strategies or deployment in modular industrial monitoring and control systems.

Other types of intensity-based sensors and applications

The most widespread intensity-modulated sensor types in industrial applications have been discussed in previous sections. This section covers other not-so-commonly used sensor types, as well as different yet still intriguing types of applications of all the sensor types mentioned before. It first focuses on the FBG, then on coupler-based, cavity-based, and external-coupling-based types of intensity-modulated sensors, and then it shifts to other possible applications in industry-related fields, as even those sensors pose an interesting view on possibly more niche applications, overlapping into various industrial domains. Sections based on a particular sensor type will also include an introductory theoretical part, where the working principle is discussed further.

FBG-based intensity-modulated sensors

An FBG is a structure inside an optical fiber formed by a periodic change in refractive index in the fiber’s core. FBGs are used in many applications across the sensory domain. Their ability to reflect certain wavelengths under the influence of the measured phenomenon is widely used and is one of the most commonly used in the sensing field. Another approach is to use intensity modulation. One possible realization of intensity-based FBG interrogation uses two FBGs and translates the wavelength shift to light intensity variation. One of the FBGs is used as a reference and the other for measuring. Only the spectrum of the measuring FBG experiences a shift, and by shifting, it overlaps with the spectrum of the reference FBG. The measured light intensity is then the total reflected power determined by the area under both spectra.114,115 Another possible realization is using light with a gradual intensity change in its spectrum. As the wavelength of the light reflected by the FBG shifts, its intensity also changes based on the used spectrum. Unlike traditional wavelength-based FBG sensors, intensity-modulated sensors monitor changes in the reflected or transmitted light intensity, offering simpler interrogation schemes and cost-effective solutions. The changes in light intensity are caused by external perturbations such as strain, temperature, or pressure. A summary table is presented at the end of this section.116,117,118

A high SNR system incorporating FBG was presented and tested for temperature measurement. A series of two FBGs and circulators was used, with the first FBG connected to the first circulator and the output connected to another FBG and circulator pair. As the reflected light from the first FBG is fed into the second FBG, the resulting reflected light is an overlap between both specters. The received voltage is then transformed into temperature via a calibrated relationship. Practical tests resulted in the accuracy of ±0.5°C, having a repeatability error of 1.8%, a linearity error of 3%, and a hysteresis error of 1.5% in the 10°C to 90°C range. SNR was improved by 24.5 dB.114

Temperature and liquid level measurement was also achieved with an air bubble between a single-mode and a no-core fiber with FBG attached to the end. Temperature dependence is achieved through the air bubble, and the water level is monitored with the FBG. Two light peaks are reflected. The first has a liquid level sensitivity of −0.6899 dB/mm and the linearity of 0.9903, and the temperature sensitivity reaches 0.1487 dB/°C with the linearity of 0.9937. For the second one, liquid level sensitivity reaches −0.6269 dB/mm with the linearity of 0.9832, and the temperature sensitivity is 0.118

Two FBGs and a coupler were used to measure strain. A 1×2 coupler was used to divide light from the LED into two FBGs. Tension and compression were applied to the first FBG to generate strains. The second FBG was used for temperature compensation. The measurement resolution was about 6 μϵ.115

Another FBG-based system was used to measure strain and vibrations. A bent single-mode fiber was used to induce the whispering gallery mode (WGM) interference as an edge filter with FBG as a sensor. Coupler-based sensors

Coupler-based sensors operate on the principle of evanescent field coupling between two closely spaced optical waveguides, due to the overlap of their evanescent fields. External physical perturbations—such as strain, temperature, pressure, or changes in the surrounding refractive index—alter these parameters by modifying the phase mismatch between the propagating modes and the effective refractive indices of the coupled cores. For instance, axial strain can elongate the coupling region and slightly change the core spacing, while temperature variations induce thermo-optic effects and differential thermal expansion, and refractive index changes in the surrounding medium affect the penetration depth of the evanescent field. These variations shift the coupling ratio, thereby redistributing optical power between the output ports in a predictable manner. The resulting intensity changes can be detected with simple photodetectors and directly correlated to the measurand.125,126,127 These sensors offer high sensitivity, inherent multiplexing capabilities, and compatibility with all-fiber systems. A similar structure was tested with a 1×2 coupler, this time not incorporating an arm with a reference photodetector. Measurable frequencies were reduced to 0–1,300 Hz with a sensitivity of 2.45 mV/μm and a 1 μm resolution for the amplitude of vibration.128

Cavity-based sensors

Cavity-based sensors represent a distinct class of intensity-modulated devices in which the measurand is transduced through variations in the optical coupling efficiency across a free-space or structured gap—referred to as the cavity—between two fiber ends or between a fiber and a reflective element. In the simplest configuration, light emerging from the transmitting fiber expands in free space according to the fiber’s numerical aperture, and only a portion of this diverging beam is captured by the receiving fiber. The coupling efficiency is determined by parameters such as axial separation, lateral displacement, angular misalignment, and the refractive index of the medium within the cavity.5,129,130 External perturbations—such as displacement, vibration, pressure, strain, temperature, or changes in the surrounding medium—affect these parameters either directly, by physically altering the fiber alignment or separation, or indirectly, by modifying the optical properties of the cavity medium. A summary of the cavity-based sensors is presented in Table 5 at the end of the next subsection.

A high-resolution displacement sensor based on the face-coupling method was tested. This sensor uses one optical fiber of 2 mm diameter with a hollowed-out cavity of 0.5 mm diameter in its core and a second optical fiber of 250 μm diameter positioned inside the cavity. Multiple lengths of the cavity were tested, and the best result was achieved with a length of 4 mm. This sensor has achieved a sensitivity of 6.742 nW/μm and a resolution of 14.83 nm in the 3,500–4,000 μm range and a sensitivity of 11.56 nW/μm and a resolution of 8.647 nm in the 0–2,500 μm range.5

A cantilever beam vibration sensor based on the face-coupling method was also tested. The sensor was fabricated by fixing the faces of two optical fibers at the ends of a capillary tube facing each other, with one fiber protruding 12 mm inside the cavity. The sensor schematic can be seen in Figure 16. Vibration then causes the misalignment of the optical fiber ends. The sensor achieved the measuring range of 50 to 192 kHz with a resolution of 1.64 Hz.129Figure 16Schematic diagram of the vibration sensor, based on two single-mode fibers and an air cavity between themThis whole structure is fitted into the capillary glass tube with fixed entry points.129 Reprinted with permission from Liu, C. et al., Optical Fiber Technology, 67. ©2021 Elsevier.

A curvature sensor based on a no-core fiber (NCF) section spliced with a hollow core fiber (HCF) section spliced on both sides to a single-mode fiber was demonstrated. External-coupling-based sensors

External-coupling-based sensors differ fundamentally from side-coupling-based designs in that the energy transfer between fibers is mediated by an independent external optical path or source. In such configurations, the sensing region is typically formed by positioning one or more fibers in proximity to a separate optical beam path, so that light from an external source is injected into the sensing fiber or light from the fiber is reintroduced after interacting with the environment.132,133 External perturbations, such as vibration, displacement, acoustic pressure, refractive index changes, or scattering variations in the surrounding medium, can modulate the amount of light that reaches the receiving fiber by altering either the optical path geometry or the beam’s propagation characteristics. A summary of the external-coupling-based sensors is presented in Table 5 at the end of this subsection.

One such sensor was developed for monitoring electrical arcs. Light created by an electrical arc is coupled into the fiber and detected on both ends by receivers. Location can then be extrapolated based on experimentally tested values of attenuation. On a 10-m fiber, spatial resolution reached circa 10 cm. This method’s limitation was mainly the high attenuation of the fiber, which limited the measurable length.132

In another study, an external-coupling-based sensor for multiplane angle assessment was introduced. Weight-in-motion monitoring is also a potentially interesting field in logistics. A simple intensity-based optical fiber sensor with a reference and a measuring arm was proposed. The measuring arm is made up of fiber, elliptically wrapped around a rubber, and placed in a pad. The sensor was successfully tested.136

A U-bent macrobending sensor with a bend radius of 30 mm was used for soil moisture monitoring. This soil moisture level is an important parameter for farmers to monitor when growing plants. The sensor achieved a sensitivity of 2.885 · 1010 W/%VWC, R2 97.14%, and a standard deviation of 0.6%.137

Another reflection-based sensor was employed for food deformation monitoring during the drying process, which is important for food quality control. The sensor was constructed by attaching a glass tube with a metallic disc at the end of the fiber and encasing it in a silicone buffer sleeve. As the food dries and shrinks, it deforms the sleeve and causes a change in the distance between the fiber end and the reflective metallic disc, changing the amount of light reflected to the fiber. The four sensors tested achieved a dynamic range of 0%–20%, a sensitivity of 3–27 V, and a resolution of 0.08%–0.72% in external strain monitoring.144

A reflection-based sensor was also developed for ice thickness and type detection. The sensor probe consisted of a prism and four fibers. One of them was transmitting and three were receiving. Two of them were used to measure ice thickness, and one with an oblique face was used to detect the type of ice. Practical experiments showed the ability of the sensors to measure thickness from 0.5 to 5 mm at temperatures of −5°C, −20°C, and −40°C, with a maximum error of 0.283 mm.145

A macrobending sensor for pavement strain monitoring was constructed by coiling an optical fiber around a rubber tube. As the tube is stretched, the winding pitch of the fiber changes, changing the bending radius. The sensor can monitor a large strain with a strain measurement range of up to 11% with a measurement error within an acceptable range.138

A microbending sensor was constructed for reused concrete aggregate strain and corrosion monitoring. The sensor worked well for determining both the initiation of corrosion and the later progression of corrosion in rebar.142

Strain was also monitored using a sensor comprising a fiber lead at one end of a cavity with a circular hole in the middle and multiple fibers at the other end arranged in a circle. As the strain is applied to the sensor, the circular hole moves, changing the amount of light coupled into the fibers at the end of the cavity. The sensor is meant for monitoring the deformation and deflection of the supporting structural elements of buildings and structures.146

A microbending sensor was used for strain monitoring in concrete. Multiple configurations of the sensors were tested: straight, sinusoidal, loop, and triangle shapes. The sensors were also tested at different angles of embedding in the concrete. The triangle configuration embedded at 60° achieved a range value of 1.975 μW, the sensitivity of 0.790 μW/cm, and a resolution of 0.001 cm.141

A macrobending sensor was constructed for displacement and monitoring cracks in materials or structures. The first test was done by winding the fiber around a cylinder that turns based on displacement. Another test was able to ensure temperature independence using splitters. The maximum measurement error was 0.277 mm, and the minimum error was 0.009 mm.140

Deep lateral displacement, which is important for geological body safety evaluation, was monitored using a macrobend sensor constructed by spirally twining a fiber around an elastic rubber rod. The lateral deformation of the rod causes a change in the bending radius of the fiber. This sensor achieved a relative measurement error between the measured displacement and the actual displacement of less than 10%.139

A completely new approach was tested, with a sensor based on scattered light intensity used for displacement monitoring. It was constructed by placing a fiber between two toothed plates. As the plates get closer, bending is introduced to the fiber, and the intensity of Raman Stokes in the back-scattered light changes. The sensor has achieved a sensitivity of 0.343 dB/mm and a range of 0 to 5 mm, with a measurement accuracy of 0.1 mm.147

Industrial impact and deployment potential

In contrast to widely adopted intensity-based sensor types, the sensors described in this section are more uniquely tailored for specific applications in various industries. As their reach is immense, the main fields cannot be generalized. Even with a more complex design and alignment, new possibilities are presented where more traditional designs could fall short. With this in mind, point-based application-specific deployments are more typical, thus limiting scalability, although more advanced multiplexing methods can enable their use in industrial sensor platforms.

Performance comparison and benchmarking

The diversity of intensity-based optical fiber sensors makes a unified comparison challenging. Each of the aforementioned sensor types has been optimized for specific applications, resulting in varying sensitivities, resolutions, and ranges. With this in mind, this section will be structured around each of the sensor types and their respective performance and scalability. This will make this section clearer even for those interested in only one or more types.

The performance of intensity-based optical fiber sensors is fundamentally defined by their ability to accurately, reliably, and rapidly transduce physical or chemical changes into measurable optical signals. This ability may vary depending on the sensor architecture, as each type exhibits unique operational advantages tailored to specific sensing environments and requirements. The potential for scalable and multiplexed systems is substantial, especially thanks to their simplicity and low cost. While not all sensor types are suited for large-scale integration, several have proven their effectiveness.

Macrobending sensors are widely recognized for their reliable performance in displacement, pressure, and angular sensing. They offer high accuracy and resolution while maintaining low measurement errors. Their response is linear and stable across a broad range of mechanical deformations. The mechanical nature of these sensors ensures a rapid response to changes, which makes them ideal for real-time monitoring. Multiplexing and thus scaling abilities were also presented, where configurations using multiple in-series sensors differentiated by wavelength-dependent intensity were used for pressure and load monitoring.47,48

Microbending designs exhibit excellent results in detecting localized pressure, force, and surface anomalies due to their high sensitivity to small-scale deformations. In well-controlled mechanical assemblies, accuracy and resolution can be high; however, random microstructure-induced perturbations may introduce noise if not adequately managed. Their response speed is typically high, as the working principle is mostly mechanical. Possible scalability was confirmed by basic multiplexing through spatial separation, which was tested using OTDR. This has been applied for corrosion, with the resolution limited by the OTDR.29

Side-coupled configurations based on twisted or parallel fibers performed highly in multi-axis displacement, angle, and liquid level sensing. In these fields, high accuracy, resolution, and reliability were achieved. The working principle here is also dependent on the mechanical changes, rendering the response time low. This category offered the most straightforward potential for multiplexing in their cascaded configurations, which allowed for not only 1D but also 2D and 3D tracking.35,86,87,88,92 The cascaded agarose-coated sensors also allowed multi-node environmental humidity sensing.99 This multiplexing design is helped in its simplicity by the individual receiving fibers being separated from each other, allowing for good scalability.

Reflection-based structures provide high accuracy and excellent resolution in static and dynamic sensing scenarios. Due to the reflection-based design, response speed is fast enough to capture fast transient phenomena such as mechanical vibrations or rapid displacement on the smallest scale. Over time, reflective surface quality and alignment precision can negatively influence the accuracy and repeatability. These sensors are typically deployed as discrete sensor heads and do not introduce much possibility for multiplexing and scalability. Possible multiplexing could be achieved if wavelength or time division techniques are employed.

Evanescent field sensors are optimized for refractive index and analyte concentration detection with high sensitivity and accuracy. Response time is typically greater than in other types due to reliance on molecular diffusion or surface adsorption. For perfect results, environmental stability is critical; furthermore, this pushes the need for steady-state or slow-changing surroundings. They also face challenges in multiplexing and scalability due to the need for individual coatings and precise calibration. Nevertheless, sensors using identical coatings could also be arrayed along a fiber using wavelength or time division techniques.

Comparative analysis of plastic and silica optical fibers

Most of these sensors are based on silica (SOF) or POF; thus, comparison should also be made between them. SOFs typically exhibit very low attenuation and maintain stable performance over a wide spectral range. With these properties, these fibers are characterized by long range and high precision even in harsh industrial environments. Silica fibers are also more rigid and have a smaller core diameter, which could lead to complications with handling and applications requiring tight bends.66,85,108 POFs, on the other hand, represent a cheaper and more flexible counterpart. Core diameters are typically bigger, simplifying the coupling of light from lower-cost sources like LEDs and ensuring tolerance for smaller bending radii and repeated mechanical deformation.36,86,93 POFs exhibit higher attenuation, lower thermal and chemical tolerance, and a smaller spectral range. SOFs excel in applications where long range, precision, and high environmental resilience are required. POFs are better suited for short-range scenarios where mechanical flexibility and easier handling are dominant.

Balancing simplicity and performance in IM-FOS designs

The simplicity of IM-FOS is one of its greatest strengths in possible industrial applications, although this same characteristic also creates potential disadvantages. The balance between keeping the sensor design cost-effective, simple, and scalable versus pushing for more complex, precise, and sensitive systems is a recurring theme. For example, bending solutions are usually robust, cheap, and easy to multiplex but offer lower sensitivity compared to the evanescent-field-based, interferometric, or FBG designs. One such example could be the achieved sensitivity for magnetic field sensing of 0.0003 dB/Gauss52 for macrobending, −0.193 dB/Oe (−0.1533 dB/Gauss) for FBG,120 and 2.481 dB/mT (0.2481 dB/Gauss) for an evanescent-field-based sensor.85 A similar case can be found for the microbend-based (0.6 nW/N56) and coupling-based (0.750 μW/N5) sensor for force measurement. As was demonstrated before, sensors based on the evanescent field interaction exhibit high precision and resolution at the cost of complicated handling and higher cost. Side-coupled sensors offer an intermediate path, retaining the simple manufacturing and low cost, but for higher sensitivities, cascading multiple sensors and higher sensor complexity is often required.35,87,88 Another example could be the pressure monitoring, where the achieved sensitivity for the side-coupling configuration was 421 nW/MPa,101 compared to −7.2 mV/MPa107 for the reflection-based sensor. Reflection-based configurations are also more sensitive and precise at the cost of more complex alignment and susceptibility to contamination. From the provided examples, it is visible that a trade-off for lower cost and overall increased simplicity is a decrease in precision and sensitivity compared to more expensive and complex alternatives. Advantages and limitations

The intensity-based optical fiber sensors offer a compelling combination of simplicity, versatility, and cost-effectiveness. Many configurations were designed to detect a plethora of environmental changes, making them suitable for a wide range of industrial applications. Due to some aspects of their design, these sensors have great advantages over other measurement methods. From a technical point of view, these sensory structures also have noticeable disadvantages that must be taken into account during design and implementation. Both of these perspectives will be discussed further.

Strengths

The advantages of intensity-based sensors lie in their construction, realization, and maintenance especially compared to other types of fiber optic sensors. Macro/microbend, reflective, and side-coupled sensors often use commercially available single or multimode fibers and other components. This makes production and sustainability simple, relatively cheap, and scalable. This structural simplicity also greatly accelerates prototyping and research in this particular field, simplifying the process of integrating new sensors into existing systems.4,5,6

Compared to fiber optic sensors based on wavelength or phase-shift, the interrogation system needed to extract measurement data from the optical signal is considerably simpler and cheaper. For most sensor systems, a basic photodetector is used, further reducing the complexity of the whole system.4,5,6

The immunity to electromagnetic interference (EMI) makes them ideal for use in high-voltage or electromagnetically noisy settings. Due to the low attenuation of the optical fiber, monitoring can be done over long distances and in areas with a hazardous environment, increasing the safety of the workers.2,3

The multiplexing and scalability capacity is another strength of the intensity-based sensors. Typically, spatial, geometric, or time/wavelength division multiplexing technologies are applied for these cases. Multi-point sensing is also possible with a suitable topology or distributed layout designs.3,7

Weaknesses

While the advantages have been discussed, the weaknesses should also be noted. Non-measurand factors such as connector losses, fiber misalignment, and power source fluctuations can add undesirable effects and negatively influence the whole system. In real-world conditions, mainly, measurement accuracy and reproducibility can be affected by such changes. Careful calibrations are therefore necessary, further complicating data interpretation in dynamic environments.3,6 In long-term deployments, equipment fatigue should also be considered. The most critical parts are photodetectors and light sources. Their aging leads to signal degradation and potential intensity variations or noise insertion, which could be interpreted as a measurand. Mechanical wear can also affect splices and connectors, leading to an increase in attenuation over time.1,29

Temperature instability is also an important factor. These sensors exhibit sensitivity to thermal expansion, as the refractive index changes in both the core and cladding of the fiber and in the surrounding environment. Alterations of the refractive index directly influence the behavior of transmitted light, thus the behavior of the sensor. Instability can be negated via compensation or insulation.1,2,6 Mechanical stress and vibrations are another potential negative phenomenon that is common in industrial scenarios. Such an effect could cause unintended microbends or bigger alterations in the optical path. Prolonged exposure to vibrations could lead to loosening of components. In harsh environments, contamination is also an important factor, particularly in open components such as connectors. In such environments, encapsulation degradation can also negatively affect the sensing system.2,3

Many of the sensors mentioned in previous parts of the review are one of a kind. This creates a disadvantage in the form of a lack of standardization. This inconsistency produces the need for individual calibration for each of the sensors and could cause a complicated deployment at a larger scale. Even slight changes to the sensor structure can completely change the outcome of the measurement, resulting in high sensor-to-sensor variability.2,9 Previously mentioned limitations, such as environmental interference and component fatigue, are closely related to calibration issues. Long exposure to harsh environments can lead to sensor drift, causing measurement errors and requiring regular calibration and system inspections. The same applies to equipment fatigue. Some sensors have nonlinear response characteristics, further increasing the calibration difficulty.4,5

Another concern is mechanical fragility, especially for polished, tapered, or highly bent structures. Simple fiber breakage or component failure is a relatively common difficulty, especially in heavy industry settings. Also, improper deployment or hidden defects that were not noticed during implementation could lead to fatal consequences.1,2,29

In real-life scenarios, typically, all of the negative effects affect the whole system, creating the need for robust and well-engineered sensing platforms.

Future directions and research challenges

Despite their simplicity and economic benefits, IM-FOSs face a set of challenges that must be addressed to enable their broader adoption in critical and high-reliability applications. Future research must balance the drive for performance improvements with the need for cost-effective, scalable, and certifiable solutions.

A key direction lies in the integration of machine learning algorithms into sensor interrogation systems. These tools offer powerful means to compensate for nonlinearity, environmental drift, and cross-sensitivity, which are common in IM-FOS architectures. Data-driven models can improve resolution and robustness, especially in dynamic or noisy environments, and allow for intelligent self-calibration and condition-based diagnostics.4,9 However, implementing such solutions in real-time, embedded systems remains an ongoing technical hurdle that will require further miniaturization and optimization of processing units. Examples of successful machine learning implementation can be found in Zhu et al. and Zhu and Huang,134,135 where machine learning was used to expand the dynamic range.

Advances in hybrid materials and structural integration are also reshaping the potential of IM-FOSs. The use of nanostructured coatings, responsive polymer layers, and low-dimensional materials such as graphene or MoS2 (molybdenum disulfide) enables selective sensing, signal amplification, and even multi-analyte detection.8,9 Embedding optical fibers into composite materials, textiles, or soft robotic structures allows the development of “smart” systems with integrated sensing capabilities.2 These innovations expand the application domain but also impose new requirements on compatibility and mechanical resilience.1

Ensuring long-term reliability is another crucial frontier. In real-world deployments, IM-FOSs must withstand thermal cycling, mechanical stress, UV exposure, and moisture ingress—factors that can degrade optical performance over time. Research into protective encapsulation, use of flexible or polymer optical fibers, and the development of robust connector systems is essential to extend service life and reduce maintenance requirements.1,2,29

To move from laboratory prototypes to commercial products, sensors must also undergo substantial miniaturization and system integration. This includes the development of compact, alignment-free sensor heads, integrated photodetectors, and standardized interrogation electronics that reduce cost and improve manufacturability.4,5 Applications such as wearable systems, IoT nodes, or aerospace monitoring demand plug-and-play functionality with minimal power consumption and form factor.

Finally, despite the growing number of publications and prototypes, the field still lacks clear standardization frameworks. Without consistent definitions of performance metrics, calibration methods, and durability testing, it remains difficult to compare results or certify devices for regulated use.6 Establishing reference architectures and engaging with metrology institutes and industry stakeholders will be necessary steps toward certification and widespread adoption.2,9 Few existing standards could be potentially used, such as IEC 61757, which can be used to define a foundational standard for sensors. While it does not delve deeply into intensity modulation, it offers a structural framework for sensor specifications. The European initiative COST Action 299 “FIDES” developed the “Guideline for Use of Fibre Optic Sensors,” which outlines performance specifications and testing methodologies for various fiber-optic sensor types, including those based on intensity modulation. For calibration purposes, ISO/IEC 17025 could be used. Although not specific to fiber optic sensors, this standard outlines the general requirements for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories and is widely adopted for ensuring the accuracy and reliability of calibration processes.148,149

Given the current rise in IoT sensing demand, IM-FOSs could be a viable candidate, especially in regard to their inherent scalability, low-cost architecture, and multiplexing capability. Their compactness and relatively low energy demands are ideal for such deployments. Compact, low-power interrogation units can be co-located with wireless communication nodes, enabling on-site optical-to-electrical conversion, preliminary signal processing, and seamless transmission to cloud-based platforms. Energy harvesting could be utilized for longer life and maintenance-free deployments.76 Potential examples for such a system can be long pipeline monitoring53 or monitoring of structures affected by saline water.112 A working example was demonstrated in Su et al.,76 where LoRa was used to transmit measurement data from a sensor. A different approach to all-optical ultra-low-energy transmissions was introduced, with MEMS optical cavities being used. Transmission is then based on the light reflection, and the energy needed for such communication can be as low as 44 pJ.150 Although many of the previously mentioned research challenges must be addressed before the IM-FOSs can be fully utilized in IoT sensing networks, the main challenges are miniaturization, long-term reliability, robust interrogation hardware, and standardization.

All of the previously mentioned act as a barrier to widespread commercialization. These economic hurdles start with the unstandardized manufacturing, which leads to costly, limited production series of mostly hand-made products.6 This type of production process tends to create sensors with slightly different characteristics, and without concrete regulations and standards, mass production is limited.4 Economically, the cost difference over competing electronic, wavelength, and phase-based configurations can be eroded by the need for periodic alignments, inspections, and calibrations of specialized and custom interrogating systems. Some fields, such as medical diagnostics or civil engineering, require rigorous certifications, which can slow adoption and decrease market share, especially given the lack of internationally adopted standards. Addressing these concerns is crucial for the widespread commercialization and utilization of IM-FOSs.2,9

Conclusion

IM-FOSs have demonstrated broad applicability across industrial sectors due to their simplicity, cost-efficiency, and adaptability. This review highlighted a wide array of sensor types—including macrobending, microbending, evanescent field, side-coupled, and reflective configurations—each optimized for specific physical phenomena such as displacement, pressure, curvature, vibration, temperature, refractive index, and analyte concentration. Despite generally lower sensitivity compared to interferometric or Bragg-based alternatives, IM-FOSs offer unmatched scalability, robustness, and multiplexing capability. Technological advancements in fiber structuring, signal processing, and coating materials have significantly improved their reliability, miniaturization potential, and sensing selectivity.26,29 Comparative performance analysis and benchmark data clearly show that intensity-based sensors are suitable for both static and dynamic environments, offering viable solutions even in scenarios with limited space, variable temperatures, or exposure to electromagnetic interference.85

With the increasing demand for scalable, flexible, and cost-effective sensing technologies, intensity-based fiber optic systems are emerging as a cornerstone of modern sensing architectures. Their cost-effectiveness, ease of integration, and compatibility with wearable, structural, and environmental monitoring platforms make them ideal for Industry 4.0, IoT, smart infrastructure, biomedical diagnostics, and robotics. The growing need for real-time, distributed, and resilient sensing networks strongly favors the adoption of IM-FOSs, especially in applications where large sensor arrays or custom geometries are required. Future implementations will likely benefit from hybrid sensor designs combining intensity modulation with spectral or phase-based principles, the integration of AI-assisted data interpretation,29 and further advances in smart materials. These innovations will support high-sensitivity, multi-parameter detection while maintaining mechanical flexibility and robustness.

This review provides a comprehensive reference framework for researchers and engineers aiming to develop, refine, or apply intensity-based optical sensing systems. The simplicity and versatility of IM-FOSs offer a compelling alternative to more complex optical sensors, ensuring their continued relevance and evolution in a wide spectrum of applications. As the field progresses, collaboration across material science, photonics, and data science will be key to realizing the full potential of IM-FOS technologies. The continued exploration of novel fiber designs, interrogation schemes, and packaging techniques will further solidify their position in next-generation sensing architectures. By embracing interdisciplinary innovation and focusing on scalable manufacturing processes, IM-FOSs can transition from laboratory prototypes to mature technologies that reliably serve critical monitoring tasks in energy, transportation, healthcare, and beyond.
