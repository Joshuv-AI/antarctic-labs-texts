Association of allostatic load with functional disability in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Wanyu Zhao, Yanjun Si, Xueqin Li, Yunli Zhao, Shuli Jia, Birong Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAllostatic load (AL) is an index that manifests as cumulative wear and tear on multiple physiological systems resulting from repeated adaptation to stressors throughout the life course. Higher AL was found to be associated with increased risk of adverse health outcomes, but studies on functional disability among large Chinese older population were limited. We aimed to investigate the associations of AL with functional disability among Chinese older adults.Research design and methodsThis prospective cohort study included 5880 older adults who were at least 50 years old and participated in the CHARLS at baseline (2011 wave), with 3 follow-ups (2013, 2015 and 2018 waves). We selected 11 biomarkers from 4 physiological systems to construct AL. AL score was classified into three categories based on tertiles: 0–1 (low burden), 2–3 (medium burden) and 4–11 (high burden). Functional disability was assessed by activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL). Multivariable logistic regression models examined the association between baseline AL, transitions of AL burden and incidence of functional disability.ResultsIn cross-sectional analyses, medium and high AL burden were associated with an increased prevalence of ADL disability, while association was only significant between high AL burden and IADL disability. During the 7-year follow-up, 1102 and 1777 participants incident ADL disability and IADL disability, respectively. Those with medium and high AL burden had an increased risk of new-onset ADL disability than those with low AL burden (“medium”: OR = 1.25, 95%CI = 1.01, 1.55; “high”: OR = 1.69, 95%CI = 1.32, 2.18). As for IADL disability, association was only significant in “high” group (OR = 1.50, 95% CI = 1.24, 1.83). We also found keep medium/high AL burden in 2011 and 2015 was associated with new-onset ADL (OR = 2.27, 95%CI = 1.50, 3.44) and IADL disability (OR = 1.51, 95%CI = 1.11, 2.05) in 2018.ConclusionThese results show that higher AL predicts functional disability among Chinese older adults. Monitoring AL and paying close attention to those with medium/high AL burden may aid prevention of adverse health outcomes, thus giving a healthier ageing experience to a large proportion of the population.Keywords: Allostatic load, Functional disability, China health and retirement longitudinal study, Chinese older adults

Introduction

Allostatic load (AL) was first raised by McEwen and Stellar in 1993 [1], which defined as cumulative physiologic “wear and tear” resulting from repeated or chronic adaptation to stressors. In response to these stressors, different physiological systems interact at varying degrees of activity [1]. Repeated or chronic stress could first activate the neuroendocrine and immune system consistently, resulting in abnormal secretion of primary mediators (i.e., catecholamines, glucocorticoids, leukocytes and cytokines), which in turn affects metabolic and cardiovascular systems (known as secondary mediators) [2,3]. Because of this, allostatic load was usually calculated by a composite score derived from several biological markers such as blood pressure, C-reactive protein (CRP) and cholesterol from multiple physiologic systems [4]. In recent years, allostatic load was found to be a better predictor of adverse outcomes of multiple diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus and cancers [[5], [6], [7]]. Higher allostatic load was also suggested to be related with increased mortality in a recent meta-analysis [8].

As life expectancy of the global population is increasing, maintaining functional status required for independent living is of growing interest in later life. With the largest population of older people in the world, the number of people aged 60 years or above is expected to increase to 479 million by 2050, of which 66 million older people with functional disability [9], which not only threatens the quality of life of older people, but also exerts heavy burdens on individuals, families, and society. Although allostatic load associated with a decline in physical functioning in older adults was reported previously [[10], [11], [12], [13]], studies focused on the association between AL and activities of daily living (ADL)/instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) disability are limited, especially among large Chinese older population. López et al. found that baseline AL score may predict 5-year incident physical disability among Puerto Rican adults [14]. Regarding the mechanism, higher AL was related to physical functional decline (e.g. balance, limb strength and dexterity, gait) [11], impairing the ability to complete self-care activities directly. Allostatic overload may also affect neurological function [15] and further reduce the coordination of various tasks of daily living. Besides, AL reflects the vulnerability of older people [16] and assessment of AL may help to demarcate differences among older adults who otherwise seem deceptively similar before functional disability happens [17].

Therefore, we hypothesized that a higher burden of AL would be associated with increased risk of functional disability among older people in China. In this study, using nationally representative data from 2011 to 2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Survey (CHARLS), we aim to investigate the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between baseline AL burden and functional status among older adults. In addition, for AL is the time-varying variable, we also explore the associations between longitudinal transitions of AL with incident functional disabilities.

Material and methods

Study population and setting

The CHARLS is an ongoing, national representative longitudinal study of adults aged 45 and older in China. Study participants were interviewed beginning in 2011 and followed up every 2 years thereafter, and details on health, economic, and social circumstances were collected. Further details of study design and survey instruments have been reported previously [18]. Informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to data collection. The CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015).

For this study, we used data from four waves of the CHARLS run in 2011, 2013, 2015 and 2018. The study was divided into two parts: (1) In the cross-sectional analysis, we included data from participants aged 50 years and over, with information available on AL at baseline; (2) In the longitudinal analysis, we excluded subjects were lost or with missing data determining functional status during follow-up, and with ADL/IADL disability in 2011. The flowchart to assess the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between baseline AL and functional status was presented in Fig. 1. For blood test data was only collected in 2011 and 2015, we determined AL transition according to AL burden in 2011 and 2015 and incident functional disabilities were assessed in 2018.

Allostatic load

Based on previous research [19] and availability of data in the CHARLS, we selected 11 biomarkers from 4 physiological systems to construct AL, including systolic blood pressure(SBP), diastolic blood pressure(DBP), body mass index(BMI), high-density lipoprotein(HDL), low-density lipoprotein(LDL), total cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin, glucose, C-reactive protein(CRP), cystatin C and blood urea nitrogen. SBP, DBP and BMI were collected from physical examinations. BMI was calculated as body weight (kilograms) divided by height (metres) squared. SBP and DBP were measured three times (with 45 s apart) using a HEM-7200 electronic monitor (Omron (Dalian) Co., LTD., Dalian, China) [18]. We used the average of three measurements for further analyses. The methods of laboratory assay for the blood-based biomarkers were described in detail elsewhere [18,20].

To be in line with previous study of CHARLS [19], cutoff points for high-risk levels of each AL components were based on commonly used clinical cutoff points [20] and presented in Table S1. AL score was calculated as a count of these dichotomous indicators, ranging from 0 to 11. In addition to considered as continuous variable, AL score was classified into three categories based on tertiles [21,22]: 0–1 (low burden), 2–3 (medium burden) and 4–11 (high burden). According to AL burden in 2011 and 2015, We further defined transitions of AL as four categories: (1) Keep low AL: low AL burden in both 2011 and 2015; (2) Improved AL: medium/high AL burden in 2011 and low AL burden in 2015; (3) Worsened AL: low AL burden in 2011 and medium/high AL burden in 2015; (4) Kept medium/high AL: medium/high AL burden in both 2011 and 2015.

Functional status

Functional status was measured by ADL and IADL. The ADL/IADL disability was determined by self-reported difficulty and requiring assistance in at least one ADL (dressing, bathing, eating, getting into and out of bed, toileting and controlling urination and defecation [23]) and IADL (cooking, shopping, doing housework, taking medication and managing money [24]) activity, respectively. Answer options of each item in ADL and IADL included: (1) don’t have any difficulty, (2) have difficulty but can still do it, (3) have difficulty and need help, (4) cannot do it [25]. ADL/IADL disability was defined as ‘had some difficulty and need help’ or ‘could not do the activities’ in any items in ADL/IADL. In longitudinal analyses, once one ADL/IADL disability emerged or died during 3 waves follow-up, those people would be defined as incident ADL/IADL disability. Those without ADL/IADL disability should be without ADL/IADL disability in all 3 waves.

Covariates

To address potential confounding, we controlled for several sociodemographic and lifestyle at baseline, including age, sex (male/female), residence (urban/rural), marital status (married/widowed/others), education (illiteracy/ unfinished primary school/primary school/middle school/high school and above), smoking status (never/former/current smoke), drinking status (never/former/current drink), cognitive impairment (no/yes) and depression symptoms (no/yes). Cognitive function was evaluated by episodic memory and executive function, totaling a score of 21, which we defined cognitive impairment if the score was less than 6 [26]. Depression symptoms were measured by the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, yielding a total score of 30, while 10–30 indicated depressive symptoms [27].

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Chi-square tests were used to test the differences of the baseline sociodemographic and lifestyle across AL burden (low, medium and high) groups. Logistic regression was performed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) of association between baseline AL (shown as continuous and categorical variables) and prevalent ADL/IADL disability in cross-sectional analyses, as well as cumulative incident cases from follow-up visits in longitudinal analyses. Estimated ORs were adjusted for age, sex, residence, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, cognition function and depressive symptoms. Similar regressions were conducted to assess the associations between AL transition and incident ADL/IADL disability. All analyses were conducted using Stata 15.1 software (Stata Corp, College Station, TX), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Overall, we included 5880 participants in this study. The mean (SD) age of participants was 63.4 (8.6) years (age range, 50–96 years), 2864 (48.7%) were male. The mean (SD) AL score was 2.30 (1.61). 2060 (35.0%), 2544 (43.3%) and 1276 (21.7%) participants had an AL score of 0–1 (low burden), 2–3 (medium burden) and 4–11 (high burden), respectively. Participants with medium and high AL burden had a higher ADL and IADL disabilities prevalence than those with low AL burden. Baseline characteristics by AL burden are shown in Table 1. Except for sex, cognition function and depressive status, all other characteristics, including sociodemographic and lifestyle were significantly different among the 3 groups.

Cross-sectional association between baseline AL and ADL/IADL disability

The cross-sectional associations between baseline AL and ADL/IADL disability are given in Table 2. Adults with higher baseline AL score have higher prevalence of ADL and IADL disability (all, p < 0.05) in unadjusted and adjusted models. Compared to those with low AL burden, individuals with high AL burden experienced the greatest odds for ADL disability (OR = 2.54, 95%CI = 1.77, 3.65, p < 0.001), with medium AL burden being associated with 1.54-fold higher odds of ADL disability (95%CI = 1.09, 2.16, p = 0.014) after adjustment. For IADL disability, increase of OR was only significant in “high” group (OR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.19, 1.92, p = 0.001) in the adjusted model.

Longitudinal association between baseline AL and ADL/IADL disability

Table 3 presents the results of association between baseline AL and incident ADL/IADL disability over 7 years. Over a follow-up period of 7 years, 1102 ADL disability and 1777 IADL disability were occurred. In longitudinal analyses, baseline AL was related to an elevated risk of incident ADL/IADL disability, when using AL as a continuous variable. Both medium and high AL burden were significantly associated with incident ADL and IADL disability (all, p < 0.05) in the crude models. After adjusting all covariates, the results remained similar with cross-sectional analyze. Individuals with medium and high AL burden had higher risk for incident ADL disability (medium: OR = 1.25, 95%CI = 1.01, 1.55, p = 0.038; high: OR = 1.69, 95%CI = 1.32, 2.18, p < 0.001), only those in “high AL burden” group had an increased risk of incident IADL disability (OR = 1.50, 95%CI = 1.24, 1.83, p < 0.001). Considering missing data on ADL/IADL assessment in longitudinal analyses, we compared baseline AL of participants who with and without missing data on ADL/IADL assessment (Table S2). AL score is lower in participants who with missing data on ADL. As for IADL, there were no significant differences between baseline AL of two groups.

Associations between transitions of AL and 3-year ADL/IADL disability

The 4-year transitions of AL burden are presented in Fig. 2. Through 4 years period, 26.9% participants kept low AL burden, 20.0% had improved AL burden (from medium/high to low AL burden), 10.7% had worsened AL burden (from low to medium/high AL burden) and 42.4% kept medium/high AL burden. In order to identify associations between transitions of AL and ADL/IADL disability in 2018 (excluded participants who had ADL disability or IADL disability at baseline), logistic regressions were performed (Table 4). 430 ADL disability and 597 IADL disability were recorded over 3 years of follow-up. Kept medium/high AL burden (vs kept low AL burden) was associated with higher risk of incident ADL and IADL disability when controlling for covariates (ADL: OR = 2.27, 95%CI = 1.50, 3.44, p < 0.001; IADL: OR = 1.51, 95%CI = 1.11, 2.05, p = 0.008).

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the association between AL and functional disability in a nationally representative sample of Chinese adults aged at least 50 years. This finding is somewhat consistent with previous evidence suggesting that AL score at baseline may be predictor of 5-year incident physical disability among Puerto Rican adults [14]. Except for this article, other earlier studies mostly revealed that allostatic load was an independent predictor of functional decline (such as walking speed, balance, lower extremity strength and dexterity, etc.) in older people [[10], [11], [12]]. Moreover, these studies were conducted in European and American populations, our study further confirmed this association in Chinese populations with long-term follow up.

This finding may be explained by several points below. First, AL was a predictor of physical functional decline [11], which may directly impair the ability to complete self-care activities (e.g., dressing, eating, cooking, doing housework) [28]. Second, allostatic overload may be also related with reduced neurological function [15] and difficult to complete some activities such as taking medication and managing money. Besides, dysfunction of the nervous system could further exacerbate physical functional decline as well as reduce the coordination of various tasks of daily living [29]. Third, health-damaging lifestyle habits, such as lack of physical activity, unhealthy diet and poor sleep may contribute to higher AL [[30], [31], [32]], which play a great role in the process of functional disability.

It's worth noting that allostatic load suggests that human physiological system is not a fixed system but a dynamically stable one throughout the life course and allostatic load is time-varying [33]. Only a few studies drew longitudinal trajectories of allostatic load in a national sample of older adults [33,34], but the relationships between allostatic load trajectories and health outcomes have not yet been studied. Unlike expected, there is no significant association observing between worsened AL and incident ADL/IADL disability after adjustment despite worsened AL showed high risk of incident ADL disability in unadjusted model. Positive associations between longitudinal increases in AL and incident cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality were reported in a recent study [35]. One possible explanation might be disability is a chronic course compared to cardiovascular disease and mortality. In addition, the negative results may be explained by discontinuous AL records and short follow-up time. However, our study demonstrated that those with medium/high AL burden in both 2011 and 2015 can predict ADL/IADL disability, emphasizing predictive effect of higher AL burden on functional disability again. Therefore, dynamic monitoring of AL should be encouraged among older adults.

Allostatic load, which was developed from biopsychosocial model, could quantify chronic pathological stresses throughout entire lifecycle and reflect the mechanism of ageing from another perspective [36]. With a number of biological systems including cardiovascular, endocrine, immune, inflammatory, and autonomic nervous systems involved [17], AL score is more objective, comprehensive and mostly included in routine health examination. As functional disability would cause more burden to individuals, caregivers, and healthcare systems [37,38], promoting health and preventing disability of older adults for as long as possible is the problem which the whole world pay attention to and solve. According to our results, people with high risk may be identified several years by high AL score before disability happening. AL reflects the vulnerability of older people [16] and assessment of AL may help to demarcate differences among older adults who otherwise seem deceptively similar before functional disability happens [17]. So, AL may consider to be calculated and presented on the health examination report to better understand health status for both patients and clinicians [39].

Our study has several strengths. First, the results provided allostatic load which was an objective composite score that reflected cumulative effects of chronic stress on physiology in older adults, as well as a universally applicable predictor of functional disability in Chinese population. Furthermore, we not only investigated associations between baseline AL and ADL/IADL disability, but also associations between AL transitions and ADL/IADL disability. Third, the prospective study design and large sample size made the results more reliable. Despite these strengths, we acknowledge some limitations. First, blood samples were only collected in 2011 and 2015. Therefore, we could not analyze trajectory of allostatic load and its association with disability. Second, missing data determining disability over 7 years resulted in limited sample size in longitudinal analyses. Finally, we defined AL in line with previous study. However, the heterogeneities of measures across studies might be warranted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, higher baseline and follow-up AL burden was associated with an increased risk of ADL and IADL disability among older adults in China. Dynamically monitoring AL is essential in later life. This index might also contribute to early interventions, preventing allostatic overload and its long-term adverse consequences as early as possible.
