Development of an AI chatbot to support shared decision-making in rheumatoid arthritis: a two-phase usability study
Authors: Se Rim Choi, Seongjun Yoon, Soo-Bin Lee, Ha Rim Park, Hye-Rin Ko, Yu-Seon Jung, Eunwoo Nam, Soo-Kyung Cho, Yoon-Kyoung Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShared decision-making is central to the care of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, particularly when adjusting treatment. Conventional decision aids convey balanced information about therapeutic options but often fail to help patients clarify their preferences. We aimed to develop and evaluate a generative artificial intelligence chatbot to facilitate preference deliberation after exposure to standard decision aids during the shared decision-making process.MethodsFollowing the International Patient Decision Aid Standards and contemporary rheumatoid arthritis treatment guidelines, we developed a patient-facing conversational chatbot. The chatbot was designed to guide patients to reflect on treatment expectations, concerns, and daily-life fit. Twenty adults with rheumatoid arthritis and moderate-to-high disease activity completed two iterative rounds of usability testing. Primary outcomes were the System Usability Scale and the Usability Metric for User Experience. Secondary outcomes included five usability domains (usefulness, attractiveness, accessibility, reliability, convenience) and, in phase 2, the Decisional Conflict Scale.ResultsAfter refinements based on phase 1 feedback, mean (± standard deviation) System Usability Scale and Usability Metric for User Experience scores increased from 66.0 (± 11.4) to 70.0 (± 10.6) and from 60.4 (± 21.4) to 73.6 (± 16.3), respectively. Among the usability domains, accessibility improved significantly from 3.1 (± 1.3) to 4.6 (± 0.5) (p = 0.007). Mean (± standard deviation) Decisional Conflict Scale scores decreased from 35.3 (± 18.1) to 27.6 (± 9.0) after chatbot use.ConclusionsA generative artificial intelligence chatbot showed favourable usability and preliminary evidence of reduced decisional conflict. By helping patients deliberate and articulate their treatment preferences, the system may bridge to value-concordant decision-making while preserving the physician–patient relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03467-4.

Background

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease characterised by synovial inflammation, pain and progressive joint destruction. The advent of biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs has markedly expanded treatment options, enabling personalised strategies aligned with the treat-to-target principle [1, 2].

Shared decision-making (SDM) is recognised as a cornerstone of high-quality RA care, aligning medical evidence with patient preferences and circumstances [3, 4]. Both the American College of Rheumatology and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology endorse SDM as a guiding principle in RA management [1, 2]. Through SDM, patients participate meaningfully in treatment choices, which can enhance adherence, satisfaction and the therapeutic alliance [5, 6].

Despite these benefits, SDM is difficult to achieve in routine practice. Consultations are brief, treatment trade-offs are complex, and many patients feel unprepared to express what matters most to them [7, 8]. Traditional decision aids improve knowledge and comprehension [9, 10], but seldom enable individualised value clarification [11].

Elwyn’s updated three-talk model—team talk, option talk and decision talk—distinguishes the stage at which personal values must be clarified before a choice is made [3, 12]. Although frameworks such as SHARE and SEEK exist, the three-talk model is widely used in medical decision-making because of its clear, practical structure. While option talk delivers information, decision talk requires a deeper understanding of patient priorities. Tools are needed that not only educate patients but also help them reflect on and articulate their values during consultations [11, 12].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing now enable conversational systems to interact directly with users, engaging them in personalised, adaptive dialogue [13, 14]. AI-driven conversational systems have been used for patient education, triage, and symptom management in health care [15–17]. However, systems designed specifically to elicit patient preferences in the context of SDM remain uncommon. In RA, no published chatbot has been developed with the primary purpose of helping patients articulate their preferences in preparation for SDM [18].

We therefore developed and evaluated a generative AI-based chatbot to bridge the gap between option talk and decision talk in the SDM process for RA care. This study aimed to evaluate the chatbot’s usability and to explore its potential to support patients in clarifying their treatment preferences for SDM.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a two-phase iterative usability study at a tertiary rheumatology centre (Seoul, Korea). Adults aged 19 years or older who met the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria for RA and had moderate-to-high disease activity (DAS28-ESR [Disease Activity Score in 28 joints–erythrocyte sedimentation rate] > 3.2) were eligible. We excluded patients with cognitive impairment or who were unable to use a smartphone.

Participants were recruited from the outpatient clinics of two rheumatologists during routine care. A nurse researcher screened potentially eligible patients scheduled for outpatient visits using electronic medical records. Attending rheumatologists then confirmed eligibility during the clinic visit. Patients were invited to participate, and those who provided written informed consent were enrolled.

We enrolled ten participants per phase (phase 1, prototype evaluation; phase 2, refined version). Recruitment for phase 1 took place between 20 September and 2 October 2024, and recruitment for phase 2 took place between 6 and 8 November 2024. The institutional review board of Hanyang University Hospital approved the study (approval No. HYUN 2023-07-035); all participants provided written informed consent before enrolment.

Chatbot development

The generative AI chatbot was developed in accordance with the International Patient Decision Aid Standards [19], emphasising a patient-centred approach to help individuals reflect on and clarify their treatment preferences and values. The objective was to provide a familiar, comfortable conversational experience that allows patients to express ‘what matters most’ and communicate these preferences to clinicians in a structured format before the next consultation (Fig. 1).

The development process comprised seven iterative stages (Table 1). In the initial stage, a secure cloud-based platform was established with two linked components: a patient-facing chat application and a clinician-facing web dashboard for reviewing summaries of chatbot–patient conversations (Supp Fig. 1). A generative language model was selected and integrated into the system, equipped with natural language understanding, intent recognition, and tolerance for typographical errors.

The chatbot was developed through a multidisciplinary process involving rheumatologists, engineers, and nurse researchers. One rheumatologist created an initial conversational framework based on contemporary RA treatment guidelines, structured to support patient reflection on treatment preferences during decision-making. Two additional rheumatologists reviewed this framework, and a software engineer implemented it as a chatbot application. Three nurse researchers performed prototype testing; their feedback on technical errors informed subsequent revisions. Team communication was maintained through regular online meetings and email correspondence as needed. The resulting prototype was evaluated in two phases of usability testing, and the chatbot was refined based on user feedback obtained during this process.

User participation and assessment of feedback

Before using the chatbot, participants received a brief introduction to treatment options from the attending rheumatologist or a nurse educator. A research nurse provided participants with a smartphone on which the chatbot was installed or helped them install the chatbot application on their own smartphones. The nurse researcher then demonstrated how to access and use the chatbot. Participants also received a paper-based user guide. They used the chatbot without a time limit and, after completing the session, provided feedback through a paper-based survey. The survey included both structured questionnaire items and free-text questions. The questionnaire comprised the System Usability Scale (SUS) [20], the Usability Metric for User Experience (UMUX) [21], and a usability questionnaire developed for this study. This study-specific usability questionnaire included five domains—usefulness, attractiveness, accessibility, reliability, and convenience—based on Peter Morville’s User Experience Honeycomb model [22]. The questionnaire items were developed by adapting items from a previous Korean study that assessed the usability of a library chatbot service and revising them to reflect the specific characteristics of the chatbot evaluated here [23]. The free-text questions were also developed by the study team to capture participants’ overall impressions and suggestions for improvement. All questionnaires were administered in Korean.

Outcomes and measurements

Primary usability outcomes were scores from the SUS (0–100) [20] and the UMUX (0–100) [21]. Secondary measures were five usability domains—usefulness, attractiveness, accessibility, reliability, and convenience—each rated on a 5-point Likert scale [22, 23]. In the second user test (phase 2), we assessed decisional outcomes using the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS; 0–100) [24] before and after chatbot use; higher scores indicate greater uncertainty or conflict.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as mean (± standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]). Independent t-tests compared usability outcomes between phases 1 and 2, and paired t-tests analysed pre- and post-chatbot DCS scores in phase 2. Two-sided p < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Chatbot development outcomes

The dialogue framework of the chatbot addressed key treatment-related considerations, including efficacy, safety, monitoring burden, route of administration, cost, and lifestyle compatibility. The chatbot first presented the available treatment options and explained their main characteristics. It then asked patients for their views on specific treatment attributes. Feedback obtained during the phase 1 user test informed targeted refinements to the conversation flow, user interface, and language-recognition functions (Supp Table 1). Participants found detailed questions about their prior medication history to be redundant and disengaging. Therefore, these questions were removed, and the conversation flow was refocused on the patient’s upcoming treatment decision and the attributes of the available options. For individuals with moderate-to-high RA disease activity, these options included biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and Janus kinase inhibitors. Several additional modifications were introduced to improve interaction quality and user engagement, including the addition of quick-reply buttons to reduce typing burden, restructuring of the dialogue into shorter and more value-centred blocks, introduction of pause-and-resume alerts to support continuity, and expansion of the response library to address frequently asked questions. The revised prototype was subsequently evaluated in the phase 2 user test. The finalised application was deployed on both Android and iOS platforms under the name Melody (DYPHI Inc.). A representative example of chatbot-guided preference clarification using the finalised application is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Participant characteristics

Twenty adults with RA participated: ten in phase 1 and ten in phase 2 (Table 2). Participants were predominantly female (90% and 100%, respectively), with median (IQR) ages of 49.0 years (39.5–50.8) and 47.0 years (40.3–58.5). Median (IQR) disease durations were 10.5 years (4.4–16.0) in phase 1 and 13.8 years (9.5–16.9) in phase 2. Most had completed university education (90% and 60%) and lived with family members (both 90%). Employment was less common in phase 2 (70% vs. 20%). Median DAS28-ESR scores were similar between groups (3.3 vs. 3.7), indicating moderate disease activity across both phases.

Usability outcomes

Overall usability improved after refining the chatbot (Table 3). The mean (± SD) SUS score increased from 66.0 (± 11.4) in the initial user test to 70.0 (± 10.6) in the secondary test, and the UMUX rose from 60.4 (± 21.4) to 73.6 (± 16.3), although neither change reached statistical significance (p = 0.440 and p = 0.147, respectively). Of the five usability comparison domains, accessibility showed a statistically significant increase from 3.1 (± 1.3) to 4.6 (± 0.5; p = 0.007), indicating that users found the refined version easier to access and interact with. Attractiveness and convenience also improved numerically (3.3 to 3.5 and 3.5 to 4.0, respectively), whereas usefulness and reliability remained similar (3.7 to 3.7 and 3.4 to 3.3, respectively). Participants consistently noted that the revised chatbot required less typing and offered a clearer, more intuitive interface via quick-reply buttons and pause–resume notifications.

Decisional outcomes

Nine participants completed both the pre- and post-intervention assessments of decisional conflict (Table 4). One patient enrolled in the second user test did not complete the post-intervention questionnaire. The mean (± SD) DCS score decreased from 35.3 (± 18.1) before using the chatbot to 27.6 (± 9.0) afterwards, suggesting reduced decision-related uncertainty, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.214).

Among the DCS subscales, the largest numerical improvements occurred in the informed (from 43.3 to 24.1; p = 0.066) and values clarity (from 45.0 to 33.3; p = 0.252) domains, indicating that patients felt more informed and clearer about what mattered to them after interacting with the chatbot. Changes in the support, uncertainty, and effective decision subscales were modest and not statistically significant. Qualitative feedback echoed these findings: participants reported that the chatbot helped them organise thoughts about treatment priorities and prepare to discuss what mattered before the next visit.

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated an AI-based chatbot designed to support SDM in patients with RA. The chatbot helped patients reflect on treatment options, clarify personal values, and prepare for more meaningful discussions with their physicians. Usability improved across iterations—particularly accessibility—and DCS scores trended downward, suggesting the chatbot was acceptable and potentially beneficial for patients.

Traditional decision aids improve patient knowledge and reduce decisional conflict [4]; however, their effectiveness in routine practice may be attenuated when patients have limited time to reflect on options presented by decision aids during clinical encounters [25]. Elwyn’s three-talk model distinguishes option talk from decision talk, emphasising that the reflective work between them is essential for value-based care [12]. The chatbot was designed to address this reflective phase. It guided patients through structured yet adaptive conversations after information had been provided but before a decision was made, bridging education and deliberation. In doing so, it helped patients to organise priorities, clarify values, and enter consultations more ready for collaborative SDM. Indeed, decisional conflict decreased after chatbot use in our study. However, the post-intervention DCS score (mean ± SD, 27.6 ± 9.0) remained above the threshold associated with decision implementation (DCS score < 25) [24], suggesting that the chatbot may facilitate value clarification within the SDM process but is unlikely to resolve decisional conflict completely without further clinician–patient discussion.

In the broader digital health landscape, most chatbots have been developed for education, triage, or symptom management rather than preference elicitation [11, 16, 17]. This study, therefore, represents a shift from transferring information to facilitating reflection [12, 18]. The approach is particularly relevant in health care systems where time for in-depth discussion is limited. Even when physicians have sufficient opportunity to explain treatment options, patients often need additional time to deliberate, balance risks and benefits, and discuss choices with their families. The chatbot provides this space for thoughtful reflection, allowing patients to process information at their own pace and form stable preferences before the next clinical encounter. In ongoing work, a deliberation period of approximately 3–4 weeks appears sufficient for patients to reach a confident decision [12]. In Korea, where consultations are brief and family participation is common, such guided reflection may substantially improve the quality of ensuing dialogue and enhance SDM [26].

This study had several strengths. It was grounded in the three-talk model, providing a theory-based framework for SDM; it adopted a user-centred iterative design that directly incorporated patient feedback; and it leveraged generative AI to enable flexible, human-like dialogue that elicited nuanced patient perspectives. These features distinguish the chatbot from traditional decision aids and demonstrate its potential to support value-based decisions in clinical care.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. The evaluation focused primarily on patient usability and experience, and evidence of benefit to physicians remains limited. Whether the chatbot improves physician satisfaction, perceived efficiency, or the quality of SDM has yet to be demonstrated. This was also a single-centre study with a small sample, which restricts generalisability. Future multicentre studies involving a diverse range of physicians and patients are needed to confirm these findings. In addition, long-term outcomes such as treatment adherence, continuity of care, and sustained trust were not assessed and should be explored in future longitudinal research.

Overall, this work shows that AI can be used not only to deliver information but also to protect time for reflection during SDM. By enabling patients to think, question, and express themselves outside the clinic, the chatbot helps bridge the gap between knowledge and decision, potentially enhancing both patient preparedness and the efficiency of clinical encounters. However, technology can only complement—not replace—the empathy, respect, and partnership that define effective SDM. A well-designed chatbot should be considered an adjunct that extends and enriches human dialogue rather than a substitute for it.

Conclusions

The AI-based chatbot demonstrated good usability and a trend towards lower decisional conflict when used to assist treatment decision-making in patients with RA. These findings support its feasibility as a tool for promoting patient reflection within SDM. Multicentre studies with diverse clinician–patient dyads are needed to validate its impact on clinical communication and SDM outcomes.
