A novel method for cell deconvolution using DNA methylation in PCA space
Authors: Huan Xu, Ge Zhang, Jing Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn this study, we present a novel method for reference-based cell deconvolution using data from DNA methylation arrays. Different from existing methods like IDOL-Ext, which operate on probe-level data, our approach represents features in the principal component analysis (PCA) space for cell type deconvolution.ResultsOur method’s accuracy in estimating cell compositions is validated across various public datasets, including blood samples from glioma patients. It demonstrates precision comparable to IDOL-Ext, with R2 values ranging from 0.73 to 0.99 for most cell types, while offering improved discrimination between similar cell types, particularly T cell subtypes in glioma patient samples (R2 0.42–0.75 vs. 0.36–0.66 for IDOL-Ext). However, both methods showed lower accuracy for certain cell types, such as memory CD8 T cells in glioma patients (R2 0.42 vs. 0.36 for IDOL-Ext), highlighting the challenges in distinguishing closely related cell populations. We have made this method available as an R package “BloodCellDecon” on GitHub.ConclusionsOur study confirms the efficacy of cell type deconvolution in PCA space. The results indicate wide-ranging applicability and potential for adaptation to other forms of genomic data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10652-0.

Background

DNA methylation microarrays have been widely used for estimating cell compositions, especially in heterogenous tissues like peripheral blood [1–5]. Reference dataset, comprising methylation profiles of purified cell samples, are pivotal in reference-based approaches for cell fraction quantification. Recently, Salas et al. [6] developed an enhanced DNA methylation reference library from adult peripheral blood. Leveraging Illumina’s Human-MethylationEPIC technology, this library contains 12 leukocyte subtypes with expanded coverage for CpG sites, allowing cell deconvolution at a higher resolution than its predecessors [7, 8].

In addition to a comprehensive reference dataset, the deconvolution method itself is crucial for accurate cell proportion estimation. Typically, this involves a two-step process: initial probe selection from the reference data, followed by cell deconvolution based on the selected probes. Although different deconvolution algorithms, including constraint projection/quadratic programming (CP/QP) [8], support vector regression (CIBERSORT [9]), expectation-maximization (EMeth [10]) and robust partial regression (EpiDISH [11]) exhibit comparable performances [12], their performances can vary based on probe selection strategies [6, 13]. Among them, IDOL (identifying optimal libraries) [14] and IDOL-Ext [6], which employ iterative probe selection in a training set after creating a candidate probe search space via t-tests, achieved high precisions in previous studies [6, 15].

While all existing methods perform cell deconvolution using probe-level data [13], recent studies in other fields suggest that alternative approaches to handling high-dimensional data may further improve the performance of these methods. In genetic analysis, Arriaga-MacKenzie, I.S., et al. developed an algorithm to estimate ancestry proportions in a cohort from the allele frequencies in summary statistics of genetic variations [16]. Prive further built upon this concept by introducing a method that utilizes principal component analysis (PCA) for ancestry inference [17]. PCA is a technique that simplifies complex, high-dimensional data (like millions of genetic variations) by condensing it into a smaller number of dimensions, called principal components. Each principal component captures a key pattern of variation within the data. Prive’s method leverages this by projecting SNP (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism) reference allele frequencies into the PCA space. This transformation, from millions of SNPs to just 16 principal components, allows for more precise differentiation between populations with similar ancestries compared to methods operating at SNP level. Inspired by Prive’s work, we propose that a similar approach using PCA space can be applied to cell deconvolution of methylation data. In this context, “PCA space” refers to the reduced-dimensional space where each principal component represents a unique aspect of how methylation patterns vary across cell types. We hypothesize that working in this PCA space, rather than the original probe space, might not only simplify the computational process, but also enhance our ability to distinguish between different cell types within a mixed cell population. To test this hypothesis, we implemented our method and evaluated its performance using public datasets that were also used by Salas et al. [6].

Methods

The key to our method is the projection of probe-level methylation into PCA space, a technique that more robustly and efficiently captures cell type differences than traditional probe-level data analysis. The cell deconvolution is then performed in PCA space using the CP/QP optimization, enabling the accurate determination of cell type compositions that best explain the observed overall methylation patterns in the test sample. The workflow diagram illustrating our approach is shown in Fig. 1.

We downloaded the raw data for our reference samples and test sets from GEO (listed in Supplemental Table 1). The 56 samples of purified cells in GSE167998 were used as the reference data for the 12 cell types. The IDAT files for all samples were preprocessed using the SeSAMe pipeline [18] with the recommended steps for human methylation arrays, generating beta-value matrices of all datasets.

When preparing the reference data for deconvolution, we excluded probes of poor design and quality detected by the SeSAMe pipeline [18]. These include the probes containing common SNPs, with non-unique mapping to the bisulfite-converted genome, and probes with off-target hybridization due to partial overlap with non-unique elements [19]. Additionally, we excluded probes aligned to chromosomes X and Y to avoid potential gender bias. Following IDOL’s approach, to identify cell type-specific probes, we performed a two-sample t-test for each cell type against all other cell types combined. Specifically, for each probe, we compared the methylation levels of samples from one cell type to those from all other cell types. We then ranked the probes based on their t-statistic values and selected the top 75 probes with the highest positive t-statistics (most hypermethylated in the target cell type against the other cell types) and the top 75 probes with the lowest negative t-statistics (most hypomethylated in the target cell type against the other cell types) for each cell type. This process resulted in a total of 150 probes per cell type and 1800 probes across all 12 cell types (150 probes per cell type × 12 cell types). Unlike IDOL’s iterative probe selection, which relies on an independent training set, we performed PCA on these 1800 probes in the 56 reference samples. This created a projection (loading) of each probe into PCA space.

For deconvoluting cells in a test sample, we first projected its probe-level beta values to PCA space. The CP/QP optimization process was then executed in the PCA space. Results

We implemented our method as an R package “BloodCellDecon”. Similar to the IDOL approach, our method started with a t-test-based probe selection, choosing the top 150 probes for each of the 12 cell types, resulting in a total of 1800 probes. PCA on these probes for the reference samples was then performed to create the projection of probes to PCA space. The PCA plots (shown in Supplemental Fig. 1) clearly demonstrate that the top 12 PCs effectively captured the cell type differences. Additionally, a visualization of an independent dataset of purified cells in PCA space (shown in Supplemental Fig. 2) suggested high consistency between reference and test samples in PCA space, indicating that shrinkage bias correction was not necessary when projecting new samples [17]. We evaluated the impact of the number of top probes and PCs on the performance of our approach based on a dataset with 12 artificial mixture samples of 12 cell types from adult blood (GSE182379). We found that the number of top probes and PCs have significant impact on the performance, and the performance was optimal when the number of top probes is between 150 and 300, and when the first 12 PCs are included (shown in Supplemental Fig. 3).

To evaluate our deconvolution method, methylation data with known cell fractions generated by methylation arrays were utilized (Supplemental Table 1). The scatter plots in Fig. 2 show the predicted versus the actual cell proportions across four representative datasets. Our method consistently demonstrated high precision in cell proportion inference, as indicated by high R2 and low RMSE values.

Discussion

In our study, we introduced a novel approach for cell type deconvolution using DNA methylation data by performing the analysis in PCA space. This method stands in contrast to existing approaches that operate at the probe level, such as IDOL-Ext, CIBERSORT, EpiDISH and EMeth. The key distinction of our approach lies in the data representation used for deconvolution, which is independent of the specific probe selection and cell type proportion estimation techniques employed. We chose to compare our method with IDOL-Ext as a representative example of a widely used and well-established prove-level deconvolution approach, allowing for a more direct evaluation of performing deconvolution in PCA space. While there are other probe selection strategies and proportion estimation techniques, the PCA-based approach we introduced could potentially be combined with various methods, to further improve the accuracy and robustness of cell type deconvolution.

In our t-test based probe selection, we chose the top 150 probes per cell type, based on our evaluation and to be consistent with that used in IDOL-Ext. In deconvolution in the PCA space, the first 20 PCs were considered. Although incorporating at least the first 12 PCs was essential due to their high correlation with cell types, we observed no significant performance difference between using 12 and 20 PCs. In addition, scaling (transforming data to similar range) and centering (shifting data to have mean of 0) are normally performed before PCA. As our data are beta values, which are ratios of methylation already in the range between 0 and 1, and we observed negligible effect of scaling and centering on the results of cell type deconvolution, we therefore did not perform scaling and centering in both the projection generation and the estimation of test samples, to keep our method simple.

While we evaluated our method’s performance in deconvoluting cord blood cells using the GSE180970 dataset, it is important to note that the reference dataset used in this analysis was derived from adult blood samples. As Gervin et al. [15] demonstrated, accurate deconvolution of cord blood DNA methylation data, especially for estimating the proportion of nucleated red blood cells (nRBCs), requires the use of a clean and high-quality reference dataset specific to cord blood. To extend the applicability of our method to cord blood studies, future work should focus on generating or identifying a comprehensive and well-characterized cord blood reference dataset that includes methylation profiles of nRBCs and other relevant cell types. By incorporating such a dataset into our PCA-based deconvolution approach, we can further evaluate and optimize its performance in estimating cell type proportions, including nRBCs, in cord blood samples.

The lower accuracy of IDOL-Ext in glioma samples (GSE180683) could be attributed to two factors: firstly, its dependence on a training set composed solely of artificial cell mixtures, and secondly, the subtleties in distinguishing closely related T cell subtypes. By circumventing the need for a training set and performing deconvolution in PCA space, our method has reduced risk of overfitting the training data, and enhanced power to differentiate between similar cell types. Although our method consistently showed better results for T cell subtypes compared to IDOL-Ext, both methods had significantly lower accuracy in glioma samples compared to other datasets, particularly for memory and naïve CD8 T cells. This highlights the challenges in distinguishing closely related cell populations and the necessity for improving computational methods. In addition, we acknowledge the limitation of having only one dataset with flow cytometry-measured T cell subtype proportions for validating our method’s performance in this regard. The scarcity of publicly available datasets suitable for this purpose poses a challenge in comprehensively evaluating the ability of cell type deconvolution methods to accurately estimate T cell subtypes. Despite this limitation, our preliminary results on the glioma dataset are promising, suggesting the reliability of our PCA-based method for various sample types. Further validation on a wider range of datasets, as they become available, will help evaluate the robustness and generalizability of our method.

Finally, it is important to note that while our method currently focuses on methylation arrays, its applicability can extend to other platforms, such as sequencing-based methylomes [20]. There is also a potential for application to other types of genomic data, such as bulk RNA-seq [21].

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the potential of cell type deconvolution in PCA space for estimating cell compositions from DNA methylation arrays. This approach shows promise in matching the accuracy of conventional probe-level data methods while potentially offering improved discrimination between similar cell types, particularly in the context of T cell subtypes. In conclusion, our findings highlight the promise of PCA space-based deconvolution as an alternative method for analyzing cell compositions in blood samples from DNA methylation data. With continued research and development, this approach has the potential to contribute to the growing toolkit of computational methods for dissecting cellular heterogeneity in complex biological samples.
