Relationship of atrial fibrillation and N terminal pro brain natriuretic peptide in heart failure patients
Authors: Entezar Nasab Mehrabi, Vahid Toupchi‐Khosroshahi, Seyyed Shamsadin Athari
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractN terminal pro brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) plays an important role in the diagnosis and prognosis of heart failure (HF). The plasma level of NT‐proBNP in atrial fibrillation (AF) patients was higher than of sinus rhythm patients. In HF, NT‐proBNP levels are affected by the concomitant presence of AF, making it difficult to distinguish between HF and AF in patients with elevated NT‐proBNP. Several other diseases, such as renal failure and pulmonary embolism, are known to further increase NT‐proBNP levels in patients with concomitant HF. Therefore, NT‐proBNP is a sensitive but non‐specific marker for the detection of HF. AF is very important in this regard because among patients with HF regardless of ejection fraction, symptoms such as shortness of breath and atrial enlargement develop and can mimic HF. In the present study, we investigated whether the prognostic value of natriuretic peptides in HF holds true for patients with concomitant AF.Keywords: Arrhythmia, BNP, Cardiac diseases

Introduction

Natriuretic peptide, NT‐proBNP, is an important factor for the diagnosis and prognosis of HF. 1 , 2 In addition to HF, in several other diseases, such as AF, renal failure, and pulmonary embolism, NT‐proBNP is also increased. 3 , 4 So this marker has high sensitivity, but low specificity for HF diagnosis, and also, it is useful for rule out HF. 5 Among the causes of increased NT‐proBNP, AF is important in this respect because it is very common among patients with HF regardless of ejection fraction and mimics symptoms of HF such as dyspnoea and atrial enlargement. 3 , 4 , 6

One of the main mechanisms in the occurrence of AF is stretching and remodelling of the atrium, which can be caused by various cardiac complications such as acute and chronic left ventricular systolic dysfunction, diastolic dysfunction, coronary artery disease, and high blood pressure. 7 , 8 Recently, researchers found that an inflammatory state in the atrial tissue was found only in patients with AF, suggesting the possibility of a strong connection between the occurrence of AF and the occurrence of inflammatory factors. 9

In fact, C‐reactive protein (CRP), which is an acute phase protein, increases following inflammation and can be known as a predictive factor for the development of AF. Following this hypothesis, BNP and NT‐proBNP were suggested as biomarkers for AF management. 10 Also, BNP is one of the best indicators of the occurrence of acute HF, and it is considered as an important prognostic factor because it is closely related to the New York Heart Association (NYHA) class and to mortality. Interestingly, BNP is more accurate than NYHA class for predicting mortality. 11 In patients with HF, after adjusting for clinical confounding factors, NT‐proBNP levels are significantly affected by the presence of AF. 12 It is often unclear how elevated NT‐proBNP levels in patients with HF and AF should be interpreted. 3 , 4 , 6 Therefore, to date, different NT‐proBNP thresholds are used for HF patients with and without AF. 12 , 13 This study was mentioned in order to learn more about NT‐proBNP and to discover solution to solve the mentioned cardiac diseases and problems.

Atrial fibrillation

The most common cardiac arrhythmia is AF, and the prevalence of AF in the United States is 2.3 million cases that is estimated to increase to 5.6 million by 2050. 14 AF is a scattered and chaotic pattern of electrical activity in the atria that suppresses and replaces the normal sinus rhythm. But this disturbed electrical activity leads to the deterioration of mechanical performance. 14 Unfortunately, AF is associated with many side effects, and its most important complication is the occurrence of thromboembolic events, especially ischaemic stroke. It is estimated that AF causes a five‐fold increase in stroke and is the cause of 15% of all stroke cases. 15 AF can double the risk of death, and the prevalence of AF is age‐dependent that is less than 1% in people aged 60 to 65 years, but it is about 8% to 10% in people over 80 years old. Also, the prevalence is related to gender and race, and it is more common in men than women and in Whites more than Blacks. 12 Cases of underlying heart diseases such as HF and valvular heart diseases can also cause AF. The most common symptoms of AF are heart palpitations, shortness of breath, chest pain, exercise intolerance, weakness, and dizziness. Some patients, especially the elderly, may not show any symptoms. 16 , 17 Symptoms can be caused by increased ventricular rate, irregular ventricular rate, or loss of atrial contribution to cardiac output. In physical examination, sign of AF includes heart rate, which may be faster than expected or within normal range, but what is important is irregular rhythm, and in auscultation, heart rate is vary irregularly in both rate and amplitude. 18

Only one electrocardiogram (ECG) recorded during the arrhythmia is sufficient for a definitive diagnosis. But a normal ECG does not rule out the diagnosis of AF because this arrhythmia is sometimes paroxysmal. Therefore, when AF is suspected, but the ECG is normal, monitoring for a long time can be useful. In patients with daily symptoms, continuous 24 or 48‐h, Holter monitoring is usually sufficient for diagnosis. But in patients with less frequent symptoms, longer periods of monitoring with an electrocardiographic loop recorder may be necessary. These long‐term monitoring are both annoying for the patient and bring high health costs to the health system. 18

AF can be classified into three groups: paroxysmal, persistent, or permanent. ‘Paroxysmal’ AF means that the episode's end in less than 7 days without intervention. ‘Persistent’ AF means that episodes last more than 7 days or require an intervention such as cardioversion to restore sinus rhythm. ‘Permanent’ AF means that the arrhythmia is persistent, and interventions to restore sinus rhythm have either failed or have not been performed (Figure 1 ). 19 Cardiovascular events related to atrial fibrillation 20 , 21 , 22 and cardiovascular diseases and other conditions associated with atrial fibrillation 23 , 24 , 25 are listed in Table 1 .

There are two mechanisms for the AF initiation and continuation that include (A) focal mechanisms: One of the main proposed mechanisms for the AF initiation and continuation is the focal mechanism, which includes re‐entry and triggered activity and, pulmonary veins have a strong potential to AF initiate and maintain 23 ; (B) multiple wavelet mechanism: AF is perpetuated by continuous conduction of several independent wavelets propagating via the atrial musculature in a seemingly chaotic manner. Fibrillation wave‐fronts continuously undergo wave‐front‐wave‐back interactions, resulting in wave‐break and the generation of new wave‐fronts, while collision, block, and fusion of wave‐fronts tend to reduce their number. As long as the number of wave‐fronts does not decline below a critical level, the multiple wavelets will sustain the arrhythmia. While in most patients with paroxysmal AF localized sources of the arrhythmia is identified, such attempts are often not successful in patients with persistent or permanent AF. 23

Over time, any type of structural heart disease can gradually cause the remodelling of the ventricles and atria. The fibroblasts proliferation and the deposition of connective tissue and collagen and the fibrosis creation cause the remodelling of the chambers. This structural remodelling can cause mechanical and electrical heterogeneity. In homogeneities cause initiation and continuation of an AF. 26 AF gradually causes changes in the electrophysiological and mechanical properties of the atrium over time. These changes make the rhythm of AF more stable. In the case of rapid cardioversion, before remodelling progresses, it may be possible to stop the remodelling process. 26

Heart failure

HF is not a single disease, but a ‘syndrome’, and when the heart's pumping function is disrupted, heart failure occurs. The most common symptom is shortness of breath and other symptoms include fatigue, oedema of limbs, orthopnoea, and night cough (Figure 2 ). There are different classifications for this syndrome; the first type of classification is based on acute or chronic form. Acute HF develops quickly and is severe and requires emergency treatment. Chronic HF has existed for a long time and sometimes the severity of its symptoms fluctuates. 27 Chronic HF is divided into three groups, including (1) HF with reduced ejection fraction; (2) HF with partial reduction of ejection fraction; and (3) HF with preserved ejection fraction (Table 2 ). 27

In order to correctly diagnose HF, one must first pay attention to the patient's symptoms, then the examination and finally the anomalies and functional disorders of the heart, which are determined by imaging methods and some tests such as BNP and NT‐proBNP levels measurement. N terminal pro brain natriuretic peptide and brain natriuretic peptide

Natriuretic peptides (NP) are a class of structural proteins with natriuretic and diuretic effects. There are five different classes of natriuretic peptides: brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), C‐type natriuretic peptide (CNP), Dendroaspis natriuretic peptide (DNP), and urodilatin. 29 These peptides are produced from a precursor molecule that is processed through different pathways and leads to making of different types of natriuretic peptides. NT‐proBNP is the inactive biological breakdown product of BNP, and both are considered cardiovascular biomarkers. 30

The role of NP in the body is to determine natriuresis and diuresis and the dilation of arterial and venous. The half‐life of BNP and NT‐proBNP are 21 min and 60–120 min, respectively. All NP actions in the body are mediated by three receptors: NPR‐A, NPR‐B, and NPR‐C. After binding the peptide to the receptor, cGMP‐dependent signalling pathway is activated. This signalling pathway is involved in all renal and vascular NP activities. NPR‐A binds to atrial and brain natriuretic peptides and prefers atrial natriuretic peptide. NPR‐A is more abundant in large blood vessels. NPR‐B is present in the adrenal medulla and kidney, which binds to C‐type natriuretic peptide. The third receptor is NPR‐C, which has the role of clearing NPs from the cardiovascular system. NPR‐C has a different affinity for NPs (ANP > BNP > CNP). 10

Although NP type C is more powerful than others, different NPs generally have the same activity. 31 NPs improve renal glomerular filtration rate and sodium removal. They also reduce arterial and venous tone and cause vasodilatation and venodilatation. In addition, they also have anti‐proliferative effects, so they can show anti‐cardiac regeneration effects. Also, NPs can reduce sympathetic tone and regulate vagal tone, thus inhibiting the secretion of renin and aldosterone. 10

ANP and BNP are released in various physiological and pathological conditions. The first and most important reason is the stretching of the heart wall. Other items include trans‐parietal pressure in heart wall, tachycardia and abnormal secretion of thyroid's hormones and glucocorticoids, and endothelin and angiotensin II. The secretion mode of the two NPs is very different. ANP is secreted from the atrium, but BNP is secreted from the atrium and ventricle, and due to the larger size of the ventricle, its volume is greater, so the BNP secreted by the ventricle is more than the atrium. ANP is synthesized only during stimulation, and small amounts of it are stored, which can be ignored. It is also secreted in a pulsatile manner, and its plasma concentration is changed greatly over time. But the BNP secreted from the ventricle is synthesized and stored before stimulation. Also, BNP is secreted in a more stable way, which is less excreted by the clearance systems, and it is less affected by changes in cardiovascular homeostasis, so it has a more stable concentration. 32 Due to the superiority of BNP and NT‐proBNP over ANP, they are considered the best way to detect the activation of the NP system today. Also, NT‐proBNP performs better than BNP and has a longer half‐life compared with BNP. 33

NT‐proBNP is one of the best indicators of the occurrence of HF and is considered an important prognostic factor and is related to the NYHA class and mortality. Interestingly, NT‐proBNP is more accurate than NYHA class for predicting mortality. 11 NT‐proBNP can be used in patients who complain of shortness of breath to differentiate cardiac causes from pulmonary causes, and if it is negative, cardiac causes of breath shortness can be easily ruled out. It is especially useful in the differential diagnosis of shortness of breath. 5

Factors that cause stretching of the heart wall can increase the blood level of NT‐proBNP. Among the causes of increased heart wall tension, the following can be mentioned: systolic heart failure, diastolic dysfunction of the heart, restrictive cardiomyopathy, acute coronary syndrome, valvular heart diseases, AF rhythm, and amyloidosis. There are a variety of non‐cardiac diseases that can affect BNP and NT‐proBNP levels and cause them to increase, such as renal failure, pulmonary trombo‐embolism, pulmonary artery hypertension, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. 34 Plasma concentrations of BNP and NT‐proBNP are more closely related to measures of cardiac structure, function, and cardiovascular prognosis than ANP or other circulating neurohumoral markers of heart disease. 35

The diagnostic thresholds of BNP and NT‐proBNP are considered different based on a series of factors, include: age, sex, body mass index, and the type of measurement methods. 36 Another important application of BNP and NT‐proBNP is their use as markers of response to treatment. During the treatment, their levels should be checked frequently, and their downward trend indicates a suitable response to the treatment. If it does not decrease with treatment, it indicates an unstable condition and a poor prognosis in the patients. 37

The relationship between AF and high levels of BNP and NT‐proBNP is well known. 38 , 39 , 40 The mean level of NT‐proBNP in AF was between 800 and 1100 pg/mL and with the duration of AF and size of left atrium is related. This means that natriuretic peptide is higher in persistent AF than in paroxysmal AF. Also, the larger the size of the left atrium, the higher the serum level of NPs, because the amount of stretching on the heart walls will be higher, and the amount of remodelling is also higher. 39 An exception has been seen in AF, where NT‐proBNP is mainly secreted from the atrium, because the amount of remodelling and stretching on the left atrium is much higher than the ventricle, 33 and it is interesting to note that the level of NT‐proBNP decreases rapidly as the heart rhythm becomes sinus rhythm. This issue shows a strong relationship between NT‐proBNP level and AF rhythm. 39 , 41 Small studies have been conducted to determine whether re‐elevation of NT‐proBNP is able to predict the likelihood of AF recurrence after sinus rhythm. Although some of them did not show significant correlation, and as a result, it is still difficult to make a decision in this field. 42 , 43 However, it has been found that NT‐proBNP levels can be a reliable measure to estimate the probability of AF recurrence in conditions after heart surgery or in patients with mild heart failure. 44 , 45

The most important stimulus for their release from the atrial and ventricular myocardium is the increase in end‐diastolic wall stress, which can occur due to increased pressure or volume overload. 46 , 47 BNP and NT‐proBNP correlate with the clinical severity of HF. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 HF and AF are both chronic diseases that both cause an increase in BNP and NT‐proBNP levels, and these two diseases have a lot of overlap. 52 Therefore, in the presence of AF, the diagnosis of acute HF with BNP and NT‐proBNP is not reliable. 53 Although the diagnostic interference between the causes of NT‐proBNP elevation in cases of acute HF with AF has been suggested. But the data on chronic HF with AF are still challenging and need further investigation. 53

Another important point is that, after ischaemic stroke, if the patient has elevated NT‐proBNP in the absence of HF, long‐term rhythm monitoring should be performed for unrecognized AF. Likewise, outpatients with a significant increase in NT‐proBNP may benefit from AF screesning if HF is ruled out. 54 Some studies show that in stable outpatients but with cardiovascular risk factors, high peptide is more powerful for predicting the occurrence of fibrillation than the occurrence of heart failure. 55 Studies have shown that serum levels of NT‐proBNP, although significantly associated with a reduction in ejection fraction, do not correspond well with NYHA functional classification of heart failure. 56 Various side factors affect the NT‐proBNP serum level, such as age, sex, blood pressure, body mass index, kidney function, and heart rhythm (sinus rhythm vs. AF). 57 Even today, a relationship between metabolic components and lower plasma NT‐proBNP concentration has also been reported. 58 , 59

Different aetiologies can be considered for the high level of NT‐proBNP in patients with fibrillation, such as, first, sometimes the cause of increased NT‐proBNP is directly related to immediate hemodynamic changes during AF 60 ; second, high levels of NT‐proBNP are sometimes related to chronic structural or functional remodelling of the heart as a result of persistent episodes of AF; and third, an increase in this marker sometimes only indicates that patients with AF have more advanced HF. 12

It is often unclear how elevated NT‐proBNP levels in patients with HF and AF should be interpreted. 3 , 4 , 6 Therefore, to date, different NT proBNP thresholds are used for heart failure patients with and without AF. 12 Perhaps this is the best solution to increase the specificity of this marker in heart failure patients.

Conclusion

Plasma NT‐proBNP plays an important role in the diagnosis and management of HF. In fact, natriuretic peptides are related to haemodynamic load, and NT‐proBNP is a strong predictor of mortality in HF. These peptides can increase diuresis and vasodilation by neutralizing the harmful effects of renin‐angiotensin system activation in HF and improve the clinical condition of patients. Although NT‐proBNP is mainly used to diagnose of HF, but it is not specific for HF. Plasma levels of NT‐proBNP are higher in patients with AF compared with patients with sinus rhythm, and the NT‐proBNP is even used to predict the AF possibility. AF is of great health importance, because the incidence and prevalence of this arrhythmia are increasing all over the world and it also causes high complications and mortality. AF is important in this respect because it is very common among patients with HF regardless of ejection fraction and mimics symptoms of heart failure such as dyspnoea and atrial enlargement. Considering that about 30% of HF have AF, the diagnosis and prognosis cannot be judged solely on the basis of high peptide levels. Due to the low specificity of NT‐proBNP, it is necessary to consider the cutoff for HF with AF higher than the cutoff for HF patients without AF. Also, a different threshold should be defined in each patient based on age, gender, presence of AF, diabetes, and body size. Considering that in patients with HF, NT‐proBNP levels are significantly affected by the presence of AF at the time of measurement, the use of other serum markers can be useful. Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF‐15) is a very useful factor in these patients. GDF‐15 levels are independent of the presence of AF in patients with HF and better reflect the overall clinical status of HF patients. 61 , 62 GDF‐15 is expressed and secreted in response to inflammation, hypoxia, and oxidative stress. The main cause of GDF‐15 production is cardiovascular disease, and GDF‐15 can be measured in serum and plasma by immunoassay method. Studies have shown that GDF‐15 levels in AF patients without HF are almost similar to levels in the elderly community. 63 Diagnosis of HF (especially HFpEF) in patients with AF remains a clinical challenge. Simultaneous measurement of GDF‐15 and NT‐proBNP is useful for accurate and better diagnosis of aetiology. Because signs and symptoms, echocardiographic findings, and increased NT‐proBNP levels can be caused both by AF alone and by AF with concomitant HFpEF. Therefore, it can be said that the presence of GDF‐15 is a useful tool in these patients. Searching to identify other markers also seems useful and necessary.
