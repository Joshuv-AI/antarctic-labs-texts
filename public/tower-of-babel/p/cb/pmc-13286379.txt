A study on the relationship between perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intention in home care for older adult patients under the background of doctor-patient disputes: A cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Pengfei Xu, Qi Ren, Xiajun Sun, Jihua Chen, Xinfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the relationships among perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intention in home care for older adult patients in the context of doctor-patient disputes and examines the moderating role of trust. This cross-sectional observational study used convenience sampling to recruit 306 older adult patients who had received home care services within the past year. Data were collected via a structured questionnaire comprising 26 items across 7 constructs (functional, emotional, efficiency, and social value; satisfaction; behavioral intention; and trust), each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The questionnaire demonstrated good reliability and validity. Structural equation modeling was performed using AMOS 24.0 to test path relationships, with model fit evaluated by multiple indices (chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio = 1.289, root mean square error of approximation = 0.031, comparative fit index = 0.983, Tucker-Lewis index = 0.980). Mediation effects were examined using the bootstrap method (5000 resamples), and moderation effects of trust were tested using PROCESS macro (Model 1) in SPSS 26.0. The results showed that social value most strongly predicts satisfaction (β = 0.330, P < .001), followed by emotional (β = 0.236, P < .001) and functional value (β = 0.192, P < .01); efficiency value is nonsignificant (β = 0.080, P > .05). For behavioral intention, emotional value has the strongest direct effect (β = 0.208, P < .01), followed by functional (β = 0.172, P < .01) and efficiency value (β = 0.159, P < .05), social value is nonsignificant (β = 0.114, P > .05). Satisfaction significantly predicted behavioral intention (β = 0.285, P < .001), and partially mediated the relationship between perceived value and behavioral intention. The indirect effect between efficiency value and behavioral intention was not significant (0.027, P > .05), and the 95% CI was 0 (–0.011, 0.115), thus not playing a mediating role. Trust moderates perceived value’s effect on satisfaction, but not for efficiency value (β = –0.043, P > .05). Findings suggest a need for human-centered home care models emphasizing emotional support and social recognition, moving beyond technology-driven approaches. This research supports policymakers in evaluating older adult care services and building trust-based, satisfaction-centered, and value-driven doctor-patient interactions for healthy aging and high-quality elder care.

1. Introduction

In the Chinese healthcare system, the persistent rise in doctor-patient disputes has undermined mutual trust between physicians and patients, adversely affecting service quality and operational efficiency.[1] This challenge is further exacerbated by the ongoing demographic shift towards an aging population: according to the “9073” elderly care model, approximately 90% of seniors depend on home-based care.[2] However, the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases and the uneven distribution of medical resources have made it an urgent need to improve the accessibility and quality of home-based older adults care. Existing studies indicate that although the utilization rate of home care services among the older adults is rising annually, their satisfaction and loyalty levels remain relatively stable, a phenomenon closely related to the failure of service supply to fully meet the diverse needs of the older adults.[3] In an environment of fragile doctor-patient trust, older adult patients’ perceived value of care services has become more complex, and service optimization focusing solely on technical aspects can hardly meet their in-depth requirements.

Existing relevant studies have primarily focused on the exploration of individual influencing factors, including the functional dimension of service quality or the efficacy of doctor-patient communication.[4,5] However, they failed to comprehensively elucidate how older adults integrate multiple service experiences to develop perceived value, and how this perceived value influences satisfaction and subsequent behavioral intentions. Rao et al highlighted the significance of perceived value and customer expectations in their study of hospitalized patients. However, they did not account for the unique social and emotional needs of the older adults in home care settings.[6] Al-Hammouri et al focused on predicting patient satisfaction from nursing quality, however, they ignored the moderating effect of trust on the transformation of perceived value into satisfaction.[7] Additionally, multiple studies are based on Western or other high-trust medical settings,[8] whereas in the low-trust environment characterized by frequent doctor-patient disputes in China, the value-evaluation logic of older adult patients can vary significantly, thereby limiting the applicability of existing research findings. This limitation in the research perspective has resulted in a lack of targeted theoretical guidance and empirical support for optimizing current home-based older adults care services.

The “perceived value-satisfaction-behavioral intention (PV-S-BI)” framework provides a valuable analytical perspective.[9] In healthcare, perceived value is multidimensional: functional (clinical outcomes), emotional (empathy and reassurance), efficiency-related (convenience and timeliness), and social (dignity and sense of belonging).[10,11] Unlike in commercial settings, home care for the older adults integrates medical, emotional, and daily living support, developing a uniquely complex framework of value perceptions. Previous studies have frequently overlooked this complexity, focusing narrowly on functional aspects while disregarding the increased significance of emotional and social value for vulnerable older adults.

Patient satisfaction, formed through the comparison between expectations and experience, mediates the transformation of perceived value into behavioral intentions, including continued use or recommendation. This pathway has been validated in retail and certain overseas healthcare systems (such as Pakistan and India).[4] However, its effectiveness reduces in high-conflict settings, where perceived risk and insurance-related factors complicate the relationship. Therefore, contextual factors play a critical role.

Trust, defined as patients’ confidence in providers’ competence and integrity, serves as a key moderator. Trust increases with age and directly impacts satisfaction and behavior. In low-trust environments, even high-value services can struggle to cultivate loyalty, whereas in high-trust settings, value perceptions are amplified, and satisfaction more effectively translates into positive behavioral intentions.[1,5] Also, trust mediates the effects of other factors, including digital tools and insurance, and can be rebuilt through transparency and proactive communication.

This study applied the PV-S-BI model within the context of home-based older adults care. The aim of this study is to investigate the relationships among perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intention in home care for older adult patients in the context of doctor-patient disputes. The study categorizes perceived value into 4 dimensions: functional, emotional, efficiency, and social value. Additionally, it systematically investigates their effects on satisfaction and behavioral intentions (continued use and recommendation intention). This dimensional division is based on the research framework of Rao et al and is adapted to address the specificities of older adults care. Also, this study explores the moderating role of trust in depth: high trust improves positive outcomes, whereas low trust, a frequent issue in Chinese tense healthcare environment, reduces them. The following hypotheses are proposed based on the above content: Perceived value (multidimensional) in home-based older adults care positively affects patient satisfaction; Perceived value (multidimensional) in home-based older adults care positively affects behavioral intention; Satisfaction with home care services positively affects behavioral intention among older adult patients; Satisfaction mediates the relationship between perceived value (multidimensional) and behavioral intention in older home care adult patients; Trust positively moderates the relationship between each dimension of perceived value (multidimensional) and satisfaction, as well as between satisfaction and behavioral intention, in home care for the older adults. Guided by the theoretical framework and hypothesized pathways, the conceptual model of this study is depicted in Figure 1. By empirically validating this theoretical framework, this study will not only enhance the application of service theory to vulnerable populations but also offer practical strategies for restoring doctor-patient trust, improving the effectiveness of older adults care services, and ultimately advancing healthy aging and high-quality older adults care objectives.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

This cross-sectional observational study aimed to investigate the relationships among perceived value (functional, emotional, efficiency, and social value), satisfaction, and behavioral intention among older adult patients receiving home care, with trust as a moderating variable, in the context of doctor-patient disputes. The study was conducted in communities covered by Zhejiang Hospital, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China, with data collection carried out from July 2025.

The study was carried out in strict adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revised version). All participants provided written informed consent following being fully informed of the study purpose, procedures, potential risks, and rights. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Hospital [approval number: 2025-097 (K)]. Reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline.

2.2. Participants

2.2.1. Sample size determination

This study used structural equation modeling (SEM) to test hypotheses. To ensure model stability and statistical power, the sample size must meet specific requirements. According to the general rule of thumb (at least 10–20 samples per observed variable), the questionnaire in this study contains 26 observed items, theoretically requiring 260–520 samples. Additionally, considering the plan to use the Bootstrap method for mediating effect testing (which requires a larger sample), the target sample size of this study was set at 350.

2.2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included the following: Age ≥60 years old; Receiving continuous or intermittent home care services in the past 12 months; Ability to remain conscious, demonstrate basic understanding and communication skills, and complete the questionnaire independently or with the support of the investigator; Willingness to participate and provision of the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria were as follows: Severe cognitive impairment (including moderate to severe dementia) or mental illness resulting in an inability to complete the survey; Severe visual, auditory, or language impairments that affect effective communication; Acute illness or terminal condition.

2.2.3. Data collection and sample statistics

This study collected data through a questionnaire administered to older adult patients using convenience sampling. This method is suitable in exploratory research. However, it can limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of older individuals receiving home care. Data were collected by research assistants or community nurses who had received uniform training. The survey was conducted through one-on-one interviews or self-completion (for those with good eyesight and no writing difficulties) to ensure that the older adult participants fully understood the meaning of the items. The average completion time was approximately 15–20 minutes. To ensure data quality, attention-check items were incorporated in the questionnaire, and responses with unusually short completion times were excluded. The survey was conducted over 7 days, yielding 350 responses. After data cleaning and screening, 306 valid questionnaires were retained, with an effective response rate of 87.43%. Basic characteristics of the sample.

The basic characteristics of the study sample align with the typical profile of the older adult patient population. Demographically, the sample exhibited a relatively balanced gender distribution. The age composition was predominantly between 60 and 79 years, reflecting common age distribution patterns observed among the older adults. Education levels were generally low, consistent with the educational background of older adults in China. Additionally, the distribution of urban and rural residents was proportionate and representative.

Regarding health and living conditions, most older adult patients were married and primarily live with their children, although a significant proportion live independently. Chronic diseases were highly prevalent in the sampling population, with only a small minority reporting no chronic conditions. The frequency of home-based care service was generally low, with most occurring once per month or less. It is important that approximately one-quarter (23.86%) of older adult respondents reported experiencing unpleasant medical or nursing-related incidents, which aligns with the medical dispute context central to this study.

Also, older adult patients exhibited diverse requirements for home-based care services, with basic nursing and rehabilitation training being the most frequently used categories. Mobility limitations were the primary reason for seeking home care, while chronic disease management, lack of family caregiver support, and postoperative recovery also served as significant motivating factors.

2.3. Evaluations

2.3.1. Questionnaire design

This study used a questionnaire to collect empirical data. The questionnaire was divided into 3 main sections: The first section introduced the survey by providing respondents with key background information, research objectives, main content, and instructions. The second section comprised demographic and personal background details, including gender, age, education level, region of residence, marital status, living arrangements, and chronic health conditions, all collected anonymously. The third section formed the core of the instrument, containing 26 items across 7 constructs: functional value, emotional value, efficiency value, social value, satisfaction, behavioral intention, and trust. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). For all positively phrased items, a higher score indicates a stronger agreement with the statement, thereby reflecting a higher level of the corresponding construct (perceived value, satisfaction, behavioral intention, and trust). Additionally, higher scores on the perceived value dimensions (functional, emotional, efficiency, and social) indicate a stronger perception of these values among older adult patients regarding home care services. In the satisfaction dimension, higher scores denote greater satisfaction with the services. For behavioral intention, higher scores predict a stronger willingness to continue using and recommending the services. Finally, higher scores on the trust dimension indicate greater trust in service providers. No reverse-coded items were included in the instrument. The level of each construct was primarily determined by calculating the mean score of its constituent items. Additionally, in this study, no specific clinical or statistical cutoff points were established to differentiate between various levels of the constructs. The theoretical midpoint of the 5-point Likert scale (3.0) was adopted as a practical reference point for interpretation, consistent with established practice in health services research when population norms are not available. A mean score above 3.0 was interpreted as reflecting a “positive” or “favorable” evaluation (moderate to high levels of satisfaction or trust), whereas a score below 3.0 indicated a “negative” or “low” perception. All statistical analyses used the continuous scores without dichotomization. The 3.0 threshold was used exclusively for descriptive purposes to interpret effect direction and magnitude.

To ensure scientific rigor, validity, and structural coherence, the questionnaire was developed based on well-established theoretical foundations. The multidimensional structure of perceived value, including functional, emotional, efficiency, and social dimensions, was adapted from Rao et al.[6] Satisfaction measurement items were adapted from the validated scales developed by Al-Hammouri et al.[7] Behavioral intention items, including continued usage and recommendation intentions, were derived from the work of Alodhialah et al.[12] Trust measurement was included in the theoretical framework proposed by Zhao et al.[13] underscoring its significance in healthcare contexts. Also, studies by Norvilaitė et al and Liu et al.[8,14] provided valuable insights specific to the evaluation of home-based and older adults care services, aligning closely with the focus of this study. Before widespread distribution, a panel of experts in nursing, geriatrics, and doctor-patient relationships reviewed the questionnaire in a pilot study. Their feedback was incorporated through several rounds of revisions, resulting in the finalized, validated instrument.

2.3.2. Reliability and validity

2.3.2.1. Reliability testing

Reliability testing of the questionnaire is a critical step in evaluating the scale’s overall consistency, stability, and item reliability. This study used Cronbach’s α as the indicator of questionnaire reliability. According to established research, a Cronbach’s α value above 0.9 indicates excellent reliability; values between 0.8 and 0.9 suggest good reliability; values from 0.7 to 0.8 represent acceptable reliability; values between 0.6 and 0.7 are considered marginally acceptable; values below 0.6 indicate that the scale requires revision.

The research team conducted a reliability analysis of the collected questionnaire data using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26.0). The results are presented in Table 2. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for all variables exceeded 0.8. Specifically, the values were 0.888 for functional value, 0.857 for emotional value, 0.824 for efficiency value, 0.879 for social value, 0.824 for satisfaction, 0.800 for behavioral intention, and 0.893 for trust. These results indicate that the questionnaire designed for this study exhibits good reliability. Also, all corrected item-total correlations were >0.6. The Cronbach’s α values after item deletion were lower than those of their respective dimensions. This indicates strong internal consistency within each measurement dimension, and no items were removed from the scale.

2.3.2.2. Validity testing

Questionnaire validity is generally assessed through content validity, construct validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. The validity evaluation of the sample data in this study was conducted as follows.

First, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed using SPSS. Statistically, a KMO value above 0.7 indicates acceptable validity, above 0.8 suggests good validity, and above 0.9 reflects excellent validity. The KMO value obtained in this study was 0.875. Bartlett’s test exhibited a chi-square value of 4272.919 with 325 degrees of freedom, and a significance level of P < .001. These results indicate that the sample data are suitable for factor analysis and that the questionnaire exhibits satisfactory validity.

Second, exploratory factor analysis was performed on the sample data. Principal component analysis was used to extract factors with eigenvalues >1, followed by orthogonal rotation using the varimax method. 7 common factors were extracted, with a cumulative variance explained of 72.713%, exceeding the 60% threshold and indicating a satisfactory level of explanatory power. The rotated factor-loading matrix exhibited that all measurement items had loadings above 0.7 on their intended factors and below 0.4 on other factors, confirming that the items effectively represent their underlying constructs and supporting good construct validity.

Third, convergent and discriminant validity were performed. In statistical research, convergent validity is considered acceptable when standardized factor loadings exceed 0.6, composite reliability (CR) for each dimension exceeds 0.7, and average variance extracted (AVE) exceeds 0.5. In this study, all standardized factor loadings were above 0.6, CR values for all dimensions exceeded 0.8, and AVE values were >0.5, demonstrating strong convergent validity.

Finally, discriminant validity refers to the extent to which measures of different constructs are distinct. According to statistical standards, a scale is considered to have good discriminant validity if the square root of the AVE for each construct is greater than its correlations with other constructs. In this study, the square roots of the AVEs for all variables were greater than their corresponding inter-construct correlations, further supporting the questionnaire’s strong discriminant validity. 2.4. Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS (version 24.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) were used for statistical analysis: reliability test: Cronbach’s α coefficient and corrected item-total correlations were used to evaluate internal consistency. validity test: KMO test, Bartlett’s test of sphericity, exploratory factor analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis were performed to determine the validity of content, construct, convergent, and discriminant. Model fit evaluation: SEM was used to analyze the path relationships among variables, with fit indices including chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio, root mean square residual, goodness-of-fit index, root mean square error of approximation, normed fit index, comparative fit index, Tucker-Lewis index, and incremental fit index. (4) Mediation effect test: Bootstrap method (5000 resamplings) was used to calculate 95% CIs to determine the significance of mediation effects. Moderation effect test: The PROCESS macro (Model 1, developed by Andrew F. Hayes, The Ohio State University, USA) in SPSS was used to test the moderating role of trust, and simple slope analysis was used for visualization.

3. Result

This study systematically investigated the relationships among perceived value (functional, emotional, efficiency, and social value), satisfaction, behavioral intention, and trust in home care for older adult patients within the context of medical disputes. The analysis used SEM, Bootstrap mediation analysis, and PROCESS moderation analysis to validate the proposed theoretical framework. The detailed statistical results are presented below.

3.1. Model fit indices evaluation

This study used AMOS 24.0 to evaluate the constructed SEM, beginning with an assessment of overall model fit. SEM fit indices can be categorized into 3 types: absolute, incremental, and parsimonious. Absolute fit indices evaluate the model’s ability to reproduce or predict the observed data. Incremental fit indices compare the fit of the target model to a baseline model, typically the independence model. Parsimonious fit indices account for model complexity and favor simpler models.

All fit indices of the SEM constructed in this study met the desired criteria (Table 4). Specifically, chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio was 1.289, below the ideal threshold of 3. root mean square residual was 0.043, meeting the criterion of <0.05. goodness-of-fit indexo reached 0.931, exceeding the minimum standard of 0.90. root mean square error of approximation was 0.031, well within the acceptable range of 0.08 or lower. Regarding incremental fit indices, normed fit index was 0.930, comparative fit index was 0.983, Tucker-Lewis index was 0.980, and incremental fit index was 0.983, all of which were above the ideal cutoff value of 0.90. These results indicate that the constructed SEM fits the sample data well and effectively captures the relational structure among perceived value, satisfaction, behavioral intention, and trust in home care for older adult patients. This provides a solid foundation for further path analysis and hypothesis testing.

3.2.1. In-depth analysis of unsupported paths

During SEM, two hypothesized paths were not supported: “efficiency value → satisfaction” and “social value → behavioral intention.”

3.2.1.1. Analysis of the non-significant path from efficiency value to satisfaction

The impact of efficiency value on satisfaction was not significant (β = 0.080, P > .05). Theoretically, this result aligns with the core proposition of socioemotional selectivity theory: as individuals age, their time perspective shifts from a “future-oriented” to a “present-oriented” perspective, and the priority of emotional fulfillment and safety requirements is significantly higher than that of instrumental goals, including efficiency. Also, the risk-averse nature of older adult patients makes them instinctively vigilant about potential compromised service quality and safety hazards arising from an “efficiency-first” approach in the context of doctor-patient disputes, thereby reducing the significance of efficiency value in satisfaction evaluation.

3.2.1.2. Analysis of the non-significant path from social value to behavioral intention

Social value did not have a direct effect on behavioral intention (β = 0.114, P > .05), however, it was completely mediated by satisfaction. This result can be explained by Social Identity Theory: the core of social value lies in an individual’s perception of group belongingness and identity recognition, and the behavioral transformation of such abstract values frequently requires specific emotional experiences (satisfaction) as a bridge. For older adult patients, the dignity and recognition conferred by social value must be transformed into immediate psychological satisfaction through continuous service interactions before further stimulating behavioral intention to reuse or recommend the service. Additionally, 52.94% of the older adult patients in the sample live with their children, and their behavioral decisions are easily influenced by family support. As an explicit indicator collectively recognized by both families and individuals, satisfaction serves as the primary mediator in the transformation of social value into behavioral intention.

3.3. Mediation effect analysis

To more robustly investigate the mediating effect of satisfaction between perceived value and behavioral intention, this study used the Bootstrap method for mediation analysis. Bootstrap is a nonparametric resampling method that does not depend on the assumption of normality and is particularly suitable for distributions of product terms, including mediating effects, which are often non-normal. By repeatedly drawing a large number of (5000) subsamples from the original sample and calculating the CI of the mediating effect, a more accurate estimation can be obtained. In this study, using AMOS 24.0, the Bootstrap resampling size was set to 5000 to ensure stable estimation and to calculate the 95% CI.

Also, satisfaction partially mediated the relationship between functional value and behavioral intention (Table 7). The total effect of functional value on behavioral intention was 0.256 (P < .01), with a direct effect of 0.194 (P < .05), representing 75.78%, and an indirect effect of 0.062 (P < .01), accounting for 24.22%. Since both direct and indirect effects were significant and the 95% CI did not include 0, satisfaction partially mediated the relationship. Similarly, a partial mediation effect was observed between emotional value and behavioral intention: the total effect was 0.292 (P < .01), the direct effect was 0.220 (P < .01, 75.34% of the total), and the indirect effect was 0.071 (P < .01, 24.32% of the total), with CI excluding 0.

The total effect was significant (0.211, P < .01), however, the indirect effect was not significant (0.027, P > .05), and the 95% CI included 0 (–0.011, 0.115). Therefore, no mediation effect was present. For social value and behavioral intention, satisfaction demonstrated full mediation: the total effect was significant (0.188, P < .01), the direct effect was not significant (0.103, P > .05) with a 95% CI of 0 (–0.029, 0.266), whereas the indirect effect was significant (0.085, P < .01) with a 95% CI of 0 (0.029, 0.217). It suggests that the effect of social value on behavioral intention is fully mediated by satisfaction.

In the context of medical disputes, older adult patients perceive the efficiency value of home-based care primarily through objective indicators. Due to abundant personal time and increased risk sensitivity, they frequently interpret high efficiency as indicative of rushed service or potential safety concerns, thereby preferring a “slow but steady” approach. Also, their satisfaction judgments highlight relational and safety-related considerations over procedural efficiency. Consequently, efficiency improvements do not significantly improve overall satisfaction, thereby rendering satisfaction an ineffective mediator between efficiency value and behavioral intention. A significant direct effect of efficiency on behavioral intention was observed, however, no significant indirect effect through satisfaction was present.

In response to patient-provider disputes, they have adjusted their expectations, accepting lower efficiency and prioritizing transparency and trust-related aspects. Therefore, the trust’s role is more impactful in strengthening other value-satisfaction relationships than in improving efficiency. 4. Discussion

Based on perceived value theory and trust theory, this study developed a structural model to investigate the determinants of home care services for older adult patients in a medical dispute context. Through SEM, the complex relationships between perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intention were empirically validated. The results demonstrated that the measurement instrument exhibited satisfactory reliability and validity. All model’s fit indices reached acceptable thresholds, and the proposed hypotheses were supported. These outcomes provide robust empirical support for the application of perceived value theory in healthcare services. Also, the study offers practical insights for home care providers seeking to improve service design and delivery. The implications are particularly significant in settings characterized by low doctor-patient trust, underscoring the critical role of trust-building in promoting patient satisfaction and maintaining positive behavioral intentions.

The novelty of this study is threefold. First, it applies the PV-S-BI model to home care for older adult patients within the high-tension context of doctor-patient disputes in China: a setting where perceived value mechanisms have not been empirically examined. Second, unlike previous studies that treat trust as a direct predictor or mediator, this study introduces trust as a moderating variable, thereby uncovering how trust level changes the strength of the value–satisfaction relationship. Third, the study reports counterintuitive findings that challenge conventional service management assumptions: efficiency value does not significantly affect satisfaction, and social value influences behavioral intention only indirectly through full mediation by satisfaction. These contributions extend perceived value theory to vulnerable populations and low-trust medical environments.

4.1. Influence of perceived value on satisfaction

In medical disputes, 3 dimensions of perceived value (social, emotional, and functional) significantly improve older adult patients’ satisfaction with home care. Among the key dimensions, social value demonstrated the strongest influence, followed by emotional and functional value. These findings suggest a distinct evaluative pattern specific to older adult patients, with social value emerging as the most critical factor influencing satisfaction with home-based older adults care. This contrasts with conventional service marketing theories, which frequently emphasize functional value as the primary driver of satisfaction, yet aligns with recent studies that highlight the psychosocial needs of vulnerable populations.[15,16] It is important because this pattern differs from findings in the U.S. healthcare system, where functional value (clinical effectiveness and service professionalism) dominates older adult patients’ satisfaction with home care. The U.S. home care sector is predominantly market-oriented, depending significantly on commercial insurance and private payment, which encourages patients to prioritize measurable functional outcomes. In contrast, within the context of reduced doctor-patient trust in China, older adult patients seek emotional security and social recognition through care services to mitigate insecurity, underscoring the importance of social and emotional value.[17] This divergence can arise from older adult patients’ increased experiences of social isolation and marginalization, particularly within the stressful context of medical disputes. Elements including respect, recognition, and a sense of belonging, which are core aspects of social value, not only address basic requirements but also provide emotional security and psychological support, thereby significantly improving satisfaction. Thus, social connectedness matters greatly for older adult patients’ satisfaction. For service providers, fostering community inclusion and promoting meaningful social engagement should be prioritized to improve satisfaction in home care services.

Also, emotional value exerts a significant positive effect on older adult patients’ satisfaction, highlighting its vital role in restoring strained physician-patient relationships. This finding aligns with prior service marketing research that highlights the importance of emotional factors in shaping consumer experiences.[18] In contrast to emotional influences in conventional service settings, emotional support in post-conflict care serves to rebuild trust and reduce dissatisfaction. Negative experiences from medical conflicts can distort patients’ perceptions, whereas compassion, patience, and respect from caregivers can elicit positive emotional responses and facilitate cognitive reappraisal. Considering their physical and psychological vulnerability, older adult patients demonstrate increased dependence on emotional support. The trust established through caregiving resembles a secure attachment, serving as an emotional anchor that promotes recovery. This supports the relevance of attachment theory in service recovery contexts.[19] Accordingly, service providers should strengthen caregivers’ emotional communication skills and capacity for humanistic care to better address older adult patients’ affective needs, improve satisfaction, and support psychological and relational healing following disputes.

Also, functional value was confirmed to significantly improve older adult patients’ satisfaction, confirming that practical service effectiveness remains a fundamental element of satisfaction. This result supports established service quality theories and reinforces the importance of functional performance in service evaluation.[20] The present study further highlights the role of functional value in trust restoration within conflictual medical environments. Delivering professional and effective care helps rebuild patients’ confidence in the healthcare system. older adult patients continue to engage in rational assessments of service quality, despite the significant effects of social and emotional value. Functional value, manifested through measurable improvements in health and daily living, serves both as a prerequisite for satisfaction and a basis for regaining trust. Competent professional services result in better health outcomes and a higher quality of life, which in turn rebuilds trust. Even when emphasizing emotional and social benefits, functional reliability remains indispensable. older adult patients seek measurable health progress and practical assistance, which constitute the foundation of their satisfaction. Consequently, home care providers should consistently improve technical capabilities and service professionalism. Ensuring high functional quality is vital not only for patient satisfaction but also for rebuilding trust, particularly following physician-patient conflicts.

4.2. Direct effects of perceived value on behavioral intentions

The analysis of the direct effects of perceived value on behavioral intention demonstrates that emotional, functional, and efficiency value all exert significant and positive affect on behavioral intention, whereas social value has no direct effect. Social value serves as the primary driver of satisfaction, however, the findings indicate a distinct mechanism for behavioral intention in which emotional value emerges as the strongest predictor. This statistical difference indicates that social recognition results in patient satisfaction, whereas the emotional bond fostered through empathy and respect is what truly transforms that satisfaction into loyalty. Additionally, a decision-making mechanism shaped by the interplay of emotional resonance and practical utility, which together play a central role in forming older adult patients’ willingness to continue using or recommend home care services, especially in settings characterized by strained doctor-patient relationships.

Emotional value exerts a significant, direct, positive influence on behavioral intentions, underscoring the importance of affective factors in decision-making under high-stress conditions. This finding contrasts with traditional rational decision-making theories that emphasize cognitive components, including attitude, and aligns more closely with contemporary consumer behavior research that underscores the role of emotion in driving behavioral choices.[21,22] In the context of medical disputes, increased uncertainty and perceived risk cause older adult patients to depend more on emotional indicators when evaluating home care options. Demonstrations of care, empathy, and respect by caregivers elicit positive emotions, which subsequently serve as direct motivation for patients to continue using and recommending the service. According to the socioemotional selectivity theory, older adults progressively prioritize emotional satisfaction and present well-being. Therefore, emotional value serves as a key driver of behavioral intentions. These insights enhance the comprehension of older adult patients’ decision-making processes and suggest that service providers should actively foster emotional connections to strengthen loyalty and encourage favorable word-of-mouth.

Functional value exhibited a significant direct positive effect on behavioral intentions, confirming that service functionality remains a critical factor in patient decision-making. This result supports rational decision-making models, including the Theory of Planned Behavior, which asserts that attitudes toward behavioral outcomes, particularly functional benefits, directly affect intentions.[23] This study further underscores the significance of functional value in rebuilding trust and motivating service adoption in contexts where trust has been compromised. According to the theory of planned behavior, functional value shapes patients’ evaluative attitudes regarding outcomes, including health improvement and quality of care.[24] In low-trust environments, patients depend more heavily on observable competence and measurable outcomes to regain confidence in the healthcare system. These practical benefits provide a rational foundation for continued service use. Consequently, providers should strengthen service functionality, ensure technical quality, and deliver measurable health outcomes. These initiatives can not only strengthen behavioral intentions but also promote trust recovery and support long-term engagement.

Efficiency value has a significant direct positive effect on behavioral intentions, underscoring the importance of convenience and operational ease in patient decision-making. This finding is consistent with existing research indicating that consumers value service accessibility and usability when making decisions.[25] Efficiency can have a limited impact on overall satisfaction, however, it has been observed to directly shape behavioral intention across multiple service industries. This study contributes by highlighting how efficiency reduces perceived “transaction costs” and facilitates service adoption, particularly in the sensitive setting of medical disputes. From a behavioral economics perspective, while emotional and experiential factors frequently influence satisfaction, decision-making tends to emphasize cost-effectiveness and operational convenience. older adult patients can not consider efficiency central to their satisfaction, however, they highly value convenience, responsiveness, and streamlined procedures when deciding whether to continue or recommend a service. Accordingly, providers should focus on process optimization, including simplifying appointment systems, reducing wait times, and offering convenient payment methods, to reduce decision barriers, improve service accessibility, and strengthen patient retention.

Social value does not directly affect behavioral intention, however, it exerts an indirect effect through satisfaction. Besides, social value improves patients’ overall service evaluation and promotes emotional attachment, which are fundamental components of satisfaction and subsequently increase the probability of positive behavioral outcomes. This suggests that social value serves as a foundational element that shapes cognitive and affective perceptions, providing a basis for other values to operate. Its impact on behavior is thus mediated through satisfaction. This mechanism aligns with social identity theory, which proposes that an individual’s sense of belonging and group identification significantly influence attitudes and, indirectly, behavioral intentions.[26]

4.3. Mediating role of satisfaction

Bootstrap mediation analysis indicated that satisfaction partially mediated the effects of functional and emotional value on behavioral intention. Also, satisfaction fully mediated the effect of social value. However, no significant mediation was observed for the efficiency value path. These findings quantitatively validate the cognitive-affective-behavioral sequence in the model and demonstrate that perceived value requires satisfaction as a mediating factor to translate into patient loyalty.

Satisfaction plays a significant mediating role between perceived value and behavioral intention among older home care adult service users. This finding suggests an underlying cognitive-affective-behavioral mechanism in patients’ service acceptance process, particularly in the context of medical disputes, and aligns with established theoretical frameworks.[27]

This mediation pattern differs from that observed in cross-system studies. A South Korean community-integrated care study found that social value can directly influence behavioral intention, without mediating through satisfaction.[28] This is based on the South Korean community-centric care system, which promotes direct social identity formation through community activities, allowing social value to translate into behavioral intentions without an emotional filter. Conversely, U.S. standardized healthcare system exhibits a stronger direct association between perceived value and behavioral intention, with satisfaction exerting a relatively weaker mediating effect. It may be attributed to the extensive standardization and maturity of U.S. medical services, which improve the direct predictability of value on behavior.

In environments still emotionally charged from prior medical disputes, the mediating function of satisfaction becomes especially critical. It integrates patients’ cognitive assessments of service into a consolidated emotional evaluation, thereby facilitating more accurate prediction of behavioral intentions. Also, the strength of this mediation is moderated by trust. Patients with lower trust depend significantly on satisfaction to guide their behavioral decisions, whereas individuals with higher trust base their decisions on a pre-established sense of reliability. This underscores the pivotal role of trust in shaping service acceptance, particularly within challenged relational contexts. When trust is compromised, satisfaction becomes a more direct and influential predictor of patient behavior.

4.4. Moderating role of trust

The PROCESS moderation analysis indicates that trust significantly and positively moderates the relationships between satisfaction and functional, emotional, and social value. However, there is no moderating effect on the efficiency value path. The significant interaction effects observed in the moderation analysis confirm that trust is not simply a contextual factor but a catalytic moderator that alters the strength of the value-satisfaction relationship. These statistical interactions indicate that the conversion of functional and emotional value into satisfaction is highly conditional: it increases under high trust and decreases under low trust.

Trust significantly moderates the relationships between perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intentions among older adult recipients of home care. Distinct psychological mechanisms were identified across different trust-level groups, providing novel insights into patient decision-making in post-dispute healthcare settings. The moderating effect of trust is well established in sectors including finance and e-commerce, where it reduces perceived risk and facilitates transactional decisions.[29] However, this study extends its application to the highly sensitive and relational context of medical care. Besides, the results reveal two contrasting psychological patterns: compensatory dependence under low trust and stability tolerance under high trust. These mechanisms challenge the conventional hypothesis of trust as uniformly beneficial, highlighting its context-dependent and dynamic nature. The intensity of this moderating effect varies across national contexts. In the healthcare systems of some middle- and low-income countries, patients’ dependence on trust is relatively lower.[30] This is because the highly market-oriented structure of the system, which provides patients with multiple service choices and explicit quality evaluation criteria, reduces their dependence on interpersonal trust. In contrast, research on the Canadian “doctor-patient partnership” model confirmed that trust can strengthen the conversion of perceived value into satisfaction, underscoring its positive role in medical service interactions.[31] In the Chinese context of strained doctor-patient relationships, trust exerts a more significant moderating effect, serving as a “value amplifier” that enhances the positive impact of perceived value (especially social, emotional, and functional value) on satisfaction.

In the low-trust group, a compensatory reliance mechanism was observed. Among these patients, the influence of social value on satisfaction increased from β = 0.330 to β = 0.487 (P < .001), emotional value from β = 0.236 to β = 0.358 (P < .001), and functional value on behavioral intention from β = 0.172 to β = 0.264 (P < .01). This pattern corresponds with cognitive dissonance and psychological defense theories, suggesting that patients exaggerate perceived service benefits to compensate for underlying uncertainty and discomfort.[32]

Conversely, the high-trust group exhibited a stability tolerance mechanism. Here, the corresponding coefficients decreased to β = 0.198 (P < .05), β = 0.142 (P < .05), and β = 0.108 (P > .05), respectively. This aligns with the “buffering effect” of trust, in which patients with established trust exhibit greater tolerance for variations in service performance.

It suggests that intervention strategies should be tailored to patients’ trust levels. For low-trust patients, strengthening social, emotional, and functional values is essential to rebuild confidence. For high-trust patients, maintaining consistent service quality is essential to preserving trust. The study thereby provides both theoretical insight and practical guidance for improving doctor-patient relationships, particularly in environments susceptible to conflict.

4.5. Non-significance of the efficiency value

The statistical results indicate that the efficiency value has no significant effect on satisfaction and is not moderated by trust, contradicting the conventional wisdom in service management that efficiency drives satisfaction. This statistical non-significance yields a counterintuitive theoretical finding: for older adult patients in low-trust contexts, the objective metric of speed is decoupled from the subjective feeling of satisfaction. This indicates that slowness is redefined as carefulness rather than inefficiency.[33] Home care systems in European countries, including Germany and France, are highly standardized,[34,35] patients have a strong association between efficiency and quality. Accordingly, efficiency (response speed) positively affects satisfaction. In the U.S., older adult patients value efficiency more than their Chinese counterparts due to a stronger perception of time value.[36] Against the context of doctor-patient disputes in China, older adult patients associate “high efficiency” with hurried care and safety risks, thereby exacerbating negative perceptions of efficiency. Meanwhile, most older adult patients in China are retired with flexible schedules, and their demand for efficiency is lower than for safety and emotional needs (interpersonal interaction and empathetic care). older adult patients exhibit an increased risk aversion. In contrast to younger populations who frequently prioritize speed, older adult individuals generally value stability and safety in healthcare services. Within the context of frequent medical disputes, they can associate an emphasis on efficiency with rushed procedures, potential quality compromises, or inadequate attention, which could further undermine trust. For these patients, a caregiver’s patience, comprehensive communication, and procedural diligence, even when time-consuming, can increase perceived security and psychological comfort, thereby reducing the influence of efficiency on satisfaction.

Trust plays a limited role here. Its absence of a moderating effect suggests that efficiency, as a relatively objective and quantifiable indicator, is less susceptible to subjective trust. Patients appear to evaluate efficiency based on observable process characteristics, whereas trust predominantly shapes the perception of more qualitative value dimensions, including emotional and social value. In home care for the older adults, indiscriminate efficiency improvements do not significantly improve satisfaction. However, providers should focus resource allocation on strengthening emotional connection, social respect, and functional support, while ensuring efficiency without compromising stability, safety, or humanistic care.

4.6. The potential influence of personality traits

Beyond the demographic and clinical factors examined in this study, individual personality traits may importantly shape older adult patients’ perceptions of care value and satisfaction. A growing body of evidence suggests that Type D personality which characterized by high negative affectivity and social inhibition is associated with worse health status, lower quality of life, and reduced healthcare satisfaction across various chronic conditions. Among patients undergoing spine surgery, those with Type D personality reported significantly lower satisfaction compared to non-Type D patients.[37] Similarly, in a large Danish cohort (n = 6756), low agreeableness and high neuroticism scores were associated with lower patient satisfaction and an increased wish to complain about healthcare services.[38] Moreover, personality traits may interact with the patient’s decision-making process. A randomized controlled trial in a type 1 diabetes population found that lower self-control significantly attenuated clinical outcomes under a patient-initiated care model, underscoring the need to consider behavioral heterogeneity in chronic disease management.[39]

4.7. Implications for practice

4.7.1. Establishing a service system centered on social value

Considering the significant impact of social value on older adult patients’ satisfaction, service providers should prioritize strategies that enhance it. This can be accomplished through developing robust social support networks, including regularly organized health education seminars and rehabilitation experience-sharing meetings, to help maintain older adult patients’ social connectedness. Personalized dignity care plans should be developed to fully respect their autonomy and decision-making rights. Also, mechanisms that facilitate family involvement should be established to strengthen patients’ sense of belonging and enhance perceived social support.[40]

4.7.2. Developing a systematic trust management mechanism

To address the critical need to restore trust in medical dispute contexts, service providers should implement targeted strategies to foster confidence. Transparent service processes should be adopted, including clear communication of care plans, expected outcomes, and potential risks to older adult patients. Standardized communication protocols between caregivers and patients must be established, requiring caregivers to conduct regular in-depth dialogs and adapt care plans responsively.[41] Additionally, a dedicated complaint-handling and feedback mechanism should be established to ensure timely and effective resolution of patient concerns. Specifically, providers can implement a service transparency card system that details care procedures and cost standards to reduce distrust caused by hidden costs. Also, regarding complaint management, a 24-hours rapid-response mechanism should be implemented that mandates intervention by a dedicated supervisor within 1 hour of a dispute. This efficient crisis intervention functions as an institutional safeguard to prevent conflict escalation and preserve trust.

4.7.3. Implementing differentiated trust management strategies

For older adult patients with lower trust levels, service providers should increase both the frequency of services and the intensity of interactions to cultivate trust through enhanced face-to-face engagement.[42] Providing comprehensive service records and outcome evaluation reports can help patients visually recognize the measurable benefits of care services. Consistency in caregiver assignment should be maintained to avoid frequent personnel changes, thereby promoting stable and continuous service relationships that gradually build a reliable foundation of trust. For actionable implementation, enforcing a primary caregiver responsibility system is recommended to ensure that each older adult patient is attended to by a dedicated caregiver or a fixed nursing team, thereby minimizing anxiety associated with personnel turnover. Additionally, service providers should adopt visualized care logs in mobile applications to push real-time service reports to family members, including pre- and post-care photos and vital sign data. This transparent information-sharing mechanism serves as a practical approach to eliminate information asymmetry and effectively rebuild trust within the current high-tension medical environment.

4.7.4. Optimizing the delivery mechanism of emotional value

Efforts should focus on enhancing caregivers’ emotional communication skills through specialized training, thereby strengthening their capacity to establish meaningful emotional connections with older adult patients. Standardized emotional care protocols should be developed to ensure that warmth and empathy are consistently conveyed during service provision. Also, specialized emotional support services should be established to provide personalized emotional care plans, tailored to older adult patients with specific psychosocial needs.

4.8. Limitations and future research directions

This study provides meaningful insights. However, several limitations should be addressed, which point to productive avenues for future research. First, cross-sectional surveys limit the ability to establish causal relationships among perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intentions. Future research could adopt longitudinal tracking designs, measuring multiple times at the start of home care services for the older adults (T1), mid-service (T2), and some time post-service (T3), to more clearly elucidate the dynamic influence mechanisms and causal directions among these variables. Additionally, quasi-experimental designs (including comparing patients who participated in specific trust-building intervention projects with those who did not) can be used to evaluate the direct effects of trust enhancement. Second, the target sample size for this study was set at 350 or even higher, and ultimately 306 valid samples were collected. This meets the minimum sample size requirement for SEM analysis and has been supplemented by robust statistical methods, including Bootstrapping. However, we acknowledge that the use of convenience sampling can result in a sample that is not fully representative of the population. This is because convenience sampling generally recruits participants primarily from specific regional groups, which can limit the generalizability of the findings to the overall population of older adult family care service users nationwide. Future studies should adopt stratified random sampling or multi-center designs to increase the diversity, representativeness, and external validity of the sample. Besides, this study collected data through self-report questionnaires, which introduced common-method bias. To minimize its impact as much as possible, clarity of the items was ensured and anonymity in the questionnaire was maintained. In addition, Harman’s single-factor test was performed in the statistical analysis to identify the issue. The results indicated that common-method bias was not significant. Third, the data were collected exclusively through self-reported questionnaires from older adult patients. The measurement tools demonstrated good reliability and validity, however, common method bias cannot be ruled out. Future studies could integrate multiple data sources, including evaluations from service providers, objective service usage metrics, or in-depth qualitative interviews, to facilitate triangulation and strengthen methodological rigor. Fourth, this study primarily focused on medical-patient disputes as the macro background and did not perform a detailed measurement of the exposure degree of individual patients to disputes (including personal experience, experience of relatives and friends, and frequency of media contact) and their subjective perception (including risk perception and sense of injustice). Fifth, this study did not measure individual personality traits, which recent evidence suggests may explain a substantial portion of variance in patient satisfaction above and beyond sociodemographic and service-related factors. The absence of personality assessment may confound the observed relationships between perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intention, and we cannot rule out the possibility that the effects reported in this study are partly attributable to unmeasured personality differences.

Future research can develop or adopt scales to quantify individuals’ dispute-related experiences and perceptions, and incorporate them as covariates or moderating variables into the model to more accurately reveal the influence path of the dispute background on patients’ psychology and behavior. In addition, future studies should also incorporate validated personality instruments to account for this individual-level heterogeneity.

5. Conclusion

This study developed a conceptual model of factors influencing home care services for older adult patients in the context of medical disputes, grounded in perceived value theory. Empirical analyses validated the relational mechanisms among perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intention. The results demonstrate that social value exerts the strongest effect on satisfaction, that emotional value has the most significant direct impact on behavioral intention, that satisfaction acts as a critical mediator, and that trust serves as the key moderator across most of these relationships.

From a theoretical perspective, this study offers 3 key contributions. First, it effectively provides perceived value theory into the complex context of home-based older adults care, characterized by vulnerable patients and eroded trust, thereby significantly expanding the theory’s applicability and contextual relevance. Second, it presents trust as a key moderating variable, shifting the analytical focus from a conventional “direct-effect” paradigm to a more sophisticated “conditional-influence” mechanism. Third, by focusing on the emerging field of home care services, the study establishes a measurement framework specifically adapted to the dual challenges of serving a high-need older adult population within fragile doctor-patient dynamics.

In practical terms, the proposed frameworks, including a social value-oriented service system, systematic trust management mechanisms, differentiated trust-building strategies, and an enhanced emotional value delivery model, provide actionable guidance for home care providers seeking to improve service quality. These strategies have significant theoretical and practical implications for promoting more harmonious patient-provider interactions and improving the overall standard of home-based care for the older adults.
