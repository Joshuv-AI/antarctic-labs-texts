Karyotype-based evaluation of ovarian reserve before ovarian tissue cryopreservation in Turner syndrome patients
Authors: Juan Du, Jiaojiao Cheng, Xiangyan Ruan, Muqing Gu, Fengyu Jin, Yurui Wu, Jing Jin, Zecheng Wang, Yanqiu Li, Yanglu Li, Lingling Jiang, Mingzhen Zhang, Anming Liu, Alfred O. Mueck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTurner syndrome (TS) girls experience accelerated ovarian reserve depletion, exhibiting a strong demand for ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) to preserve fertility and ovarian function. However, the current knowledge of OTC in TS is limited, and predicting follicular presence is insufficient. This study aims to investigate ovarian follicular reserve based on karyotypes, thereby contributing empirical data to informed decision-making in TS populations.MethodsThe retrospective case-control study comprised 26 TS patients and 52 age-matched non-TS (NTS) controls who underwent OTC between January 2022 and March 2025. The TS cohort included 45,X (n = 2), 45,X/46,XX (n = 6), and ‘other’ (n = 18), the latter encompassing 7 cases with 47,XXX mosaicism and 11 with structural X-chromosome abnormalities (SCA). Ovarian volume, estimated total follicle count (TFC), anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) were compared between the TS and NTS across age cohorts (0–5, 6–10, and > 10 years), and further analyzed across TS karyotype categories.ResultsFollicles were found in 91.7% of TS cases. Estimated TFC in TS showed no significant difference from NTS in the 0–5 age group. However, it was significantly reduced in TS aged 6–10 and > 10 years, reaching only 1.2% and 4.6% of NTS estimated TFC, respectively. The FSH levels (range 0.83–13.55 IU/L) in TS patients were elevated but below the diagnostic threshold for premature ovarian insufficiency, despite the absence of detectable follicles. No correlation was observed between AMH and estimated TFC. When comparing parameters across different karyotypes, the 45,X/46,XX group had the highest AMH, ovarian volume, and estimated TFC, which declined sharply with age (r = -0.858). Patients with a 45,X karyotype exhibited bilateral streak ovaries and no follicles. In 45,X/47,XXX, estimated TFC correlated positively with ovarian volume, independent of age (r = 0.922). TS with SCA generally exhibited low estimated TFC and AMH.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that ovarian reserve in TS is associated with karyotype, supporting the need for individualized counseling regarding OTC. Girls with 45,X appear to have a low probability of retaining follicles. For those with 45,X/46,XX mosaicism, the observed rapid decline in ovarian reserve suggests that timely consideration of OTC may be beneficial. In patients with 45,X/47,XXX, ovarian volume may be a helpful indicator for estimating TFC, potentially supporting OTC while ovarian volume remains substantial. Patients with SCA generally presented with reduced ovarian reserve in this study.

Background

Girls with Turner syndrome (TS) lack a partial or complete sex chromosome, which causes an accelerated decline of their ovarian reserve [1]. TS is the most prevalent aetiology for non-iatrogenic premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) in young females, accounting for up to 43% of cases in specific populations [2]. The estimated number of pediatric TS cases globally was 240,598 in 2021, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 2,500 live female births [3]. Only 30% of TS girls will have spontaneous breast development, and 10% to 15% will have spontaneous menarche [4]. The prevalence of natural conception ranged from 15% to 48% in women with 45,X/46,XX, 1% to 3% in women with 45,X, and 4% to 9% in women with other TS karyotypes [5]. Moreover, the inevitable occurrence of POI will further affect the bone health, mental health, cardiovascular health, neurological functions, and other aspects of TS patients [6].

TS girls exhibit a strong demand for preserving fertility and ovarian function, as exemplified by techniques such as ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC), which offers procedural efficiency, prepubertal applicability, and concurrent preservation of both reproductive potential and ovarian endocrine function [7]. OTC may effectively preserve primordial follicles in TS girls before POI [8].

However, current knowledge of OTC in TS is limited [9], with only 6 cohort studies and 5 case reports involving a total of 195 girls worldwide [5]. Furthermore, while current clinical practice enables a qualitative prediction of the “presence or absence of ovarian follicles” in TS patients [4], it lacks the ability to provide a personalized assessment of ovarian reserve. This limitation makes it difficult to guide individualized decision-making regarding OTC for TS girls and their parents [10]. Prediction models are lacking to determine which girls with TS might benefit from OTC [5]. These critical gaps underscore the necessity for international collaboration and expertise in OTC for TS patients.

To our knowledge, this study seeks to explore ovarian follicular reserve across different karyotypes in patients with TS, providing empirical data to inform individualized counseling on fertility preservation options in this population.

Methods

Patient population

This study included 26 TS patients (aged 2–24 years) and 52 age-matched NTS chemotherapy-naïve controls (aged 1–24 years). All TS and NTS participants underwent unilateral ovariectomy for OTC at a single center between January 2022 and March 2025. No statistically significant differences were observed between the TS and NTS groups in any age cohort (0–5, 6–10, or > 10 years).

Within the TS cohort (n = 26), karyotype distribution was as follows: 2 cases of monosomy 45,X; 6 cases of mosaicism 45,X/46,XX; and 18 cases of ‘other’ karyotypes (including 7 cases of mosaicism with 47,XXX and 11 cases of structural X-chromosome aberration). Karyotypes were determined in lymphocytes.

Within the NTS cohort (n = 52), all patients were without prior chemotherapy and scheduled to undergo hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), comprising 13 cases with aplastic anemia, 11 cases with mucopolysaccharidosis, 9 cases with chronic active Epstein-Barr virus infection, 6 cases with platelet function disorders, 3 cases with Niemann-Pick disease, 3 cases with myelodysplastic syndrome, 2 cases with thalassemia, and 1 case each of osteopetrosis, chronic neutrophilic leukemia, metachromatic leukodystrophy, primary immunodeficiency disorder, and congenital dyserythropoietic anemia.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2022-KY-066-02). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all patients or from their parents/legal guardians for those under 16 years of age. The patient characteristics are described in Table 1.

Hormone assays before OTC

Before laparoscopy and cryopreservation, we collected blood samples from patients to evaluate their sex hormone levels, including Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH). None of the included patients were on hormonal treatment. Blood analysis of FSH was determined by the ADVIA Centaur XP Chemiluminescence Analyzer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc.), and AMH was determined by an enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) method (Guangzhou Kangrun Biological Technology Co., Ltd.). The Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecological Hospital measured the detections.

Transabdominal ultrasound before OTC

A transabdominal ultrasound was performed during OTC counseling to systematically evaluate the pelvic anatomy, focusing on uterine and ovarian morphology, dimensions, and follicular status. The examination was conducted by experienced ultrasound specialists at our hospital, using standardized protocols. Patients were instructed to maintain a moderately filled bladder to optimize acoustic window visualization.

Evaluation of ovarian volume per ovary

The ovarian volume is calculated as (π/6) × long diameter × short diameter × short diameter [10]. After ovarian tissue retrieval, the long and short diameters were measured using a straight ruler.

Ovarian tissue retrieval and cryopreservation

All participants (TS group and NTS group) underwent unilateral oophorectomy by laparoscopy, reporting no complications after surgery. After retrieval, the ovary was transported to our cryobank in sterile Custodiol® (Histidine-Tryptophan-Ketoglutarate solution, HTK) at 4–8 °C [11, 12]. Ovarian cortical fragments with a thickness of 1 mm were prepared for cryopreservation as previously described; meanwhile, standard biopsies were obtained using the punch with a diameter of 2 mm (PFM Medical AG, Cologne, Germany) for follicle density (FD) analysis and routine viability assay [12].

Evaluation of follicle density and estimated total follicle count

To evaluate the FD, 2 mm cortical biopsies were incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 2 h in 0.8 mg/ml collagenase Type 1 A (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Munich, Germany) supplemented with 0.2% calcein-AM (Promega GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and DPBS (Gibco by Life Technologies, Paisley, UK) for digestion and staining. After pipetting and incubating at room temperature for 30 min, follicles settled to the bottom of the well, allowing FD to be measured by fluorescence and light microscopy [13–15]. Primordial or primary follicles were identified by a single layer of flattened or cuboidal granulosa cells (GCs). Secondary preantral follicles were identified by two or more layers of GCs and the absence of an antrum [13]. The estimated total follicle count (TFC) was defined as the absolute number of follicles present within one intact ovary, which was calculated as (Follicle number/mm³) × (Total ovarian cortical volume [mm³]).

Histological staining in the ovarian cortex

Fresh ovarian cortex specimens from all participants underwent histological evaluation with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Following staining, tissue sections were digitized through automated whole-slide imaging using the Panoramic P250 Flash III slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). Digital images were systematically analyzed with CaseViewer software (version 2.3; 3DHISTECH). Follicle classification criteria for primordial and growing follicles were applied according to established morphological protocols [16].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD or mean ± SEM and were compared using t-tests (two groups) or one-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc test (multiple groups). Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (min, max) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Mann-Whitney U tests (multiple groups). Chi-square test was used for proportion comparisons. Pearson correlation was applied for normally distributed variables. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The comparison between TS and NTS

Age

No statistically significant differences were observed between TS and NTS groups across all age cohorts: the 0–5 years group (mean ± SD: 3.60 ± 1.14 vs. 3.41 ± 1.33, P = 0.777), the 6–10 years group (mean ± SD: 8.46 ± 1.13 vs. 7.67 ± 1.19, P = 0.071), and the > 10 years group (mean ± SD: 16.50 ± 4.14 vs. 14.31 ± 3.84, P = 0.837) (Fig. 1A-C).

Ovarian volume per ovary

For each patient, ovarian volume was determined by direct measurement following oophorectomy. In the 0–5 years cohort, the TS group demonstrated a significantly smaller ovarian volume per ovary compared to the NTS group (median [range]: 226.08 [56.52-461.58] mm³ vs. 763.00 [117.00-3184.00] mm³; P = 0.009), with only 29.6% of NTS controls. This trend persisted in the 6–10 years cohort, with the TS group showing a reduced ovarian volume per ovary (median [range]: 159.09 [27.21-2562.24] mm³ vs. 1158.00 [157.00-5668.00] mm³; P = 0.042), which corresponds with 13.7% of the ovarian volume of NTS controls. No statistically significant difference was observed between groups in subjects older than 10 years (median [range]: 3349.34 [31.40-25693.57] mm³ vs. 3910.00 [167.00-11775.00] mm³; P = 0.528) (Fig. 1D-F).

Estimated TFC

The estimated TFC was obtained by performing enzymatic digestion of the ovarian cortical samples from each patient, followed by microscopic counting of the isolated follicles, and then calculated using the formula: TFC = (Follicle density [number/mm³]) × (Total ovarian cortical volume [mm³]). The current study analyzed ovarian cortex samples from 24 of 26 TS patients, identifying follicular presence in 91.7% of cases (22/24), in contrast to their consistent presence in all NTS control samples. In the 0–5 years cohort, no significant difference in estimated TFC was observed between the TS and NTS groups (median [range]: 2141 [86-126665] vs. 32566 [3996-478341]; P = 0.099). However, in the 6–10 year age group, the TS group exhibited a significantly lower estimated TFC than NTS controls (286.5 [0-25000] vs. 24609.5 [1468-177461]; P < 0.001), with only 1.2% as many follicles as NTS controls. This trend persisted in subjects older than 10 years, where the TS group demonstrated markedly reduced estimated TFC (655.5 [0-6359] vs. 14356 [401–122950]; P = 0.002), with only 4.6% of the number of follicles in NTS controls (Fig. 1G-I).

Serum AMH and FSH

In the 0–5 years cohort, no significant difference was observed in AMH levels between the TS group and the NTS control group (median [range]: 0.81 [0.06–1.47] ng/ml vs. 1.31 [0.06–4.91] ng/ml, P = 0.058), but TS group demonstrated significantly higher FSH levels than the NTS group (mean ± SD: 5.78 ± 2.93 IU/l vs. 3.25 ± 1.51 IU/l, P = 0.016). In the 6–10 years age, the TS group exhibited significantly lower AMH levels than the NTS group (median [range]: 0.25 [0.01–5.05] ng/ml vs. 2.78 [0.07–10.89] ng/ml, P < 0.001), while showing higher FSH levels (mean ± SD: 6.85 ± 4.35 IU/l vs. 2.32 ± 1.44 IU/l, P = 0.009). For subjects older than 10 years, AMH levels remained significantly reduced in the TS group compared to controls (median [range]: 0.17 [0.06–3.74] ng/ml vs. 2.03 [0.06–7.19] ng/ml, P = 0.013), while showing higher FSH levels (mean ± SD: 6.31 ± 3.08 IU/l vs. 3.63 ± 2.59 IU/l, P = 0.038) (Fig. 1J-O).

Furthermore, correlation analysis between AMH and estimated TFC revealed no significant association in either the TS group (Pearson’s r = 0.187, P = 0.381) or the NTS control group (Pearson’s r = 0.003, P = 0.984).

Ovarian volume per ovary

Estimated TFC and follicular development stages

No follicle was observed in the ovaries of girls with 45,X (median [range]: 0 [0–0]). The 45,X/46,XX group demonstrated significantly higher estimated TFC (median [range]: 1,594.5 [602 − 126,665]) when compared with the ‘Other’ group (215 [17–25,000]), P = 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

In 45,X/46,XX ovarian biopsies, primordial and primary follicles represented 89.77% of the estimated TFC, and secondary preantral follicles accounted for 10.23%. Of the ‘Other’ karyotype estimated TFC, 95.91% were primordial and primary follicles, and only 4.09% were secondary preantral follicles. When using the Chi-square tests to evaluate the differences in ratios, a significantly higher proportion of secondary preantral follicles and a lower proportion of primordial and primary follicles were observed in girls with 45,X/46,XX than in ‘Other’ karyotype (P < 0.01).

Serum AMH and FSH

The serum AMH levels in the 45,X/46,XX group were significantly higher than those in both the 45,X group (median [range]: 1.48 [0.90–3.74] ng/ml vs. 0.06 ng/ml, P = 0.029) and the ‘Other’ group (median [range]: 0.19 [0.06–5.05] ng/ml, P = 0.01). However, no significant difference was observed in serum AMH levels between the 45,X group and the ‘Other’ group (P = 0.99) (Fig. 2C).

Regarding serum FSH levels, there were no statistically significant differences among the three groups (median [range]: 45,X vs. 45,X/46,XX vs. ‘Other’ group: 5.78 [2.56-9.00] IU/l vs. 3.91 [0.95–8.16] IU/l vs. 7.36 [0.83–13.55] IU/l, P = 0.259) (Fig. 2D).

The characteristics of different TS karyotypes

45,X karyotype

Girls with 45,X karyotype demonstrated streak-like ovaries without antral follicles (Fig. 3A). Additionally, no follicles were detected in the ovarian cortical samples by Calcein-AM or HE assessment in TS with a 45,X karyotype (Figs. 4A and 5A).

Discussion

In the present study, we analyzed ovarian reserve and OTC outcomes in 26 TS patients and 52 matched NTS controls across different age groups. We also analyzed the variations among different karyotypes within the TS cohort, providing empirical data to inform fertility preservation decision-making in TS populations. The current study analyzed ovarian cortex samples from 24 of 26 TS patients, identifying follicular presence in 91.7% of cases (22/24). The variable and low rates of follicular detection (26–67%) reported in earlier OTC studies of TS patients [4, 17, 18] highlight the historical challenge of identifying optimal candidates for fertility preservation. Our study addresses this challenge by employing a karyotype-guided assessment to pre-select a cohort with a relatively high likelihood of follicular presence, as reflected in our detection rate of 91.7%. This strategy offers a clinical pathway to optimize resource utilization and minimize unnecessary interventions.

Our study revealed that, compared with NTS, the estimated TFC in girls with TS did not differ significantly in the 0–5 age group. However, in patients aged 6–10 and > 10 years, the estimated TFC was reduced considerably in TS girls, reaching only 1.2% and 4.6% of that in NTS participants. These findings demonstrate that age is a determining factor in ovarian reserve. Given the accelerated depletion of the ovarian follicle pool in most TS patients, fertility preservation counselling and strategies may be considered as early as possible after diagnosis, with consideration of OTC in the prepubertal years [19, 20].

In contrast, some studies consider spontaneous puberty as an OTC eligibility parameter in TS [4, 5, 21]. We suggest that relying solely on spontaneous puberty might be suboptimal for several reasons. First, since less than 30% of TS patients experience spontaneous puberty [20], this criterion would exclude the majority of patients from a potentially time-sensitive intervention. Second, our data and others indicate that follicular reserves are often critically depleted by pubertal onset, with the inevitable occurrence of POI at a mean age of 16–18 years [22, 23]. Therefore, waiting for spontaneous puberty may result in missing the narrow window for effective OTC.

However, recommendations regarding OTC in TS may be tempered by an acknowledgment of its current limitations. It is crucial to note that the efficacy of OTC and transplantation specifically for TS patients remains to be fully established. To date, live birth following OTC and transplantation in patients with TS has not been reported. The literature includes only one case of pregnancy after OTC in TS, which resulted in miscarriage [24]. Fertility counseling in TS needs to be comprehensive and extend beyond technical feasibility to include a thorough assessment of pregnancy-associated risks, particularly the elevated cardiovascular morbidity, which mandates pre-conception evaluation and multidisciplinary management in a high-risk obstetric setting [20].

Furthermore, we would like to note that the alternative for Patient No. 8, with an AMH level of 3.74 ng/mL, FSH of 3.42 IU/L, and observed AFC, could be oocyte cryopreservation. However, in mainland China, assisted reproductive technologies, including oocyte freezing, are strictly regulated. After consulting multiple local fertility centers, Patient No. 8 was declined to perform oocyte cryopreservation due to the essential administrative and ethical prerequisites, making it an unfeasible option for her at the time. Consequently, OTC was the only immediate option to preserve her fertility.

Notably, despite FSH levels in TS patients being elevated compared to controls, all TS patients exhibited FSH concentrations (range 0.83–13.55 IU/L) that were substantially below the diagnostic level for POI, including 2 cases without detectable ovarian follicles (with FSH levels of 2.56 and 9.00 IU/L, respectively). A possible explanation is that the patients’ young age and sex chromosome abnormalities led to an immature hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, resulting in low FSH levels [18]. From these findings, we suggest, as other authors have also noted, that low FSH may have limited clinical utility as a predictor of ovarian reserve and spontaneous puberty in patients with TS [18, 25, 26].

Although AMH is widely used as a biomarker for predicting ovarian reserve in reproductive-aged women, its utility in pediatric populations remains highly contentious. AMH is secreted by granulosa cells of small antral follicles and reflects the size of that specific follicle cohort. It becomes a reliable indicator of the primordial follicle pool—and thus true ovarian reserve—only when an equilibrium exists between small antral follicles and the resting primordial follicle pool [27]. In children, however, follicular development regulation is still immature, and such a functional equilibrium has not yet been established. As a result, TS girls with substantial ovarian reserve may exhibit low AMH levels, and AMH levels vary widely between girls, as observed in the current study. Liebenthron et al. also found that AMH was not correlated with cortical follicle counts in women ≤ 20 years [28]. Therefore, reliance on proposed AMH cutoff values for clinical decisions, such as the recommendation of AMH > 1.5 ng/mL by van der Coelen et al. for considering OTC [5], may be problematic in pediatric TS populations. While this cutoff aims to identify patients with sufficient reserve for future successful transplantation, its reliance on AMH may be of limited reliability in children. Given that AMH may not accurately reflect the true primordial follicle pool in young girls, using such a cutoff risks excluding individuals who, despite low AMH levels, may still have a meaningful number of primordial follicles worthy of cryopreservation.

This study demonstrates that the estimated TFC in patients with TS displays karyotype-dependent characteristics.

Patients with a 45,X karyotype exhibited the smallest ovarian volumes compared to other karyotypes, presenting with bilateral streak ovaries and no detectable follicles. Although previous studies have reported one patient with 45,X TS fertile case, the patient exhibited one ovary of normal size and morphology, and subsequent karyotyping confirmed a hidden mosaicism [29, 30]. Therefore, in 45,X patients with the ultrasonographically normal ovary, genotyping of two cell lines, including lymphocytes and buccal cells, may be advisable [31].

The 45,X/46,XX group exhibited significantly higher ovarian volume and TFC than other Turner syndrome karyotypes, with antral follicles observed in all cases. However, the TFC declined sharply with age, and it has been reported that POI emerged at a median age of 15 years in 45,X/46,XX [5]. Therefore, we suggest that prompt consideration of OTC upon TS diagnosis in 45,X/46,XX patients may be warranted, given the observed accelerated decline. We hypothesize that this accelerated follicular depletion may result from overactivation of quiescent primordial follicles, which is supported by our observation of a significantly elevated proportion of growing follicles in 45,X/46,XX patients, aligning with xenograft studies showing up to 50% growing follicles in the transplanted ovarian tissue from such individuals [32].

However, girls with 45,X/47,XXX demonstrated a significant positive correlation between TFC and ovarian volume independent of age. Furthermore, ovarian morphology in 45,X/47,XXX showed substantial interindividual variability, encompassing streak gonads, a unilateral streak gonad with a contralateral normomorphic ovary, and dumbbell-shaped ovaries. These features may result from the limited ability of the 47,XXX cell line to ameliorate the consequences of haploinsufficiency in 45,X cells, with a reported mean age at menopause being 28.5 years [33]. Fertility preservation may be considered for patients with 45,X/47,XXX mosaicism who exhibit well-developed ovarian morphology before extensive follicular depletion. TS patients with SCA generally exhibited low TFC, particularly when a high proportion of 45,X cell lines and streak ovary is present. Moreover, morphologically abnormal follicles were observed at a high frequency, consistent with the findings reported by Nadesapillai et al. [4].

Our findings suggest that karyotype, age, and ovarian volume may be important predictors of ovarian reserve in TS patients, whereas sex hormone levels may offer limited guidance in prepubertal girls with TS. Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. As a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, particularly within specific karyotype subgroups, the findings should be considered preliminary. The limited cohort restricted our analyses to correlation assessments with trend-based observations, rather than enabling the establishment of definitive clinical thresholds. The generalizability of our findings may be influenced by these factors, and larger, multi-center studies are needed to validate the observed associations between karyotype, ovarian volume, and ovarian reserve. Future expansion of the cohort will facilitate the determination of more precise cutoff values for ovarian volume as a clinical parameter in TS fertility counseling.

Conclusions

Understanding ovarian reserve is critical for enabling TS girls and parents to make informed decisions regarding fertility preservation. Ovarian reserve characteristics varied substantially among TS karyotypes, underscoring the need for karyotype-based individualized counseling. The current study demonstrated that karyotype, age, and ovarian volume are important predictors of ovarian reserve, whereas sex hormones demonstrated limited prognostic utility. Based on these findings, we propose the following: girls with 45,X and bilateral streak ovaries are unlikely to retain follicles; those with 45,X/46,XX mosaicism experience a rapid decline in estimated TFC with age, and timely consideration of OTC may be warranted; in 45,X/47,XXX mosaicism, estimated TFC correlated positively with ovarian volume, suggesting that OTC could be considered while ovarian volume remains substantial; patients with SCA generally presented with reduced ovarian size and diminished reserve. Future studies with expanded sample sizes are needed to refine OTC indications in TS and establish cutoff values and reference ranges for ovarian reserve parameters.
