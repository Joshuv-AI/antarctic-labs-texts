Real‐world pharmacovigilance for anti‐Aβ therapies using a large language model

INTRODUCTION

Anti‐amyloid beta (Aβ) therapies, approved for early‐stage Alzheimer's disease (AD), carry serious risks, including amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs). 1 Approximately 2.2 million individuals in the United States are eligible for anti‐Aβ therapies 2 and this number is expected to rise, highlighting the need for real‐world strategies to enhance pharmacovigilance 3 , 4 , 5 and detect treatment emergent adverse events (AEs). As anti‐Aβ therapies increase in use for early‐stage AD, there is a critical need for scalable pharmacovigilance to reduce harm among the vulnerable populations with early‐stage AD.

Large language models (LLMs) have the potential to enhance pharmacovigilance and transform AE detection and optimize clinical workflows in many clinical contexts. 6 LLMs are advanced systems trained on massive amounts of text to recognize language in a way that mimics how humans use language. 7 LLM has extensive applications 8 and holds particular promise for automating AE extraction from structured and unstructured clinical data, which would allow for clinical practice to move away from expensive and time‐consuming manual chart review. 9 , 10 Despite this potential, it is unclear what constitutes a “gold standard” to evaluate LLM performance in the context of anti‐Aβ therapies. To integrate LLMs into clinical practice, methods are needed to construct an adjudicated gold standard that resolves discrepancies between human reviewers and LLM outputs.

In response to this gap, our study seeks to compare standard expert review to an LLM‐augmented pharmacovigilance workflow for detecting treatment‐emergent AEs associated with anti‐Aβ therapies in routine care.

METHODS

This retrospective study analyzed 20,123 clinical documents from 46 patients who received at least one dose of anti‐Aβ therapy between June 24, 2024 and July 30, 2025 at a large mid‐Atlantic health‐care system. Clinical documents included outpatient and inpatient clinician notes (e.g., neurology, primary care, geriatrics, telehealth), radiology reports, and infusion records. For note‐level evaluation, we defined eligible notes as narrative clinical documents occurring on or after the first anti‐amyloid therapy infusion date, including clinician notes (e.g., neurology, primary care, geriatrics, urgent care), telehealth encounters, radiology reports, and patient‐generated communications (e.g., phone messages/calls). Specific note types included Assessment and Plan documentation, History and Physical, History of Present Illness documentation, neurology and primary care office/clinic notes, telehealth notes, triage notes, urgent care notes, and reports. Administrative and non‐informative documents (e.g., authorization forms, templated patient instructions) were excluded. In total, 3567 eligible notes were processed in the note‐level workflow comparison. The extraction task was to identify clinical documentation consistent with treatment‐emergent AEs potentially related to anti‐Aβ therapy. During expert review, events of interest included ARIAs with edema or effusion (ARIA‐E) and with hemorrhage or siderosis (ARIA‐H), as well as headache, syncope, hypersensitivity reactions, gastrointestinal symptoms, and infusion reactions. Outputs were generated at two levels: a patient‐level indicator reflecting whether a patient experienced any treatment‐emergent AE during the observation period, and a note‐level indicator reflecting whether an individual clinical note contained documentation relevant to an AE.

We evaluated a single LLM, an institutionally hosted GPT‐4 Omni deployment, 11 accessed through a closed endpoint operating behind the health‐care system's firewall within a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant research infrastructure. All model inference was executed within a secure Azure Databricks environment and no patient‐level data were transmitted outside the secure environment; outputs were retained within the same workspace for downstream clinician review and adjudication. The GPT‐4 Omni deployment corresponded to the May 13, 2024 release.

We conducted a comparison between the standard of care pharmacovigilance workflow to an LLM‐augmented pharmacovigilance workflow (Figure 1) with both approaches simultaneously applied to the same set of clinical documents. The standard of care workflow reflected existing practices for identifying potential AEs, while the LLM‐augmented workflow used a LLM to identify and classify text‐based signals of potential AEs within structured and unstructured clinical documentation. For the standard of care pharmacovigilance workflow, two clinical pharmacists conducted a retrospective chart review to manually annotate anti‐Aβ therapy AEs related to one or more of the following categories: ARIA‐E (edema/effusion), ARIA‐H (microhemorrhage/ siderosis), headache, syncope, hypersensitivity reaction, gastrointestinal symptoms, and infusion reaction.

Prompt design and implementation

We developed prompts iteratively with clinician input across five refinement cycles to support AE extraction from heterogeneous electronic health record text. Prompt refinement used a development 20% subset of expert‐annotated notes, which were reviewed by the two clinical pharmacists involved in the primary annotation process. This subset included both positive and negative cases, including 16 notes with confirmed AEs. These notes were used iteratively to refine prompt performance across note types. This subset was not fully held out from the final evaluation, and performance was not separately assessed before and after prompt refinement. The final prompt instructed the model to determine whether a note contained documentation of a treatment‐related AE and extract a supporting text span. To enforce structured and reproducible outputs, responses were constrained using a Pydantic schema. We incorporated few‐shot prompting by including representative examples of AEs that were frequently missed during early prompt iterations. The final system prompt and output schema are provided in the Appendix in supporting information to support reproducibility.

The finalized prompt was then applied uniformly to the full eligible note corpus under a standardized inference configuration. Note‐level outputs were stored in structured form and aggregated to the patient level by labeling a patient as having an AE if at least one note contained model‐identified AE documentation. Outputs remained within the secure environment for clinician review and discordance adjudication. In addition, we extracted demographic information and International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision codes corresponding to potential AEs for comparison.

Construction of the adjudicated gold standard

Because no pre‐existing gold standard exists for anti‐Aβ therapy AEs, we implemented a discordance adjudication process to derive an adjudicated gold standard for the head‐to‐head comparison. We first identify discordant cases in which the LLM and initial human review disagreed. Two clinical reviewers re‐evaluated all discordant patient notes. Disagreements were resolved by consensus discussion. During adjudication, reviewers based decisions solely on the underlying clinical documentation; LLM outputs were used only to identify discordant cases. While this approach may introduce potential circularity, it reflects a pragmatic strategy to focus human review in settings in which exhaustive annotation is resource intensive, similar to targeted annotation strategies used in active learning. 12

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were patient‐level sensitivity and positive predicted value (PPV) for AE detection using the adjudicated label as the reference. Secondary outcomes included note‐level overlap between expert‐flagged and LLM‐flagged AE notes and the volume of notes flagged for downstream review.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

We included 46 patients who received at least one dose of anti‐Aβ therapy (61% female), with a median age of 76 years (range, 58–85). We retrieved 20,123 clinical documents spanning clinician documentation, radiology reports, and infusion records. For note‐level evaluation, 3567 eligible notes were processed. After discordance adjudication, 76% (35/46) of patients experienced an AE. The most common AEs were headache (39%, 18/46), dizziness (28%, 13/46), nausea (20%, 9/46), and infusion reactions (11%, 5/46); ARIA‐E occurred in 17% (8/46) and ARIA‐H occurred in 9% (4/46).

Patient‐level performance

Using the adjudicated label as a reference, expert review identified 31 true positives, 11 true negatives, 0 false positives, and 4 false negatives. This corresponded to sensitivity 88.6% and PPV 100%. The LLM‐augmented workflow identified 35 true positives, 7 true negatives, 4 false positives, and 0 false negatives (Table 1). This corresponded to sensitivity 100% and PPV 89.7%. In clinical review, the LLM‐augmented workflow captured four clinically meaningful events not recognized during expert review.

Note‐level performance

Across 3567 eligible notes, expert review flagged 81 notes as AE relevant. The LLM flagged 422 notes as AE relevant. The overlap between workflows included 76 notes flagged by both expert review and the LLM; 5 notes were expert flagged but not LLM flagged; and 346 notes were LLM flagged but not expert flagged. Phone messages and calls accounted for 44% (153/346) of the notes that were flagged by the LLM but not by the expert. These notes frequently contained brief, patient‐ or caregiver‐reported symptom descriptions using informal language “pt feeling week, feeling like vomiting,” “memory and fatigue issues, particularly after recent infusion,” and “pt going downhill and unable to do anything for herself.” Overall, the LLM identified 94% (76/81) of the expert‐flagged AE notes.

LLM error analysis

Among the five notes identified by expert review but missed by the LLM, four consisted of long clinical documentation (two clinic notes, one telehealth note, and one History and Physical note), each > 1500 words and 11,000 characters. These notes contained templated sections, inconsistent spacing, and multiple clinical concepts, which may have introduced noise and reduced the model's ability to identify relevant AE signals. The fifth missed case was a radiology report describing findings such as “enlarged ventricles and subarachnoid space,” which were noted as stable compared to prior imaging. The presence of “similar to prior exam” language may have led the model to deprioritize this finding.

Among the four patient‐level false positives, two were related to phone messages and one was relating to an Assessment and Plan note. All three contained patient instructions, which the model may have misinterpreted as active AEs. The remaining case included an incomplete radiology report lacking sufficient clinical context. These errors suggest sensitivity to sentence fragments and patient instructions.

DISCUSSION

Our findings provide preliminary indications that LLM are a potentially important tool for identifying AEs among patients receiving anti‐Aβ therapy in which our incidence of AEs is consistent with other studies requiring enhanced monitoring especially during the first 14 weeks of treatment. 13 The LLM‐augmented workflow demonstrated higher sensitivity than the standard of care workflow; identifying all adjudicated AEs and detecting four clinically meaningful events not recognized during expert review. It is important to note that the larger number of LLM‐flagged AE notes introduces a trade‐off between increased sensitivity and downstream review burden. In practice, these signals could be operationalized through dashboard‐based cohort monitoring or targeted notifications to clinicians, allowing prioritization of actionable information within neurologist‐led workflows. At the population level, results can be aggregated into dashboard‐based views used by pharmacists, care navigators, or safety teams to monitor cohorts of patients receiving anti‐amyloid therapy. These dashboards can support prioritization of patients and notes requiring follow‐up based on factors such as symptom type, severity, or recency. At the point‐of‐care level, prioritized signals can be delivered to neurologists through targeted notifications, such as alerts highlighting patients scheduled for visits who have recent documentation suggestive of potential AEs. This allows neurologists to review curated information without needing to manually search across fragmented documentation. These examples illustrate the potential role of LLMs as safety “amplifiers” within pharmacovigilance pipelines for anti‐Aβ therapy in which AE documentation is diffuse, time consuming, heterogeneous, and lacks a gold standard. 14 These findings provide preliminary indications that LLMs may support scalable pharmacovigilance signal detection; however, further work is needed to evaluate prioritization strategies, integration into clinical workflows, and impact on patient care. This has important implications for those receiving anti‐Aβ therapy as well as the millions of family caregivers who provide care and support to people living with AD.

The larger number of LLM‐flagged AE notes likely reflects several factors. First, human reviewers often stop reviewing a patient's notes once an AE is identified, while the LLM continues to process all notes for the patient. Second, subjective symptoms (e.g., depression, mood changes) mentioned as pre‐existing to therapy initiation are often ignored by human reviewers but captured by the model. These findings suggest that contextual understanding (i.e., timing relative to treatment) are important for improving specificity as well as providing additional timely guidance to the clinical team within the workflow. Finally, the LLM was more sensitive to unstructured, symptom‐focused documentation in phone messages and calls, suggesting that expert review may be more amenable to other encounter types.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single health‐care system with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Second, prompt refinement was conducted on a subset of annotated notes that was not fully held out, introducing potential bias toward the annotation schema. Third, the adjudication process, while necessary in the absence of a gold standard, may introduce bias. Finally, the study does not evaluate real‐world clinical integration or impact on patient outcomes. However, with the growing interest in and prescribing of anti‐Aβ therapies, larger and more diverse datasets will become available to build upon this work. Future work should incorporate temporal reasoning, structured imaging data, and multicenter validation to further improve accuracy and generalizability. Moreover, it is critical to take into consideration encounter type and documentation style when evaluating the performance of current and future LLMs to detect AEs.
