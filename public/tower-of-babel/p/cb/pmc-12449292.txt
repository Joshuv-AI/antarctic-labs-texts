Advancing our understanding of the influence of myeloid-derived suppressor cells in chronic myeloid leukemia
Authors: Xing Meng, Yue Zhang, Hong Xu, Yanyan Zhang, Hao He, Jianmin Ma, Xiaoyan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a malignant clonal proliferative disease originating from hematopoietic stem cells. While treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) effectively eliminates the majority of leukemia cells in patients, residual leukemia cells can still be detected in those achieving deep molecular remission, ultimately leading to drug resistance or relapse. But the exact mechanism is unclear. In recent years, the immune microenvironment has been a hot research topic in hematologic malignancies. CML patients exhibit abnormalities in antitumor immunity. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which possess immune-suppressing functions, inhibit the proliferation and activation of CD4+/CD8+ T cells, Tregs, B cells, and NK cells, and play a central role in the antitumor immune response in a wide range of cancers. Abnormalities in numbers and functions of MDSCs are exhibited in CML patients, which affect the immune status of CML patients through multiple mechanisms. This review summarizes the biological properties of MDSCs, their alterations in CML patients, their roles and specific mechanisms in the development of CML, and how these mechanisms can be leveraged to develop new therapeutic strategies, aiming to provide novel insights and approaches for the treatment of CML.Keywords: Chronic myeloid leukemia, Myeloid-derived suppressor cells, Drug resistance, Immunosuppression

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative disorder driven by the BCR/ABL fusion gene, characterized by the abnormal proliferation and impaired differentiation of leukemic cells. Current treatments with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting BCR/ABL, such as imatinib, dasatinib, and nilotinib, have achieved remarkable results and have become the standard of care for patients (Mauro et al. 2025; Toptas et al. 2025). However, residual leukemic stem cells remain detectable in some patients after achieving deep molecular remission (DMR) with TKIs therapy, leading to disease relapse or progression(Atallah and Schiffer 2020; Krishnan et al. 2023). CML patients exhibit abnormal immune system function, with dysfunction of NK cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CTLs) resulting in diminished immune activation. Conversely, increased numbers of MDSCs and regulatory T cells (Tregs) enhance immunosuppressive effects. Meanwhile, the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, T-cell immunoglobulin mucin-3 (TIM-3), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) is elevated. These factors collectively enable residual leukemia cells to evade immune surveillance, contributing to CML progression, but the exact mechanism remains unclear. Immunotherapy has the potential to restore antitumor immunity and eliminate residual leukemia stem cells in CML patients, enabling them to achieve long-term, sustained treatment-free remission (TFR) or even a cure (Brück et al. 2018; Hsieh et al. 2021; Mumprecht et al. 2009). MDSCs, which possess immune-suppressing functions, inhibit the proliferation and activation of T cells, B cells, and NK cells, and play a central role in the antitumor immune response in a wide range of cancers (Liu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024). Abnormalities in numbers and functions of MDSCs are exhibited in CML patients (Kapor and Santibanez 2021), which affect the immune status of CML patients through multiple mechanisms, thus promoting the progression of the disease.

Biological characterization of MDSCs

Classification and phenotypic characterization of MDSCs

MDSCs are a heterogeneous group of cell populations lacking clear cell morphology or surface markers. Most studies suggest that MDSCs primarily consist of granulocytic myeloid-derived immunosuppressive cells (Gr-MDSCs) and monocytic myeloid-derived immunosuppressive cells (Mo-MDSCs) (Bronte et al. 2016; DeNardo et al. 2021). Human leukocytes do not express Gr-1 and have significantly reduced or absent levels of HLA-DR expression. Gr-MDSCs exhibit the phenotype HLA-DRlowCD33+CD11b+CD15+CD14−CD66b+, while Mo-MDSCs have the phenotype HLA-DRlow/− CD11b+CD14+CD15−CD33+. Mouse MDSCs co-express the myeloid antigens GR-1 and CD11b, with Gr-MDSCs displaying the CD11b+Gr-1+Ly6GhighLy6Clow phenotype and Mo-MDSCs characterized by CD11b+Gr-1+Ly6Glow/−Ly6Chigh (Gabrilovich 2017). Early-stage MDSCs (e-MDSCs) are also present in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), phenotypically resembling immature myeloid precursor cells. But few studies on the development of e-MDSCs have been reported. They are characterized by CD33+CD11b+HLA-DR−CD14−CD15−, CD45+Lin−HLA-DR−/lowCD11b+ CD16−CD14−CD15−CD33+ or Lin−HLA-DR−CD33+ phenotype (Cole et al. 2021; Veglia et al. 2018). In hormonal mice and cancer patients, Gr-MDSCs constitute the major subpopulation of the total MDSCs population, accounting for 70–80%, although their immunosuppressive effect is not as pronounced as that of Mo-MDSCs.Additionally, tumor-associated fibroblasts with the phenotypic HLA-DR+CD11blow CD11clow CD33+IL-4Ra+ features, termed fibrocytic MDSCs (F-MDSCs), exhibit immunosuppressive effects on T cells but can be phenotypically distinguished from Gr-MDSCs and Mo-MDSCs(Gunaydin et al. 2015). In the early stages of tumor progression, a subpopulation of myeloid precursor cells with an MDSCs phenotype but lacking immune function, called MDSCs-like cells (MDSC-LCs), can also be detected (Table 1).

According to recent studies, Mo-MDSCs from hormonal mice can transform into Gr-MDSCs. This phenotypic change is triggered by the silencing of Rb1 transcription via epigenetic modification by histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC-2). CD14+ monocytes can develop into Mo-MDSCs through IL-10 and PGE2 in the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Tomić et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2017). Neutrophils can be transformed into Gr-MDSCs expressing LOX-1 through endoplasmic reticulum stress (Nan et al. 2018). These studies support the origin of MDSCs as pathologically activated neutrophils and monocytes (Pettinella et al. 2024), although it remains controversial whether MDSCs also represent the pathological state of mature myeloid cells.

Immunosuppressive mechanisms of MDSCs

MDSCs exhibit significant immunosuppressive functions but play distinct roles in benign diseases and malignant tumors. In benign pathological states, MDSCs maintain immune homeostasis by suppressing excessive immune responses, such as promoting organ transplantation tolerance. However, in the tumor microenvironment, the immunosuppressive properties of MDSCs facilitate immune escape and disease progression (Li et al. 2021; Ostrand-Rosenberg and Fenselau 2018; Santibanez 2025). MDSCs achieve immunosuppression through multiple mechanisms.

MDSCs secrete arginase-1 (Arg-1), which depletes L-arginine, causing T-cell nutrient depletion. This downregulates T-cell receptor complexes (TCRs), inhibits antigen-activated T-cell proliferation, and induces cell cycle arrest in the G0-G1 phase (Ibrahim et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2024; Rodriguez et al. 2009). In vitro supplementation with L-arginine reverses this phenomenon. Additionally, MDSCs-derived indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) depletes L-tryptophan and generates kynurenine, further suppressing T-cell activity (Park et al. 2023). Tumor hypoxia and lactate accumulation upregulate PD-L1 expression in MDSCs and promote metabolic conversion to fatty acid oxidation (FAO) (Hossain et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2019). FAO further induces the generation of Arg-1, NO, and peroxynitrite. MDSCs also exacerbate immunosuppression by generating oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (Fresno and Gironès 2021; Huang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). The produced peroxynitrite can lead to nitration of multiple proteins in tumor and immune cells, causing TCR desensitization and T-cell apoptosis (Ferrer-Sueta et al. 2018). Nitration can also induce conformational changes in the TCR-CD8 complex, rendering CTLs unresponsive to antigen-specific stimuli (Jia et al. 2023). MDSCs interfere with immune cell function through receptor-mediated mechanisms, with MDSCs expression of ADAM17 downregulating CD62L on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, limiting their migration to lymph nodes (Lu et al. 2024). MDSCs also express immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and CTLA-4. These checkpoints induce T cell dysfunction and apoptosis through the PD-L1/PD-1 and CTLA-4/CD80-CD86 pathways, and promote Tregs proliferation, which interacts with APC-like DCs via CTLA-4/CD80-CD86, resulting in impaired APC-dependent T cell activation (Haist et al. 2021). Recent studies have identified new immune checkpoint molecules such as VISTA, Gal-9, and CD155 that play roles in MDSCs immunomodulation (Deng et al. 2024; Knickmeier et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2023).

MDSCs can also induce NK cell dysfunction, primarily by reducing the expression of natural killer cell-associated molecule 2D (NKG2D) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) via transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) (Hu et al. 2025). MDSCs can also inhibit NK cell function by interacting with the NKp30 receptor via the NKp30 ligand or by downregulating the expression of CD247 on NK cells (Tavakoli et al. 2025). Additionally, IDO expressed by MDSCs reduces NK cell activity by downregulating NCR, NKG2D, and DNAM-1 receptors and decreasing NK cell secretion of interferon-γ. The specific molecular mechanism involves signal transduction and transcription activator 3 (STAT3)-mediated nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activation (Lu et al. 2024).

Moreover, MDSCs can indirectly promote immunosuppression by interacting with other myeloid cells, for example, by secreting IL-10 to induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype(Lopes et al. 2016; Su et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2023), inhibiting IL-12 secretion by DCs to weaken T cell activation, and promoting Treg cell proliferation to further enhance immunosuppression (Chaib et al. 2020). Together, these mechanisms constitute a multilevel immunosuppressive network of MDSCs in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1).

Alterations of MDSCs in CML patients

There is controversy regarding the alteration of MDSCs numbers in CML. Most studies have found that in patients with newly diagnosed CML, MDSCs are significantly increased and correlated with Sokal score and BCR/ABL1 transcript levels, with the increase primarily dominated by the Gr-MDSCs subtype (Das et al. 2013; Hong et al. 2022; Volti et al. 2014). However, it has also been suggested that the number and percentage of Gr-MDSCs in the peripheral blood and bone marrow of patients with primary CML are significantly lower than those in the healthy control (HC) and complete hematologic remission (CHR) groups(Ahn et al. 2021). In contrast, the number and percentage of Mo-MDSCs in patients were higher than those in HC and CHR groups, suggesting that an increase in Mo-MDSCs may be associated with the malignant course of CML. Even some investigators found no statistically significant difference in the percentage of Mo-MDSCs in patients with first diagnosis of CML in chronic phase compared to healthy individuals, and no significant difference in the percentage of Mo-MDSCs after TKIs treatment, suggesting that Mo-MDSCs are produced by normal monocyte precursors and that they are not affected in CML (Table 2).

Changes of MDSCs in the different phases of CML disease

The reason for the controversy may be partly due to the fact that, unlike other malignancies, chronic granulocytic leukemia CML cells are mainly composed of immature myeloid cells (IMCs) (Giallongo et al. 2017). While MDSCs are also IMCs, some Gr-MDSCs and Mo-MDSCs can express BCR/ABL proteins (Palumbo et al. 2019). CD34, a marker for CML leukemia cells, is expressed by 35% of MDSCs. Thus there is an overlap between CML leukemia cells and MDSCs. Investigating MDSCs markers in the CML cell lines K562 and BV-173 revealed that approximately half of the cells in both cell lines express MDSCs markers (Das et al. 2013). This suggests that some tumor cells may be considered MDSCs. The exact mechanism for the origin and development of MDSCs in CML and other tumors is unknown. Do these cells originate from bone marrow cells that are induced to become immunosuppressive by tumor-produced cytokines or are they immunosuppressive cells produced directly by the bone marrow in response to tumor stimulation?

The application of TKIs to treat patients with CML has an effect on MDSCs in their body while removing leukemia cells. Most studies have found a significant decrease in Gr-MDSCs after treatment with imatinib, nilotinib, and dasatinib (Giallongo et al. 2017; Hong et al. 2022). In particular, the percentage of Gr-MDSCs was significantly lower in patients who achieved major molecular remission (MMR) and DMR, and was not significantly different from healthy controls. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the proportion of Gr-MDSCs between the first- and second-generation TKIs treatment groups. Gr-MDSCs was associated with whether or not patients with CML were treated with a TKIs, but not with tumor load or disease remission status (Hong et al. 2022). In contrast, the reduction in the subpopulation of Mo-MDSCs during imatinib and nilotinib treatment was not significant, being effectively reduced only in dasatinib-treated patients (Giallongo et al. 2017). This may be due to dasatinib's ability to inhibit both the Src and NF-κB signaling pathways in MDSCs and Tregs, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation. There are also studies confirming that dasatinib treatment activates immunity, resulting in an increase in the number of CD8+ T cells and NK cells and a decrease in the number of Tregs (Harrington et al. 2023). A significant correlation between the number of Mo-MDSCs and MMR was observed in CML patients treated with dasatinib(Fan et al. 2022). Therefore, it was concluded that Mo-MDSCs could be used as an observational indicator for prognostic prediction in CML patients after dasatinib treatment (Kapor and Santibanez 2021). Similar findings have been reported in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL).

Similarly, a study found an increase in Mo-MDSCs in patients presenting with molecular relapse after TKIs cessation, whereas the number of Gr-MDSCs did not differ significantly between patients with TFR and those with molecular remission (MolR), suggesting that Gr-MDSCs may not be directly involved in the relapse process after TKIs discontinuation, whereas elevated levels of Mo-MDSCs appear to be associated with a poor prognosis (Irani et al. 2020).

Roles of MDSCs in the development of CML

Effects of MDSCs on T cells

In patients with CML, T cells underexpress CD62L, and plasma levels of soluble CD62L are elevated, creating a pro-inflammatory leukemic microenvironment and correlating with Sokal scores (Kwaśnik and Giannopoulos 2021). Nilotinib induces increased CD62 L expression on T cells and promotes recovery of host immune function (Ureshino et al. 2020). Compared with T cells from healthy individuals, patients with CML have increased expression of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells and decreased expression of the TCR-f chain on CD4+ T cells at the time of initial diagnosis and prior to MMR(Yao et al. 2023), with impaired immune effects against leukemia-associated antigens such as Wilms tumor-1 (WT-1), B lymphoma Mo-MLV insertion region homolog-1 (BMI-1), proteinase 3 (PR3), and preferentially expressed antigen of melanoma (PRAME)(Matsushita 2021). Meanwhile, CTLs express higher levels of exhaustion markers such as PD-1, T cell immunoglobulin and mucin structural domain 3 (TIM-3), and CTLA-4(Harada et al. 2021). These inhibitory receptors, upon binding to the corresponding ligands, inhibit the immune activating effects of T cells (Montecchi et al. 2024; Xiao et al. 2023). MDSCs from CML patients highly express PD-L1, which can bind to PD-1 on T cells, inducing inactivation of TCR signaling within T cells, thus leading to T cell exhaustion (Abdalsalam et al. 2024). In a CML mouse model, PD-L1 blockade improves the survival of CML mice in the acute phase (Tanaka et al. 2022). Meanwhile, Gr-MDSCs from CML patients highly express immunosuppressive factors such as Arg-1 and iNOS, which deplete L-arginine and L-cysteine required for T-cell proliferation in TME, thereby inhibiting T-cell activation and proliferation(Zhou et al. 2020), and promoting the progression of CML.

Interaction between MDSCs and Treg cells

CD4+CD25+FoxP3hi Treg cells can suppress the anti-tumor activity of CD4+, CD8+ T cells, Teff and NK cells, thereby inducing tolerance to cancer antigens(Haist et al. 2021). MDSCs can promote the transformation of naive CD4+CD25− T cells into induced Treg cells (iTregs) by secreting IL-10 and TGF-β, and promote their further proliferation and activation. However, TGF-β and IL-10 secreted by Tregs can enhance the secretion of IL-10, TGF-β, Arg-1, iNOS, etc. by MDSCs, and upregulate the expression of co-stimulatory ligands CD86 and PD-L1 (Lee et al. 2016). The PD-L1-PD-1 pathway can promote Tregs autosecretion of IL-35, thereby further enhancing IL-10 secretion and forming a positive feedback regulation (Lian et al. 2019). Reduced TGF-β secretion by Tregs can lead to decreased expression of Arg-1 and iNOS in Mo-MDSCs and reduced immunosuppressive activity (Haist et al. 2021).

The percentage of Tregs in the peripheral blood of patients with primary CML is significantly higher and directly correlates with the number of MDSCs (Saj et al. 2024). Tregs counts were significantly lower in patients with chronic phase CML compared to those in accelerated and acute phases (Hughes and Yong 2017). Tregs counts were reduced after TKIs treatment and were significantly lower in patients with CMR compared to those who did not achieve CMR. The proportion of CD3+CD4+ CD25+CD127−FoxP3+ Tregs was elevated in patients who relapsed after drug discontinuation (Irani et al. 2020). Gr-MDSCs subsets in CML patients with intermediate or high-risk Sokal scores can promote the proliferation of Tregs and CML disease progression compared with low-risk (LR) patients (Konopleva et al. 2016).

Effects of MDSCs on NK cells

NK cells play a crucial role in clearing drug-resistant leukemia cells. The number of mature NK cells is the most critical immunological parameter for predicting TKIs discontinuation. NK cells can trigger cytotoxic effects through signaling transitions between inhibitory receptors (KIR and NKG2A) and activating receptors (NKG2C, NKG2D, NKp46, and DNAM-1) (Kokiçi et al. 2024). When NK cells overexpress the NKG2C gene, it is predictive of disease remission, whereas overexpression of NKG2A is associated with resistance to TKIs therapy and early relapse. NK cells from CML patients also overexpress immune checkpoints such as TIM-3, TIGIT, and PD-1. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that NK cells in CML upregulate genes involved in the cell cycle and the cytokine signaling family gene suppressor Cish gene. In CML, Cish upregulation indicates NK cell activation and exhaustion. This nonspecific NK cell activation, which is unable to eliminate TKIs-resistant cells, may lead to leukemia progression.

In newly diagnosed CML patients in the chronic phase, NK cells are decreased in number, with reduced expression of the activating receptors NKp46 and DNAM1, impaired degranulation, and poor cytokine production(Hong et al. 2022; Kuznetsova et al. 2025). In CML patients who achieve MMR and MR, NK cells are increased in number (Zhang et al. 2025a, b), especially the more mature cytotoxic CD57+CD62L−NK cell phenotype.

Further studies revealed that Gr-MDSCs were able to inhibit NK cell proliferation and enhance NK cell killing activity by secreting Arg-1. The reason for the enhanced NK cell killing activity of Gr-MDSCs may be due to the feedback regulation of the human immune system between MDSCs and NK cells. When NK cells are reduced in the human immune system, especially when effector NK cells are reduced, MDSCs promote the function of NK cells and maintains immune homeostasis through feedback regulation (Hong et al. 2022).

MDSCs promote the proliferation of leukemia cells

MDSCs play a crucial role in the progression of CML. In addition to suppressing immune responses and helping tumor cells escape immune surveillance, Mo-MDSCs can promote the proliferation of K562 cells and CD34+ cells of newly diagnosed CML patients in vivo and in vitro, and promote the cell cycle progression of K562 cells or CD34+ cells. In the TKIs withdrawal model established by the K562 cell line, K562 cell-derived microvesicles (MVs) can also promote the proliferation of MDSCs, and with the effect of MVs derived from K562 cells in the TKIs withdrawal model on the proliferation of MDSCs being more pronounced(Xu et al. 2020). Tumor cells and MDSCs promote each other's proliferation, and the specific mechanism is still unclear. MSCs in the CML tumor microenvironment can also promote Gr-MDSCs to express high levels of Arg-1, TNF-α, IL-1β, cyclooxygenase-2(COX-2), and IL-6, thereby further enhancing the immunosuppressive effect(Konopleva et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2022). The bidirectional interaction between tumor cells and MDSCs leads to a vicious cycle and promotes the progression of CML disease. Therapeutic strategies targeting MDSCs

After CML patients are treated, the immunosuppressive effect of the organism is weakened, and the anti-tumor immune function is restored, exerting immunosurveillance and immunocidal effects. This immune effect can further eliminate tiny residual lesions in the body, promote the body to achieve DMR, and may achieve TFR, or even cure (Hsieh et al. 2021; Hughes et al. 2017). Therefore, immunotherapy is currently an important method to solve the problem of relapse and refractory disease and prevent disease progression. MDSCs play a central role in the antitumor immune response in hematological malignancies and are an important potential target. Reducing the number of MDSCs is one of the ways to target MDSCs. MDSCs have a short survival time in the blood, and some chemotherapeutic agents can remove MDSCs while inducing apoptosis in tumor cells. The proportion of Gr-MDSCs, as well as serum Arg-1 and iNOS levels, were significantly reduced, and the proportion of effector NK cells increased after TKIs treatment of patients with CML (Giallongo et al. 2017; Hong et al. 2022). Mo-MDSCs were significantly reduced after treatment of CML with dasatinib (Yu et al. 2022), and the number of Mo-MDSCs increased in patients who relapsed after discontinuation of the drug. In acute myeloid leukemia, humanized Fc-engineered CD33 monoclonal antibody (BI 836, 858) reduces the number of MDSCs through antibody-dependent cytotoxic effects and blocks the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines (Wang et al. 2019). In addition, some drugs can promote MDSCs differentiation. All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), an acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) drug, can eliminate Gr-MDSCs and stimulate MDSCs to develop into macrophages and dendritic cells (Zhang et al. 2025a, b). Cucurbitacin B (Cu B), a selective inhibitor of JAK2/STAT3 signaling, can promote MDSCs differentiation into DCs (Wang et al. 2023). However, the specific differentiation mechanism in hematological tumors needs further study. Activated MDSCs express large amounts of Arg-1 and NOS2. The use of inhibitors of these two enzymes can reverse the immunosuppressive effect of MDSCs in MM (Giannotta et al. 2023). Tadalafil, a phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE-5) inhibitor, can reduce the expression of ROS, Arg-1 and iNOS in MDSCs in patients with advanced relapsed/refractory MM and restore the anti-tumor immune response of T cells after treatment(Yu et al. 2022).

Additionally, PI3K inhibitors were able to block MDSCs recruitment to the tumor microenvironment and reduce MDSCs in tumors and spleen in a mouse model (Ren et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). The inhibitors duvelisib and ibrutinib are in a phase II clinical trial for the treatment of lymphoblastic leukemia (Li et al. 2021).

Conclusion

In recent years, the immune microenvironment has been a hot research topic in hematologic malignancies, and the role played by MDSCs in it has received increasing attention. In this review, we examined the biological properties of MDSCs, their alterations in CML, and their immunosuppressive roles and specific mechanisms in CML. Similar to their roles in the development of other cancers, MDSCs inhibit the body's immune response and promote immune evasion of leukemia cells in CML by regulating the functions of CD4+, CD8+ T cells, Tregs, and NK cells. Most studies have found markedly elevated levels of Gr-MDSCs and Mo-MDSCs in newly diagnosed CML patients, which decrease following TKI treatment. Some researchers propose using Mo-MDSCs as a prognostic indicator for CML patients. However, other studies report no difference in Mo-MDSCs levels between newly diagnosed patients and healthy controls. Therefore, this remains a subject of debate. Due to the lack of specific phenotypic markers for MDSCs, differences in the pathogenesis of MDSCs in different tumors, and the complexity of their genomic and metabolic differentiation mechanisms, in-depth studies and targeted therapy of MDSCs in CML still face challenges. The development and popularization of multi-omics and single-cell sequencing technologies will help to gain a deeper understanding of the origin and differentiation of MDSCs and their specific regulatory roles in different hematological tumors. Targeting MDSCs and remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment is a very promising direction for immunotherapy of hematologic malignancies.
