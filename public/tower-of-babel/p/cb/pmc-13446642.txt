Decoding MAPK cascades in plant immunity: Activation, regulation, integration, and pathogen manipulation
Authors: Guitao Zhong, Zhanchun Wang, Dingzhong Tang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK/MPK) cascades are conserved signaling modules that play crucial roles in plant growth, reproduction, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Many breakthroughs have been made recently in understanding the functional and regulatory mechanisms involving MAPK cascades in plant immunity. In this review, we summarize the conserved composition and activation of MAPK cascades and update advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms by which plants maintain or inhibit MAPK activation. We also highlight the functional links between MAPK activation and other immune events, suggesting crosstalk in the plant immune signaling network. Furthermore, we outline the strategies by which pathogen effectors inhibit plant immunity by manipulating MAPK signaling and illuminate the contribution of MAPK cascades to effector‐triggered immunity. Overall, this review provides major evidence for understanding the importance and complexity of MAPK signaling and reveals that the MAPK cascade serves as an essential signaling hub in activating plant immunity.

INTRODUCTION

Plants deploy a sophisticated immune system to sense pathogens and prevent their invasion, which includes two layers of immunity (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Jones et al., 2024). The first layer is initiated by cell surface‐localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) upon the perception of pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), termed pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI). However, some pathogens secrete effectors into plant cells or apoplasts to manipulate PTI for further invasion. As countermeasures, plants have evolved intracellular nucleotide‐binding and leucine‐rich repeat domain receptors (NLRs) to specifically recognize effectors, leading to effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), which is the second layer of immunity (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Jones et al., 2024). Immune responses in both PTI and ETI include reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, calcium (Ca2+) influx, mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK/MPK) cascade activation, and callose deposition, which are considered the foundation for crosstalk in PTI and ETI (Wang et al., 2020a; Yuan et al., 2021b).

MAPK signaling is a well‐characterized signal transduction pathway that plays critical roles in plant immunity (Sun and Zhang, 2022; Zhang and Zhang, 2022). In PTI, PRRs, including receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) and receptor‐like proteins (RLPs) lacking the intracellular kinase domain, generally form a PRR complex with coreceptors, such as BRASSINOSTEROID‐INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1)‐ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (BAK1) and SUPPRESSOR OF BIR1 (SOBIR1), upon sensing extracellular stimuli. The activated PRR complex subsequently transmits immune signals downstream to receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs), such as BOTRYTIS‐INDUCED KINASE 1 (BIK1) and BRASSINOSTEROID SIGNALING KINASE 1 (BSK1), which activate the MAPK cascade via phosphorylation (DeFalco and Zipfel, 2021). Transcription factors and other downstream components are ultimately phosphorylated by activated MAPK cascades to completely promote immune responses (Sun and Zhang, 2022; Zhang and Zhang, 2022). In ETI, MAPK cascades also serve as fundamental components. For example, WOUND‐INDUCED PROTEIN KINASE (WIPK) and SALICYLIC ACID‐INDUCED PROTEIN KINASE (SIPK) are two of the earliest identified MAPK members that are activated by Cf‐9 after the recognition of the fungal effector Avr9, although Cf‐9 is not the canonical NLR (Romeis et al., 1999). The MAPK kinase kinase NICOTIANA PROTEIN KINASE 1 (NPK1) contributes to immune responses mediated by multiple NLR genes, such as N, Bs2, and Rx (Jin et al., 2002). SlMAPKKKε positively regulates Pto/Prf‐mediated ETI in tomato (Melech‐Bonfil and Sessa, 2010). Furthermore, the effector AvrRpt2‐triggered ETI has also been found to activate MPK3/6 in Arabidopsis (Guan et al., 2015). These findings indicate that MAPK cascades are essential for both PTI and ETI.

The MAPK cascade is highly conserved and typically comprises a MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKKs/MEKKs), a MAPK kinase (MAPKKs/MKKs/MEKs), and a MAPK/MPK. There are approximately 69 MAPKKKs, 10 MKKs, and 20 MPKs in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), while 75 OsMAPKKKs, 9 OsMKKs, and 15 OsMPKs have been identified in rice (Oryza sativa) (Zhang and Zhang, 2022; Wang et al., 2026b). The activation of the MAPK cascade often occurs in two stages during plant immunity: MAPKKKs are phosphorylated and activated by upstream RLCKs and transphosphorylate downstream MKKs; activated MKKs transphosphorylate and activate MPKs (Sun and Zhang, 2022; Wu and Wang, 2024). Recently, many breakthroughs have been made in understanding the molecular mechanisms involved in the regulation of MAPK signaling. In this review, we focus on the most recent findings concerning the role of MAPK cascades in plant immunity and summarize recent breakthroughs in terms of their functional conservation across plant species, regulatory molecular mechanisms, roles in the growth–immunity trade‐off, crosstalk with other immune events, such as Ca2+ influx, ROS bursts, callose deposition, and phytohormone signaling. We also update how pathogens manipulate plant immunity by targeting MAPK components.

THE MEKK‐TYPE MAPK SIGNALING PATHWAY IS FUNCTIONALLY CONSERVED IN PLANT IMMUNITY

MAPK cascades are crucial for plant development, growth, reproduction, tolerance, and immunity. The composition of the MAPK cascade is conserved across plant species (Manna et al., 2023). One type of plant MAPK cascade is closely related to mammalian extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) and named MAPK and ERK kinase kinase (MEKK). For example, the well‐characterized MEKK‐type MAPK cascade MAPKKK3/4/5‐MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 plays an important role in immunity in Arabidopsis (Bi et al., 2018; Rao et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022b). However, why Arabidopsis has evolved three redundant MAPKKKs in the same cascade remains obscure. One possible hypothesis is that pathogen effectors target MAPKKKs to inhibit their functions, while redundant MAPKKK members can disperse effector‐mediated impairments and maintain disease resistance as much as possible in plants. Similarly, rice plants employ the OsMAPKKK11/16/18/19/24‐OsMKK4/5‐OsMPK3/6 module to regulate chitin‐triggered immune responses (Chen et al., 2021b; Yamada et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024b). Moreover, the GmMAPKKK5‐GmMKK4‐GmMPK3/6 module is activated to regulate resistance to soybean cyst nematode (SCN), a devastating disease that significantly reduces soybean yield (Rui and Wang, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a). These findings reveal that this MAPK cascade is highly conserved across plant species (Figure 1), although additional MAPKKKs or parallel components might also be involved. In addition, plant immunity is mediated by another MAPK cascade comprising MEKK1, MKK1/2/6, and MPK4 in Arabidopsis. While the MEKK1‐MKK1/2/6‐MPK4 cascade was previously demonstrated to act largely as a negative regulator of basal defense against biotrophic pathogens, recent evidence indicates that MPK4 also phosphorylates specific substrates to positively regulate resistance under certain specific conditions or against necrotrophs (Gao et al., 2008; Zhang and Zhang, 2022). For instance, ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR (ERF) is a type of transcription factor belonging to the APETALA2 (AP2) superfamily. MPK4 phosphorylates ERF8 at four positions in Arabidopsis. Mutation of all four positions in ERF8 compromises its ability to mediate cell death, indicating that MPK4 promotes resistance and cell death by directly phosphorylating ERF8 (Cao et al., 2018; Manna et al., 2023). Similarly, OsMPK4 also positively regulates rice resistance to bacterial pathogens by phosphorylating the VALINE‐GLUTAMINE (VQ) proteins OsVQ14 and OsVQ32 in rice (Li et al., 2021a), indicating the diverse roles of MPK4 in plant immunity (Figure 1). Thus, it will be interesting to investigate whether the MEKK1‐MKK1/2/6‐MPK4 cascade is conserved in other plants and how plants balance its function in activating resistance. The related components and mechanisms are helpful for disease‐resistant crop breeding. Additionally, an AhMEKK1‐AhMKK2‐AhMPK3 cascade was identified in response to Ralstonia solanacearum in peanut (Yang et al., 2022), suggesting that there is crosstalk between MAPKKK3/4/5‐MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 and MEKK1‐MKK1/2‐MPK4, the two conserved MAPK cascades.

Conservation of MAPK cascades in plant immune signaling

Mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades are essential signaling hubs for immune signal transduction in plants. Upon pathogen invasion, plant cell surface‐localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) sense the extracellular stimuli delivered from pathogens and activate intracellular receptor‐like kinases (RLCKs) via phosphorylation. The amplification and transduction of RLCK‐mediated immune signals depend primarily on the MAPK cascade, which is generally composed of a MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKK/MEKK), a MAPK kinase (MKK), and a MAPK/MPK. To date, the well‐characterized MAPK cascades involved in plant immunity are MEKK1–MKK1/2/6–MPK4 and MAPKKK3/4/5–MKK4/5–MPK3/6, which are conserved across plant species, such as Arabidopsis, rice, soybean, and peanut. Crosstalk between these two cascades is detected via the phosphorylation of AhMPK3/6 by AhMKK1/2 in peanut. Both MAPK cascades present positive feedback regulation, in which MPK4 and MPK3/6 increase MAPK activation by directly phosphorylating upstream MEKK1 and MAPKKK5, respectively. Moreover, calcium‐activated CALCIUM‐DEPENDENT PROTEIN KINASE 5/13 (OsCPK5/13) can phosphorylate and activate OsMPK3/6 in rice under salt tolerance. To ultimately activate plant immune responses, activated MPKs directly phosphorylate their substrates, such as diverse transcription factors and VALINE‐GLUTAMINE (VQ) proteins, to modulate downstream transcriptional reprogramming, stomatal closure, and phytohormone crosstalk, thereby promoting disease resistance. To transmit immune signals and regulate plant immunity, activated MPKs phosphorylate diverse substrates. These processes are also conserved. For example, WRKY transcription factors are common substrates of MPKs (Figure 1). MPK3/6 positively regulate the biosynthesis of camalexin, a phytoalexin required for disease resistance, by phosphorylating and activating WRKY33 in Arabidopsis (Zhou and Wang, He, et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020a). The MPK3/6‐WRKY33 module also contributes to systemic acquired resistance (SAR) via the regulation of defense‐related gene expression and pipecolic acid (Pip) production (Wang et al., 2018b; Han et al., 2019). Interestingly, activated BnWRKY33 binds to its own promoter in oilseed rape (Brassica napus). The BnaA03.MKK5‐BnaA06.MPK3/BnaC03.MPK3 module phosphorylates BnWRKY33 and enhances its transcriptional activity, which triggers a transcriptional burst of BnWRKY33 and strongly increases disease resistance (Zhang et al., 2022). These results reveal a positive feedback regulatory loop in MAPK‐mediated transcriptional activation. Similarly, OsMPK3/4/6 phosphorylate OsWRKY31 in rice, which increases its DNA‐binding capacity and subsequent rice blast resistance (Wang et al., 2023c). Furthermore, OsMPK6 regulates the biosynthesis of sakuranetin, a phytoalexin that confers rice resistance to Ustilaginoidea virens, by phosphorylating OsWRKY67 (Ma et al., 2025a). AhMPK3 also phosphorylates multiple WRKYs to induce the expression of defense‐related genes in response to R. solanacearum in peanut (Yang et al., 2022). The investigation of how these MPK‐mediated activations converge on similar targets and whether they have additive or redundant effects on immunity is interesting for future work. Notably, pretreatment with flg22 activates MPK3/6 to phosphorylate WRKY18, which upregulates the expression of AP2C1 and PP2C5, two protein phosphatases (PPases) that suppress ETI in Arabidopsis (Wang et al., 2023a), indicating that plants can also maintain their fitness via the MPK3/6–WRKY module. Taken together, these findings indicate that plants have evolved a precise spatiotemporal regulatory mechanism by which MPKs phosphorylate diverse WRKYs at different stages of pathogen infection, thereby modulating plant defense signaling and maintaining the trade‐off between growth and immunity. However, how MPKs dynamically and efficiently mediate the activation of diverse targets during programmatic plant immunity needs to be addressed in further studies. A likely explanation is that the abundance of MPK‐targeted proteins is also dynamically regulated upon pathogen infection; therefore, highly abundant targets at a certain stage are more easily phosphorylated by MPKs, thereby altering downstream immune responses.

In addition to WRKYs, many other transcription factors can be phosphorylated by MPKs. For instance, MPK3/6 phosphorylate CALMODULIN‐BINDING TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATOR 3 (CAMTA3), a negative regulatory transcription factor of plant immunity, to alter its protein stability and promote disease resistance in Arabidopsis (Jiang et al., 2020). OsMPK3 phosphorylates the positive immune regulatory transcription factor OsNAC29, which inhibits its degradation and increases rice blast resistance (Lu et al., 2024). Similarly, GmMPK6 phosphorylates GmERF113 to trigger resistance to Phytophthora sojae in soybean (Gao et al., 2022b). OsMPK3/6 phosphorylate OsMYB4 to control vascular immunity in rice (Lin et al., 2022). These findings indicate that the MAPK cascade contributes to disease resistance by activating transcriptional reprogramming via phosphorylation of diverse transcription factors (Figure 1). However, how these transcription factor‐mediated transcriptomic changes cooperate during plant immunity remains unclear. Furthermore, MPK3/6 can regulate immune gene expression by phosphorylating the kinase CYCLIN‐DEPENDENT KINASE C (CDKC), which subsequently phosphorylates the RNA polymerase II C‐terminal domain (CTD), resulting in transcriptional reprogramming. However, CTD PHOSPHATASE‐LIKE 3 (CPL3) serves as a negative regulator that dephosphorylates CTD to counteract MPK‐mediated transcriptional regulation (Li et al., 2014), which indicates that plants employ a fine‐tuning mechanism to tightly control transcriptional reprogramming during immunity. It will be interesting to investigate whether any transcription factors are involved in this process. In addition, a more recent study revealed that MPK3/6 fine‐tune diurnal stomatal movements by phosphorylating ROP (RHO OF PLANTS) GUANINE NUCLEOTIDE EXCHANGE FACTOR 1 (RopGEF2) (Yuan et al., 2026). MPK3/6 also phosphorylate VILLIN3 (VLN3) to modulate actin remodeling and activate stomatal defense in Arabidopsis (Zou et al., 2021), indicating that MPK3/6 can regulate plant immunity by modulating stomatal movements (Ding et al., 2026). Similarly, the MPK4 protein highly accumulates in stomatal guard cells (Witon et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023), suggesting that MPK4 may play a crucial role in stomatal immunity, but the specific targets involved remain to be defined. Furthermore, MPK4 positively regulates disease resistance by suppressing the negative function of C3H14, a CCCH‐type zinc finger protein, via phosphorylation (Figure 1), which confers disease resistance in Arabidopsis (Wang et al., 2022a). OsMPK3/4/6 phosphorylate ARGONAUTE 1a (AGO1a), which is required for the regulation of miRNA expression during Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) infection, to confer plant resistance (Singh et al., 2024). SlMPK4 interacts with and phosphorylates the zinc finger‐homeodomain transcription factor SlZHD7, leading to its stabilization, which in turn promotes resistance against Phytophthora infestans in tomato (Li et al., 2026a). In barley (Hordeum vulgare), HvMPK3 interacts with and phosphorylates barley yellow striate mosaic virus (BYSMV) nucleoprotein to trigger immunity against BYSMV infection (Ding et al., 2022). Taken together, these findings indicate that MAPK cascades play conserved roles in regulating immune responses by phosphorylating similar substrates in diverse plant species.

Furthermore, constitutive activation of immune signaling often damages plants. The MAPK cascade also contributes to the fine‐tuning of plant immunity. For example, the exocyst subunit EXO70B2 contributes to PTI responses by associating with EXO70B1 and modulating the accumulation of FLAGELLIN SENSING 2 (FLS2), a PRR that specifically perceives flg22, at the plasma membrane (PM) (Gomez‐Gomez et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2020b). To fine‐tune FLS2‐mediated immune responses, activated MPK3/6 phosphorylate EXO70B2, leading to its ubiquitination and degradation via the E3 ligase PLANT U‐BOX‐TYPE UBUBIQUITIN LIGASE 22 (PUB22). Interestingly, PUB22 is also phosphorylated and stabilized by MPK3, resulting in the inhibition of FLS2 trafficking to the PM (Stegmann et al., 2012; Furlan et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020b; Brillada et al., 2021). These results indicate that plants have evolved a precise mechanism by which immunity can be maintained at a certain threshold via the same signaling module. Moreover, to maintain the trade‐off between immunity and growth, PHYTOCHROME‐INTERACTING FACTOR 3 (PIF3), a key transcription factor controlling photomorphogenesis, is employed to negatively regulate plant immunity. MPK3/6 phosphorylate PIF3 to enhance its role in inhibiting defense gene expression in plants; ultimately, immunity is fine‐tuned in Arabidopsis (Zhao et al., 2023). In rice, the RLK protein BLAST DISEASE RESISTANCE 1 (OsBDR1) negatively regulates rice blast resistance. Its negative role is executed by phosphorylating OsMPK3, whose activation affects jasmonic acid (JA) signaling (Wang et al., 2023b). However, whether this inhibitory process requires the involvement of transcription factors, such as WRKYs, remains unclear. Moreover, OsMPK6 acts as a negative regulator in response to U. virens in rice (Zheng et al., 2022). These findings reveal that MPK3/6 have dual functions in plant immunity, which contribute to the plant growth–immunity trade‐off. Similarly, MPK4 phosphorylates DIACYLGLYCEROL KINASE 5 (DGK5) and inhibits its activity (Figure 1), hence reducing phosphatidic acid (PA) and ROS production and negatively regulating immunity in Arabidopsis (Kong et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest that the function of MPKs in regulating plant immunity is highly conserved in different plants. However, the spatiotemporal regulatory mechanism by which MPKs regulate diverse substrates during the same attack remains to be addressed. Interestingly, it was recently reported that extracellular stimuli cause broad activation of several MPKs, which leads to a wide range of outputs through combinatorial activation of common and specific components. However, in response to certain signals, only particular MKK–MPK modules are highly activated (Ma et al., 2024b). Nevertheless, it is necessary to investigate whether MPKs bind to various substrates because of their flexible binding surfaces. MPKs might exhibit specific conformational changes under certain conditions. Furthermore, further studies are needed to understand how plants balance the positive and negative roles of MAPK cascades in modulating immunity, which is helpful for the development of elite disease‐resistant crop cultivars.

THE FUNCTION OF RAF‐LIKE MAPKKK PROTEINS IN PLANT IMMUNITY

The abovementioned MAPK cascades are activated by MEKK‐type MAPKKK proteins. Another large MAPKKK family, named the Raf‐like MAPKKK family, is present in plants (Wang et al., 2026a). The so‐called “Raf‐like” kinases in plants are members of the tyrosine kinase‐like (TKL) group on the basis of phylogenetic research on the genomes of several plant species. Additionally, they are not related to the Raf kinases found in metazoans (Lehti‐Shiu and Shiu, 2012). For example, Arabidopsis contains 69 MAPKKKs, among which 21 are MEKK‐type MAPKKKs and 48 are Raf‐like MAPKKKs. Similarly, rice has 75 MAPKKKs, including 43 Raf‐like MAPKKKs (Zhang and Zhang, 2022). In animals, Raf‐like MAPKKKs have been shown to mediate signaling by forming cascades with MEKK‐type MKKs and MPKs. For instance, the MAPK cascade Raf‐MEK1/2‐ERK1/2 is extensively distributed throughout diverse organs and plays a role in regulating cell division and proliferation (Yang et al., 2019). Raf‐like MAPKKKs also contribute to plant immunity. However, only a few members are reported to transmit immune signals by phosphorylating MKKs (Figure 2). In Arabidopsis, the Raf‐like MAPKKK protein MAPKKKδ‐1 (MKD1) interacts with and phosphorylates MKK1/5 to provide resistance against Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pto) DC3000 (Asano et al., 2020). The Raf‐like MAPKKK protein INTEGRIN‐LINKED KINASE 5 (ILK5) activates MPK3/6 and initiates MAPK signaling, which is linked to an enhanced immune response (Kim et al., 2023), indicating that Raf‐like MAPKKKs can function as MEKK‐type ones that directly activate MAPK cascades by phosphorylating MKKs or MPKs. Further evidence is needed to determine whether this regulatory mechanism is conserved across plant species. Interestingly, OsMKK4 is suppressed upon being phosphorylated by OsMAPKKK43, a Raf‐like MAPKKK also known as INCREASED LEAF ANGLE 1 (OsILA1), in rice (Chen et al., 2021a). Furthermore, the transcription factor GhWRKY40a is a direct substrate of GhMPK9 involved in activating the expression of Verticillium wilt‐induced defense‐related genes and initiating sufficient resistance to Verticillium wilt disease in cotton. However, GhMPK9 alone cannot phosphorylate GhWRKY40a. Further studies revealed that Raf‐like MAPKKK GhRAF39_1 can be phosphorylated by GhMPK9 and serves as a scaffold to modulate the phosphorylation of GhWRKY40a by GhMPK9, but GhRAF39_1 does not phosphorylate GhWRKY40a (Figure 2), indicating that GhRAF39_1 serves as an intermediate that connects MPK and its substrates in plant immunity (Mi et al., 2024). These results also suggest that Raf‐like MAPKKKs may play expanded roles in regulating MAPK signaling.

Raf‐like MAPKKKs play both positive and negative roles in plant immunity

Raf‐like MAPK kinase kinases (Raf‐like MAPKKKs) are important components of mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that Raf‐like MAPKKKs are crucial for plant immune regulation. For example, MAPKKKδ‐1 (MKD1) and INTEGRIN‐LINKED KINASE 5 (ILK5) positively regulate MAPK activation via the phosphorylation of MAPK kinases (MKKs) in Arabidopsis, whereas rice INCREASED LEAF ANGLE 1 (OsILA1) and tomato SlMAPKKK18 phosphorylate MKKs and negatively regulate MAPK activation, indicating that Raf‐like MAPKKKs can phosphorylate MKKs, but the regulatory effects on plant immunity are diverse. In addition, most investigated Raf‐like MAPKKKs have been shown to function as scaffolds. For instance, ENHANCED DISEASE RESISTANCE 1 (EDR1) negatively regulates the activation of the MAPKKK3/4/5–MKK4/5–MPK3/6 cascade by reducing the protein levels of MKK4/5 via the E3 ligase KEEP ON GOING (KEG) and inhibiting the MPK3‐mediated feedback phosphorylation of MAPKKK5 in Arabidopsis. Similarly, OsEDR1 negatively regulates the kinase activity of OsMKK10.2, and this inhibition is relieved after OsEDR1 is phosphorylated and degraded via OsMPK6 upon pathogen infection in rice. Moreover, EDR1 suppresses the interaction between ENHANCED DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY 1 (EDS1) and PHYTOALEXIN DEFICIENT 4 (PAD4). EDR1 also phosphorylates the transcription factor MYC2 and inhibits its ability to bind to target promoter sequences, thereby inhibiting pathogen‐induced defense‐related gene expression and subsequent disease resistance in Arabidopsis. Furthermore, Raf‐like MAPKKKs act as positive scaffolds. In rice, OsMAPKKK72 enhances rice blast resistance by increasing the interaction between OsMKK9 and OsMPK3/6. Nevertheless, most Raf‐like MAPKKKs cannot directly phosphorylate the MKK–MPK module during plant immunity. For example, ENHANCED DISEASE RESISTANCE 1 (EDR1) is a well‐studied Raf‐like MAPKKK that negatively regulates plant immunity in Arabidopsis. There is insufficient evidence that EDR1 can activate any MKK, although it has kinase activity (Tang and Innes, 2002; Lin et al., 2020). However, EDR1 and its homologs RAF3/4/5 inhibit MPK3/6 activation and protein accumulation (Zhao et al., 2014; Zhong et al., 2023). Further studies revealed that EDR1 interacts with MKK4/5 and decreases their protein levels via stabilization of the E3 ligase KEEP ON GOING (KEG) (Gao et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2022a), which is consistent with a previous report that all resistance characteristics of edr1 mutants can be suppressed by the keg‐4 recessive missense mutation (Wawrzynska et al., 2008). Furthermore, the activation of the MAPKKK5‐MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 cascade can be maintained via the MPK3‐mediated positive feedback phosphorylation of MAPKKK5 in Arabidopsis, which stabilizes the protein levels of MAPKKK5, where EDR1 also plays a negative role (Wang et al., 2023d). These findings indicate that EDR1 suppresses disease resistance by acting as a scaffold but not directly via its kinase activity (Figure 2). Similarly, OsEDR1 interacts with but does not phosphorylate OsMPKK10.2 in rice, which inhibits its kinase activity, thereby reducing downstream signaling and rice disease resistance (Ma et al., 2021). These findings further indicate that OsEDR1 is associated with the canonical MAPK cascade but functions as a negative scaffold (Figure 2). To release this OsEDR1‐mediated suppression of MAPK activation, activated OsMPK6 phosphorylates and destabilizes OsEDR1, thereby increasing OsMPKK10.2 activity and promoting rice resistance (Ma et al., 2021). These findings also suggest that plants have evolved a precise regulatory mechanism that relieves Raf‐like kinase EDR1‐mediated immune suppression and promotes sufficient resistance upon pathogen attack. Loss of function of EDR1 in wheat also leads to increased disease resistance, similar to rice and Arabidopsis mutants (Zhang et al., 2017a), indicating that EDR1 is functionally conserved across plant species, which makes it a potential editing gene that can be used to improve crop disease resistance.

However, which direct substrates are employed by EDR1 to negatively regulate plant immunity is largely unknown. A recent study performed an immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP–MS) analysis and revealed that EDR1 interacts with and phosphorylates the transcription factor MYC2 to negatively regulate powdery mildew resistance in Arabidopsis (Zhong et al., 2026a). MYC2 contributes to powdery mildew resistance by directly binding to the promoter of RESPONSIVE TO DEHYDRATION 22 (RD22) and promoting its expression. However, EDR1 phosphorylates MYC2 at two positions, Thr‐353 and Thr‐357, and inhibits its ability to bind to the RD22 promoter, thereby reducing powdery mildew resistance in Arabidopsis (Figure 2). Similarly, the transcription factor WRKY8 negatively regulates RLP23 expression. To activate plant immunity, CPK4/11 directly phosphorylates WRKY8 at Thr‐210 and inhibits its ability to bind to the RLP23 promoter (Ren et al., 2024), indicating that plants use a similar regulatory mechanism to modulate immunity. To ensure sufficient disease resistance, EDR1‐mediated inhibition of MYC2 is released via dephosphorylation of MYC2 by the phosphatase PP2A Bα during fungal infection (Zhong et al., 2026a). These findings indicate that EDR1 plays a critical role in suppressing unnecessary immune responses via direct phosphorylation of MYC2 in resting cells and that plants have evolved the EDR1–PP2A phospho‐regulatory module to dynamically maintain an appropriate level of resistance. Furthermore, MPK6 phosphorylates MYC2 at Thr‐328, which reduces its stability and transcriptional activity within the JA signaling pathway (Im et al., 2025). Given that the mpk6 mutant is unable to suppress enhanced disease resistance in edr1 mutants (Zhao et al., 2014), whether this MPK6‐mediated regulation of MYC2 contributes to powdery mildew resistance remains unknown. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that plants can modulate the function of MYC2 via diverse MAPK cascade members. However, mutation of myc2 only partially suppresses powdery mildew resistance in edr1 mutants (Zhong et al., 2026a). Further studies are needed to determine other direct upstream and downstream components associated with EDR1 in plant immunity and investigate the role of EDR1 and its homologs in the crosstalk between biotic and abiotic stress signaling networks. In addition to EDR1, a few other Raf‐like MAPKKKs have been reported to be involved in plant immunity. For instance, SERINE/THREONINE/TYROSINE KINASE 8 (STY8) and STY17/46 negatively regulate disease resistance to Botrytis cinerea by reducing the accumulation of MKK7 in Arabidopsis (Chen et al., 2024), which is similar to the suppression of MKK4/5 protein levels by EDR1. However, which E3 ligases participate in the ubiquitination and degradation of MKK7 remains to be identified. Similarly, FERONIA (FER) is a well‐characterized PRR in plant immunity. The tomato FER homolog Solanum lycopersicum FERONIA‐Like (SlFERL) phosphorylates Raf‐like SlMAPKKK18 to inhibit MAPK activation by modulating the protein levels and/or kinase activities of SlMKK2/4 (Ji et al., 2023). These findings suggest that most Raf‐like MAPKKKs negatively regulate plant immunity (Figure 2). However, OsMAPKKK72, a Raf‐like MAPKKK in rice, increases the formation of the OsMKK9–OsMPK3/6 module and subsequently enhances MAPK activation (Figure 2), thereby positively regulating rice blast resistance by acting as a scaffold protein (Wang et al., 2026b). These findings reveal the functional conservation and diversity of Raf‐like MAPKKKs in plant immunity. Genetic investigations combined with bioinformatics analyses should be performed to determine the conserved function of Raf‐like MAPKKKs in plant immunity. The demonstration of the underlying molecular mechanisms may lead to a better understanding of the MAPK signaling pathway and improve plant resistance.

THE FINE‐TUNING OF MAPK ACTIVATION IN PLANT IMMUNITY

Activation mechanisms and positive feedback loops of MAPK activation in plant immunity

As a critical signaling hub, the activation, expression, abundance, and interaction of members of the MAPK cascade are tightly controlled in plants. First, the canonical MAPK cascade is usually activated by upstream RLCKs, which receive immune signals from the PRR complex (Zhang and Zhang, 2022). For example, the RLCK proteins AVRPPHB SUSCEPTIBLE1‐LIKE 19 (PBL19), BSK1, and BIK are phosphorylated by the FLS2–BAK1 complex after the recognition of flg22 and subsequently interact with and phosphorylate the MEKK‐type MAPKKK3/4/5 to activate the MAPK cascade in Arabidopsis (Yamada et al., 2016; Bi et al., 2018; Rao et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2018). In rice, OsRLCK176 and OsRLCK185, two Arabidopsis BIK1 orthologs, associate with CHITIN ELICITOR RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (OsCERK1) and mediate immune responses triggered by chitin and peptidoglycan via phosphorylation of OsMAPKKK18/24 (Wang et al., 2017; Yamada et al., 2017). OsBSK1‐2, another RLCK that positively regulates rice blast resistance (Wang et al., 2024), may also control plant immunity via the OsMAPKKK16/18/19‐OsMKK4/5‐OsMPK3/6 cascade, as OsBSK1‐2 interacts with OsMAPKKK16/18/19 (Li et al., 2024b). However, due to the low kinase activity of OsBKS1‐2, whether it can directly phosphorylate and activate OsMAPKKK16/18/19 remains to be tested. Furthermore, a recent study on soybeans revealed that the PRR GmLecRK08g phosphorylates the RLCK protein CDG1‐LIKE1 (GmCDL1), which subsequently triggers MAPK activation by phosphorylating GmMAPKKK5, leading to the protection of soybean against SCN infection (Zhang et al., 2024a). Taken together, these findings indicate that the phosphorylation and activation of MAPKKKs by RLCKs are important and conserved across plant species (Figure 1). However, whether there is functional redundancy or superposition of different RLCKs in regulating MAPK activation remains obscure. Interestingly, the ROS‐triggered cytoplasmic kinase OXIDATIVE SIGNAL‐INDUCIBLE1 (OXI1) directly activates the MAPK cascade independent of any RLCKs downstream of the LYSINE MOTIF RECEPTOR KINASE 5 (LYK5)–CERK1 complex in Arabidopsis (Ma et al., 2024a). After the perception of chitin, the accumulation of H2O2 leads to the oxidation and activation of OXI1, which subsequently phosphorylates MAPKKK5 to regulate MAPK activation and disease resistance (Ma et al., 2024a), indicating that additional kinases can be employed to regulate the activation of MAPK cascades (Figure 3), by which plants can maintain sufficient MAPK activation under uncertain conditions, such as the loss or suppression of the function of upstream RLCKs. Therefore, further studies are needed to identify these potential kinases and investigate the synergistic mechanisms by which plants activate MAPK activation via diverse kinases involved in immunity. In addition, whether H2O2 accumulation directly activates any MAPK cascade members via oxidation remains to be addressed.

Crosstalk between MAPK activation and other plant immune events

Crosstalk between different immune responses is essential for plant defense activation. For example, mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs/MPKs) and calcium‐dependent protein kinases (CDPKs/CPKs) display mutual phosphorylation. To negatively regulate rice blast resistance, OsCPK18 activates OsMPK5 by phosphorylating its Thr‐14 and Thr‐32 residues. In turn, OsMPK5 phosphorylates OsCPK18 and OsCPK4 to synergistically regulate rice blast resistance. In Arabidopsis, several convergent substrates, such as WRKY33 and CALMODULIN‐BINDING TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATOR 3 (CAMTA3), have been reported to be phosphorylated by both MPK3/6 and CPK5 to activate transcriptional reprogramming. In addition, MPK3/6 colocalize with WRKYs and CAMTA3 in the nucleus, indicating that MPK3/6‐mediated phosphorylation may occur within the nucleus. Moreover, MAPK activation contributes to callose deposition, which can be perceived by the CHITIN ELICITOR RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (CERK1) complex at the cell surface and further increases MAPK activation. Furthermore, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst is a common early immune event that is mediated mainly by respiratory burst oxidase homolog (RBOH) members. In both Arabidopsis and rice, the expression of RBOHs is regulated by WRKY transcription factors, whose activities are modulated by MPKs via direct phosphorylation. For instance, the OsMPK1–OsWRKY90 module regulates ROS bursts by mediating the expression of OsRBOHB in rice. MPK3/6 facilitates ROS bursts by promoting RBOHD expression, where WRKY33 is phosphorylated and activated by MPK3/6 within the nucleus, in Arabidopsis. In effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), two MPKs, WOUND‐INDUCED PROTEIN KINASE (NbWIPK) and SALICYLIC ACID‐INDUCED PROTEIN KINASE (NbSIPK), were also revealed to increase ROS accumulation by negatively regulating the expression of ROS scavenging genes via phosphorylation of the transcription repressor Alfin‐like 7 (AL7) in Nicotiana benthamiana. Furthermore, the ROS‐activated protein kinase OXIDATIVE SIGNAL‐INDUCIBLE1 (OXI1) leads to MAPK activation independent of receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs) and potentiates plant immunity. In addition, MAPK activation positively regulates salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ET) signaling by increasing their biosynthesis or amplifying their downstream transcriptional reprogramming. In rice, OsMPK3 was also shown to negatively modulate JA signaling, revealing the complicated functional link between MAPK activation and phytohormone signaling pathways. In addition, because constitutive activation of immunity is harmful to plants, the phosphorylation of MAPKKKs is precisely controlled in plants. For instance, RLCKs usually activate MAPKKKs by phosphorylating their C‐termini. However, the intramolecular interaction between the N‐ and C‐termini of MAPKKK5 prevents RLCKs from accessing its C‐terminus in the resting state in Arabidopsis, which keeps the MAPK cascade in an inactive state and maintains plant growth. Upon pathogen attack, two 14‐3‐3 proteins, G‐BOX REGULATING FACTOR 6 (GRF6) and GRF8, interact with the C‐terminus of MAPKKK5, which relieves its self‐inhibition and facilitates its activation mediated by RLCKs (Dong et al., 2023), indicating that the 14‐3‐3 protein acts as a scaffold or activator in regulating the RLCK–MAPKKK5 module. Consistently, 14‐3‐3 proteins from N. benthamiana and tomato exhibit similar functions that stabilize protein levels or facilitate the activation of MAPK cascade members (Gao et al., 2022e; Sheikh et al., 2023). These findings indicate that the scaffold role of 14‐3‐3 proteins in activating MAPKKKs is conserved. Further studies are needed to analyze whether the activation of all MAPKKKs, including Raf‐like proteins, requires 14‐3‐3 proteins and whether the activated MAPK cascade can in turn phosphorylate 14‐3‐3s to modulate their functions, as the function of 14‐3‐3 depends on its posttranslational modifications (PTMs). For instance, phosphorylation of the 14‐3‐3 protein OsGF14b contributes to the inhibitory role of BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (OsBZR1) in brassinosteroid signaling in rice (Gao et al., 2022d). Therefore, investigating which PTMs of 14‐3‐3 proteins mainly promote their interactions with MAPKKKs and activate the MAPK cascade during plant immune signaling will be interesting. Furthermore, noncanonical EXTRA‐LARGE G PROTEINs (XLGs) are subunits of plant heterotrimeric G proteins. XLG2/3 associate with FLS2, BIK1, and RESPIRATORY BURST OXIDASE HOMOLOG D (RBOHD) to regulate PAMP‐induced ROS bursts in Arabidopsis. Recently, these XLGs were also revealed to maintain MAPK activation via interactions with all MAPKKK3/5, MKK4/5, and MPK3/6 (Wang et al., 2023e), which is consistent with the findings of an early study that RECEPTOR FOR ACTIVATED C KINASE 1 (RACK1), a scaffold protein, binds to both the MAPK cascade and G proteins to regulate MAPK activation (Cheng et al., 2015). However, the underlying mechanism by which G proteins contribute to MAPK activation remains to be elucidated. As G proteins associate with FLS2 to stabilize BIK1, and activated BIK1, in turn, phosphorylates XLGs in Arabidopsis (Liang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018a), it will be interesting to investigate whether G proteins play a role in increasing RLCK‐mediated phosphorylation of MAPKKKs and whether this regulation is conserved across plant species. Moreover, whether activated MPK3/6 can phosphorylate G proteins to suppress or maintain their self‐activation remains obscure.

Additionally, activation of MAPK cascades can be maintained via positive feedback regulation. Activated MPK3/6 phosphorylate MAPKKK5, which stabilizes its protein levels and further increases MPK3/6 activation in Arabidopsis (Bi et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023d). Similarly, activated MPK4 can in turn phosphorylate upstream MEKK1 at Ser‐603, the same position that is phosphorylated by RLCKs, which results in increased MPK4 activation (Bi et al., 2018). These findings indicate that the MAPK cascade is self‐controlled when plants face strong pathogen attack (Figure 1). Another positive feedback mechanism occurs via the activation of GmMPK3/6, which is further increased by GmMPK3/6‐mediated phosphorylation of GmCDL1, an upstream RLCK, in soybean (Zhang et al., 2024a). Similarly, OsMAPKKK5 regulates rice plant architecture and yield by phosphorylating upstream OsBSK1‐1 (Yan et al., 2025a), indicating that the MAPK cascade also uses this feedback regulation to balance plant immunity and growth. However, how plants precisely modulate this feedback regulation and maintain the homeostasis of MAPK activation remains largely unclear. Further studies are needed to determine whether MPK3/4/6 can phosphorylate all their upstream components via feedback and whether the MPK3/4/6‐mediated feedback regulation of MAPK activation is conserved across plant species. Furthermore, transcriptional regulation contributes to the abundance of MAPK cascade members. For example, the expression of MPK3 and MPK6 is mediated by the transcription factor MYB44 after treatment with PAMPs (Wang et al., 2023f). Interestingly, MYB44 is a substrate of MPK3/6, and its phosphorylation by MPK3/6 increases its transcriptional activity, leading to MPK3/6 expression and further activating downstream defense responses (Wang et al., 2023f). These findings indicate that plants have evolved sophisticated feedback regulatory mechanisms to maintain the activation of MAPK cascades, the better characterization of which is helpful for fully understanding the plant immune system and engineering elite crops.

Negative regulation and signaling homeostasis of MAPK cascades in plant immunity

Excessive MAPK activation is detrimental to plants. To restrict this autoimmunity, plants have evolved negative regulators in the absence of pathogens. For instance, to control the homeostasis of MKK4/5 protein levels, the Raf‐like MAPKKK protein EDR1 is employed to interact with MKK4/5 and decrease their protein levels via the E3 ligase KEG (Zhao et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2021). KEG self‐ubiquitinates following self‐phosphorylation, and EDR1 inhibits this phosphorylation through some unknown intermediate components (Gao et al., 2021), which leads to the stabilization of KEG and subsequent ubiquitination of MKK4/5 (Figure 2). The protein levels of MPK3/6 are also negatively regulated by EDR1 (Zhao et al., 2014). However, the accumulation of MPK3/6 is independent of the 26S proteasome, as treatment with MG132, an inhibitor of the 26S proteasome, does not affect MPK3/6 protein levels (Gao et al., 2021), indicating that EDR1 regulates the protein abundance of MAPK cascade members in different manners, which needs to be investigated in further studies. Many mutations in autophagy also result in autoimmunity and increased MAPK activation. Therefore, it will be interesting to investigate whether MAPK cascade members, such as MPK3/6, can be degraded in the vacuole via the autophagic pathway.

Furthermore, phosphorylation is a reversible PTM mediated by kinases and phosphatases (Su et al., 2022). Increasing evidence illuminates that the activation of MAPK cascades can be blocked by phosphatases. For example, type 2 C protein phosphatase (PP2C) is a common protein phosphatase family that regulates several plant signaling pathways. Tomato PP2C immunity‐associated candidate 14 (SlPic14) physically interacts with and dephosphorylates SlMKK2, which leads to reduced activation of the MAPK cascade and inhibition of cell death induced by the NLR protein Prf in tomato (Chakraborty et al., 2024). Similarly, SlPic6 was recently identified to act as a negative regulator of the MAPK cascade by dephosphorylating SlMKK1/2 (Chakraborty et al., 2025), indicating that PP2Cs are essential for the suppression of the activated MAPK cascade, thereby avoiding excessive immune responses and maintaining the growth–immunity trade‐off in plants. Furthermore, MAPK PHOSPHATASE 1 (MKP1) regulates the phosphorylation of MYB4 mediated by MPK3/6, which inhibits lignin production and hence negatively regulates vascular lignification (Lin et al., 2022). In addition, the PM‐bound form of DUAL‐SPECIFICITY PHOSPHATASE 4 (DSP4) can interact with and dephosphorylate MPK3/6 to negatively regulate plant immunity (Luan et al., 2025). Notably, by perturbing the GmMKK1/2–GmMPK4 cascade, GmMEKK2 potentiates immune signaling and confers enhanced resistance to soybean mosaic virus (Zhong et al., 2025). To inhibit the MAPKKK3/4/5–MKK4/5–MPK3/6 cascade, the Raf‐like MAPKKK protein EDR1 serves as a scaffold to suppress the MPK3‐mediated positive feedback phosphorylation of MAPKKK5 and the self‐phosphorylation of KEG via unknown elements (Gao et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023d). Thus, whether any phosphatases are mediated by GmMEKK2 and EDR1 and involved in these regulatory processes should be analyzed in the future. Consistently, TaMKP1 interacts with and dephosphorylates TaMPK3/4/6 in wheat (Liu et al., 2024c). Knockout of TaMKP1 in wheat results in enhanced disease resistance and a high yield, indicating that it is possible to develop crops with high yields and disease resistance by modifying MAPK signaling.

Additionally, a recent study revealed that nitric oxide inhibits MPK6 activity via S‐nitrosylation, thereby positively regulating stomatal growth (Wang et al., 2025a), indicating that MAPK cascade members undergo multiple PTMs in plants, which ultimately maintain MAPK activation at a certain threshold and balance plant growth and immunity. Interestingly, a more recent study showed that S‐nitrosylation of PROTEIN TYROSINE PHOSPHATASE 1 (PTP1) reduces its dephosphorylation activity, which in turn activates MPK6 in response to salt or cold stress (Li et al., 2026b), indicating that the same PTM can operate through distinct regulatory mechanisms in different environmental conditions. Furthermore, S‐palmitoylation of the MPK UvSlt2 by the palmitoyltransferase UvPfa4 enhances the virulence of the rice false smut fungus U. virens. This modification boosts the kinase activity of UvSlt2 and expands its hydrophobic solvent‐accessible surface area, which strengthens the interaction between UvSlt2 and its substrate UvRlm1 (Duan et al., 2024). However, it is still unknown whether any plant MAPK cascade members can undergo S‐palmitoylation and thereby alter plant immunity. Therefore, a multiomics analysis is needed to investigate how plants precisely modulate the activation of MAPK cascades through different PTMs and how these PTMs collaborate to modify the same substrates. The underlying molecular mechanisms and involved components are helpful for disease‐resistant crop breeding.

CROSSTALK BETWEEN MAPK ACTIVATION AND OTHER IMMUNE SIGNALING PATHWAYS

When plants are infected with pathogens, a series of immune events is activated to prevent the spread of pathogens, including MAPK activation, Ca2+ influx, ROS bursts, callose deposition, and phytohormone biosynthesis (Ngou et al., 2022). Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the crosstalk between MAPK activation and other immune events results in the formation of the complete immune signaling network (Ngou et al., 2021). Therefore, we have also summarized these crosstalk signals here (Figure 3; Table 1).

Summary of the crosstalk between MAPK activation and other immune events

Like MAPK activation, pathogen infection leads to fast Ca2+ influx (Ngou et al., 2022), which can be sensed and decoded by cytoplasmic calcium‐dependent protein kinases (CDPKs/CPKs). Several studies have suggested that a subset of defense components is synergistically regulated by CPK5/6 and MPK3/6. For example, CPK5/6 regulate camalexin biosynthesis by interacting with and phosphorylating WRKY33 at the Thr‐229 residue, which increases its DNA‐binding ability (Zhou, Wang, He, et al., 2020). Moreover, MPK3/6 phosphorylate the N‐terminal Serine residues in WRKY33 to enhance its transactivation activity, resulting in increased camalexin biosynthesis (Zhou, Wang, He, et al., 2020), which indicates that WRKY33 is a convergent substrate of CPK5/6 and MPK3/6 and that MAPK signaling cooperates with Ca2+ signaling to regulate downstream immune responses. This conclusion is further confirmed by the results that the camalexin content is lower in cpk5/6 mpk3/6 quadruple mutants than in cpk5/6 or mpk3/6 double mutants after infection with Botrytis cinerea (Yang et al., 2020a). Similarly, the negative transcription factor CAMTA3 is phosphorylated by both CPK5 and MPK3/6, which results in the degradation of CAMTA3 and the upregulation of CAMTA3‐suppressed defense‐related genes (Jiang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024b). Furthermore, MPK3/6 can phosphorylate the transcription factors WRKY8/28/48, which have been found to be substrates of CPK5/6 (Gao et al., 2013), indicating that the crosstalk of pathogen‐induced transcriptional reprogramming is mediated by CPK5/6 and MPK3/6 via different transcription factors. Notably, recent studies revealed that both the MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 cascade and CPK21/23 confer plant aluminum resistance via regulation of the C2H2‐type zinc finger transcription factor STOP1 (Zhou et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2025). Moreover, MPK3/6 phosphorylate STOP1 to modulate hypoxia responses (Wang et al., 2025b). Similarly, CPK4 phosphorylates ROP (RHO OF PLANTS) GUANINE NUCLEOTIDE EXCHANGE FACTOR 1 (RopGEF1) and leads to its degradation, which modulates ABA signaling and plant development (Li et al., 2018). MPK3/6 also fine‐tune diurnal stomatal movements via phosphorylation of RopGEF2 (Yuan et al., 2026), indicating that plants may respond to various environmental conditions by regulating the crosstalk of the MPK and CPK signaling pathways (Table 1). In addition, as kinases, MPKs and CPKs may enhance each other's kinase activities and functions via interaction and transphosphorylation. Accordingly, OsCPK18 functions upstream of OsMPK5 in negatively regulating rice blast resistance, and OsCPK18 can activate OsMPK5 by phosphorylating its Thr‐14 and Thr‐32 residues, which are extensively conserved in MPKs from land plants (Xie et al., 2014). However, OsCPK18‐mediated OsMPK5 phosphorylation does not occur on its TD/EY motif, the conserved residues that are activated by upstream MKKs (Group, 2002; Xie et al., 2014), indicating that plants can directly link Ca2+ signaling to MAPK activation in an MKK‐independent manner. Furthermore, OsMPK5 can phosphorylate OsCPK18 and OsCPK4 at Thr‐505 and Ser‐512, respectively, to synergistically regulate disease resistance (Li et al., 2022). Nevertheless, OsMPK5 and OsCPK18 regulate development‐related genes differently (Li et al., 2022), indicating that they exhibit functional differentiation. However, how plants balance the roles of OsMPK5 and OsCPK18 in different physiological processes remains unclear. Notably, rice yields and immunity are simultaneously increased by CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated editing of Thr‐505 of OsCPK18 and Ser‐512 of OsCPK4, which prevents OsMPK5‐mediated phosphorylation but maintains Ca2+‐dependent activation of kinase activity (Li et al., 2022), indicating that precise manipulation of the phosphorylation between the MPK–CPK module could simultaneously balance plant growth and immunity and increase yields. Remarkably, a recent study revealed that OsCPK5 and OsCPK13 confer rice blast resistance via dimerization and mutual phosphorylation (Wang et al., 2025d). Furthermore, OsCPK5/13 phosphorylate OsMPK3/6 at several residues (Figure 1), including the TD/EY motif, to activate rice salt tolerance (Su et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest that maintaining or increasing the activation of MPKs by activated CPKs may be a conserved mechanism. Investigating how plants balance the phosphorylation of the same TD/EY motif in MPKs by different kinases in further studies will be interesting. Additionally, Raf‐like kinases of the C7 subfamily can be phosphorylated by CPK28, which causes their relocalization to endomembranes in Arabidopsis (Goncalves Dias et al., 2024). This relocalization of Raf‐like kinases may affect their role in plant immunity, which needs to be tested in further studies. Moreover, whether Raf‐like kinases can phosphorylate CPKs and hence regulate their functions remains unknown. As most Raf‐like kinases play negative roles in plant immunity, it will be interesting to determine whether plants employ these Raf‐like kinases to maintain the activation of CPKs and which directional transphosphorylation primarily occurs. In addition to CPKs, Ca2+/CaM‐dependent protein kinases (CCaMKs), another Ca2+ decoder family member, have been revealed to engage in crosstalk with MAPK signaling. For example, OsCCaMK interacts with and transphosphorylates OsMKK1/6, thereby regulating rice salt tolerance via the modulation of auxin signaling (Chen et al., 2021c; Yang et al., 2021). Furthermore, OsDMI3, another OsCCaMK, directly phosphorylates OsMKK1 to activate the MAPK cascade and regulate ABA responses (Chen et al., 2021c). Although whether the OsCCaMK/OsDMI3–OsMKK1/6 module plays a role in disease resistance remains unknown, these findings indicate that the interaction between MAPK activation and Ca2+ signaling is conserved in plants. The molecular mechanisms by which plants precisely coordinate these two signaling pathways during immunity need to be further investigated in the future.

In addition to MAPK activation and Ca2+ influx, ROS bursts are early immune responses induced by pathogen infection. The RBOH‐mediated ROS burst was previously demonstrated to be activated by RCLKs, which can directly phosphorylate the N‐terminus of RBOHs (Li et al., 2021b; Singh et al., 2022). Activated CPKs can also phosphorylate RBOH members and trigger ROS bursts, which play important roles in both PTI and ETI (Weralupitiya et al., 2024). Moreover, accumulating studies have shown that MAPK signaling is involved in regulating RBOH‐mediated ROS bursts (Table 1). For example, NbSIPK is an MPK that functions downstream of NbMEK2 and contributes to ROS bursts by regulating the expression of NbRBOHB (Asai et al., 2008). Further studies found that activated NbSIPK and other MPKs, such as NbWIPK and NbNTF6, can directly phosphorylate several WRKYs, including NbWRKY7/8/9/11/12/14, which bind to the W‐box motif in the NbRBOHB promoter and facilitate its expression (Adachi et al., 2015), indicating that plants have evolved the MPK–WRKY module to regulate ROS bursts by enriching the accumulation of RBOH members (Figure 3). Consistently, the OsMEK2–OsMPK1–OsWRKY90 module regulates ROS bursts and subsequent ferroptosis by mediating the expression of OsRBOHB in the rice–Magnaporthe oryzae interaction (Dangol et al., 2021; Jwa and Hwang, 2025). MPK3/6‐activated WRKY33 also binds to the promoter of RBOHD and initiates its expression, thereby facilitating ROS bursts during plant immunity in Arabidopsis (Zhao et al., 2020). Therefore, mutations in MPK6 lead to reduced pathogen‐induced ROS bursts (Chuang et al., 2022). Moreover, a recent study revealed that ZmMAPKKK45 increases ROS generation to improve disease resistance by upregulating ZmRBOH genes in maize (Zea mays) (Zhong et al., 2026b). Further studies are needed to investigate whether these MPKs directly phosphorylate RBOHs upon pathogen infection and whether CPK‐mediated RBOH phosphorylation requires the participation of MPKs. Furthermore, a more recent study revealed that phosphorylation of the catalase ZmCAT2 by ZmMKK1 modulates H2O2 homeostasis, thereby conferring enhanced thermotolerance in maize. Future investigations are necessary to investigate whether similar mechanisms are operative in the regulation of plant immunity (Chen et al., 2026). In addition, NbSIPK and NbWIPK interact with and phosphorylate Alfin‐like 7 (AL7), a transcription repressor that binds to the promoters of ROS scavenging genes, leading to the negative regulation of ROS scavenging and increased immune responses (Zhang et al., 2023), indicating that the MAPK cascade regulates ROS bursts in multiple ways. In turn, exogenous supplementation with H2O2 can activate MPKs, including NbSIPK and NbWIPK in N. benthamiana as well as MPK3/4/6 in Arabidopsis (Rentel et al., 2004). However, the underlying mechanisms behind this phenomenon have long been unclear. Recently, the characterization of OXI1, a cytoplasmic kinase that has been shown to contribute to MPK3/6 activation (Rentel et al., 2004), perfectly fills this gap (Figure 3). OXI1 is activated by ROS via oxidation and directly phosphorylates MAPKKK5 in an RLCK‐independent manner in Arabidopsis (Ma et al., 2024a). Furthermore, RBOHD is phosphorylated by OXI1 (Ma et al., 2024a), suggesting that there may be a feedback regulatory mechanism by which plants activate and strengthen long‐term ROS bursts and MAPK activation. Interestingly, the correct expression of OsMAPKKK18 during M. oryzae invasion is also regulated by H2O2‐triggered phosphorylation of the RNA‐binding protein (Lu et al., 2025), indicating close crosstalk between the ROS burst and MAPK activation. Additionally, the RLK protein HYDROGEN‐PEROXIDE‐INDUCED CA2+ INCREASES 1 (HPCA1) acts as an H2O2 receptor at the PM and plays a crucial role in linking ROS to Ca2+ signaling (Wu et al., 2020; Fichman et al., 2022). Therefore, it will be interesting to investigate whether HPCA1 contributes to MAPK activation during H2O2 accumulation and whether plants employ HPCA1 to finely connect ROS, Ca2+, and MAPK signals and completely activate immunity.

To prevent the invasion and spread of pathogens, plants produce and mediate the deposition of callose to strengthen the cell wall (Ngou et al., 2022). Callose is a linear β‐1,3‐glucan polymer with hundreds of glucose units that can accumulate in specialized plant cell walls (Ostergaard et al., 2002). Constitutive expression of GLUCAN SYNTHASE‐LIKE 5 (GSL5), which encodes a PM‐localized catalytic subunit of β‐1,3‐glucan synthase, increases β‐1,3‐glucan synthase activity and callose deposition in the mpk4 mutant in Arabidopsis (Ostergaard et al., 2002), indicating that the regulation of callose synthesis and deposition is related to MAPK signaling (Table 1). However, whether MPK4 modulates the activity of β‐1,3‐glucan synthases via phosphorylation remains unknown. Furthermore, β‐1,3‐glucan is synthesized mainly at the PM; therefore, the correct PM localization of callose synthases is necessary for callose deposition. POWDERY MILDEW RESISTANT 4 (PMR4) is a PM‐localized β‐1,3‐glucan synthase that participates in callose deposition during plant immunity (Figure 3). The correct localization of PMR4 at the PM requires exocyst subunit EXO70H4‐mediated vesicle trafficking in Arabidopsis (Kulich et al., 2018). Although there is no evidence that the MAPK cascade contributes to this process, further studies are needed to investigate whether MPKs regulate the trafficking of PMR4 by phosphorylating EXO70H4, as another exocyst subunit, EXO70B2, can be phosphorylated by MPK3 (Brillada et al., 2021). Additionally, EXO70H4 interacts with MILDEW RESISTANCE LOCUS Os (MLOs), and this interaction is essential for the trafficking of PMR4 to the PM (Huebbers et al., 2024). Interestingly, MLOs function as Ca2+ channels in Arabidopsis (Gao et al., 2022c). Therefore, these findings suggest that pathogen‐induced callose deposition may require the involvement of both Ca2+ signaling and MAPK activation, which remains to be addressed in the future. In addition, as a key element that accumulates in the extracellular space, linear β‐1,3‐glucan is recognized by CERK1, which subsequently activates the MAPK cascade (Melida et al., 2018), suggesting that callose may serve as a DAMP to increase immune responses. Remarkably, the activation of MAPK cascades after infection with the RNA virus tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) promotes virus spread in N. benthamiana. Further studies demonstrated that NbMPK3/6 phosphorylate P7, a pathogenicity‐determining protein of ToCV, which subsequently interacts with NbREM1.1 to prevent callose deposition (Feng et al., 2024), indicating that viruses utilize the host MAPK signaling pathway to activate their pathogenic proteins and promote infection by inhibiting callose deposition, resulting in the continuous development and complexity of plant–pathogen interactions involving MAPK signaling.

Furthermore, plants synthesize phytohormones, such as salicylic acid (SA), JA, and ethylene (ET), to enhance immunity during pathogen infection (Ngou et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2025). The functional link between MAPK activation and phytohormone signaling is complicated, as exogenous treatment with phytohormones regulates MAPK activation, which in turn affects the biosynthesis of phytohormones (Figure 3; Table 1). For instance, NbSIPK is the first identified MPK member that can be activated by SA in N. benthamiana (Zhang and Klessig, 1997), indicating that SA can increase plant immunity by activating MAPK signaling. Consistently, the mRNA expression levels of MPK3/6 are increased by benzothiadiazole (BTH), a functional mimic of SA (Beckers et al., 2009). Moreover, the overexpression of MKK7 and MPK3 leads to increased SA accumulation and enhanced disease resistance in Arabidopsis (Genot et al., 2017), suggesting that MAPK activation contributes to the biosynthesis of SA. However, loss of function of SALICYLIC ACID INDUCTION DEFICIENT 2 (SID2), an isochorismate synthase required for SA biosynthesis, does not affect autoimmunity in Arabidopsis plants that constitutively express MPK3 in its active form (Genot et al., 2017), suggesting that MPK3 may not regulate the biosynthesis of SA via direct phosphorylation of SID2. SA biosynthesis also involves the phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) pathway in the cytoplasm (Peng et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025c). Further studies are required to analyze whether the MAPK cascade activates SA accumulation by phosphorylating and activating PALs during plant immunity. Moreover, constitutive activation of MPK3 leads to a high level of ET in Arabidopsis (Genot et al., 2017). Accordingly, MPK3/6 phosphorylate the ET synthases ACC SYNTHASE2 (ACS2) and ACS6, leading to their stability and activation in response to Botrytis cinerea (Han et al., 2010; Li et al., 2012). MPK3/6 also contribute to the expression of ACS2/6 by phosphorylating the transcription factor WRKY33, which directly targets the promoters of ACS2/6 in Arabidopsis (Mao et al., 2011; Li et al., 2012). Furthermore, MPK6 interacts with and phosphorylates the ethylene response factor ERF104 to activate the expression of the defense‐related genes PLANT DEFENSIN 1.2a (PDF1.2a) and PDF1.2b (Bethke et al., 2009). The MKK9–MPK3/6 module was reported to regulate ET signaling by modulating transcription factor ETHYLENE‐INSENSITIVE3 (EIN3)‐mediated transcriptional reprogramming, in which the Raf‐like MAPKKK protein CONSTITUTIVE TRIPLE‐RESPONSE1 (CTR1) plays a negative role (Yoo et al., 2008). Additionally, the MKK1/3–MPK3/6 module functions downstream of CTR1 to regulate ethylene signaling in Arabidopsis guard cells (Zhang et al., 2021). Recently, S‐ADENOSYL‐L‐METHIONINE SYNTHETASE 1 (SAMS1) was reported to be phosphorylated by OsMKKK22, leading to its degradation and the inhibition of ET production (Ma et al., 2025b). These findings indicate that the MAPK cascade regulates ET signaling beyond the biosynthesis of ET in plant immunity. Interestingly, the ET signaling pathway cooperates with the MPK3/6–WRKY33 module as well as JA signaling to regulate the production of camalexin in response to pathogens in Arabidopsis (Zhou et al., 2022), in which WRKY33 and ERF1, transcription factors that integrate the ET and JA pathways and activate camalexin biosynthetic genes, form a transcriptional complex and mediate downstream gene expression. Moreover, MPK3/6 further enhance ET and JA signaling by phosphorylating ERF1 (Zhou et al., 2022). As SA can trigger MPK3/6 activation, these findings indicate that these phytohormone signaling pathways function synergistically to modulate plant immunity by regulating the biosynthesis of camalexin via MAPK signaling, which expands our understanding of the antagonistic relationship between SA and JA in pathogen resistance. Consistently, the synergistic regulation of SA and JA is crucial for disease resistance in rice. OsMKK10.2 activation enhances defense responses by increasing SA and JA accumulation in a synergistic manner (Wang et al., 2023c), which is negatively regulated by OsMPK15, leading to reduced disease resistance (Hong et al., 2019). Furthermore, ZmMPK4 contributes to JA signaling by phosphorylating ZmMYC2, the master regulator of JA, in maize (Li et al., 2024a). In turn, OsMYC2 upregulates the transcript levels of OsMKKK55/62/70 after herbivore attack in rice (Wu et al., 2025). Similarly, the OsMYC2–OsMKKK55/62/70–OsMKK3–OsMPK7/14 module positively regulates rice resistance to brown planthoppers in a JA‐dependent manner (Wu et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that SA and JA synergistically regulate plant immunity via MAPK signaling. However, the JA signaling pathway was also reported to be negatively regulated by the OsBDR1–OsMPK3 module in rice blast resistance (Wang et al., 2023b), suggesting a dual role of the MAPK cascade in phytohormone signaling. Additionally, the biosynthesis of phytohormones mainly occurs in the chloroplast (Rui et al., 2025a, 2026). Therefore, further studies are needed to investigate how MAPK cascades contribute to phytohormone biosynthesis and whether activated MPKs can be relocalized into the chloroplast. The role of MAPK cascades in phytohormone signaling crosstalk also needs to be well characterized, as the underlying mechanisms have the potential to engineer new disease‐resistant crops with high yields.

THE MAPK CASCADE IS TARGETED BY PATHOGEN EFFECTORS AND IS INVOLVED IN ETI SIGNALING

Owing to the crucial role of the MAPK cascade in plant immunity, components in these cascades are ideal direct targets of pathogen effectors (Figure 4), followed by the manipulation of immune responses by pathogens (Zhang and Zhang, 2022). For example, previous studies have shown that the effector HopAI1 from Pto DC3000 has special phosphothreonine lyase activity and interacts with MPK3/6, thereby inactivating MPK3/6 by removing the phosphate group from phosphothreonine and reducing disease resistance in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al., 2007). Similarly, a recent study revealed that the kinase activity of TaMPK3 is attenuated by CEE1, a conserved effector from Fusarium graminearum, through the disruption of ATP binding (Kang et al., 2026). HopF2 acts as an ADP‐ribosyltransferase to modify and block the kinase activity of MKK5 (Wang et al., 2010). HopZ1a acetylates and inactivates MKK7 (Rufian et al., 2021). Similarly, a recent study revealed that the kinase activity of TaMKK4 is inhibited by the effector HAUSTORIUM‐ASSOCIATED SECRETED PROTEIN 98 (HASP98) from the devastating wheat pathogen Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst) via direct interaction in wheat (Wei et al., 2023). Furthermore, TaMKK2 activates immune responses by phosphorylating and activating TaMPK6 in wheat. TaMKK2 is the target of another Pst effector, HASP215, which interferes with the interaction between TaMKK2 and TaMPK6, thereby disturbing signal transduction and inhibiting TaMPK6 activation (Shu et al., 2025). The positive role of OsMPK6 in disease resistance was also revealed by targeting and dephosphorylation by the effector SCRE6 from U. virens in rice (Zheng et al., 2022). Moreover, the coat protein (CP) of the melon necrotic spot virus (MNSV) was recently shown to interact with and inactivate NbMAP3Kε1, which inhibits MAPK activation and subsequently facilitates viral infection (Saiz‐Bonilla et al., 2025). Similarly, StMAP3Kβ2 interacts with and phosphorylates the chloroplast elongation factor StTuB to promote its translocation into chloroplasts to increase immunity, while the P. infestans RXLR effector Pi22926 suppresses plant immunity by attenuating the association between StMAP3Kβ2 and StTuB (Qi et al., 2024). These findings indicate the direct inhibition of MAPK signaling by effectors through the targeting of MEKK‐type MAPK members. Furthermore, the rice blast fungus M. oryzae secretes the effector AvrPib to manipulate MAPK activation via interference with the interaction between the Raf‐like protein kinase OsMAPKKK72 and OsMKK9. OsMKK9 positively regulates MAPK activation and rice blast resistance by phosphorylating OsMPK3/6, with OsMAPKKK72 acting as a scaffold for increasing the interaction between OsMKK9 and OsMPK3/6. The targeting of OsMAPKKK72 by AvrPib reduces the OsMAPKKK72–OsMKK9 interaction and subsequent MPK3/6 activation (Figure 4), thereby inhibiting rice blast resistance (Wang et al., 2026b), indicating that pathogens suppress MAPK activation by interfering with the assembly of a functional MAPK cascade via effectors. In addition, some pathogen effectors inhibit MAPK activation by utilizing host negative immune regulators (Figure 4). For example, the Raf‐like MAPKKK protein TaRaf46 negatively modulates plant immunity. Although the exact function of TaRAF46 in negative immune regulation is still unclear, the serine‐rich effector Pst27791 from the wheat pathogen Pst suppresses host disease resistance by stabilizing TaRaf46 in wheat (Wan et al., 2022). Similarly, the MKK member StMKK1 negatively regulates PTI responses by phosphorylating and activating the protein tyrosine phosphatase StPTP1a in potato (Li et al., 2023). To suppress host resistance, the P. infestans RXLR effectors PITG20303 and PITG20300 target and stabilize StMKK1, leading to increased activation of StPTP1a (Du et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings reveal a dual strategy employed by pathogen effectors in suppressing plant immunity: They directly target members of the MAPK cascade to simultaneously inhibit their positive functions by interfering with intermolecular interactions and kinase activities and potentiate their negative roles through stabilization, ultimately inhibiting the complete activation of MAPK signaling.

The targeting of members of MAPK cascades by pathogen effectors and viral proteins

Owing to the crucial role of the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK/MPK) cascade in plant immunity, members of this family are ideal targets of pathogen effectors and viral proteins. All MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKK), MAPK kinase (MKK), and MPK members can be targeted and modified by these effectors. Effectors from different pathogen groups show preferences in manipulating MAPK cascade members: Bacterial effectors often inhibit their kinase activities (e.g., HopF2, HopZ1a, and HopAI1), fungal effectors frequently interfere with their interactions and stabilities (e.g., HASP215, PITG20303, Pst27791, and AvrPib), and viral effectors focus on disrupting the initiation of MAPKKK activation (e.g., CPs). Pathogens can also reduce MAPK activation by targeting receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs) and pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). Furthermore, plants have evolved intracellular nucleotide‐binding and leucine‐rich repeat domain receptor (NLR) proteins to recognize pathogen effectors and activate effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), and MAPK cascades play important and complicated roles in ETI signaling. In addition to direct target members in MAPK cascades, pathogens can also suppress MAPK activation by targeting its key upstream components, followed by reduced signal transduction to MAPK cascades. For instance, the effector AvrPtoB, an E3 ligase from Pto DC3000, ubiquitinates FLS2, EF‐TU RECEPTOR (EFR), and their coreceptor BAK1, while the other Pto DC3000 effector HopAO1 dephosphorylates tyrosine residues of EFR via tyrosine phosphatase activity, both of which ultimately prevent downstream signaling and inhibit MAPK activation (Gohre et al., 2008; Macho et al., 2014). Similarly, a recent study showed that the NbRALF1–NbFER module, which can boost MPK3/6 accumulation to confer disease resistance, is targeted by the TuMV protein 6K2 in N. benthamiana, resulting in the degradation of NbRALF1 and the inhibition of plant immunity involving MAPK signaling (Rui et al., 2025b). Furthermore, Raf‐like SlMAPKKK18 negatively regulates MAPK signaling by impairing the protein level and kinase activity of SlMKK4. Moreover, the activation of SlMAPKKK18 is modulated by the upstream RLK protein SlFERL via phosphorylation. To suppress SlMKK4‐activated immune signals, the effector BcPG1 secreted from Botrytis cinerea binds to SlFERL and accelerates its ability to phosphorylate SlMAPKKK18 (Ji et al., 2023), thereby inhibiting MAPK activation. These findings reveal that pathogens have evolved precise infection mechanisms by which effectors hijack and utilize host negative immune regulators to manipulate MAPK activation. Further studies are needed to analyze whether these mechanisms are conserved and how plants cope with these hijacks to maintain sufficient disease resistance. In addition, the effector XopR from Xoo suppresses PAMP‐induced stomatal closure by targeting RLCKs and reducing MAPK activation (Wang et al., 2016). Consistently, RXLR25, a Phytophthora capsici RXLR effector, directly targets RLCK‐VII proteins to inhibit PTI, including MAPK activation (Liang et al., 2021), suggesting that the targeting of upstream RLCKs by pathogen effectors is also a common way to inhibit MAPK activation. Due to the positive role of 14‐3‐3 proteins in facilitating and maintaining the activation of MAPK cascades, pathogen effectors also target 14‐3‐3s to inhibit MAPK activation (Figure 4). For instance, the 14‐3‐3‐interacting RXLR effector FIRE was demonstrated to be a conserved effector from several Phytophthora species and to contain a canonical 14‐3‐3‐binding motif. FIRE can interact with multiple 14‐3‐3 proteins and alter their localization during plant immunity, thereby manipulating host immune responses, including MAPK activation (Evangelisti et al., 2023). Consistently, to undermine NbMAPKKKα‐mediated antiviral defense in N. benthamiana, necrovirus CPs bind Nb14‐3‐3a to interfere with the interaction between Nb14‐3‐3a and NbMAPKKKα in a dose‐dependent manner, resulting in the instability of NbMAPKKKα and subsequently reduced MAPK activation (Gao et al., 2022e). Furthermore, the PM‐localized phosphatase DSP4 can dephosphorylate MPK3/6. To ensure sufficient resistance, DSP4 is released from the PM during immune priming. However, to reduce MPK‐mediated immunity, the P3 protein of turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) can hijack DSP4 and retain it on the membrane, thereby suppressing the activation of MAPK cascades (Luan et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that pathogens have a good understanding of plant MAPK signaling and have evolved diverse effectors to inhibit MAPK activation in multiple dimensions. However, how plants deal with these effectors and maintain necessary disease resistance remains largely unknown. Further studies are needed to investigate whether plants avoid being targeted and maintain MAPK activation upon pathogen effector attack by evolving multiple members of the MAPK cascade.

Actually, plant MAPK cascade members can also be monitored by intracellular NLRs to specifically recognize pathogen effectors and initiate ETI signaling (Yan et al., 2025b). For example, the mekk1, mkk1/2, and mpk4 mutants display enhanced disease resistance and dwarf phenotypes, and these phenotypes rely on the coiled‐coil (CC) ‐type NLR protein SUPPRESSOR OF mkk1 mkk2 2 (SUMM2), which monitors the disruption of the MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 cascade (Zhang et al., 2012; Su et al., 2013); therefore, the loss and inactivation of any members in this cascade activate SUMM2‐mediated ETI signaling. Moreover, a Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor (TIR)‐type NLR, RESISTANT TO P. SYRINGAE 6 (RPS6), was identified to collaborate with SUMM2 and contribute to autoimmunity in mekk1 and mpk4 mutants (Takagi et al., 2019, 2022). These findings not only reveal the importance of this MAPK cascade in plants but also provide evidence for the potentiation of ETI signaling through distinct NLRs. Interestingly, a study also revealed that the dephosphorylation of SlMKK2 leads to reduced MAPK activation and the inhibition of NLR protein Prf‐induced cell death in tomato (Chakraborty et al., 2024), indicating that the MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 cascade also plays a positive role in ETI, further revealing the importance and complexity of this MAPK cascade in plant immunity. In addition to SUMM2, MEKK2 was defined as SUMM1, and the cytoplasmic kinase CALMODULIN BINDING RECEPTOR‐LIKE CYTOPLASMIC KINASE 3 (CRCK3) was identified as SUMM3; both are guarded by SUMM2 to monitor the MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 cascade. Unlike MEKK1, which contributes to MPK4 activation, MEKK2 inhibits MKK‐mediated phosphorylation of MPK4, leading to the expansion of SUMM2‐mediated cell death (Nitta et al., 2020). This process requires two malectin‐like RLKs, LETUM 1 (LET1) and LET2, to interact with both SUMM2 and MEKK2, in which MEKK2 functions as a scaffold protein (Liu et al., 2020). Moreover, CRCK3 is phosphorylated by the MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 cascade and contributes to SUMM2‐mediated ETI by increasing the protein level of MEKK2, which in turn associates with and stabilizes CRCK3 (Zhang et al., 2017b; Yang et al., 2020b). Furthermore, a recent study revealed that the plant U‐box E3 ligase PUB5 stabilizes CRCK3, while PUB44 degrades CRCK3, leading to alterations in MEKK2 protein levels and disease resistance in Arabidopsis (Liu et al., 2024a). Taken together, these findings illuminate the complicated immune signaling associated with the MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 cascade, in which SUMM2 plays a critical role in the activation of ETI responses (Figure 1). However, which component–the MAPK cascade member, MEKK2, or CRCK3–is the primary factor that is monitored by SUMM2 warrants further investigation. Further studies are also needed to analyze whether RPS6 activates ETI by monitoring these components. Additionally, constitutive activation of MKK4 causes cell death, and overexpression of MPK3 may be lethal in Arabidopsis (Ren et al., 2002; Genot et al., 2017). Whether any NLRs are employed to monitor the excessive activation of the MAPKKK3/4/5‐MKK4/5‐MPK3/6 cascade in plants remains obscure.

In contrast, MPK3/6 are activated during ETI signaling and in turn promote ETI. For example, ETI‐induced photosynthetic suppression and chloroplastic ROS buildup are impaired in chemically genetically rescued mpk3/6 double mutants, which is correlated with reduced disease resistance and delayed cell death in Arabidopsis (Su et al., 2018), indicating that the complete activation of ETI requires MPK3/6. Similarly, the MPK3/6 homologs WIPK and SIPK also contribute to the NLR protein N‐mediated ETI in N. benthamiana. NbAL7 is a transcription repressor for ROS scavenging genes and interacts with N. During TMV infection, NbAL7 is phosphorylated by WIPK/SIPK, which reduces the interaction between NbAL7 and N, releasing NbAL7 to promote ROS accumulation by binding and suppressing the expression of ROS scavenging genes, thereby increasing disease resistance (Zhang et al., 2023). These findings indicate that MPK3/6 and their homologs are essential for ROS production in ETI. However, to restore ROS homeostasis and maintain plant fitness, NbAL7‐mediated ROS accumulation should be turned off. This process requires the involvement of an E3 ligase, N NLR‐RECOGNIN 7 (NbUBR7), which ubiquitinates WIPK/SIPK‐phosphorylated NbAL7 and rescues the expression of ROS scavenging genes (Zhang et al., 2025), revealing that plants have evolved a sophisticated mechanism to regulate protein function in which MPK‐induced phosphorylation plays a pivotal role. Furthermore, the accumulation of NLRs is required for ETI activation. Previously, SUPPRESSOR OF G2 ALLELE OF skp1 (SGT1) was defined as a molecular chaperone that regulates NLR accumulation (Boter et al., 2007). Upon the activation of immune signaling, the C‐terminal domain of SGT1 is phosphorylated MPK3/6, which enhances the accumulation of the NLR protein RPS2 and subsequent ETI responses (Yu et al., 2020), indicating that MPK3/6 also contributes to ETI by increasing the accumulation of NLRs. However, the effector RipAC from the bacterial pathogen R. solanacearum inhibits the interaction between SGT1 and MPK3/6 to suppress NLR‐mediated immunity (Yu et al., 2020), suggesting that pathogens have also evolved effectors to inhibit ETI responses by targeting the involved MAPK family members. Whether any NLRs have evolved to recognize such effectors by monitoring MPK3/6 remains unknown. In addition, MPK3/6 contribute to ETI via the induction of NLR gene expression after recognition of the effector AvrRpt2 by RPS2 in Arabidopsis (Lang et al., 2022). However, whether and which transcription factors are involved in this process need to be determined. Further investigations are needed to examine whether the MAPK cascade controls ETI signaling by phosphorylating NLRs, which can help us better understand the plant immune system. Furthermore, ETI potentiates and reboots PTI, in which MAPK activation is significantly induced. For example, two helper NLRs, NRC2 and NRC3, activate MAPK signaling in response to the effector AvrPto in tomato, although the underlying molecular mechanism remains unknown (Zhang et al., 2024b). Consistently, the P. syringae effectors AvrRpt2 and AvrRpm1 induce highly activated MPK3/6 kinase activities during ETI activation in Arabidopsis. However, ETI activation triggered an increase in protein levels only for MPK3, not for MPK6 (Guan et al., 2015; Yuan et al., 2021a), suggesting that MPK3 and MPK6 have evolved distinct roles in regulating ETI beyond redundancy. These findings are reminiscent of the contribution of MPK3, but not MPK6, to edr1 autoimmune phenotypes (Zhao et al., 2014). Interestingly, EDR1 was also shown to interact with ENHANCED DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY 1 (EDS1) and PHYTOALEXIN DEFICIENT 4 (PAD4), whose heterodimerization is known to be important for NLR‐mediated ETI responses and PTI amplification in Arabidopsis (Zhou and Zhang, 2020). EDS1 and PAD4 contribute to enhanced disease resistance in edr1 mutants, while EDR1 interferes with the heterodimerization of EDS1 and PAD4 (Neubauer et al., 2020). These findings suggest that EDR1 may play a role in suppressing the function of the EDS1–PAD4 module in PTI. Moreover, as the main functions of EDS1 and PAD4 are characterized in ETI, EDR1 may participate in the regulation of ETI, and the MAPKKK5–MKK4/5–MPK3/6 cascade may also be functionally linked with the EDS1–PAD4 module in ETI. Therefore, further studies are needed to investigate whether any NLRs are associated with EDR1 and are activated after the depletion of EDR1. Notably, a recent study showed that mutating the conserved HMD motif in the NLR protein R‐GENE ANALOG 3 (RGA3) to generate RGA3D605V leads to its autoactivation and confers enhanced resistance to multiple pathogens (Zhong et al., 2026c). However, subsequent research revealed that the Raf‐like MAPKKK OsILA1 suppresses RGA3D605V‐mediated autoactivation of disease resistance via direct phosphorylation at Tyr‐15 and Tyr‐138 (Zhong et al., 2026c). These findings demonstrate that plants have evolved a Raf‐like MAPKKK–NLR module to finely regulate NLR activation and maintain physiological fitness. Surprisingly, in contrast to the reported PTI enhancing ETI, flg22 priming suppresses AvrRpt2‐induced cell death and growth retardation (Wang et al., 2023a). Further studies demonstrated that this phenomenon is modulated by preactivated MPK3/6, which activate the downstream WRKY18–AP2C1/PP2C5 module via phosphorylation. The PTI‐mediated suppression of ETI responses is significantly attenuated in wrky18 and ap2c1 pp2c5 mutants. (Wang et al., 2023a). Taken together, these findings indicate that MPK3/6 also maintain plant fitness during ETI in addition to unidirectional amplification of ETI, revealing that MPK3/6 play important and complicated roles in the functional connection between PTI and ETI as well as the trade‐off between plant growth and immunity. Further studies are needed to determine the precise underlying spatial and temporal regulatory mechanisms involved, which is helpful for fully understanding the role of MPK3/6 and provides direct evidence for accurate utilization of MPK3/6 in disease‐resistant crop breeding.

CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVES

In conclusion, MAPK signaling, which is presented mainly via the MAPK cascade, is essential for plant immunity. Plant immune signals are generally initiated by cell surface‐localized PRRs upon the perception of extracellular PAMPs and are subsequently amplified via the MAPK cascade in the cytoplasm. To date, MAPKKK3/4/5–MKK4/5–MPK3/6 and MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 are two well‐characterized MEKK‐type MAPK cascades involved in plant immunity in Arabidopsis. Investigations in other plant species revealed that, although these MAPK cascades are conserved, the evolved number of MAPK cascade members is diverse across plant species, and activated MPKs play distinct roles in modulating immune signaling under different environmental conditions. In addition, increasing evidence has shown that plants employ Raf‐like MAPKKKs to fine‐tune or maintain MAPK signaling. Moreover, many breakthroughs have been made recently to improve our understanding of the role of MAPK signaling in plant immunity, including the conservation of MAPK cascades across plant species, the activation of downstream signals via activated MPKs, the functional differentiation of Raf‐like MAPKKKs and their functional links with MEKK‐type MAPK cascades, the precise regulation of MAPK activation, and the crosstalk between MAPK signaling and other immune events during pathogen infection. Furthermore, to inhibit plant immunity, pathogens secrete effectors into plant cells to manipulate MAPK signaling. Collectively, these findings not only reveal the importance and complexity of MAPK signaling pathways but also expand our understanding of the complete activation of plant immunity involving MAPK cascades.

Nevertheless, several issues still need to be carefully addressed, and challenges remain in fully understanding the role of MAPK signaling in plant immunity. For example, there are two types of MAPKKKs, MEKK‐type and Raf‐like, in plants. Why do plants evolve a large number of MAPKKKs? Do these two types of MAPKKKs have evolutionary consistency, or do they cluster together at some special evolutionary points? In addition to MAPKKK3/4/5–MKK4/5–MPK3/6 and MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4, are there any other conserved MAPK cascades that contribute to plant immunity? Do any additional elements play a role in connecting the PRR complex to MAPK signaling under pathogen attack? Furthermore, EDR1 acts as a negative immune regulator to both fungal and bacterial pathogens in Arabidopsis. However, OsEDR1 positively regulates blast resistance but negatively regulates bacterial blight resistance in rice. Do MAPK cascade members exhibit functional differentiation in defense responses against different types of pathogens, especially in different plant species? What are the underlying regulatory mechanisms? Many PTMs, such as ubiquitination, phosphorylation, SUMOylation, and oxidation, occur in plants, but how do they cooperatively function in maintaining MAPK cascade activation during plant immunity? Since plants may simultaneously face multiple biotic and abiotic challenges, how do they activate diverse downstream reactions via the same conserved MAPK cascade? Maintaining the trade‐off between plant growth and immunity is important, so can elite crops with both strong disease resistance and high yield via the modification of MAPK signaling be cultivated? Although the crosstalk of different immune events involving MAPK signaling has been studied, in‐depth studies are still required to investigate how plants coordinately regulate the activation of the MAPK cascade and other immune signaling pathways. Therefore, emerging technologies, such as single‐cell analyses of MAPK dynamics and phosphoproteomics, need to be developed for MAPK signaling studies. The underlying mechanisms will expand our understanding of how plants precisely promote immunity via MAPK signaling. Furthermore, genome editing of Raf‐like kinases, such as EDR1, to alter their functions is needed, which has the potential to improve the process of crop resistance breeding.
