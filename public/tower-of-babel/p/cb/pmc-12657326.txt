Spermidine-encapsulated chondroitin sulfate methacryloyl hydrogel delivery system for remodeling bone homeostasis in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Xiaocheng Wang, Jiaxin He, Yixiang Hong, Ligang Jie, Bing Wu, Jinquan Shen, Gengmin Zhou, Qingwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, progressive cartilage degradation, and osteoclast-mediated bone erosion. While current systemic therapies alleviate inflammation, they often fail to prevent structural joint damage or promote local tissue regeneration. Herein, we developed a chondroitin sulfate methacryloyl (ChSMA) hydrogel-based delivery system encapsulating spermidine (SPD), a naturally occurring polyamine with emerging anti-inflammatory, senescence-attenuating, and chondroprotective properties, to achieve localized and sustained treatment of RA-related joint destruction. Using an RA-mimicking co-culture model comprising RA patient-derived synovial organoids and chondrocytes, we demonstrated that ChSMA@SPD effectively attenuated chondrocyte apoptosis and suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). In addition, ChSMA@SPD significantly inhibited osteoclast differentiation in vitro using primary bone marrow-derived monocytes from mice by downregulating dendritic cell-specific transmembrane protein (DC-STAMP) expression. In vivo studies using the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model further confirmed that intra-articular administration of ChSMA@SPD reduced arthritis severity, preserved cartilage integrity, and mitigated joint inflammation. Furthermore, network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses identified key signaling pathways and potential molecular targets of SPD, such as TGFB2, XIAP, MMP8, and PLA2G1B. Collectively, our findings highlight ChSMA@SPD as a dual-functional hydrogel platform that simultaneously protects cartilage and suppresses bone resorption, offering a promising localized therapeutic strategy for RA treatment.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease that affects approximately 0.5–1 % of the global population [1]. It is characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, progressive cartilage degradation, and bone erosion [2,3]. Although biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) have improved disease management, approximately 30–40 % of patients exhibit suboptimal responses [4], and irreversible joint damage remains a major cause of long-term disability. The pathogenesis of RA involves complex interactions between immune and stromal cells, leading to synovial hyperplasia, osteoclast activation, and chondrocyte dysfunction [5]. In particular, aberrant PI3K/AKT signaling pathways exacerbates inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation [6,7].

Within the inflammatory microenvironment of RA, chondrocytes undergo significant phenotypic alterations, including increased susceptibility to apoptosis, diminished proliferative capacity, and dysregulated ECM metabolism [[8], [9], [10]]. Proinflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways in chondrocytes [11]. These pathways promote transcriptional upregulation of MMPs, especially MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-13, which collectively degrade type II collagen and aggrecan-key structural components of cartilage ECM [12]. Additionally, mitochondrial dysfunction induced by reactive oxygen species and endoplasmic reticulum stress further aggravates chondrocyte apoptosis and accelerates cartilage degradation [13]. Moreover, the inflammatory milieu suppresses chondrocyte proliferation via downregulation of cyclin D1 and activation of caspase-3, perpetuating a cycle of impaired cartilage regeneration [14,15].

Osteoclasts play a synergistic role in RA-associated joint destruction. Upon stimulation by receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), primarily secreted by synovial fibroblasts and infiltrating immune cells [5], osteoclast precursors differentiate into mature osteoclasts. This differentiation process is tightly regulated by dendritic cell-specific transmembrane protein (DC-STAMP), a key mediator in osteoclastogenesis. These mature osteoclasts not only degrade subchondral bone but also secrete cathepsin K and hydrogen ions, which penetrate the osteochondral junction and further contribute to cartilage matrix degradation [16]. For instance, chondrocyte-derived MMP-13 cleaves osteopontin to facilitate osteoclast adhesion, while osteoclast-derived TNF-α amplifies chondrocyte apoptosis and MMP production. This reciprocal signaling loop intensifies joint destruction in RA [11].

Recent advances in polyamine biology have identified spermidine (SPD) as a promising immunomodulatory agent with documented efficacy in age-related diseases [[17], [18], [19]]. This endogenous autophagy inducer exhibits dual anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective properties through NF-κB suppression and Nrf2 pathway activation [20,21]. Notably, SPD has been shown to attenuate osteoarthritis progression by inhibiting ferroptosis via the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis [22]. In addition, SPD upregulates BRG1 in cartilage tissue, regulating the Nrf2/KEAP1 and STAT3 pathways and thereby improving cartilage integrity [23]. Although SPD has shown therapeutic promise in degenerative joint conditions, its potential application in RA remains largely unexplored, particularly in three key domains: modulation of innate immune responses coupled with bone remodeling, protection of chondrocytes within the RA-specific inflammatory niche, and the molecular mechanisms underlying these pleiotropic effects.

Chondroitin sulfate (CS), a naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan and a key structural component of cartilage ECM, exhibits multiple biological functions, including anti-inflammatory [24], antioxidant [25], and chondro-inductive activities [26]. Its sulfate and carboxyl groups confer excellent hydrophilicity and modifiability, making CS an attractive candidate for hydrogel development. However, native CS lacks sufficient mechanical stability and crosslinking ability for biomedical use [27]. To address these limitations, methacryloyl-functionalized CS (ChSMA) has been developed as a photo-crosslinkable hydrogel precursor, enabling the construction of injectable, biocompatible scaffolds [28,29]. ChSMA-based hydrogels have demonstrated potential in cartilage repair due to their ability to support chondrogenesis, inhibit hypertrophy, and integrate with native tissue.

In this study, we developed a ChSMA-based hydrogel encapsulating spermidine (ChSMA@SPD) for intra-articular delivery and evaluated its therapeutic efficacy against RA-induced cartilage and bone damage. A co-culture model comprising RA patient-derived synovial organoids and chondrocytes was established to mimic the RA joint microenvironment and assess chondroprotective effects. Osteoclast differentiation was induced from primary mouse bone marrow-derived monocytes, and in vivo efficacy was evaluated using a CIA mouse model. Additionally, network pharmacology and molecular docking were employed to identify potential molecular targets and signaling pathways involved in SPD-mediated immunomodulation. This dual-functional platform integrates both bioactive delivery and structural protection, offering a promising strategy for localized treatment of RA.

Materials and methods

The materials and methods section provides detailed information about the reagents used in this study, as well as specific in vitro and in vivo methods. In addition, detailed information on some of the experimental methods is provided in the supplementary document.

Osteoclast differentiation

Osteoclast differentiation was induced in 6-well plates using α-MEM supplemented with 50 ng/ml RANKL (315–11, Peprotech, USA), 50 ng/ml M-CSF (315–02, Peprotech, USA), and 10 % FBS. Cells were obtained from mouse bone marrow, and the medium was refreshed every two days. The TRAP assay kit (G1492, Solarbio, China) was used to detect osteoclast production.

Immunofluorescence staining

After treatment with ChSMA@SPD hydrogel extraction, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X-100. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating cells in 3 % bovine serum albumin (BSA). Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (see Supplementary Table 1 for antibody details). Following incubation, cells were treated with the corresponding secondary antibody at room temperature for 1.5 h. Immunofluorescent signals were visualized by fluorescence microscopy, and nuclei were stained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with ChSMA@SPD extraction. Following treatment, total RNA was extracted, and RT-qPCR was conducted using a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche, Switzerland). Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene β-actin, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used for RT-qPCR analysis are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Western blot analysis

After ChSMA@SPD extraction treatment, cells were harvested and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Cowin Biotech Co., China) to extract total protein. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating membranes with 5 % non-fat milk in TBST. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and imaged on a Bio-Rad Chemidoc™ XRS Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses

Chondrocytes were cultured in conditioned medium collected from synovial organoids, supplemented with ChSMA@SPD for 48 h. Cells were then harvested for RNA-seq, and subsequent data analysis was performed as previously described [30].

Collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model

Male mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing approximately 20 g) were utilized to establish the CIA model. For primary immunization, bovine type II collagen (20,022, Chondrex, USA) was emulsified in an equal volume of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA, 7023, Chondrex, USA) to a total volume of 100 μl, and administered intradermally at the base of the tail. For the secondary immunization, collagen was emulsified in an equal volume of incomplete Freund's adjuvant (IFA, 7002, Chondrex, USA) and injected in the same manner. All experimental procedures were performed under sterile conditions in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Approval No.2021–118). Arthritis severity was assessed using a scoring system (0–4) based on paw swelling and erythema, with total scores ranging from 0 to 16. A total score ≥4 indicated successful model establishment [31].

In vivo therapeutic experiments

Following secondary immunization, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8 per group): control group, CIA model group, SPD-treated group (60 mg/kg), and ChSMA@SPD-treated group (60 mg/kg). From the day of grouping, mice in the treatment groups received different administration routes. SPD was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) twice per week at a dose of 60 mg/kg in a volume of 200 μL. In contrast, ChSMA@SPD was delivered via intra-articular (IA) injection into the knee joint twice per week at the same dose (60 mg/kg) in a volume of 10 μL. Throughout the treatment period, disease incidence and body weight were monitored and recorded. Paw swelling was assessed by measuring the thickness of both hind paws on day 0 (prior to immunization) and day 42 (after treatment), using the following formula:Pawswellingchange(%)=[(pawthicknessonday42−pawthicknessonday0)/pawthicknessonday0]×100%.

After three weeks of treatment, all mice were euthanized, and tissue samples were collected for subsequent histological and molecular analysis.

Micro-CT analysis

After treatment, mouse hind limbs from each group were harvested and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde overnight. After fixation, excess fixative was carefully removed from the samples using gauze. Samples were then positioned onto the scanning bed of a micro-CT imaging system (NMC-200, NEMO, China). Scanning parameters were set as follows: radial field of view (FOV) of 100 mm, maximum axial scan range of 250 mm, scanning speed of 4 s/bed, and minimum reconstruction pixel size of 2 μm. Scanning data were collected using Cruiser software, and raw images were reconstructed with Recon software. Regions of interest (ROIs) were analyzed using dedicated analysis software. Quantitative analyses of bone parameters, including bone volume (BV), tissue volume (TV), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone surface area (BS), BS/TV ratio, and BS/BV ratio, were conducted for the knee and ankle joints. Additionally, trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and trabecular number (Tb.N) were also measured.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Differences with ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001were considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Physicochemical characterization and biocompatibility of ChSMA@SPD hydrogel

To establish a bioactive platform for localized SPD delivery, we synthesized a photo-crosslinkable ChSMA hydrogel and incorporated SPD into the polymer matrix via physical encapsulation (Fig. 1A). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that both ChSMA and ChSMA@SPD hydrogels exhibited porous, interconnected microstructures with no noticeable differences in pore morphology (Fig. 1B), indicating that SPD loading did not disrupt the network architecture. The swelling ratio showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 1C), suggesting that SPD incorporation had minimal effect on water uptake capacity. Rheological analysis revealed that ChSMA@SPD exhibited a significantly higher storage modulus (G′) compared to ChSMA alone (Fig. 1D), indicating enhanced elastic behavior. In contrast, the viscosity remained stable over time and was comparable between groups (Fig. 1E). Stress-strain analysis showed similar compressive elasticity between the two hydrogels (Fig. 1F), while water retention experiments demonstrated that both hydrogels maintained high moisture levels at 4 °C, but water retention at 37 °C decreased progressively over time, with ChSMA@SPD retaining slightly more moisture in the early stage (Fig. 1G). In addition, we assessed the injectable properties of the ChSMA hydrogel. Fig. S1A shows that ChSMA hydrogel maintains its flowability under syringe pressure, confirming its injectability. Furthermore, the in vivo degradation profile of ChSMA hydrogel following subcutaneous injection was evaluated (Fig. S1B). The hydrogel exhibited slow degradation over time, demonstrating its potential for sustained, localized drug delivery applications. Drug release studies showed that SPD was released from the hydrogel in a sustained manner, with the majority of release occurring within the first 8 h, followed by a plateau phase (Fig. 1I), indicating a typical burst-sustained release profile suitable for localized delivery. UV–Vis spectrophotometric analysis and standard curve generation for SPD concentrations confirmed the accuracy of SPD quantification and release kinetics (Fig. S1C). Hemocompatibility assays demonstrated a hemolysis rate of less than 5 % (Fig. 1J), meeting the ISO standard for biomedical materials. Furthermore, live/dead staining of chondrocytes cultured within the hydrogel showed high cell viability on both day 3 and day 5 (Fig. 1K), confirming good cytocompatibility.

Taken together, these results suggest that ChSMA@SPD hydrogel possesses favorable physicochemical and mechanical properties, sustained release capability, and excellent biocompatibility, supporting its potential as an intra-articular delivery platform for RA therapy.

ChSMA@SPD inhibits the growth of RA-derived synovial organoids

The synovium is a key pathological site in RA patients, where the inflammatory environment plays a critical role in disease progression. To investigate the regulatory effects of ChSMA@SPD on synovium-associated pathologies, we constructed three-dimensional synovial organoids derived from RA patients (Fig. 2A). These organoids secreted a variety of cytokines associated with RA pathology, including inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8), angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF), and chemokines (e.g., MCP-1) (Fig. 2B). Further analysis demonstrated that ChSMA@SPD significantly inhibited the growth and activity of synovial organoids. Brightfield imaging revealed that untreated synovial organoids displayed characteristic three-dimensional structures, while ChSMA@SPD treatment markedly reduced organoid size and cellular activity (Fig. 2E–G). These findings suggest that ChSMA@SPD suppresses the pathological activity of synovial organoids, thereby modulating the RA synovial environment.

Co-culture model of RA synovial organoids and chondrocytes recapitulates RA-related chondrocyte pathological changes

Synovial inflammation and hyperplasia are key factors in joint destruction, and the dysfunction and apoptosis of chondrocytes are often closely linked to the inflammatory response in the synovium. The interaction between synovial tissue and chondrocytes, through the release of pro-inflammatory factors and cytokines, promotes cartilage degradation and pathological changes. Therefore, the co-culture of synovial organoids and chondrocytes provides an ideal platform for establishing RA-related chondrocyte pathological models.

In this study, we developed an RA-related chondrocyte pathological model by co-culturing RA synovial organoids with chondrocytes. Fig. 3A illustrates a schematic representation of the co-culture system, where RA synovial organoids are co-cultured with chondrocytes to simulate cartilage damage and pathological changes associated with RA. Under co-culture conditions, the CM secreted by synovial organoids significantly induced chondrocyte apoptosis (Fig. 3B). The results from both the CCK-8 and EdU assays confirmed that synovial organoid CM inhibited the proliferation of chondrocytes (Fig. 3C and D; S2). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining revealed that synovial organoid CM led to a marked reduction in Col2a1 expression, a key cartilage marker (Fig. 3E), and an increase in MMP9 expression, a marker of cartilage degradation (Fig. 3F). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that CM treatment suppressed the expression of Birc5 while promoting the expression of MMP3 (Fig. 3G). To further strengthen physiological relevance, we additionally implemented a contact-free Transwell co-culture configuration and a treatment arm in which synovial organoids were paired with chondrocytes in the presence of a ChSMA@SPD (Fig. S3A). This variant reproduced the injurious phenotype observed with conventional CM exposure (apoptosis and matrix catabolism), whereas ChSMA@SPD mitigated these effects-reducing chondrocyte apoptosis, dampening IL-1α/IL-1β/IL-6/IL-8 induction, lowering MMP9, and partially restoring F-actin organization and Col2a1 expression (Fig. S3B and D). Consistent with the above phenotypes, immunoblotting showed that CM robustly increased phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT without altering total protein levels, whereas the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 suppressed these phosphorylation events toward baseline (Fig. S4), confirming that p-PI3K and p-AKT were significantly elevated by CM and significantly reduced by LY294002. These data verify that CM activates PI3K-AKT signaling and that pathway blockade prevents this activation, supporting our interpretation that mitigation by ChSMA@SPD operates through dampening PI3K-AKT activity.

These results collectively demonstrate that the co-culture model of RA synovial organoids and chondrocytes effectively mimics RA-related chondrocyte pathological changes, providing a robust experimental platform for investigating the underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic strategies for RA-induced cartilage damage.

EdU proliferation assays further confirmed that ChSMA@SPD treatment enhanced chondrocyte proliferation relative to CM-treated controls (Fig. 4C; S5). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that ChSMA@SPD extract reduced the expression of matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) while restoring the expression of type II collagen (Col2a1), indicating improved ECM preservation (Fig. 4D and E). Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR showed that ChSMA@SPD downregulated inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8), apoptosis markers (Bax), and catabolic enzymes (MMP9), while upregulating the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl2 and cartilage-related marker Col2a1 (Fig. 4F). Consistently, western blotting (Fig. 4G and H) showed that ChSMA@SPD reduced CM-induced phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT while leaving total protein levels unchanged; importantly, this decrease was abrogated by the AKT activator SC-79 (reverse-rescue) and phenocopied by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (Fig. S6A and B). Functionally, EdU assays mirrored the biochemical findings: ChSMA@SPD restored CM-suppressed proliferation, and this effect was reversed by SC-79 and mimicked by LY294002 (Fig. S6C and D). These orthogonal phenocopy and reverse-rescue results across molecular and cellular readouts provide robust validation that the protective effect of ChSMA@SPD is mediated by dampening PI3K-AKT signaling.

To test whether our system modulates senescence, we established an etoposide-induced model (Fig. S7A). Etoposide markedly increased SA-β-gal-positive cells and diminished EdU incorporation, indicating senescence with proliferation arrest (Fig. S7B and C). Treatment with ChSMA@SPD significantly reduced SA-β-gal positivity and partially restored EdU-positive cells (Fig. S7B and C). At the transcriptional level, etoposide upregulated SASP cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8), senescence markers (CDKN1A/p21, CDKN2A/p16) and MMP1, while suppressing Col2a1. ChSMA@SPD reversed these changes, lowering inflammatory and catabolic transcripts and restoring Col2a1 (Fig. S7D).

Together, these findings show that ChSMA@SPD preserves chondrocyte survival and matrix integrity under inflammatory stress by dampening PI3K-AKT signaling and suppressing inflammatory, apoptotic, and senescence programs.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals differential gene expression in chondrocytes treated with synovial organoid CM

To further investigate the potential mechanisms by which the CM from synovial organoids co-cultured with chondrocyte promotes RA-related pathological features in chondrocyte, we treated chondrocyte cells with CM from synovial organoids and performed transcriptomic sequencing. Differential gene analysis was performed to present the expression, types, and chromosomal locations of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 5A). The volcano plot shows that after CM treatment, 1561 genes were upregulated, and 1187 genes were downregulated (p < 0.05, |log2Foldchange| ≥ 2) (Fig. 5B). Further analysis of these DEGs was conducted. The dumbbell plot illustrates that inflammation-related genes, including IL-6, were upregulated, while genes associated with cartilage degradation and matrix remodeling, such as MMP3, MMP9, and ADAMTS5, were downregulated. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that CM treatment promoted osteoclast differentiation and the activation of the TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). Additionally, KEGG analysis of the downregulated DEGs indicated their involvement in processes such as protein digestion and absorption and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions (Fig. 5D). GO enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were primarily involved in inflammatory responses and innate immune responses, while downregulated genes were mainly involved in the negative regulation of osteoblast differentiation and osteoblast differentiation itself (Fig. 5E; S8). CM treatment activated PI3K-AKT signaling and disrupted cartilage homeostasis, as shown by increased p-PI3K/p-AKT and elevated MMP3/MMP9, alongside reduced Col2a1 and IL-10 (Fig. 5F and G). Discussion

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, cartilage degradation, and progressive joint erosion [32]. Despite considerable advances in understanding its pathogenesis and developing therapeutic strategies, current treatments often fail to fully halt disease progression and joint damage [5]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic agents with improved efficacy and fewer adverse effects. Natural products have gained increasing attention due to their potential therapeutic benefits and safety profiles [33]. In this study, we explored the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of spermidine (SPD), a naturally occurring polyamine, using a novel ChSMA@SPD hydrogel-based sustained-release system. We employed the CIA mouse model and RA synovial organoid-chondrocyte co-culture models to investigate SPD's effects on RA-associated cartilage pathology and its mechanisms in osteoclast differentiation.

Chondroitin sulfate methacryloyl (ChSMA) is a promising biomaterial for drug delivery, with excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and the ability to form hydrogels that can encapsulate bioactive molecules like SPD. Unlike conventional systemic treatments, ChSMA-based hydrogels offer a localized, sustained-release platform that provides direct therapeutic action at the site of injury while minimizing systemic side effects. This localized delivery system, combined with ChSMA's intrinsic ability to promote chondrogenesis and inhibit hypertrophy, significantly enhances SPD's therapeutic efficacy in treating RA [[34], [35]].

SPD, a natural compound derived from dietary sources, is classified as a caloric restriction mimetic (CRM) with known immunomodulatory effects [18,36]. Prior studies have highlighted SPD's beneficial properties, including its anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and anti-apoptotic activities [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. Our study provides the first evidence that ChSMA@SPD significantly alleviates arthritis severity in the CIA mouse model without notable toxicity or adverse effects. Micro-CT imaging showed a significant reduction in bone erosion and joint destruction in the ChSMA@SPD-treated mice compared with the CIA group. Histological evaluations, including hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-fast green staining, confirmed that SPD treatment significantly ameliorated cartilage degradation and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. Immunofluorescence analysis further validated these findings, showing decreased matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3) expression and increased collagen type II alpha 1 (Col2a1) levels, indicative of preserved cartilage integrity [41,42]. Additionally, ChSMA@SPD treatment significantly reduced macrophage infiltration and activation, as evidenced by decreased expression of macrophage markers F4/80 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), suggesting potent anti-inflammatory properties.

To further investigate SPD's therapeutic mechanisms, we established a 3D human RA synovial organoid model derived from synovial tissues of RA patients. The cytokine secretion profiles of the synovial organoids analyzed by liquid-phase chip assays showed significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and angiogenic factors, confirming the model's ability to mimic the inflammatory environment in RA and providing a robust foundation for further investigation into RA-related chondrocyte pathology. Treatment with SPD notably inhibited the activity and growth of the synovial organoids, addressing synovial hyperplasia, a key pathological hallmark of RA [30,43].

Beyond organoid-level changes, fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS) are widely recognized as master effectors of rheumatoid synovitis and joint destruction. Activated RA-FLS drive pannus formation, sustain cytokine and matrix-degrading enzyme production, and promote osteoclastogenesis through RANKL, thereby linking synovial inflammation to cartilage and bone damage [2]. Although RA-FLS–specific evidence for SPD is currently limited, studies in human synoviocytes/related fibroblast systems and work on the polyamine pathway suggest that modulating polyamine homeostasis can restrain inflammatory/catabolic programs and invasive behavior [44]. Within this framework, the synovial benefits observed here are biologically plausible and support the relevance of deploying an ECM-mimetic ChSMA hydrogel to interface with FLS-rich pannus in vivo, while dedicated RA-FLS functional assays are planned as follow-up work.

Considering the critical interplay between synovial inflammation and cartilage degradation in RA pathogenesis [2,45], establishing organoid and cell co-culture models that accurately simulate the in vivo pathological environment is essential. Such models have become increasingly valuable in recent research for accurately reflecting disease-specific cellular responses and signaling pathways [[46], [47], [48]]. In our study, we developed a novel RA synovial organoid-chondrocyte co-culture model, which effectively promoted pathological changes typical of RA cartilage, including increased apoptosis, suppressed cell proliferation, and elevated expression of genes involved in inflammation and cartilage degradation, such as MMPs. Transcriptomic sequencing confirmed these findings, highlighting significant activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and a tendency toward osteoclast differentiation in the co-culture system. Notably, SPD treatment significantly reversed these detrimental changes, reducing apoptosis, restoring proliferation, and decreasing inflammatory cytokine and MMP expression. These therapeutic effects correlated with the downregulation of PI3K/AKT signaling, suggesting a potential mechanism through which SPD exerts its cartilage-protective effects. Considering SPD's anti-senescence activity, we further established a chondrocyte-senescence model (etoposide-induced) and confirmed its efficacy.

Moreover, our research uncovered an additional mechanism by which ChSMA@SPD protects against RA-induced bone destruction—by suppressing osteoclast differentiation. We demonstrated that ChSMA@SPD significantly reduced the expression of DC-STAMP, a crucial regulator of osteoclastogenesis [49,50]. Inhibition of DC-STAMP impaired osteoclast differentiation, suggesting that ChSMA@SPD may protect against bone erosion by directly targeting osteoclast activity.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying SPD's therapeutic effects, we employed network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses to predict its potential targets. A total of 24 overlapping genes were identified from RA- and SPD-related target databases. Among them, four proteins closely associated with bone and cartilage homeostasis—TGF-B2, XIAP, PLA2G1B, and MMP8—were selected for docking studies. These proteins are functionally relevant to RA pathology: TGF-B2 is a well-established cartilage-inducing factor that promotes mesenchymal stem cell chondrogenesis [51,52]; XIAP is a caspase inhibitor that regulates apoptosis and has been implicated in osteoclast survival, with studies suggesting that disrupting the XIAP–caspase axis can promote osteoclast apoptosis [53]; MMP8 is a matrix metalloproteinase involved in extracellular matrix degradation in cartilage [54]; and PLA2G1B is a phospholipase A2 enzyme that modulates lipid metabolism and inflammation, potentially contributing to joint inflammation and cartilage breakdown through its role in arachidonic acid release and pro-inflammatory mediator production [55]. In the context of our comparative docking with clinically used RA therapeutics-methotrexate-DHFR, hydroxychloroquine-KIT, and leflunomide-DHODH-SPD's predicted binding energies fall within the range of these first-line agents and, for several targets, compare favorably, supporting SPD's potential multi-target engagement and a putative chondroprotective role via TGFB2.

Collectively, the findings from our study provide robust evidence that SPD offers substantial therapeutic benefits for RA by addressing multiple pathological aspects of the disease. SPD effectively mitigates synovial inflammation, cartilage degradation, and bone erosion by modulating key signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, DC-STAMP-mediated osteoclastogenesis, and the regulation of critical targets such as XIAP, PLA2G1B, TGF-B2, and MMP8. In addition, we propose ChSMA hydrogel as an efficient drug delivery platform for SPD, enabling sustained release and localized delivery, which enhances the therapeutic outcomes and provides valuable contributions to the clinical development and administration of SPD for RA treatment. The use of CIA animal models and innovative human-derived synovial organoid-chondrocyte co-culture systems significantly enhances the translational relevance of our results, bridging the gap between preclinical models and potential clinical applications.

However, our study has certain limitations. While the synovial organoid model offers substantial translational advantages, further validation in larger clinical cohorts is necessary to confirm these findings across a broader patient population. Moreover, the pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and potential off-target effects of SPD should be thoroughly evaluated in future studies to support its clinical application.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that SPD holds significant promise as a novel therapeutic agent for RA. Its potent anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative, and anti-osteoclastogenic effects, mediated through the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway and modulation of DC-STAMP, highlight its potential to effectively prevent joint damage and improve patient outcomes. The integration of ChSMA hydrogel as a delivery platform further enhances SPD's clinical applicability. Future studies should focus on in-depth mechanistic investigations and clinical validations to fully harness SPD's therapeutic potential in RA management.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a ChSMA-based hydrogel encapsulating SPD as a promising localized therapeutic strategy for RA. Through a combination of in vitro and in vivo models, including a patient-derived synovial organoid-chondrocyte co-culture system and a CIA mouse model, we demonstrated that ChSMA@SPD exerts dual protective effects by attenuating cartilage degradation and inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Mechanistically, ChSMA@SPD suppressed chondrocyte apoptosis, downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, and inhibited osteoclast differentiation via DC-STAMP regulation. Furthermore, network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses revealed that SPD may modulate key inflammatory and bone remodeling pathways, including NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and RANKL signaling, potentially through direct interactions with target proteins such as TGFB2, XIAP, MMP8, and PLA2G1B. These findings collectively highlight ChSMA@SPD as a multi-target, intra-articularly administered hydrogel system capable of simultaneously addressing immune dysregulation and structural damage in RA, offering new insights into the design of biomaterial-based therapeutic interventions for chronic joint diseases.
