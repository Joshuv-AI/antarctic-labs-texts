Multi-omics reveals molecular basis of beef quality differences between Qinchuan and Nanyang cattle
Authors: Juntao Guo, Jianfang Wang, Haobin Ma, Shengchen Yu, Abebe Belete Kuraz, Ke Zhang, Haibing Liu, Diba Dedacha Jilo, Yueting Pan, Jiawei Du, Chugang Mei, Gong Cheng, Huabin Zhu, Linsen Zan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeef quality traits, such as intramuscular fat deposition, water-holding capacity, and tenderness, vary among breeds, yet their molecular basis remains unclear. In this study, we hypothesized that the regulation of muscle energy metabolism and lipid deposition is associated with differences in beef quality between Qinchuan and Nanyang cattle. To this end, we measured meat quality traits in the longissimus dorsi muscle of females from the two breeds (n = 5 per group) and conducted integrative analyses using transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data. The results showed that Qinchuan cattle exhibited higher intramuscular fat content, marbling score, and better tenderness and water-holding capacity. Multi-omics analysis identified 1011 differentially expressed genes, 895 differentially expressed proteins, and 12 differential metabolites, with some molecules showing consistent changes at both the transcriptional and protein levels. Pathway analysis revealed that glycolysis and energy metabolism pathways were enhanced in Nanyang cattle, whereas the PPAR signaling pathway was activated in Qinchuan cattle. Correlation network analysis indicated that energy metabolism-related molecules were positively correlated with shear force and water loss, while PPAR signaling pathway-related molecules were positively correlated with intramuscular fat content and marbling score. l-serine was associated with PPAR-related molecules and intramuscular fat content, and promoted adipogenic differentiation of intramuscular preadipocytes in vitro by upregulating PPAR-related genes. This study reveals that the regulation of energy metabolism and lipid deposition is associated with breed-specific differences in meat quality, providing candidate molecular targets and metabolic biomarkers for improving meat quality in local cattle breeds.Keywords: Intramuscular fat deposition, Tenderness, Water-holding capacity, PPAR signaling pathway, Muscle energy metabolism, Metabolite biomarkers

Introduction

Beef quality is a complex trait resulting from the integration of muscle structural characteristics, intramuscular fat (IMF) deposition, and muscle energy metabolism. These factors influence tenderness, juiciness, and flavor, which ultimately determine consumer evaluation and acceptability. Currently, these traits are assessed using phenotypic indicators such as marbling score, shear force (SF), water-holding capacity (WHC), meat color, and IMF, all of which are widely recognized as strong predictors of consumer satisfaction (Bayinbate et al., 2025; Won, Kim, Hwang, Lee, & Oh, 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). Although phenotypes have been extensively described, the biological basis underlying breed differences in beef quality remains insufficiently understood.

Genetic background or breed is the primary determinants of beef quality, accounting for approximately 40–60% of the variation observed in economically important traits such as IMF content (Bhuiyan et al., 2017; Inoue, Kobayashi, Shoji, & Kato, 2011). Chinese indigenous yellow cattle, as an important reservoir of agricultural genetic resources, exhibit extensive phenotypic diversity. Among them, Qinchuan (QC) cattle and Nanyang (NY) cattle are two representative breeds with different meat quality traits and economic value. Although it is generally believed that QC cattle may possess superior meat quality due to superior marbling, detailed data regarding the meat quality characteristics of these two breeds remain relatively limited, hindering systematic dissection of the mechanisms underlying inter-breed quality differences (Ma et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2017).

Increasing evidence suggests that post-mortem muscle energy metabolism is a key and potentially interconnected process underlying meat quality (Tan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023).Transcriptomic and proteomic studies have respectively indicated greater glycolytic capacity and mitochondrial metabolism, as well as enhanced lipid metabolism, as factors influencing tenderness and WHC variation in beef and pork (Kee, Park, & Lee, 2008; Purslow, Gagaoua, & Warner, 2021; Welzenbach et al., 2016). However, existing research often focuses on individual trait variations or remains at the level of single-omics analysis, making it difficult to comprehensively elucidate how transcriptional regulation, changes in protein abundance, and metabolite accumulation collectively contribute to genetically determined meat quality phenotypes. In particular, metabolites-the direct products of metabolism-are often underrepresented, despite being the closest to phenotype and the most immediate regulators.

Multi-omics integration offers a rational framework to address these limitations by capturing biological regulation across multiple levels, from gene expression to protein function and metabolite output (Ge et al., 2023; Hwang, Lee, Lim, & Joo, 2023; Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2018; Qiu et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2024). In recent years, livestock multi-omics studies have gradually indicated that the coordinated regulation of muscle energy metabolism and lipid deposition processes may represent an important basis for meat quality differences among breeds (Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). It remains unclear whether breed-specific differences in beef quality traits reflect variations in post-mortem glycolytic capacity, lipid deposition, or the interaction of these processes.

Based on these knowledge gaps, we hypothesized: compared with NY cattle, the superior beef quality of QC cattle may be associated with relatively reduced muscle glycolytic activity and enhanced lipid deposition capacity. To test this hypothesis, we focused on the longissimus dorsi muscle (LDM) and systematically measured key meat quality traits, including intramuscular fat content, water-holding capacity, and shear force. We employed an integrated multi-omics approach, combining transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, to systematically characterize molecular differences between the two breeds. By further linking molecular networks to meat quality phenotypes and functionally validating key metabolites in vitro, with aimed to elucidate the molecular basis of breed-specific differences in beef quality and to identify candidate molecular targets and metabolic biomarkers for meat quality improvement in indigenous cattle populations.

Materials and methods

All experimental animal protocols were approved by the Committee of Experimental Animal Management at Northwest A&F University, China (Approval ID: DK2021042), and complied with the organization's and the government's applicable rules and regulations.

Animals and sample preparation

This study included a total of 10 healthy cattle (5 QC and 5 NY), all obtained from Shaanxi Qinbao Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd. Grouping was based on inherent breed background. All cattle were of similar age and were female, and they were raised in the same commercial finishing system for three months, with consistent diet formulation and feeding management. These cattle were group-housed at a standard commercial stocking density (6–8 m2 per head) with free access to water. Given the small sample size, this study is an observational comparative investigation based on breed/genetic background.

These cattle were slaughtered at approximately 28 months of age, with average slaughter weights of 458 ± 21 kg for QC cattle and 435 ± 31 kg for NY cattle. Pre-slaughter transportation time was approximately 1 h, and these cattle were fasted for 24 h before slaughter, and slaughter order was randomized. Following electrical stunning, standard commercial slaughter procedures, including exsanguination, hide removal, evisceration, and carcass splitting, were performed. Immediately after slaughter, LDM samples were collected from the 12th–13th rib region on the left side of each carcass. To improve sample representativeness and minimize within-group variation, a five-point fixed sampling strategy was applied within the central cross-sectional area of the LDM (Fig. S1). Equal masses of tissue were collected from each sampling point, pooled, aliquoted into 2 mL cryotubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses. The carcasses were then chilled at 4 °C for 24 h for post-mortem aging. After aging, approximately 1 kg of LDM was collected from the same region, vacuum-packaged, transported to the laboratory at −20 °C, and stored at −20 °C until meat quality analysis.

Cultivation and induced differentiation of cattle intramuscular preadipocytes

Intramuscular preadipocytes were isolated from the longissimus dorsi muscle of three newborn Qinchuan cattle following the methods previously described (Yang, Ma, Mei, & Zan, 2022). The specific procedure was as follows: the muscle tissue was minced and digested with 0.1% collagenase type I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA) at 30 °C for 1 h. After digestion, an equal volume of complete medium was added to terminate the reaction. The complete medium consisted of 85% DMEM/F12 (HyClone, Logan, USA), 15% fetal bovine serum (PAN-Seratech, Aidenbach, Germany), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, USA). The cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 700 ×g for 10 min to collect the pellet. The pellet was washed twice with serum-free DMEM/F12 (700 ×g, 10 min each time), resuspended in complete medium, and seeded into 10 cm culture dishes. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After 2 h, the medium and non-adherent cells were removed. The cells were washed three times with PBS, and the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, which was subsequently changed every 2 days. When cell confluence reached approximately 90%, the cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin for 3 min, and an equal volume of complete medium was added to terminate the digestion. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in cell freezing medium (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). After programmed freezing, the cells were stored in liquid nitrogen.

For resuscitation, the cryopreserved cells stored in liquid nitrogen were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath. After thawing, the cells were gently transferred into complete culture medium, gently mixed, and centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in fresh complete medium and seeded into culture dishes. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and the medium was changed every two days.

When the intramuscular preadipocytes grow to 90%, the medium was replaced with adipogenic induction medium. This induction medium was prepared by adding 0.5 mmol/L 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, 1 mmol/L dexamethasone, and 2 mmol/L insulin (all from MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) to the complete medium. The cells were induced for 2 days. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with maintenance medium (complete medium supplemented with 2 mmol/L insulin) to sustain differentiation. Starting from the induction day, different concentration gradients of l-serine (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) were added during each medium change. For cell experiments, three biological donors were used (n = 3). Two technical replicate wells were set up per donor.

Measurement of meat quality traits

Meat quality traits were measured under blinded conditions. Before measurement, all samples from the 10 cattle were re-coded by a researcher who was not involved in the subsequent analyses. LDM samples (n = 5 per group) were thawed only once at 4 °C, with no repeated freeze–thaw cycles. pH was determined using a Testo 205 portable pH meter (Testo China, Shanghai, China), meat color was determined using a 3nh NR20XE colorimeter (Threenh, Shenzhen, China), and the L*, a*, and b* values were recorded. Marbling score was based on the national standard “Grading of Livestock and Poultry Meat Quality-Beef” (GB/T 29392–2022). The cooked meat rate was determined by steaming the meat in boiling water for 30 min and initial and final weights were recorded. Water-holding capacity was evaluated through drip loss rate, cooking loss rate. Drip loss rate was determined by bagging the meat samples in a refrigerator at 4 °C for 24 h and the initial and final weights were recorded. LDM samples (6 cm × 3 cm × 3 cm) were heated in an 80 °C water bath, and the core temperature was continuously monitored using a thermocouple thermometer. When the core temperature reached 70 °C, the sample was removed, cooled to room temperature, blotted dry to remove surface moisture, and weighed. Cooking loss was calculated as the percentage of weight loss relative to the initial weight before heating. SF was measured using a TA.Touch texture analyzer (Shanghai Baosheng, Shanghai, China) equipped with a Warner–Bratzler shear blade. After cooking-loss determination, cylindrical meat cores were collected from the cooked sample parallel to the muscle fiber direction using a 1.27 cm diameter corer. At least three valid meat cores were obtained from each LDM sample (with ≥5 mm spacing). Each meat core was positioned perpendicular to the blade and sheared at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/s, the peak shear force was recorded. Protein content and IMF content were determined using the Kjeldahl method and the Soxhlet extraction method, respectively. Each indicator for every individual cattle was measured in three technical replicates, and the mean value was calculated for all subsequent analyses.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA from LDM sample (n = 5 per group) was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent (Thermofisher, Wilmington, USA), followed by analysis of total RNA concentration and purity using Bioanalyzer 2100 and RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA), high-quality RNA samples with RIN > 7.0 were used to construct sequencing library. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaseqTM 6000 (LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) in paired-end 150 bp (PE150) mode.

After obtaining the raw data, the raw data were first filtered to obtain high-quality sequencing data (clean data), and then the reads of the samples were compared to the bovine reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1; accessed December 18, 2024). The mapped reads for each sample were assembled using StringTie (version 2.1.6; accessed December 18, 2024). StringTie and ballgown were used to calculate FPKM (fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) values for visualizing mRNA expression profiles (e.g., PCA and heatmaps). Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using DESeq2 based on the gene-level read count matrix generated by StringTie. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to correct for multiple hypothesis testing, yielding adjusted p-values (q-values). Genes with |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and q < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The DEGs were then analyzed for GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment.

Proteomics sequencing and analysis

LDM sample (n = 5 per group) proteins were extracted according to the manufacturer's instructions and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The centrifuged proteins of each sample were enzymatically digested using trypsin to obtain peptides. Aliquots of peptides were diluted using mobile phase A (5% ACN, pH 9.8) and separated by a Vanquish Neo UHPLC liquid system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA). The liquid-phase separated peptides were subjected to mass spectrometry data acquisition using the DIA (Data Independent Acquisition) mode of the Astral mass spectrometer, and the DIA mass spectrometry data were subjected to protein searching, protein identification, and protein quantification using the DIA-NN software (version 1.9.1; accessed December 18, 2024). The main parameters in DIA-NN were set as follows: the protein identification mode was DirectDIA (no experimental DDA data were required for spectral library construction; instead, a theoretical in silico spectral library was generated from the protein sequence database of the target species). The digestion enzyme was set to Trypsin/P with a maximum of two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation (C) was specified as a fixed modification, and Oxidation (M) and Acetylation (protein N-terminus) were set as variable modifications. A target–decoy strategy was used to control identification confidence, with thresholds of PSM FDR < 0.01 and protein FDR < 0.01.

Proteins with a Fold change (FC) ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 0.83 and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-adjusted q value <0.05 were defined as DEPs.

Non-targeted metabolomics detection and analysis

Metabolites were extracted from LDM samples (n = 5 per group) using 50% methanol buffer. Chromatographic separation was performed using an UltiMate 3000 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA). Reversed-phase separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC T3 column (100 mm*2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, Milford, USA). A high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer, Q-Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA), was used for the detection of metabolites eluting from the column. Q-exactive operated in positive and negative ion modes. Metabolite ion precursor spectra (70–1050 m/z) were collected at a resolution of 70,000 to achieve an AGC of 3e6. The maximum injection time was set to 100 ms. Fragment spectra were collected at a resolution of 17,500 to achieve an AGC of 1e5 and a maximum injection time of 80 ms. To assess the stability of the LC-MS throughout the entire collection process, a quality control sample (Pool of all samples) was acquired after every 10 samples. The R language program of XCMS software was used to correct the retention time of the acquired mass spectrometry data, as well as to complete the peak identification, extraction and characterization. Metabolites were annotated using the KEGG and HMDB databases by matching accurate mass (m/z) with database entries (mass tolerance <10 ppm). Identifications were further validated using an in-house spectral library containing MS/MS fragmentation patterns. Isotope distribution patterns were also used to verify molecular formulas.

Metabolite quantification and differential metabolite (DMs) screening were performed using metaX. Features with missing values in >50% of QC samples or > 80% of all samples were removed, and the remaining missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) method. The data were first normalized using probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) and then corrected for signal drift using QC-RSC; meanwhile, features with a coefficient of variation (CV) > 30% in QC samples were excluded to improve analytical reproducibility. Differential metabolite analysis was performed using Student's t-test, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) multiple-testing correction to obtain q values. In addition, PCA and PLS-DA were conducted, and VIP values were calculated from the PLS-DA model. To assess the robustness of the PLS-DA model, a 200-permutation test was performed. Metabolites satisfying all of the following criteria were defined as differential: |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1, VIP ≥ 1, and q < 0.05. Subsequently, DMs were subjected to metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) using MetaboAnalyst 6.0, with statistical significance defined as q < 0.05.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from intramuscular preadipocytes (n = 3) and LDM samples (n = 5 per group) with TRIzol. The RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Agbio, Hunan, China). cDNA was used as a substrate to detect the expression of target genes by PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). β-actin was used as an internal reference and was quantified by 2 − ΔΔCt method. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

H&E staining

H&E staining was performed under blinded conditions. The LDM (n = 5 per group) were fixed in 4% medium paraformaldehyde solution for 72 h and then embedded into paraffin sections. Sections were cut into 4um sections using a Lycra tissue slicer and deparaffinized using different concentrations of xylene and gradient ethanol, followed by hydration and dehydration. Finally, the sections were stained using hematoxylin-eosin and photographed using resin sealing.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

A cell Counting Kit-8 (UElandy, Suzhou, China) was used to assess cell proliferation according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Oil red O and Nile red staining

After discarding the complete culture medium, wash it three times with PBS and fix it in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Next, after washing with PBS three times, the preadipocytes were stained with Oil Red O working solution (Macklin, Shanghai, China) and Nile Red working solution (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) for 30 min, respectively. Finally, after washing with PBS three times, cells stained with Oil Red O were photographed using an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Nile red stained cells were photographed using the EVOS FL Auto 2 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, USA). For meat quality traits and cell experiments, between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests after confirming normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05; no multiple testing correction was applied. For omics data analysis, LDM samples from each animal were subjected to transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiling (n = 5 per breed). Based on the distinct characteristics of each omics dataset, appropriate standard statistical frameworks were applied for differential analysis. All P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For the correlation network integrating multi-omics data and phenotypic traits, Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated, and only correlations with |r| > 0.7 and P < 0.05 were visualized. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). ** indicated a highly significant difference (P < 0.01), * indicated a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Results

Differences in LDM meat quality between QC cattle and NY cattle

To investigate the meat quality differences between QC cattle and NY cattle, we measured the meat quality traits of the LDM, including pH, meat color (L*, a*, and b*), marbling score, water-holding capacity (cooking loss rate, drip loss rate), SF, cooked meat rate, IMF, and protein content. The results showed no significant differences between the two breeds in meat color, pH, or protein content (Table S2). However, QC cattle exhibited significantly higher marbling score, IMF, and cooked meat rate, along with lower SF, cooking loss rate, and drip loss rate compared to NY cattle (Fig. 1A). Consistently, H&E staining revealed a higher density of intramuscular adipocytes in the LDM of QC cattle relative to Nanyang cattle (Fig. 1B). Under the conditions that the animals were of similar age, all female, and raised under the same finishing and management system, these results indicate that QC cattle exhibited a more favorable meat quality phenotype. Our observed phenotypic differences should be interpreted as breed-related phenotypic differences, while still noting that validation in a larger population is required.

Meat quality trait analysis of QC cattle and NY cattle. (A) Comparison of meat quality trait analysis between QC cattle and NY cattle. (B) H&E staining of the LDM of QC cattle and NY cattle.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Transcriptome sequencing of LDM from QC cattle and NY cattle was performed using RNA-seq, yielding a total of 61.13 Gb of raw data, including 40.76 million reads. After quality control, 59.55 Gb of clean data including 39.70 million reads, were retained. The amount of clean data for each sample was greater than 5 G, and the mapping rate of each sample was higher than 96% (Table S3and 4). PCA showed tight clustering within groups and clear separation between groups (Fig. 2A). A total of 20,266 genes were annotated, of which 1011 were differentially genes (DEGs), 556 were up-regulated and 445 were down regulated (Fig. 2B, Table S5 and 6). The heatmap further showed distinct expression patterns of these DEGs between the two breeds (Fig. 2C).

Transcriptomic analysis of the LDM of QC cattle and NY cattle. (A) PCA analysis. (B) Volcano map of DEGs. (C) Heat map of DEGs. (D) GO function enrichment analysis of DEGs. (E) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs. (F) Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs were significantly enriched in terms related to muscle contraction, glycolytic progress, gluconeogenesis, troponin complex (Fig. 2D). In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis identified that metabolic pathways, PPAR signaling pathway, glucagon signaling pathway, AMPK signaling pathway, calcium signaling pathway, glycolysis/glycogenesis, insulin signaling pathway, insulin resistance, and other pathways were significantly enriched. (Fig. 2E, Table S7). Furthermore, GSEA analysis revealed that activation of the PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid degradation, and fatty acid metabolism in QC cattle, while the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway was inhibited (Fig. 2F).

To validate the RNA-seq results, eight DEGs were randomly selected for qRT-PCR analysis (n = 1011, including up- and downregulated genes). The results showed that the expression trends detected by qRT-PCR were consistent with the RNA-seq data (Fig. 3A and B). The Pearson correlation coefficient between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq was 0.8974 (Fig. 3C), indicating the high reliability of the transcriptomic data.

qRT-PCR validation of RNA-seq data. (A) Relative mRNA expression of eight DEGs measured by qRT-PCR. (B) Changes in expression levels of DEGs in RNA-seq and qRT-PCR. (C) Pearson correlation of DEGs validated in RNA-seq and qRT-PCR.

Identification of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs)

Proteomics was used to identify differential proteins between QC cattle and NY cattle. The PCA results showed clear separation between QC and NY samples (Fig. 4A). A total of 4822 proteins were identified between the LDM of QC cattle and NY cattle, and 895 proteins were differentially expressed, of which 811 proteins were up regulated and 84 proteins were down regulated (Fig. 4B, Table S8 and 9). GO enrichment analysis showed that these differential expressed proteins (DEPs). were involved in peptide biosynthetic process, peptide metabolic process, actin cytoskeleton organization (Fig. 4C). KEGG analysis revealed that the top 20 significantly enriched pathways included cytoskeleton in muscle cells, insulin signaling pathway, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and the glucagon signaling pathway (Fig. 4D, Table S10).

Identification of differentially abundant metabolites (DMs)

To better understand the metabolic differences in LDM between QC cattle and NY cattle, non-targeted metabolomics was used to analyze metabolites between the two breeds. PCA showed a separation trend between the two groups under unsupervised conditions, whereas PLS-DA further indicated that the two groups could be distinguished with good within-group clustering (Fig. 5A, B). Given the limited sample size in this study (n = 10), the PLS-DA results may be optimistic; therefore, a 200-permutation test was further performed to evaluate model robustness. The results showed that the R2 intercept was 0.9726 and the Q2 intercept was −0.7709, indicating that the model was not obviously overfitted (Fig. 5C). To reduce the bias in differential metabolite screening caused by instability of the VIP threshold under a small sample size, VIP ≥ 1, |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1, and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted q < 0.05 were jointly used as the criteria for defining differential metabolites (DMs). Based on these criteria, A total of 430 metabolites were identified between the LDM of QC cattle and NY cattle, of which 12 were DMs. Compared with NY cattle, 10 DMs were upregulated and 2 were downregulated in QC cattle (Fig. 5D, Tables S11 and S12). Notably, l-serine and fumaric acid showed relatively high abundance in the muscle of QC cattle, whereas taurine and 3-(Aminomethyl)-5-methylhexanoic acid showed relatively low abundance (Fig. 5E). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these DMs were primarily enriched in taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, and D-amino acid metabolism (Fig. 5F, Table S13).

Integrated analysis of LDM multi omics and meat phenotype in QC cattle and NY cattle

Overlapping analysis of the transcriptome and proteome identified 4166 genes expressed at both the mRNA and protein levels, among which 117 were differentially expressed (Fig. 6A). Correlation analysis showed that the 117 DEGs/DEPs were significantly positively correlated between the transcriptome and proteome (Spearman's ρ = 0.77, p < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 6B). A protein–protein interaction network (PPI) constructed using the STRING database (confidence >0.70) and Cytoscape software revealed strong interactions among 63 DEPs, including key proteins such as PYGM, MYL1, ADIPOQ, and FASN (Fig. 6C). KEGG Sankey diagram analysis further demonstrated that DEGs/DEPs were mainly involved in muscle energy metabolism pathways (e.g., glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, carbon metabolism, and the glucagon signaling pathway), and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 6D). Notably, in the LDM of QC cattle, PPAR signaling pathway-related DEGs/DEPs (e.g., FABP3, LPL, CD36, and ADIPOQ) were up regulated, whereas most DEGs/DEPs associated with muscle energy metabolism (e.g., PYGM, GPI, PHKG1, and PFKM) were down regulated.

Transcriptomics and proteomics integration analysis. (A) Gene-protein Venn diagram. (B) DEGs and DEPs correlation analysis. (C) PPI network of overlapping DEGs/DEPs. (D) Overlapping DEGs/DEPs enriched in energy metabolism and lipid metabolism related pathways.

Next, we constructed an exploratory Spearman correlation network integrating meat quality traits, overlapping DEGs/DEPs, and DMs. The network contained 36 nodes and 317 edges (|r| > 0.7, P < 0.05), including 5 meat quality traits, 21 DEGs/DEPs, and 12 DMs. Overall, muscle energy metabolism-related DEGs/DEPs tended to correlate positively with SF, cooking loss rate, and drip loss rate, and negatively with IMF content and marbling score. Conversely, PPAR signaling pathway-related DEGs/DEPs showed positive correlations with IMF content and marbling score (Fig. 7A).

(B) Spearman correlation analysis between DMs and Meat phenotype traits. (C) Spearman correlation analysis of DMs and muscle energy metabolism-related DEGs/DEPs. (D) Spearman correlation analysis of DMs and PPAR signaling pathway-related DEGs/DEPs.

At the metabolite level, Taurine and 3-(Aminomethyl)-5-methylhexanoic acid were positively correlated with SF, cooking loss rate, and drip loss rate (Fig. 7B), and were also positively associated with energy metabolism-related DEGs/DEPs such as PYGM, GPI, PFKM, and PHKG1 (Fig. 7C). In contrast, cis-8,11,14,17-Eicosatetraenoic acid, Fumarate, and l-serine were positively correlated with IMF (Fig. 7B). Notably, cis-8,11,14,17-Eicosatetraenoic acid, l-serine were also positively correlated with PPAR signaling pathway-related DEGs/DEPs such as LPL, ADIPOQ, and FABP3 (Fig. 7D).

l-serine promotes the adipogenesis of intramuscular preadipocytes

Given that l-serine showed high abundance in the LDM of QC cattle and was strongly correlated with IMF content, we further evaluated the effect of l-serine supplementation on the adipogenic differentiation of intramuscular preadipocytes (Fig. 8A). We used CCK-8 kit to determine the optimal concentration of l-serine, and the results showed that cell viability was highest at 20 μM (Fig. 8B). Quantitative results indicated that supplementation with 20 μM l-serine significantly upregulated the expression of key lipogenic genes in the PPAR signaling pathway, including PLIN1, FASN, SCD1, FABP3, and FABP4 (Fig. 8C). Additionally, Oil Red O and Nile Red staining further confirmed that supplementation with l-Serine promoted lipid droplet formation (Fig. 8D).

Discussion

With the growing demand for high-quality beef, enhancing meat quality remains a primary objective in modern livestock husbandry. Although IMF, SF, and WHC exhibit low to moderate heritability (Bhuiyan et al., 2017; Shan, Zhou, & Li, 2025), these parameters are well-recognized as critical factors influencing beef eating quality and consumer satisfaction. Appropriate IMF deposition produces marbling and improves tissue structure and palatability, thereby reducing SF and enhancing tenderness; WHC determines the extent of moisture loss during processing and heating, directly affecting juiciness, color stability, and product yield (Hughes, Oiseth, Purslow, & Warner, 2014; Konarska, Kuchida, Tarr, & Polkinghorne, 2017). This study showed that, compared with NY cattle, QC cattle exhibited higher IMF content and marbling score, higher and marbling score, along with better WHC and lower SF. These findings support the view that QC cattle have relatively superior meat quality and provide a more comprehensive phenotypic basis for understanding meat quality differences between the two breeds.

To characterize molecular features underlying meat quality differences, we performed an integrated analysis of the transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data from the LDM. This study was an observational comparative study based on breed grouping. To minimize the influence of confounding factors as much as possible, the animals included were of similar age, all female, and were raised for 3 months under the same commercial finishing system with an identical diet formulation and management regimen. Under these conditions, the observed phenotypic differences in meat quality and their associated multi-omics molecular features are more appropriately interpreted as breed-related differences under uniform production conditions. However, given the limited sample size, these findings should still be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive evidence. In addition, the inclusion of only female cattle was partly related to the fact that females generally show higher intramuscular fat content and marbling than males, which helped us capture more pronounced breed-related differences while, to some extent, reducing the confounding effects of sex and hormonal status (Huerta-Leidenz et al., 2022; Judge et al., 2021). Nevertheless, this design may also reduce statistical power and limit the generalizability of the findings to other sex groups. Despite these limitations, clear multi-omics separation was still observed between the two breeds, and 1011 DEGs, 895 DEPs, and 12 DMs were identified. Notably, 117 differential molecules showed concordant changes at both the mRNA and protein levels, and the overall correlation between transcriptomic and proteomic changes was relatively high (Spearman's ρ = 0.77). These results indicate that some molecular alterations associated with breed-related meat quality differences are coordinated across the transcript and protein levels, thereby providing a basis for identifying key candidate molecules associated with meat quality traits.

PPI network analysis and pathway enrichment analyses demonstrated that these differential molecules with strong interaction connectivity were primarily involved in muscle energy metabolism pathways and the PPAR signaling pathway. In NY cattle, a series of DEGs/DEPs closely related to glycolysis (such as PYGM, GPI, PFKM, and PHKG1) was upregulated. PYGM is a key subtype of glycogen phosphorylase, and its high expression has been demonstrated to be directly associated with increased cooking loss and reduced water-holding capacity in both pork and beef (Nelson & Cox, 2002; Severino et al., 2022). GPI (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase) and PFKM (phosphofructokinase-M type) are key enzymes that catalyze rate-limiting steps in glycolysis, and their upregulation suggests a higher glycolytic potential in NY cattle muscle. Previous studies have indicated that excessively rapid post-mortem glycolysis can lead to a sharp decline in muscle pH, triggering irreversible denaturation of myofibrillar proteins, thereby impairing WHC and tenderness (Sieck, Han, Prakash, & Jones, 1998). However, only the pH value at 24 h postmortem was measured in our study, and dynamic pH data at multiple postmortem time points were not available. Therefore, the relationship among glycolysis, pH changes, and meat quality traits still requires further evidence. Chao et al. reported that PHKG1 suppresses intramuscular fat deposition (Chao et al., 2024) and its mutation can improve WHC and reduce drip loss rate in pork (Liu et al., 2019; Zappaterra, Sami, & Davoli, 2019). Based on these molecular characteristics, we speculate that the higher shear force and moisture loss observed in NY cattle may be associated with the upregulated expression of energy metabolism-related molecules in their muscle tissue.

In contrast, the PPAR signaling pathway was significantly activated in QC cattle, with key members such as lipoprotein lipase (LPL), fatty acid translocase (CD36), and adiponectin (ADIPOQ), and fatty acid-binding protein 3 (FABP3), were coordinately upregulated. LPL is responsible for hydrolyzing triglycerides within circulating lipoproteins, releasing free fatty acids for uptake by peripheral tissues (Horton, 2019). CD36, located on the cell membrane, mediates the transmembrane transport of long-chain fatty acids (Glatz, Nabben, & Luiken, 2022). FABP3, as an intracellular fatty acid chaperone, participates in the intracellular transport of fatty acids (Chmurzyńska, 2006). ADIPOQ may promote intramuscular fat deposition by positively regulating the metabolism of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) (Zhang, Liu, Kong, Li, & Yue, 2022). The coordinated upregulation of these DEGs/DEPs suggests at the molecular level that QC cattle muscle may possess a stronger capacity for fatty acid uptake, intracellular transport, and deposition, thereby providing a potential explanation for its higher intramuscular fat content and superior marbling phenotype. The exploratory Spearman correlation network further supported these findings. DEGs/DEPs related to muscle energy metabolism tended to positively correlated with SF, cooking loss rate, and drip loss rate, but negatively correlated with IMF content and marbling score. In contrast, DEGs/DEPs associated with the PPAR signaling pathway showed the opposite pattern. It should be emphasized that, limited by the sample size, this correlation network only suggests potential associations between molecular features and meat quality traits and is insufficient to support causal inference.

Metabolites, as the ultimate products of gene function and a direct reflection of cellular physiological status, provide us with insights closer to the phenotypic level (Dan et al., 2024; Sadri et al., 2020). In this study, l-serine was more abundant in QC cattle muscle and showed consistent positive correlations with PPAR- related DEGs/DEPs and IMF/marbling. Previous studies have shown that l-serine serves not only as an important precursor for the synthesis of proteins, phospholipids (e.g., phosphatidylserine), and sphingolipids, but is also involved in cell differentiation, proliferation, and migration processes (Hirabayashi & Furuya, 2008; Kent, 1995; Murtas, Marcone, Sacchi, & Pollegioni, 2020). Further cell functional experiments showed that l-serine supplementation promoted adipogenic differentiation of intramuscular preadipocytes, upregulated the expression of PLIN1, FASN, SCD1, and FABP3/4, and increased lipid droplet accumulation, supporting its value as a candidate metabolic biomarker and functional candidate factor. It should be noted that the cells used for functional validation in this study were derived from newborn QC calves rather than from the LDM of adult finishing cattle. Therefore, the cell-based findings can only be regarded as supportive evidence and are insufficient to directly demonstrate a promotive effect of l-serine on intramuscular fat deposition in adult cattle. Further validation through in vivo supplementation experiments in finishing cattle is still required.

In summary, this study suggests that the meat quality differences between QC and NY cattle may be associated with remodeling of energy metabolism and PPAR-related lipid deposition processes, and provide clues for the further identification of candidate molecules and metabolites related to meat quality traits. However, due to the limited sample size and the observational study design, the identified differential molecules, enriched pathways, and association networks still require further validation in larger cohorts, independent populations, and under more comprehensive covariate control to assess their reproducibility, robustness, and biological significance.

Conclusion

QC cattle exhibited higher IMF content and marbling score than NY cattle, accompanied by improved tenderness and WHC. Integrated multi-omics analysis suggested that these meat quality differences between QC and NY cattle may be associated with remodeling of energy metabolism and PPAR-related lipid deposition processes. Notably, l-serine promoted the adipogenic differentiation of bovine intramuscular preadipocytes in vitro by upregulating PPAR-related genes, suggesting its potential as a candidate metabolic biomarker and functional target (Fig. 9). Overall, this study provides candidate molecular and metabolic evidence for elucidating differences in meat quality among local cattle breeds and for improving meat quality, although the related results still require further validation in larger populations and additional in vivo studies.
