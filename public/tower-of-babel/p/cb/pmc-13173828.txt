Multifactorial associations of non-suicidal self-injury among adolescents with depression: a network analytic perspective
Authors: Shiyu Sun, Lan Hong, Siyu Tong, Meizhi Zheng, Jiaying Sun, Shiyi Yan, Ke Zhao, Wendian Shi, He He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn clinical samples of adolescents with depression, the underlying causes of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) across different developmental stages of adolescence are not well understood. This study explored stage-specific differences by comparing psychosocial factors and investigating possible causal links between NSSI and depressive symptoms in middle adolescence (13–15 years) versus late adolescence (16–18 years).MethodsA total of 1,530 Chinese adolescents with a diagnosis of depression were enrolled in the study. The presence of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in the past year was assessed using the Functional Assessment of Self-Mutilation (FASM). Psychosocial factors were measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS), Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS), and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS). To examine stage-specific differences in network centrality and causal pathways, partial correlation networks and Bayesian network analysis were applied.ResultsDepressive symptoms demonstrated a probabilistic directional association with NSSI during middle adolescence, whereas peer victimization emerged as a primary probabilistic link to NSSI in late adolescence. Gender demonstrated a stable direct edge to NSSI, with no consistent indirect paths through other measured variables. Within the directed acyclic graph, childhood trauma emerged as a putative upstream factor, exhibiting strong connections to peer victimization and social support.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that factors including depressive symptoms, gender, and peer victimization exerted distinct effects on NSSI across developmental stages. Future research should prioritize the specific influence of adolescence as a dynamic developmental period on the etiology and maintenance of NSSI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04479-5.Keywords: Adolescent, Depression, Non-suicidal self-injury, Network analysis, Bayesian network model

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the deliberate, self-inflicted damage of body tissue without suicidal intent, encompassing behaviors such as cutting, burning, and severe scratching [48]. Epidemiological data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 revealed that self-harm constitutes the third leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among adolescents aged 10–24 years worldwide [12], representing a critical public health challenge. Although GBD reports self-harm broadly, NSSI constitutes a major component in adolescents. The prevalence of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) differs markedly between clinical and community adolescent populations. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that NSSI affects 50.0%−76.1% of adolescents with depression [45, 69], a range which substantially exceeds community prevalence rates of 18.7%−38.0% [40, 48]. Notably, within depressed populations, NSSI is characterized by greater repetition and severity and serves as a significant predictor of subsequent suicidal behavior [8]. While distinct from suicidal intent, NSSI is frequently associated with concurrent suicidal ideation in adolescents. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that NSSI elevates the risk for subsequent suicidal ideation (odds ratio = 2.95) and suicide attempts (hazard ratio = 2.00), particularly among individuals with persistent NSSI behaviors [45]. Current etiological models conceptualize NSSI as a multifactorial phenomenon arising from the complex interaction of biological predispositions, psychological vulnerabilities, and socio-environmental influences [8]. This mechanistic complexity highlights the necessity for targeted research to clarify the developmental pathways of NSSI in high-risk adolescent populations.

This study adopts a biopsychosocial-ecological systems framework to examine risk factors for NSSI. Within this model, biological (e.g., age, gender), psychological (e.g., mental disorders, alexithymia), and social (e.g., childhood trauma, peer victimization, social support) factors represent the most frequently identified contributors to NSSI in adolescents [8, 45, 48]. In a review of Chinese literature, 46 studies investigated associations between biological risk factors and NSSI, with gender being the most frequently examined factor (n = 27) [48]. Among adolescents with depression, females demonstrate a higher likelihood of engaging in NSSI compared to males [22], but those conclusions were still controversial [71]. Alexithymia (OR = 1.023, p = 0.003) and depressive symptoms (p < 0.001) were identified as significant risk factors for NSSI among adolescents with depression [69]. Adolescents with depression exhibited significantly higher prevalence rates of NSSI (65.1% vs. 7.9%) and alexithymia (77.1% vs. 41.6%) compared to the control group (p < 0.001) [50]. Sexual and physical abuse have also been associated with NSSI among adolescents with depression [32]. Victimization was identified as the most prevalent community-level risk factor (32/113) [48]. Research on protective factors against NSSI in clinical settings remains limited, though social and family support have been identified as potential buffers [38]. Consistent with this, NSSI severity demonstrates a strong positive correlation with childhood trauma (r = 0.667, p < 0.01) and a moderate negative correlation with perceived social support (r = − 0.464, p < 0.01) [61]. The observed interaction between risk and protective factors suggests that prevailing etiological models may offer an overly simplistic representation of NSSI. Consequently, analytical approaches capable of modeling these complex interactions to develop more nuanced frameworks are required.

Epidemiological evidence indicates significant age-related variation in NSSI prevalence, with incidence peaking around age 15 years after onset in early adolescence (ages 12–14) and subsequently declining [46, 48, 65]. This might be somewhat related to adolescent development. Adolescence constitutes a critical neurodevelopmental period marked by rapid physical and psychological maturation, which coincides with the emergence of maladaptive behavioral patterns [51]. Neuroendocrine research has found that cortisol reactivity did not fall in a linear relationship with age but rather via a quadratic curve, suggesting the mid-age adolescents (13–15 years old) had the highest cortisol responses to stress compared to their younger and older peers, potentially due to a dual factor of pubertal development and self-control and emotion regulation capacity [62]. In the middle of adolescence, teenagers may be more prone to hormonal fluctuations and emotional disorders. Self-control and emotion regulation capacity increased with age [9]. Social psychology research suggests that the objects of social motivation and the structure of social behavior undergo significant changes during the development of adolescence [42]. The late stage of puberty is coupled with a higher risk of depression in adolescents, partly through increased negative physical self and interpersonal stress [24]. According to previous studies examining the entire span of adolescence [33], adolescence is commonly categorized into three developmental stages: early (10–12 years), middle (13–15 years), and late (16–18 years). In the Chinese context, the progression from early to late adolescence corresponds to the educational transition from elementary to high school, accompanied by significant concurrent changes in interpersonal needs and social environments [72]. These observations raise two critical questions: (1) Do differences in NSSI prevalence between middle (13–15 years) and late (16–18 years) adolescence reflect underlying developmental transitions? (2) Are the etiological factors contributing to NSSI similarly developmentally specific? Addressing these questions is essential for informing developmentally tailored intervention strategies.

Network modeling represents a transformative methodological approach in contemporary psychopathology research [26, 60]. This paradigm conceptualizes mental health phenomena as complex systems of dynamically interacting symptoms, psychological states, and behavioral characteristics [4, 5]. In contrast to conventional methods that examine symptoms in isolation, network analysis provides a distinct advantage by elucidating the reciprocal relationships and temporal dynamics among interacting factors [23]. Empirical applications have demonstrated the utility of this approach, particularly for elucidating suicidal behavior via its network associations—specifically, positive connections with depressive symptoms and negative connections with protective factors such as self-esteem [16]. However, despite these methodological advances, the application of network analysis and Bayesian network modeling to non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in adolescent populations remains notably limited. Distinguishing the network structure of NSSI between middle and late adolescence can provide critical insight into its relationships with key risk and protective factors across development.

This study examines a sample of Chinese adolescents with depression to address two primary research questions: (1) What are the network associations linking gender, depression, peer victimization, childhood trauma, and social support to non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI)? (2) Do these networks demonstrate stage-specific variations between middle (13–15 years) and late (16–18 years) adolescence, and if so, what are their characteristic patterns? Using systematic network analysis, we aim to identify developmental transitions in psychopathological manifestations to inform developmentally targeted intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a national multicenter cross-sectional study based on the Chinese Adolescent Depression (CADC) cohort study. All procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen Kangning Hospital (IRB: 2020-k021-02). Data were collected between January and December 2021. All participants were assessed and screened by a psychiatrist with an attending title or higher according to the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5, Patient version (SCID-I/P) to clarify the diagnosis and determine enrollment. All participants met the diagnostic criteria of moderate to severe depressive episodes in adolescents or bipolar disorder with a current depressive episode. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. After passing the evaluation, participants took the questionnaire test under the guidance of psychology or psychiatry graduate students. Before the official start of the study, all the researchers were trained to ensure they were familiar with the evaluation process. The inclusion criteria of this study are as follows: (1) Participants met the diagnostic criteria of depressive episodes or depressive episodes corresponding to bipolar disorder as defined in the DSM-V; (outpatient depression status meets the criteria of depressive episode), and scored ≥ 10 on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9); (2) Patient age: 13–18 years; (3) Each subject obtained informed consent from their legal guardian before participating in this study. Participants and their legal guardians received a complete study description and voluntarily signed a written informed consent form. Exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients with severe physical diseases, infectious diseases, complications of immune system diseases, or a history of thyroid-related diseases; (2) Patients with brain trauma, epilepsy, or other known severe neurological diseases or brain organic diseases; (3) Patients with a history of severe mental disorders such as schizophrenia or intellectual disability.

Measurements

Sociodemographic information

Sociodemographic data, including age and gender, were collected using a standardized questionnaire. Participants were classified into two developmental stages based on established criteria: middle adolescence (13–15 years) and late adolescence (16–18 years).

Clinical measurement

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess depressive symptom severity, using the validated Chinese version. The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 has satisfactory psychometric properties [10]. This instrument measures the frequency of depressive symptoms over the preceding two weeks using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 3 ("nearly every day"), generating a total score from 0 to 27. This measure demonstrated strong psychometric properties for adolescent populations, with established clinical utility for distinguishing depression severity levels. It employs a validated clinical cutoff score of ≥ 10, indicating moderate-to-severe depression [21]. The Cronbach's alpha value of PHQ-9 was 0.742.

The Functional Assessment of Self-mutilation (FASM) scale was a structured interview questionnaire compiled by Lloyd in 1997. The Chinese version of the FASM scale showed good psychometric properties [47]. This paper mainly analyzes NSSI (e.g., cutting, burning, and deliberately beating oneself) in order to understand whether the participants had intentionally exhibited 13 different behaviors associated with NSSI listed in the evaluation form over the past 12 months. Moreover, frequency, severity, treatment, and duration were assessed. In this study sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.987.

The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS) was a well-validated 20-item instrument that assesses alexithymia using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) [3]. Total scores range from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher degrees of alexithymia. The Chinese version has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in adolescent populations, with established reliability and validity [31]. In our clinical sample, the scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.730).

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ): There are 28 questions in total, and each question is scored on a five-point scale, with a total possible score ranging from 28 to 140; higher scores indicate greater levels of trauma exposure [64]. The Chinese adaptation demonstrates adequate psychometric properties [43]. In this study sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.656. Although the internal consistency of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire in the present sample was modest (Cronbach’s α = 0.656), this level of reliability is comparable to that reported in prior studies of Chinese adolescents [68]. It is considered acceptable for retrospective trauma assessment in psychiatric populations [2, 70].

Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS), peer victimization was assessed using MPVS, a widely utilized 16-item self-report measure developed by Mynard and Joseph [41]. The MPVS was used to conduct a comprehensive assessment of peer aggression behaviors, which mainly included physical victimization (e.g., being hit or pushed), verbal victimization (e.g., being called names or threatened), social manipulation (e.g., rumor-spreading or social exclusion), and attacks on property (e.g., damage to or theft of personal belongings). Higher total scores reflect a greater frequency of exposure to peer aggression incidents. The measure has demonstrated robust psychometric properties across diverse adolescent samples, including strong internal consistency, good convergent and discriminant validity, and sensitivity in distinguishing between types of victimization. Its applicability and validity in Chinese adolescent populations have also been established [30]. The Cronbach’s α for the total MPVS score in this study was 0.910.

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), social support was assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), a 12-item self-report instrument [74]. The scale measures an individual's subjective appraisal of support adequacy across three distinct sources: family, friends, and a significant other. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("very strongly disagree") to 7 ("very strongly agree"), with higher total scores indicating a greater overall perception of social support. The MSPSS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, good convergent validity, and a stable three-factor structure aligned with its theoretical dimensions. Its reliability and validity have been well-established in Chinese adolescent populations [27, 63]. In the current sample, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.904.

Statistical analysis

All network analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.2; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). To investigate developmental differences in factors influencing NSSI and their potential causal pathways among adolescents with depression, we implemented two complementary network analytic approaches. First, we constructed an undirected network using a Mixed Graphical Model (MGM) to identify conditional dependence relationships among variables and characterize the global network architecture. Second, we estimated a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to infer plausible causal pathways underlying NSSI risk factors.

Partial correlation network

The mixed graphical model (MGM) was implemented to examine complex relationships among multiple variable types (continuous, ordinal, categorical) within a unified network framework. In this modeling approach, nodes represent measured variables while edges encode conditional dependence relationships between pairs of variables after accounting for all other variables in the network. Model estimation was performed using the MGM package in R. For graph fitting, we utilized a λ-regularization parameter chosen via the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion, with parameters in the MGM function specified as lambdaGam = 0.25 and alphaGam = 0.25 [14, 67]. The underlying estimation process leverages the glmnet package's elastic net regression capabilities [17], which combines L1 (lasso) and L2 (ridge) regularization to generate a coefficient matrix that comprehensively captures multivariate interaction patterns. Centrality indices were estimated to investigate the importance of each node using the R package qgraph. Two representative centrality indices were computed: strength and expected influence. Strength centrality identifies the most important nodes within a network graph. Expected influence (EI) is quantified as the sum of the weights of the edges connected to a node [13, 15]. This measure considers both positive and negative connections between nodes, providing an assessment of each node’s overall influence within the network. Compared to traditional strength centrality, EI offers a more precise method for evaluating node importance, revealing their roles in the network more comprehensively [28]. The higher the EI value of a node, the greater its influence and contribution to the network’s structure and function [49, 66]. To test the accuracy and stability of the networks, nonparametric bootstrapping was used to compute the 95% confidence interval (CI) around each edge, and case-dropping bootstrapping was conducted to compute the correlation stability coefficients (CS-coefficient) of the centrality by the R package bootnet. This indicates the stability coefficient result of the expected impact. A CS value between 0.25 and 0.50 indicates moderate stability, a value between 0.50 and 0.70 indicates good stability, and when CS is greater than 0.70, it indicates excellent stability [13, 15]. Finally, the Network Comparison Test (NCT) was used to determine whether there were significant differences between them [58]. This is of great significance for studying the differences in the network structure of self-harm functions among adolescents of different age groups.

Bayesian network model

This study constructed a Bayesian network, which is a structure that combines a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and probability distribution [7]. The DAG framework encodes conditional independence relationships through d-separation criteria while representing the joint probability distribution across all variables. Although observational data cannot definitively establish causality, DAGs provide a principled method for representing and testing hypothetical causal structures [53], yielding valuable information about both the directionality and strength of variable associations. Model construction utilized the bnlearn package in R with the hill-climbing learning algorithm [36], followed by bootstrap validation (300 iterations) to ensure network stability—only edges demonstrating consistent directionality in ≥ 85% of resamples were retained in the final network [37, 44]. This rigorous approach addresses known challenges in psychological network analysis while providing clinically interpretable results [1, 39]. Notably, the directed edges in DAG represent conditional dependency directions inferred under modeling assumptions, not definitive causal effects. It detects the directions of edges based on conditional dependencies between node pairs, with directionality indicating a causal relationship. That is, the activation of a given node (parent node) predicts the activation of another node (descendant node) [34, 37]. However, the direction here does not imply temporal precedence. Thus, causation should be interpreted with this caveat in mind.

Descriptive statistics

The final sample comprised 1,530 adolescents diagnosed with depression, equally distributed between middle (n = 765) and late (n = 765) adolescence. A significant between-group difference was observed in the prevalence of NSSI (p < 0.05). The rate was notably higher in middle adolescence (76.5%, 585/765) than in late adolescence (63.9%, 489/765).

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, significant differences were observed between the NSSI and non-NSSI groups across both middle and late adolescence in gender distribution, depressive symptoms, social support, alexithymia, childhood trauma, and peer victimization (p < 0.05).

Network estimation

To investigate relationships among gender, psychological factors, and NSSI across developmental stages, we estimated two separate MGMs for middle and late adolescence (Fig. 1a and b, respectively). In these networks, 47.6% (Fig. 1a) and 38.1% (Fig. 1b) of all possible edges were regularized to zero. Subsequent analysis highlighted several key differences in the NSSI-related network structures: In both middle and late adolescence, gender demonstrated direct edges to NSSI within the network. The edge weight between depressive symptoms and NSSI was substantially stronger in middle adolescence (weight = 0.40) than in late adolescence (weight = 0.10). In late adolescence, peer victimization emerged as a notable factor directly associated with NSSI (weight = 0.15), a connection not observed in the middle adolescent network. Here, the weight indicates the regularized partial correlation coefficient.

Centrality, accuracy, and stability

The centrality results are shown in Fig. 2. For expected influence, “non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI)” and “depressive symptoms (PHQ-9)” demonstrated the highest values in both adolescent networks. Stability analyses observed edge (CS) coefficients for both networks (CS middle = 0.672; CS late = 0.672), expected influence (CS middle = 0.672; CS late = 0.595), and strength (CS middle = 0.248; CS late = 0.248). The stability analyses indicated that the expected influence exhibited greater robustness compared to other centrality indices, suggesting it may be a more reliable metric for interpreting the relative importance of nodes within these psychopathological networks. However, the correlation stability coefficient for node strength remained suboptimal. Therefore, the rank ordering of influencing factors based on strength centrality in the current study should be interpreted as exploratory rather than conclusive. The subset self-estimation results of centrality are shown in Figures S1 and S2. The curves in the figures decline slowly, and even after excluding a large number of subjects, the centrality values of the subset and the original data still maintain a high degree of correlation, which means that the centrality estimation can be considered stable (Figure S1-S2). Network Comparison Tests (NCT) revealed a significant difference in the overall network structure between middle and late adolescence (M = 0.16, p = 0.01). However, no significant difference was found in global strength (S = 0.27, p = 0.34).

Bayesian network analysis was used to identify potential causal pathways between multidimensional factors and NSSI across middle and late adolescence (Fig. 3a and b). The primary findings are as follows: The Bayesian network indicated a probabilistic directional association from depressive symptoms to NSSI in middle adolescence. In contrast, during late adolescence, the dominant probabilistic linkage emerged from peer victimization to NSSI. Discussion

This large-scale, multicenter cross-sectional study examined a clinical sample of adolescents with depression in China. The results revealed significant developmental differences in the symptom networks associated with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) between middle (13–15 years) and late adolescence (16–18 years), confirming the primary study hypothesis. These findings underscore the need for developmentally tailored prevention and intervention strategies for NSSI.

First, employing both partial correlation network analysis and Bayesian network modeling, this study demonstrates that the symptom networks associated with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) differ significantly between middle and late adolescence. The Bayesian network identified a probabilistic directional association from depressive symptoms to NSSI in middle adolescence. In contrast, during late adolescence, peer victimization demonstrated a probabilistic linkage to NSSI, a relationship not present in the earlier developmental stage. Notably, the directed edges in DAG represent conditional dependency directions inferred under modeling assumptions, not definitive causal effects. Nevertheless, these inferred relationships carry meaningful implications for clinical research. While previous studies have consistently identified a robust association between depression and NSSI, their longitudinal or developmental relationship does not appear to be synchronous or proportional. A longitudinal clinical study reported that higher baseline depressive symptom severity was concurrently associated with greater NSSI frequency at baseline. However, depressive symptoms did not significantly mediate subsequent NSSI at a 9-month follow-up [20]. Another longitudinal study employing a latent variable cross-lagged analysis of adolescents aged 12–14 years found that depression's predictive effect on NSSI diminished with age and was no longer independent over time. The authors proposed that this attenuation might reflect increasing heterogeneity among adolescents who engage in NSSI as they develop [35]. Conversely, Tilton-Weaver et al. [56] reported that depressive symptoms and NSSI tend to co-emerge and follow parallel trajectories of stabilization or abatement in adolescents aged 12 to 16 [56]. These discrepant findings may be attributable to several factors, including the specific age range studied, methodological approaches, and the variables examined. Unlike previous studies, this study further refined the adolescent developmental stages—a refinement we consider essential given that NSSI is most prevalent during middle adolescence and subsequently declines [18].

The NCT results indicated that although the overall network structure related to NSSI differed significantly between middle and late adolescence, there was no significant change in the global strength of the networks. This finding suggests that the developmental transition from middle to late adolescence may alter the specific etiological pathways to NSSI, shifting from a primary association with internalizing symptoms (particularly depressive affect) in middle adolescence to stronger ties with adverse interpersonal contexts in late adolescence, while the overall connectivity or "activity level" within the psychopathological network remains stable. This may be associated with the biopsychosocial development of adolescents and the dynamic changes in their social environment. Neurodevelopmental research suggested that the development of different brain regions may not be uniform. The impulse reward system was largely completed in early adolescence, while the development of self-regulation ability and the cognitive control system continues until adulthood [52]. The immature neurons in the prefrontal cortex and other cortical and subcortical regions, as well as their interactions, lead to the behavior of adolescents that is inclined towards risk, reward, and emotional responses [54]. Individuals who experience interpersonal life stressors and interpret these stressors as due to internal, global, and stable causes are not only at risk for depressive symptoms, but perhaps also a pattern of stress reactivity that leads to engagement in maladaptive coping behaviors (e.g., NSSI) to deal with overwhelming negative affect [20]. As teenagers grow older, their social relationships and interpersonal needs also change. Social psychology research suggests that the objects of social motivation and the structure of social behavior undergo significant changes during the development of adolescence. Adolescents in senior high school exhibit a pronounced need for peer acceptance [42].

Second, the analysis revealed that alexithymia did not emerge as a central node in the network, and social support was not strongly connected to NSSI. Consistent with prior research, alexithymia partially mediated the relationship between childhood maltreatment and NSSI [50]. While social support significantly attenuated the associations of several other risk factors with NSSI, it did not moderate the relationship between childhood maltreatment and NSSI [55]. These patterns are consistent with our network-based observation that alexithymia and perceived social support likely function as intermediary, rather than core, variables. In both networks, depressive symptoms (PHQ-9) demonstrated the highest expected influence centrality. Unlike unidimensional constructs such as alexithymia or social support, the PHQ-9 total score represents a multidimensional aggregate, encompassing symptoms across affective, cognitive, somatic, and behavioral domains (e.g., depressed mood, sleep disturbance, appetite changes). The observed centrality of depressive symptoms (PHQ-9) may be partially attributable to the inherent multidimensionality of the construct it measures.

Furthermore, gender emerged as a common correlate of NSSI in both networks. The prevalence of NSSI was significantly higher among adolescent females than among their male counterparts. This was consistent with the results of some previous studies [6, 8]. The higher incidence of NSSI among women may be the result of multiple factors working together. Females may stem in part from superior recognition and sensitivity to the negative emotions of others [22]. Hormone differences between men and women (e.g., testosterone versus estradiol) influence gendered engagement in NSSI [6]. This finding warrants further investigation in future studies.

Finally, childhood trauma was the putative upstream factor of NSSI, which connected peer victimization and social support. A previous network analysis study found that NSSI, depression, and childhood trauma in teenagers were closely related. Individuals who have suffered emotional abuse in childhood were more likely to have depressive symptoms and NSSI [29]. That study was conducted on a community sample and did not comprehensively examine the influence of key factors such as gender, developmental stage, and interpersonal relationships on the network structure. A critical first step in preventing NSSI and its adverse outcomes is to recognize the interrelated etiological mechanisms linking childhood trauma to this behavior.

Network analyses examining relationships among anxiety, depression, and academic burnout across early, middle, and late adolescence have revealed stage-specific central symptoms, indicating a need for developmentally targeted interventions [73]. Recognizing distinct adolescents’ needs, interventions should be tailored to effectively address the unique challenges at different developmental stages [19, 25]. In both outpatient and inpatient settings, clinicians should closely monitor the negative emotional states associated with NSSI. For middle adolescence, integrating evidence-based psychotherapies that target these internalizing processes into treatment plans is recommended, such as Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) [11, 57]. For late adolescence, interventions should prioritize psychosocial development by supporting youth in constructively identifying and managing interpersonal conflicts [59]. This process is best facilitated through enhanced collaboration among families, schools, and clinical teams.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged: First, although the DAG provides insights into potential causal pathways, the cross-sectional nature of the data necessitates caution in drawing causal conclusions. Longitudinal designs are required to robustly examine the temporal and causal relationships among factors influencing NSSI. Second, the sample was restricted to adolescents diagnosed with a depressive disorder; individuals with other primary psychiatric conditions were excluded. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings to broader adolescent populations or those with different clinical presentations may be limited. Third, given the practical constraints inherent in clinical research, this study focused on a defined set of common psychosocial variables (e.g., CTQ, TAS, MPVS, MSPSS). Other relevant constructs (such as emotion regulation, impulsivity, and family functioning) were not assessed, representing a limitation in scope. Future research integrating multimodal assessment is warranted to establish a more comprehensive etiological model of NSSI. 5) The Cronbach’s alpha of the CTQ was 0.656, which is marginally below the conventional threshold of 0.70, indicating acceptable but suboptimal internal consistency. Consequently, findings related to childhood trauma should be interpreted with due caution.

Conclusion

In summary, network analysis revealed distinct symptom networks associated with NSSI between middle and late adolescence. Future research should prioritize the specific influence of adolescence as a dynamic developmental period on the etiology and maintenance of NSSI. This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of NSSI in clinical adolescent populations and offers implications for refining prevention strategies and treatment targets.
