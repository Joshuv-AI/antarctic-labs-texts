Introduction

The WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted by the 78th World Health Assembly on 20 May 2025. Under Article 33, the Pandemic Agreement will enter into force 30 days after the sixtieth nation’s ratification.1 The critical question now is whether the Pandemic Agreement can ensure that, when the next pandemic strikes, vaccines and other essential health technologies reach everyone, everywhere, without devastating delays.

During COVID-19, significant vaccine access disparities resurfaced: high-income countries (HICs) secured accelerated supplies, while low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) faced prolonged shortages.2 These LMICs struggled, hampered not only by limited financial resources but also by the complex intellectual property (IP) landscape, primarily governed by the World Trade Organization (WTO)’s Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) Agreement.3

Effective local production of complex health technologies including vaccines faces a dual IP barrier. First, patents on the core technology can be addressed through IP flexibilities under the TRIPS Agreement, such as compulsory or government use licences. The second equally critical barrier is trade secrets—proprietary manufacturing know-how that includes data on cell lines, purification methods and production processes. Unlike patents, which are published as part of the granting process, trade secrets are by definition undisclosed. Consequently, trade secrets cannot be overcome by a compulsory licence, which functions by authorising the use of publicly available patent specifications. For complex biologics, a patent licence without access to the associated know-how is often insufficient for production. Therefore, any effective solution must address both patents, through TRIPS flexibilities, and trade secrets, through mechanisms that ensure technology transfer.

Trade Secrets and Technology Transfer (Pandemic Agreement Article 11)

Article 11 promotes non-exclusive licensing, transparency in agreements, reasonable royalties and technology transfer to manufacturers in LMICs. Yet Article 11 requires technology transfer solely on ‘mutually agreed’ voluntary terms,1 which risks preserving the status quo whenever rights holders decline to cooperate. History shows that legal affirmations alone are insufficient without safeguards against the refusal of rights holders to transfer necessary technology. To facilitate vaccine production in LMICs, the Pandemic Agreement should provide for mandatory technology transfer, potentially via the Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) system’s benefit-sharing provisions.

IP flexibilities under the TRIPS Agreement (Pandemic Agreement Article 11 para 4)

Paragraph 4 of Article 11 reaffirms the right of WTO members to use IP flexibilities, including compulsory licences and government use licences, under the TRIPS Agreement. The Pandemic Agreement’s reaffirmation of TRIPS flexibilities acknowledges the availability of these tools but does not directly address the practical hurdles to their implementation. To enable wider use of compulsory licensing, the Pandemic Agreement will need a legally binding non-challenge provision modelled on WTO precedent, so that countries can withstand legal and political pressure.

The 2001 Doha Declaration on the TRIPS Agreement and Public Health affirmed that trade rules should not hinder public health measures.4 Paragraph 4 of the Doha Declaration was explicit: ‘The TRIPS Agreement does not and should not prevent members from taking measures to protect public health’.4 It explicitly endorsed compulsory licensing, which allows governments to authorise generic production of patented medicines without the patent holder’s consent, typically on payment of a royalty.

The experience of India illustrates both the potential of compulsory licensing for improving access and the political challenges that can arise. In March 2012, facing a prohibitively priced cancer medicine (sorafenib tosylate), India’s Patent Controller issued the country’s first-ever compulsory licence to a domestic manufacturer.5 The multinational pharmaceutical company, which holds the patent, had been charging approximately ₹280,000 per month (around US$5,000–US$5,500) for the medicine in India—far beyond the reach of eligible patients. The generic version of the medicine was priced at about ₹8,800 per month (roughly US$170), a 97% reduction. The compulsory licence was granted under Section 84(1) of India’s Patents Act, on the grounds that the pricing and limited distribution failed to meet the reasonable requirements of the public. The Indian generic manufacturer was required to pay the patent holder a modest royalty (6% of net sales) as compensation. The result was a dramatic improvement in affordability and availability of the life-saving drug in India.6 7 The patent holder launched multiple legal appeals to try to overturn the compulsory licence, but the compulsory licence was ultimately upheld by the Indian judiciary, culminating in the Supreme Court of India dismissing the final appeal in 2014.8

Nevertheless, the intense backlash from the multinational pharmaceutical company, industry groups and some HIC governments, citing concerns about investment, exemplified a ‘chilling effect’. This created a political climate that discouraged further use of compulsory licences.9 10 As of July 2025, India had not issued any further compulsory licences for pharmaceutical products. The primary counter-argument from the pharmaceutical industry is that compulsory licensing stifles innovation. This position, however, overlooks the substantial public investment that underpins much pharmaceutical research and development (R&D).2 Furthermore, the often-claimed chilling effect on innovation appears to be less significant than the documented chilling effect of trade and political pressure, which deters states from implementing legitimate public health measures.9

This chilling effect has systemic consequences. India, for instance, is a key supplier of affordable generics to LMICs,11 partly because Section 3(d) of its Patents Act prevents patent ‘evergreening’ (extending patents through minor modifications) and thus supports a robust generic industry.12 However, when India exports these generics to other LMICs where the products are patented, those importing countries face the same barriers. To enable local supply, they must issue their own compulsory licences. This process is frequently obstructed by trade pressures.

This political climate had a clear impact during the recent pandemic. Despite widespread calls for equitable access, no country issued a compulsory or government use licence for any COVID-19 vaccine.13 The few licences that were issued were for COVID-19 therapeutics: compulsory licences in Russia and Hungary (for remdesivir) and government use licences in Israel (for lopinavir/ritonavir) and Indonesia (for remdesivir and favipiravir).13

Non-challenge provision of WTO Ministerial Decision

A non-challenge provision could encourage both compulsory licensing and mandatory technology transfer. A critical precedent is the WTO Ministerial Decision adopted in June 2022, which provides developing countries with a limited patent waiver for COVID-19 vaccines.14 Although this WTO Ministerial Decision excluded therapeutics and diagnostics, Paragraph 7 of the Decision introduced a non-challenge clause. Under this clause, WTO members agreed not to bring specific trade complaints against countries that authorised patent waivers for COVID-19 vaccines.15 Notably, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) allows member states to challenge measures impairing expected trade benefits (GATT, Articles XXIII:1(b) and (c)). This non-challenge clause provides legal certainty by shielding countries from WTO disputes when they prioritise public health. A nation authorising a patent waiver for a COVID-19 vaccine under this WTO Decision is shielded from specific WTO disputes alleging that the action nullified or impaired expected trade benefits under the GATT.

The Pandemic Agreement affirms respect for TRIPS flexibilities including compulsory licensing (Article 11(4)). To truly encourage the use of compulsory licensing, the Pandemic Agreement should have a similarly robust and legally binding non-challenge provision, which could protect countries against WTO trade complaints and other political pressures. A non-challenge provision would provide LMICs with the legal certainty to invoke compulsory or government use licences during future pandemics without fear of retaliatory WTO disputes. Such a provision would be critical for addressing the chilling effect observed historically and, if mechanisms for mandatory technology transfer are adopted, could also shield those mandates from legal challenge. An alternative solution is that the WTO adopt another Ministerial Declaration to prohibit challenges to any measures taken in conformity with the WHO Pandemic Agreement.

PABS (Pandemic Agreement Article 12)

Under Article 31(2), the adopted text of the WHO Pandemic Agreement will be opened for signature only after the completion of negotiations for the PABS Annex.1 Article 12 establishes the PABS System, mandating that participating manufacturers provide, for distribution through a WHO-coordinated mechanism, rapid access to at least 20% of real-time production of vaccines, therapeutics and diagnostics—with at least 10% donated and the rest at affordable prices for LMICs. According to Paragraph 2 of Article 12, the detailed provisions governing the PABS System, including definitions, modalities, legal nature, terms and conditions, will be agreed in an Annex to the Pandemic Agreement. This PABS Annex is scheduled for completion at the 79th World Health Assembly in May 2026.

Conclusions

With the adoption of the main Pandemic Agreement text, the ongoing PABS Annex negotiations represent the principal remaining avenue for incorporating such binding provisions. Without this step, countries could again face the kind of delays and inequities witnessed during COVID-19. COVID-19 revealed that, despite substantial public funding, vaccines could become instruments of nationalist competition rather than global public goods.16 The Pandemic Agreement presents an opportunity to establish mechanisms for enforceable equity. Embedding a non-challenge provision and mandating technology transfer in the PABS Annex would empower LMICs, foster geographically diverse manufacturing and enhance global health security. The real work continues in the PABS Annex negotiations.17
