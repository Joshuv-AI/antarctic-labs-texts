Assisted leg cycle exercise for wheelchair users with muscular dystrophy
Authors: Nanna S Poulsen, Jonas J Loft, Rebecca K Andersen, John Vissing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWheelchair users with muscular dystrophy face health challenges from their condition, sedentary lifestyle, and wheelchair use. While exercise benefits are well-documented in healthy individuals, its effects in wheelchair users with muscular dystrophy are underexplored: low muscle mass might limit exercise benefits. This study investigates benefits and adverse events of assisted leg cycling to improve fatigue, pain, constipation, and quality of life in wheelchair users with muscular dystrophy.MethodsWheelchair users with muscular dystrophy were tested at baseline, after a 10-week control period, and after 10 weeks of assisted leg cycling (20 minutes, 3 times/week). Efficacy outcomes were changes in fatigue, lower back/leg pain, constipation, and quality of life. Secondary outcomes included changes in exercise performance, cardiovascular response, and metabolic health. A 1.5-year follow-up of voluntary training evaluated long-term effects.ResultsNineteen participants enrolled; two dropped out. Lower back pain and fatigue decreased and physical quality of life improved. Constipation did not change overall but improved in more than half of participants. HbA1c decreased by 2 mmol/mol, mainly in two participants with diabetes. Blood lipids, leg pain, cycling time, and power output showed no changes. Benefits persisted or improved over 1.5 years in participants with a high adherence.DiscussionAssisted cycling may reduce lower back pain, improve quality of life, and possibly decrease fatigue and constipation in wheelchair users with muscular dystrophy. Furthermore, exercise likely lowers HbA1c, particularly in individuals with diabetes. These findings suggest that assisted cycling is a feasible health intervention for wheelchair users with muscular dystrophy.Keywords: exercise, muscular dystrophy, wheelchair, quality of life, pain

1) Introduction

Muscular dystrophy (MD) comprises genetic disorders marked by muscle degeneration and replacement with fat and fibrous tissue. 1 Phenotypes vary from mild to severe with some patients requiring wheelchairs from early childhood. At Copenhagen Neuromuscular Center, approximately 10% of patients with MD are wheelchair users. This group commonly experience both disease- and immobility-related symptoms, including muscle pain (especially in the lower back and legs),2,3 fatigue,4,5 constipation,6,7 and an increased risk of lifestyle disorders, 8 often affecting the quality of life (QoL). 9

There is no cure for most patients with MD. Emerging therapies like gene therapy are unlikely to benefit those in advanced stages, who must rely on supportive interventions to manage symptoms and maintain health. Exercise could be one such option. In healthy individuals, exercise improves fatigue, 10 constipation, 11 pain, 12 cardiometabolic health, 13 muscle function, and QoL. 14 While exercise is well-studied in ambulatory patients with MD, 15 it remains underexplored in wheelchair users, where prior research has mainly focused on masticatory and respiratory muscles.16–24 No studies have evaluated systemic exercise effects in this population. Barriers may include assumptions that interventions are too late or technical exercise challenges (e.g., inability to operate equipment independently). This highlights the need to explore feasible, home-based exercise options.

We investigated the effects and potential harms of assisted cycling in wheelchair users with MD, hypothesising improvements in pain, fatigue, constipation, QoL, and cardiometabolic markers.

2) Methods

2.1 Participants

Participants were recruited from Copenhagen Neuromuscular Center, Rigshospitalet, Denmark between June 2021 and December 2022. The exercise periods were initiated between September 2021 and March 2023.

Inclusion criteria: 1) age >18 years, 2) genetically verified diagnosis of MD, 3) wheelchair use with a maximum assisted walking distance of ≤10 metres (Expanding Disability Status Scale (EDSS) level 6.5-8), and 4) meeting at least two of the following:

i. Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) score ≥ 4/7

i. Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) score ≥ 4/7

ii. Lower back pain rating scale (LBPRS) score ≥ 12/60

ii. Lower back pain rating scale (LBPRS) score ≥ 12/60

iii. Constipation questionnaire score ≥ 2/4

iii. Constipation questionnaire score ≥ 2/4

iv. Quality of Life in genetic Neuromuscular Diseases (QoL-gNMD) score ≤ 60/75

Exclusion criteria: 1) physical contraindications to exercise testing, 2) ongoing psychiatric/psychologic treatment (not to influence outcomes on QoL), 3) habitual exercise >2 hours/week, 4) pregnancy, and 5) inability to understand study procedures.

2.2 Study design

The study was a self-controlled, unblinded, exploratory, cross-over study with a fixed sequence.

Participants were assessed at baseline (pre-tests), after a 10-week control period (mid-test), and after a 10-week exercise period (post-test) (see Figure 1). Post-test sessions were scheduled within one week from last exercise session. After intervention, participants could keep the ergometer for 1.5 years to continue exercising independently.

Study design. Participants were tested before and after a control period and after an exercise period. The participants were invited to exercise for an additional 1.5 years and were tested again afterwards. Tests included questionnaires on fatigue, pain, quality of life, and constipation, a timed, assisted 1-km leg cycle exercise test, and blood samples for lipids and HbA1c. A blood sample was taken midway through the exercise period and analysed for CK. Every second week in the exercise period, the participants answered questions in the home diary and talked to the investigators.

Participants were instructed to maintain their usual physical activity and diet throughout the study.

2.2.1 Exercise intervention

Exercise equipment: Participants performed assisted leg cycling sessions using a motor-assisted cycle ergometer (Thera-Trainer Tigo, Teramed A/S). The ergometer was wheelchair-operable with foot straps and optional calf/thigh supports. Load (watts) and cadence (rpm) were adjustable, and active exercise was calculated as the difference between motor-assisted and actual rpm. Participants received written instructions and a demonstration on how to use the ergometer.

Exercise protocol and individualisation: Participants performed 20 minutes of continuous assisted cycling thrice weekly for 10 weeks at home. Heart rate (HR) was not used to guide intensity since reduced muscle mass may limit the HR response to exercise, making it difficult to define a target intensity applicable to all participants. Instead, the goal was to perform active cycling whenever possible, while motor assistance allowed passive cycling when fatigue limited voluntary effort. Load and cadence were individually adapted to each participant; thus, training intensity was individualised according to tolerance and functional status.

Monitoring and progression: Power output (watts), calories burned, and distance were displayed continuously on the ergometer screen. Participants logged session details and adverse events in a home diary, including photos of the ergometer screen showing distance and calories burned. Every two weeks, they consulted with investigators (medical doctors or trained medical students) to review training progress and ensure safety by adjusting load, intensity, and training intervals based on tolerance, adverse events, and functional status.

2.2.2 Outcomes

Due to limited prior research, four patient-reported efficacy outcomes were selected as co-primary outcomes: fatigue, lower back and leg pain, constipation, and QoL assessed with questionnaires. Exercise was deemed beneficial if at least one efficacy outcome improved, and the number of improved outcomes exceeded worsened outcomes.

Secondary outcomes were changes in exercise performance, cardiovascular response, and metabolic health, measured consistently at each visit, sometimes over two days to minimise fatigue.

Exercise performance and cardiovascular response

A timed 1-km assisted cycle test was performed. The test corresponds to the assisted 6-minute cycle test. 26 A timed test was chosen since the ergometer measured time changes down to 1 ms but could only detect distance changes of 100 metres.

The motor assistance was set at 25 rpm, and time to bike 1 km was measured. Stronger participants could exceed 25 rpm and generate watts. A passive test would not exceed 25 rpm or produce watts and would take 08:07 minutes to complete. The participants who were able to bike against resistance, performed the test against a fixed resistance, that was kept constant between tests. HR, Borg scores, watts and rpm were recorded continuously, and the total kcal burned was noted. Outcomes were time to finish, maximal power output (watts), and maximal cadence (rpm). To minimise the learning effect, participants familiarised themselves with the ergometer before the pre-test and repeated the test twice at pre- and mid-test. The best test at pre-test and mid-test was chosen for analysis.

Metabolic response

Blood samples assessed cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and haemoglobin A1C (HbA1c). Creatine kinase (CK) was analysed as a safety measure.

2.4 1.5-year follow-up

Following the 10-week exercise intervention, participants continued exercising without imposed frequency or intensity requirements for 1.5 years. Those who accepted were retested. Feasibility and adherence data were collected through diaries and final visit interviews.

2.5 Statistics

2.5.1 Sample size calculation

No previous study has investigated assisted cycle exercise in patients with muscular dystrophy using the outcomes of present study. Therefore, to provide a reasonable estimate of a sample size, we based our calculation on a former study on patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy doing assisted cycle exercise, in which changes in Motor Function Measure 32 (MFM32) was used as outcome. 28 Although MFM32 does not directly correspond to our outcomes, this study provided the closest available estimate of effect size. The Rstudio package “pwr” was used, with a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 0.8. The effect size (d) was calculated from a mean effect of 4.9 with a confidence interval 2.2-7.6. This resulted in a required sample size of 18 participants.

2.5.2 Descriptive statistics

Analyses were conducted in R (v4.1.0) and Rstudio (v2021.05.18) with the packages ggplot2, dplyr, tidyr, magrittr, Rmisc, and rstatix. Significance was set at p<0.05.

To minimise floor and ceiling effects, effects on efficacy outcomes were restricted to participants with a baseline score above the ones described in the inclusion criteria. Participants with baseline scores below these thresholds were evaluated descriptively to identify any potential worsening. This approach ensured that all collected data were considered while allowing that meaningful improvements could be detected.

Normality was tested via Shapiro-Wilk; sphericity by Mauchly’s Test. Repeated measures ANOVA (with pairwise t-test as post-hoc) or Friedman’s test (with pairwise Wilcoxon as post-hoc) were applied depending on whether assumptions were met. Friedman’s test was also applied to ordinal data (constipation and fatigue). Hochberg’s correction adjusted for multiple testing for post-hoc tests and QoL parameters (five tests). Median and 95 % CI were reported with bootstrapping for non-parametric tests. Missing data were handled with pairwise deletion. Linear mixed models with maximum likelihood confirmed findings, so paired tests were reported due to small sample size advantages.

Linear regression explored correlations between disease severity (measured as 1-km cycling time) and efficacy outcomes. Changes in distance and calories burned during exercise were analysed by paired t-test between first and sixteenth sessions (only sixteen were documented by all), with subgroup analysis by cycling speed (above or below 8:00 min).

The study protocol, changes to the protocol, and statistical analysis plan are available in eSAP 1-3.

3) Results

3.1 Participants

Recruitment and retention: We invited 69 individuals for participation: 25 did not respond, 19 declined, 6 met exclusion criteria, and 19 were included. Reasons for declining included an unstable team of personal assistance, acute heart failure, low energy or interest, moving abroad, and participation in other studies. Exclusion criteria applied to the invited individuals: pregnancy (one individual), high motor function (two individuals), excessive physical activity (two individuals), and unmet questionnaire criteria (one individual). Two of the 19 included participants dropped out: one in the control period due to accidental death unrelated to the study and one due to ankle contractures preventing ergometer use. One participated without blood samples.

Demographics: Of the 19 included participants, six were women, 13 men, representing five types of MD (see Table 2). The median age was 36 years (Sd 16, range 20-72) and the mean weight 72 kg (SD 18, range 35-105).

3.3 Effect on pain, constipation, fatigue, and quality of life

At pre-test (baseline, n=19), the median score on the FSS was 5.2, total QoL score 46, lower back pain 12, leg pain 12, and constipation 2.

As described in the statistical section, efficacy analyses were restricted to participants with baseline scores above the predefined thresholds to minimise floor and ceiling effects. Participants below these thresholds were analysed separately to identify any potential worsening. Only two showed minor increase in fatigue or leg pain, and no systematic deterioration was observed.

3.4.2 Metabolic health

HbA1c decreased from pre- to post-test (median -2, 95% CI: -3;-1, p=0.048, n=16; Figure 3(c)), mainly driven by three participants with type 2 diabetes (DM2). Two lowered their HbA1c from elevated to normal (decrease 10 mmol/mol and 4 mmol/mol), the third reduced insulin and metformin intake after four weeks of exercise due to low nightly blood glucose levels. No change in HbA1C was observed when excluding participants with DM2 from the analysis. Effects were sustained in the 1.5-year follow-up.

Five participants (31%) showed blood lipids out of the normal range. No changes in blood lipid profiles were seen after exercise (p-values ranged from 0.053-0.744, n=16; Figure 3(d)).

4) Discussion

Effects of exercise in patients with advanced-stage muscle disease

Our study shows that even patients with advanced-stage MD can benefit from exercise. With 20 minutes of assisted cycling three times weekly, participants experienced reduced lower back pain, improved QoL, and likely better glycaemic control in patients with DM2, reduced fatigue, and constipation. Adherence was high, with most wanting to continue exercising for 1.5 years.

Although average effects were modest, some participants showed significant improvements – for example, one participant reduced lower back pain dramatically and reported a clear link between exercise and pain relief at the 1.5-year follow-up. This variability aligns with previous exercise studies,29,30 with some showing no benefits in up to 50 % of healthy individuals. 31 Of the six participants in our study who did not respond to the efficacy outcomes after 10 weeks, two improved in following 1.5 years, and another two reported effects on other parameters.

Constipation just missed statistical significance, potentially due to the few participants experiencing constipation (n=10). Notably, most participants reported improvements, with some reporting an immediate relief (requiring a bathroom visit after tests). Only two participants showed minor increase in fatigue or leg pain, indicating that the intervention did not cause systematic worsening.

Interestingly, all participants increased their HR during exercise, suggesting potential cardiovascular benefits despite limited muscle mass. The HR rise (∼41 bpm) is unlikely to be achieved through daily activities, though the clinical impact requires further study.

Glucose control improved in the three participants with DM2, with medication reduction in one and a HbA1c reduction by 10 and 4 mmol/mol in the other two. In other populations, an absolute reduction of 11 mmol/mol in HbA1c is associated with an 18 % reduction in major cardiovascular disease events and a 28 % reduction in peripheral arterial disease in patients with DM2,32,33 an effect that this study suggests also can be obtained by patients with advanced-stage.

Active versus passive exercise

Passive exercise has been shown to be beneficial in previous studies.34,35 Some outcomes in our study could be gained from passive exercise: lower back pain and constipation could be reduced by movement of back and stomach and the QoL may improve through a changed self-view as an active person. Nevertheless, active exercise shows more benefits. We hoped the assisted cycle ergometer could enable active exercise for wheelchair users with MD. Of the 19 included participants, 13 (68 %) completed the cycle test faster than 8 minutes (passive test time: 8:07 minutes). The last six participants biked at 2-3 watts, raising questions about whether these watts were from active exercise or leg weight. Testing with five kg sandbags on each pedal showed no increase in watts, suggesting no or minimal impact from leg weight. Thus, it seems these participants likely generated some power themselves.

Exercise intensity

We observed a discrepancy between participants’ cardiac response and perceived exertion during a cycle test. While the average peak HR reached only 68 % of their predicted maximum – indicative of moderate-intensity exercise (low end) 36 – the average maximum Borg score was 16, reflecting vigorous perceived intensity. 37 This may suggest that perceived exertion is influenced more by muscle fatigue than cardiovascular load. Therefore, the choice of outcome measure used to plan and monitor exercise intensity should align with the exercise goal: HR for cardiovascular benefits, and the Borg score for muscle strengthening.

Muscle mass and response to exercise

Former studies have shown that exercise can improve extremity, masticatory, and respiratory muscle strength in wheelchair users with neuromuscular diseases. 24 In contrast, it has been suggested that individuals with less than 15 % of the normal muscle strength might not benefit from strength training. 38

This raises the question of whether the muscle mass can be too small to obtain benefits from exercise. In our study, all participants were severely affected (mean maximal watt: 8), reflecting a low muscle mass, yet benefits were observed on the primary outcomes. Only two of the six most affected participants (Table 2, participants 7 and 12) did not respond, likely reflecting advanced disease stage, age, or adherence. Notably, the remaining four improved.

However, when considering changes in power output, a different pattern emerged: all participants who improved at post-test were able to generate at least 10 watts at baseline. This suggests that improvements in physical performance may require a minimum level of preserved muscle function.

Altogether, these findings indicate that the threshold for benefiting from exercise might be lower than previously assumed, but likely depends on the outcome. While improvements in power output may require a certain level of muscle mass, benefits in pain, constipation, fatigue, and quality of life may be achievable even in individuals with very limited muscle function.

The 1-km cycle test, HbA1C, and lipids as outcome measures for advanced-stage muscle diseases

Outcome measures in exercise studies for advanced-stage MDs are challenging to find. Muscle strength is difficult to assess since patients are too weak to use a dynamometer and often have too many contractures for correct manual muscle testing. Most fitness measures like VO2max are not applicable since wheelchair users with MD cannot reach maximum HR through exercise. We introduced a timed 1-km assisted cycle test measuring maximum cadence, maximum power output, and time changes. However, no improvements were seen in these outcomes, likely due to ceiling and floor effects. Thus, the 1-km assisted cycle test is challenging to use for assessing physical improvement in advanced stages of MD. Blood-HbA1c decreased significantly with exercise, suggesting its potential as an outcome measure, especially for participants also having DM2. Blood lipids showed no change, likely due to the low number of participants with abnormal levels and the low exercise intensity, which might not have been sufficient to impact the lipids.39,40

Limitations

Although 19 participants were recruited, which is a relatively high number compared to other exercise studies in advanced-stage muscular dystrophy, 41 only 17 completed the exercise period, resulting in limited statistical power. This may partly explain why some outcomes, such as fatigue, did not reach statistical significance despite a consistent reduction in mean scores from pre- to post-test.

The placebo effect might have influenced perception of pain and QoL. While a passive or no-exercise control group could reduce the risk, blinding is not feasible in exercise studies, and passive exercise might still offer benefits. Instead, we included a control period. Despite the placebo risk, the changes in HbA1c and HR during exercise likely reflect true physiological effects.

Participants were heterogenous regarding disease type and need for mechanical ventilation. Due to the rarity of muscular dystrophies and the additional inclusion criterion of wheelchair dependency, it was not possible to recruit enough participants with a single diagnosis. Therefore, we prioritized functional homogeneity and larger sample size above clinical homogeneity. Most participants showed improvements regardless of disease type, suggesting that benefits may be generalisable across wheelchair users with different muscular dystrophies.

Requirement for mechanical ventilation might further influence exercise response on motor outcomes by limiting achievable exercise intensity. However, ventilators were dynamic, meaning that participants received additional support as needed, ensuring adequate ventilation during exercise. Participants requiring invasive ventilation did not improve in distance or power but were also among the weakest participants. Some weak participants who breathed without invasive support also did not improve in distance or power. Together these findings suggest that while invasive ventilation might influence exercise response, baseline muscle function is likely the primary determinant.

Finaly, despite efforts to minimise the learning effect with repeated cycle tests at pre- and mid-tests, this might have influenced distance and calories during the exercise period.

Adverse events

Adverse events to exercise are frequently ignored in research and often not systematically reported. We have collected and reported adverse events using broad questions, study-specific queries, observations, and CK analysis. While CK is often used as a safety marker in exercise studies of ambulant patients, its reliability in advanced-stage muscle diseases is unclear since low muscle mass might limit CK elevation. Nonetheless, CK remained unchanged.

The adverse events were mild, temporary, and expected, with fatigue and myalgia being most common, especially at the beginning of the exercise period, diminishing over time. However, one participant reported long-lasting, function-limiting fatigue when exercising more than 20 minutes. A few others with full-time work/studies and/or family commitments reported a lack of energy for more intense active exercise, still preferring low-intensity or passive exercise over doing nothing. Overall, wheelchair users with MD can exercise regularly without frequent severe adverse events, though they might be more prone to fatigue and should be more cautious with duration- and intensity increments.

Suggestions for future research

More research is needed on exercise in advanced-stage muscle diseases. Appropriate outcome measures are required to assess strength, fitness, and rehabilitation goals. These measures must differ from those used in ambulant patients, considering the differences in physiological response to exercise testing, the low muscle mass preventing use of standard equipment, and different rehabilitation goals. We propose a new set of outcome measures, centralising patient-reported pain, fatigue, constipation, and QoL.

Additionally, many participants reported better sleep quality and breathing after exercise. While these were not measured, they might improve with cycle exercise and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

We investigated effects of exercise in a patient population thought not to benefit from exercise. The study showed that wheelchair users with advanced-stage muscular dystrophy also benefit from exercise and can experience improvements in pain and quality of life, and probably glycaemic control, fatigue and constipation.
