CapeOX as neoadjuvant chemotherapy for locally advanced rectal cancer: might less be more?
Authors: Tianan Guo, Kai Liu, Yang Guo, Huan Zhang, Zhonglin Zhu, Dan Huang, Sanjun Cai, Tong Tong, Xiaoyan Zhou, Ye Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLocally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) poses unique challenges in treatment, with current neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NA-CRT) showing limitations. The CapeOX regimen emerges as a potential less aggressive neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) for LARC.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study involving treatment-naïve patients with LARC from March 2014 to March 2021 who received 2–4 cycles of CapeOX NAC followed by radical surgery. Treatment response was evaluated using tumor regression grade (TRG), MRI-based TRG (MRI-TRG), and Neoadjuvant Rectal (NAR) score.Results94.7% of patients experienced symptom improvement and 96.4% achieved sphincter-preserving surgery. Post-NAC showed significant tumor regression and MRI confirmed a tumor length reduction (P < 0.001). Clinical and pathological staging discrepancies post-NAC suggest broader therapeutic advantages. 5-year overall and disease-free survival rates were 78.4% and 73.4%. NAR scores provided better prognostic accuracy than MRI-TRG.ConclusionCapeOX NAC presents notable benefits for LARC patients and its clinical significance may be underestimated. The NAR score demonstrates superior prognostic value over MRI-TRG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-12972-6.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Due to the unique anatomical structure of the rectum, locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) is more difficult to operate on compared to colon cancer, with a higher local recurrence rate and poorer prognosis [2, 3]. These distinct characteristics, coupled with LARC’s inclination for local invasion and metastasis, underscore the necessity for effective and manageable treatment strategies to optimize patient outcomes. Currently, the standard neoadjuvant treatment for LARC is neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NA-CRT), lauded for its remarkably low local recurrence rates and the relatively high rates of pathologic complete response (pCR) it yields [4–6]. However, NA-CRT doesn’t improve DFS or OS compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), and it brings increased postoperative morbidity and certain radiotherapy-related toxicities [2, 7–14]. For lower rectal cancer patients who cannot undergo anal sphincter preserving operation, intensified treatments (e.g., chemo-radiotherapy or immunotherapy) and longer treatment duration (e.g., total neoadjuvant therapy, TNT) can improve clinical complete response rates, possibly circumventing surgery [15, 16]. However, these come at the cost of exacerbated treatment expenses and chemo-radiotherapy-related toxicities. Whether such high-intensity neoadjuvant treatment is appropriate for median or high rectal cancer patients who can undergo anal sphincter preserving operation is worth pondering.

Meanwhile, there has been a growing interest in investigating the potential of NAC as a less aggressive, alternative treatment regimen for LARC [2, 7, 17–19]. Recent literature has suggested that NAC, in comparison to NA-CRT, demonstrates comparable efficacy in terms of local recurrence and long-term survival rates, with the advantage of fewer toxicity profiles and better preservation of anal function [2, 7]. Nonetheless, the pCR rate reported for NAC is lower than that for NA-CRT, raising questions regarding the optimal therapeutic approach for LARC.

The CapeOX regimen, an established therapeutic protocol for CRC, is frequently utilized as an adjuvant therapy due to its demonstrated efficacy and safety [20]. However, its application as a neoadjuvant therapy for LARC is not been extensively studied. Prior research from our group has affirmed the effectiveness and safety of the CapeOX regimen as a neoadjuvant therapy for locally advanced colon cancer [21]. A few studies, such as the PROSPECT, FACT, and FORWAC trials, have also supported the use of standalone chemotherapy as a potentially efficacious treatment approach for LARC [2, 7, 19]. These findings hint at the potential benefits of exploring chemotherapy regimens such as CapeOX as neoadjuvant therapies for LARC. Nonetheless, comprehensive investigations specifically focusing on this area are limited, underpinning the necessity for further in-depth research to authenticate the relevance of these findings in the context of LARC.

This single-center retrospective study sought to assess the efficacy and safety of the CapeOX regimen as a short-course preoperative NAC protocol for locally advanced rectal cancer. In total, we enrolled 110 CRC patients who underwent CapeOX NAC at our institution. Through various evaluation strategies, the findings from this study suggest that CapeOX NAC could efficiently promote tumor regression in LARC, with a subset achieving excellent overall survival and disease-free survival rates. This assessment could potentially provide a foundation for optimizing neoadjuvant treatment strategies for LARC.

Patients and methods

Patients’ inclusion criteria

Between March 2014 and March 2021, a total of 3331 patients with LARC underwent surgical resection in Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center and 110 of them who received 2–4 cycles of NAC using the CapeOX regimen without radiotherapy, were included in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Aged 18–75 years; (2) Diagnosed with T3/4 or N + LARC by MRI or CT before treatment; (3) Treatment-naïve patients who have not undergone local resection or chemoradiation; (4) Underwent only 2–4 cycles of CapeOX NAC without radiotherapy, followed by radical surgery; (5) No simultaneous distant metastasis before NAC. This study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center in China. All patients provided written informed consent for the analyses of their clinicopathological information.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery

All included patients underwent 2–4 cycles of CapeOX chemotherapy, which included Oxaliplatin 130 mg/m2 dissolved in 500 ml 5% glucose solution, intravenous infusion over 2 hours on day 1, and Capecitabine 1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1–14 repeated every 21 days. After 2–4 cycles of NAC, patients were evaluated by enhanced MRI or CT and subsequently underwent radical surgery. Postoperative adjuvant treatment and regular follow-ups were conducted based on pathology reports.

Evaluation method

TRG score

The pathological response to chemotherapy was ascertained by analyzing the primary tumor and lymph node specimens gathered during surgery, independently reviewed by two pathologists. The grading of each primary tumor’s pathological response was performed following the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging criteria. The resultant TRG was based on the presence of residual tumor cells and the degree of fibrosis. The TRG score spanned from 0 to 3, representing various degrees of response: complete response with no residual tumor cells (TRG 0), presence of single cells or small clusters (TRG 1), residual cancer alongside a desmoplastic response (TRG 2), and minimal evidence of tumor response (TRG 3), respectively [22].

MRI-TRG evaluation

All MR images were performed on a 3.0T MR magnet (Signa Horizon, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI) with a phased-array body coil. The protocols mainly comprised sagittal and oblique axial T2WI with the turbo spin‒echo technique and RS-EPI DWI in oblique axial orientation. The detailed parameters of the MRI scanners are presented in Supplementary Table 1. MRI-TRG was used to assess the tumor response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which was developed to evaluate the MRI-based tumor regression grade in a parallel manner to the standard pathological TRG scores [23, 24]. The MRI scoring system included grades 1 to 5, representing a complete response to minimal or no response, respectively. The MRI-TRG was evaluated by analyzing pre- and post-chemotherapy MRI, in an agreement between two radiologists with 8 and 20 years of experience in abdominal MRI who were blind to all patient clinical information.

NAR score

The Neoadjuvant Rectal (NAR) score is calculated using a formula that combines the clinical T stage and pathologic T and N stages (ypT and ypN) [25]. The formula used to calculate the NAR score is as follows: NAR score = [5ypN – 3(cT-ypT) + 12]2/9.61. For ypT, the values of T0, T1, T2, T3, and T4 correspond to 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 respectively. For cT, the values of T1, T2, T3, and T4 correspond to 1, 2, 3, and 4 respectively. For ypN, the values of N0, N1, and N2 correspond to 0, 1, and 2 respectively. The NAR score is then categorized into three risk groups: low (0–8), intermediate (9–16), and high (17–20), with a higher NAR score indicating a poorer prognosis. These categories allow for the stratification of patients based on their risk level, assisting clinicians in making informed decisions regarding subsequent treatments and follow-up strategies.

Chemotherapy toxicity

Chemotherapy toxicity was assessed by meticulously graded adverse events (AEs), following the standardized framework set by the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE version 5.0) [26]. In addition to this objective evaluation, subjective symptoms experienced by patients were also considered, including constipation, diarrhea, and hematochezia. These were primarily evaluated based on the outpatient history records, enabling a more personalized understanding of patient experiences during the treatment process. This two-pronged approach provided an all-encompassing perspective on the toxicity of chemotherapy, ensuring that the impacts on both physical health and patient quality of life were considered.

Data analysis

Comprehensive data were collected on each patient, encompassing a broad spectrum of parameters that included demographic information, details of the chemotherapy regimen, pre- and post-chemotherapy evaluations, surgery information, and pathological data. We then carried out rigorous statistical analyses. For continuous variables, means and standard deviations were computed, and for categorical variables, we calculated percentages among different stages of treatment. Baseline clinical characteristics for each therapy stage were compared using a two-sided Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were compared between two groups by the Wilcoxon test. All statistical analyses with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0), Rstudio v.2022.02.2–485 software.

Results

Baseline characteristics and treatment

A total of 110 patients with rectal cancer who underwent 2–4 cycles of CapeOX NAC without radiotherapy were included in this study. Total mesorectal excision was carried out in all the patients. 90 of them underwent laparoscopic surgery and the other 20 patients underwent open surgery. Their baseline characteristics and treatment details are presented in Table 1. In terms of tumor location, 33.6% of the patients had tumors with lower margins less than 5 cm from the anus, 49.1% were between 5 and 10 cm, with the remaining 17.3% falling within the 10–15 cm range. Of the 76 patients for whom subjective symptoms were recorded, 94.7% (72/76) reported subjective symptom improvement following chemotherapy. Only 2.7% (3/110) patients experienced severe (Grade 3–4) AEs, primarily involving neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Sphincter-preserving surgery was successfully performed in 96.4% (106/110) of patients and R0 resection was achieved in all surgical cases. Only one patient developed postoperative anastomotic leakage and improved after conservative treatment. As to adjuvant treatment, 8 patients received postoperative chemoradiotherapy, 87 patients received XELOX chemotherapy, 6 patients received capecitabine alone, and the other 9 patients didn’t receive adjuvant chemotherapy.

Clinicopathological evaluation and prognosis

Pre- and post-NAC clinicopathological evaluation of enrolled patients are detailed in Tables 2 and 3, respectively, with corresponding associations of T and N stages represented in Fig. 1A and B. Notably, marked tumor regression was observed across all indexes following NAC with statistically significant differences. In terms of tumor markers, 25% (11/44) of CEA elevated patients and 33.3% (7/21) of CA19-9 elevated patients decreased to normal after NAC. Additionally, tumor length measured by MRI after NAC was significantly reduced compared with that before NAC (2.9 ± 1.4 vs. 4.9 ± 1.6, P < 0.001). Increased surgery complexity and worse prognosis are typically observed in patients initially staged as T4, N2, and positive mesorectal fascia (MRF+). Yet, in this study, significant downstaging was observed post-NAC in 64.3% (9/14) of initial cT4 patients, 81.4% (35/43) of initial cN2 patients, and 77.27% (17/22) of cN1 patients. Moreover, 81.8% (9/11) of initially MRF + cases and 41.3% (19/46) of initially extramural vascular invasion positive (EMVI+) cases regressed to MRF- and EMVI- respectively after NAC. 12.7% (14/110) of patients achieved a complete pathological response (TRG = 0) and a major pathological response rate (TRG = 0 or 1) was recorded in 18.2% (20/110) of patients. Meanwhile, a TRG score of 3 was still assigned to 34.5% (38/110) of patients.

Interestingly, discrepancies were observed between the clinical staging post-NAC (ycT and ycN) and the pathological staging post-surgery (ypT and ypN). 53.0% (9/17) of ycT2 cases were stages as ypT0-1, 12% (6/50) of ycT3 cases were staged as ypT1-2, and 33.3% (2/6) ycT4 cases were staged as ypT3 postoperatively. Similarly, 31.8% (7/22) of ycN1 cases and 25% (2/8) of ycN2 cases were staged as ypN0 and ypN1 respectively. These discrepancies suggest that more patients may have benefitted from NAC than initially estimated.

The OS and DFS of 110 patients are shown in Fig. 2. After 2.5 to 9.5 years of follow-up, only 2 cases of local recurrence and 21 cases of distant metastasis were observed in the 110 cases, including 2 cases of anastomotic recurrence, 4 cases of liver metastasis, 8 cases of pulmonary metastasis, 1 case of osseous metastasis and 8 cases of multiple metastases. Additionally, only 3 patients died due to a second primary tumor or COVID-19. The 5-year OS and 5-year DFS of 110 patients were 78.4% (0.681–0.902) and 73.4% (0.646–0.834) respectively.

NAR score and MR-TRG

The consistency of NAR scores, MR-TRG, and pathological TRG of enrolled patients was shown in Fig. 3A. DFS and OS of patients with different NAR scores and MR-TRG groups are shown in Fig. 3B. There were significant statistical differences in DFS and OS of patients with high and median/low NAR scores (P < 0.01). Statistical difference was also observed between MRI-TRG 1–3 cases and MRI-TRG 4–5 cases in DFS (P < 0.05) but not in OS (P > 0.05). In this study, NAR score seems to have an advantage over MRI-TRG in predicting prognosis.

Discussion

In recent years, NAC has been reported as an alternative to NA-CRT in LARC patients [2, 7, 19, 27]. This study is a single-center retrospective study that investigated the efficacy and safety of CapeOX regimen as a short-course neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen for LARC to explore whether LARC patients could benefit more from relatively less neoadjuvant treatment.

NA-CRT is the standard treatment for LARC, lauded for its remarkably low local recurrence rates and the relatively high rates of pCR it yields [4–6]. However, NA-CRT is less effective in controlling distant metastasis and improving DFS and OS, and is associated with increased postoperative complication rate and radiotherapy-related toxicities [8–14]. It is commonly believed that there was no significant difference in local recurrence rate and long-term survival rate between NAC and NA-CRT groups, with NAC being associated with less toxicities and better preservation of anal function [2, 7, 17]. However, it’s important to note that the pCR rate in the NAC group was lower compared to the NA-CRT group [2, 7]. In cases of low rectal cancer where anal preservation surgery isn’t feasible, more aggressive treatments such as chemo-radiotherapy or immunotherapy, and extended treatment duration as seen in TNT, can enhance complete clinical response (cCR) rates, potentially obviating the need for surgery [4–6, 28–31]. Nevertheless, these approaches entail heightened chemo-radiotherapy-related side effects and increased risk of recurrence. The question of whether such non-surgical, high-intensity and expensive treatments are necessary for middle-high LARC patients who are candidates for anal preservation surgery warrants careful consideration.

Rectal cancer, in comparison to colon cancer, more frequently presents with noticeable subjective symptoms, such as hematochezia, diarrhea, and constipation [32]. In this study, most patients experienced significant symptomatic relief after 1–2 cycles of chemotherapy. Moreover, the CapeOX regimen, which is a well-established chemotherapy protocol, was favored due to its cost-effectiveness and superior safety profile, leading to high patient compliance. In our study, only 3 patients (2.7%) developed grade 3–4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, and all of them recovered after treatment.

Among the patients in this study, 49.1% had middle LARC and 17.3% had high LARC. The remaining 33.6% of cases were classified as having low LARC (distance from the anus ≤ 5 cm), but they were still expected to undergo sphincter preservation surgery. Patients with very low LARC, who cannot undergo sphincter preservation surgery, typically opt for a more aggressive neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen instead of CapeOX, with the hope of achieving cCR and avoiding surgery. In this study, most of the enrolled patients (106 cases, 96.3%) received sphincter preservation surgery, of which 57 cases received preventive stoma due to low anastomotic position. Only 4 patients underwent Miles surgery due to the tumor’s low location.

Our study results attest to the strong efficacy of CapeOX as a preoperative NACT regimen for LARC. Most patients achieved downstaging in both T and N categories after chemotherapy. The decrease in the N category was more pronounced than in the T category, suggesting CapeOX’s superiority in controlling lymph node metastasis compared to local tumor control. While the effect of radiotherapy in the local control of tumors is usually known as superior to its effectiveness in controlling lymph node metastasis [33]. In terms of local control of the tumor, surgeons often pay more attention to T4 and MRF+, which usually determine whether an R0 resection can be achieved [34]. In this study, we observed significant reductions in T-grade and MRF-conversion rates after NAC, and all patients achieved negative circumferential and inferior resection margins, resulting in a 100% R0 resection rate. When considering the prognosis, the local recurrence rate among enrolled patients was remarkably low at 1.8% (2/110), with a 5-year DFS of 73.4%, and a 5-year OS of 78.4%. These findings underscore the effectiveness of CapeOX regimen in preventing both local recurrence and distant metastasis of LARC. For patients with LARC who are candidates for anal preservation surgery, the above therapeutic results are acceptable to both patients and physicians.

Last but not least, despite most patients being sensitive to CapeOX neoadjuvant therapy, there are invariably chemo-resistant patients who may need further intensified treatments before surgery. The NAR score, MR-TRG, and pathologic TRG grading are the main means of assessing the tumor regression after neoadjuvant therapy. Even though pathologic TRG grading is relatively more objective, it is known postoperatively. Therefore, MRI-TRG is more meaningful in decision-making for treatment regimen adjustments. Besides, endoscopic assessment is another mean of assessing tumor regression preoperatively [35]. The value of NAR score in evaluating the prognosis of LARC remains unclear. Only one study reported that NAR score has advantages in predicting local recurrence free survival of LARC patients than other grading systems [36]. There are few literature comparing NAR score and MR-TRG in predicting prognosis after surgery [37, 38]. We compared these three methods and found that NAR score was more advantageous in predicting OS and DFS than MR-TRG score. The NAR score may have been underestimated in evaluating tumor regression and predicting the prognosis of patients with LARC after neoadjuvant treatment. Of course, this inference needs to be further verified in a larger sample cohort.

Conclusions

In summary, our study, while limited by its single-center retrospective nature, adds to the emerging literature suggesting that the CapeOX regimen can serve as an effective and safe alternative to NA-CRT for LARC. The question of balancing intensity and benefits in neoadjuvant treatment remains open, and future prospective studies are required to confirm these findings.
