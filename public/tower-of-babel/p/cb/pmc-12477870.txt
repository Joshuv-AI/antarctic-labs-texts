Natural killer cells in adoptive cell therapy: current landscape of genetic engineering strategies
Authors: María Burón, Anne Etxebarria, Maite Álvarez, Irene Romayor, Cristina Eguizabal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmong adoptive cell-based immunotherapies, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapy has shown promising results in cancer treatment. Treatment with CAR-T cells has produced remarkable clinical responses, especially in cases of relapsed and refractory leukemia and lymphoma. However, CAR-T cell therapy still presents several limitations, including some safety concerns related to neurotoxicity and aggressive inflammatory responses. As an alternative to T-cells, natural killer (NK) cells have been developed as an attractive option for efficient cancer immunotherapy. As they do not express T cell receptor (TCR), NK cell-based therapies are not associated with cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or graft versus host disease (GvHD), which enables a safer therapy and the ability to generate an allogeneic “off-the-shelf” product. Despite the innate cytotoxic activity of NK cells against malignant cells, the therapeutic application of unmodified NK cells has been compromised by the inhibitory tumor microenvironment (TME), which is responsible for poor cell expansion, inactivation, insufficient tumor infiltration, and limited in vivo persistence, leading to the dysfunction of NK cells after infusion. Advances in the genetic modification of NK cells can address some of these limitations and improve their therapeutic efficacy. In this review, we describe the advances in the development of engineered NK cells for cancer immunotherapy. As such, we provide an overview of recent viral and non-viral approaches for the genetic modification of NK cells. We also discuss their current clinical status in the field of immunotherapy, and their use in other clinical applications.KEYWORDS: Natural killer cells, immunotherapy, genome engineering, viral transduction, non-viral transfection, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)

Introduction

Gene therapy is based on the modification of gene expression to alter the biological properties of cells to treat genetic disorders that are resistant to conventional therapies. This technology paves the way for the development of novel strategies in which different cells can be altered for therapeutic use. In recent decades, adoptive cell therapy (ACT) has led to the development of innovative and more effective therapeutic strategies. Among them, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-based immunotherapy has emerged as a potential treatment for different types of tumors, including hematological malignancies and solid tumors. This strategy involves the use of genetically engineered immune cells that express a CAR to better recognize a specific tumor antigen and kill target cells.1–3 CAR-T cells have shown promising results in different preclinical and clinical trials, especially for the treatment of relapsed and refractory leukemia and lymphoma. However, CAR-T cell therapy still presents several limitations, including some safety concerns related to toxicity, such as immune effector cell-associated neurological syndrome (ICANS) and cytokine release syndrome (CRS).4,5 Moreover, T cells recognize and initiate a response against non-self through the T cell receptor (TCR); therefore, CAR-T cell products that have currently been approved for clinical use are all autologous. The production of an autologous treatment takes several weeks to activate, expand, and modify the cells, depending on the health status of the patients. Patients with insufficient T cell counts to initiate manufacturing are unable to receive CAR-T cell therapy.6 For these patients, allogeneic therapy could be a good alternative; however, there is a risk of graft versus host disease (GvHD). Furthermore, in comparison to hematological malignancies, CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors has not achieved such remarkable results due to the heterogeneous and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which is responsible for poor cell expansion, inactivation, limited persistence, and insufficient tumor infiltration.7,8 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop alternative strategies for overcoming these challenges. In that sense, other immune cell types, such as natural killer (NK) cells, have been proposed as potential candidates for genetic modification. As they do not express TCR, NK cell-based therapies are not associated with CRS or GvHD, which enables a safer therapy and the ability to generate an allogeneic “off-the-shelf” product.9,10 However, genetic modification of NK cells faces important technical challenges, including the difficulty of gene delivery into the cells, their subsequent vulnerability to apoptosis, and the low expression levels of the CAR transgene. Recently, several methodologies have been developed and adapted to improve the genetic engineering of NK cells and boost their anti-tumor function.

In this review, we describe the advances in the development of engineered NK cells for cancer immunotherapy. As such, we provide an overview of recent viral and non-viral approaches for the genetic modification of NK cells. We also discuss their current clinical status in the field of immunotherapy, and their use in other clinical applications.

Understanding NK cells

NK cells account for 2–10% of circulating lymphocytes and constitute the first barrier of the immune system. NK cells are innate immune cells that exert cytotoxic and inflammatory responses against virally infected and tumor cells.11 Based on the expression levels of CD56 (neural cell adhesion molecule, NCAM) and CD16, NK cells are categorized into two groups: CD56bright CD16neg NK cells, which have an immunoregulatory role and produce cytokines, and CD56dim CD16pos NK cells, which possess strong cytotoxic capabilities.9 Even though NK cells belong to the innate immune system, studies have reported that some populations display memory-like responses during viral infections, contact hypersensitivity reactions, and following stimulation by pro-inflammatory cytokines.12,13

Unlike T cells, NK cells do not express antigen-specific receptors and do not need a prior encounter with the antigen; thus, they kill target cells spontaneously in an antigen-independent manner.14 The regulation of NK cell activation depends on multiple factors, such as the cytokine/chemokine environment surrounding the NK cells and the presence of immunosuppressive cells (regulatory T cells, myeloid derived suppressor cells, or the immunosuppressor or tumor-promoting subsets of macrophages [M2] or neutrophils [N2]),15 but is the balance between a wide range of surface activating and inhibitory receptors that determine the fate of NK cells and allow them to discriminate between “normal” and malignant cells (Figure 1A).16,17

Different inhibitory receptors, such as killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), are in charge of the mechanism known as “self-tolerance,” by which NK cells recognize major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I molecules expressed on normal cells and prevent NK cell auto-reactivity. It is important to highlight that the expression of these inhibitory receptors on NK cells is stochastic, which means that there are NK cells with inhibitory receptors that recognize self-MHC-I and NK cells that do not. To understand how NK cells carrying inhibitory receptors for non-self-MHC-I do not destroy normal cells and how self-tolerance is formed, several groups have launched the notion of NK cell licensing/arming/education that argues about the higher response of licensed/armed/educated NK cells. Because NK cells expressing inhibitory receptors that bind to self-MHC-I are known to be functionally stronger, they do also exhibit a tighter regulation when compared to unlicensed/unarmed/uneducated NK cells to prevent the response against normal self-MHC-I expressing cells and thus, the threshold for activation is higher.18–20 The hyporesponsiveness of unlicensed NK cells could be overcome in a scenario of high inflammatory signals, and this subset has a role in allograft rejection and immune reconstitution after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.18,21,22 Considering this concept, it is not surprising that inhibitory KIRs play an important role in NK cell cytotoxicity against tumor cells by exploiting the concept of “missing-self recognition.”23 In a scenario of tumors with no expression of self-MHC-I, inhibition of NK cells cannot be achieved by any inhibitory KIR, independently of their ability to bind to self- or non-self-MHC. However, in a scenario of self-MHC-I expression by tumor cells, NK cells can be selected based on the expression of inhibitory receptors that do not bind self-MHC-I, forcing missing-self recognition and tumor clearance. Conversely, activating receptors such as NKG2D and natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCRs, namely, NKp46, NKp30 and NKp44) are highly expressed upon binding to stress-induced ligands on malignant or virally infected cells, producing activating signals and triggering the corresponding “induced-self” mechanism.24 In addition, NK cells express the low-affinity Fc receptor for type G immunoglobulins, FcRg or CD16, which recognizes the Fc portion of antibody-coated target cells and performs antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)25 (Figure 1B).

Upon activation, NK cells directly kill malignant cells by releasing cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzyme, in a process known as degranulation. Perforin forms pores in the target cell membrane, allowing granzyme entry to induce apoptosis. NK cells can also trigger target cell death via death receptor-mediated cytotoxicity. NK cells express the Fas ligand, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), and lymphotoxin alpha (LTα), which bind to the surface receptors of target cells, activating the caspase cascade that leads to apoptosis.26 In addition, NK cells can modulate immune responses through the secretion of immunomodulatory cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ), TNF, and granulocyte/monocyte colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), which facilitate T cells and other immune mediating cells activation27,28 (Figure 1C).

NK cells in the clinic: an overview

The exploitation of NK cells in the clinic for cancer treatment came naturally, given the NK cell property of spontaneously eliminating tumor cells in an MHC-I independent manner. Early clinical trials analyzed the safety and efficacy of IL-2 combined with patient-derived NK-cell infusion for metastatic melanoma, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), breast cancer, and hematological malignances.29–38 The results obtained from these trials showed that the addition of NK cells was safe. However, NK cell infusion did not improve the anti-tumor responses when compared to the administration of IL-2 alone.32 Several reasons have been attributed to the lack of response when autologous NK cells are used, such as the NK cell dysfunction frequently observed in cancer patients or the inhibition of NK cells through their KIRs that recognize self-HLA expressed on the surface of tumor cells. Given the discouraging results from autologous NK cell infusion, several trials started exploring the use of alloreactive donor-derived NK cells in which NK cell clones were selected based on the lack of expression of inhibitory KIR for self-MHC-I.39 In fact, it was proven that these alloreactive clones were able to induce a graft vs leukemia (GvL) effect without graft rejection and protect patients from developing GvHD in mismatched hematopoietic transplants.40 The first successful experience with unmodified allogeneic NK cells came from a 2005 clinical trial that evaluated the response of patients with poor-prognosis acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in non-HSCT settings. In this study, Miller et al. reported complete hematologic remission in five out of 19 patients who received haploidentical NK cell infusions.41

Exploiting NK cell-mediated ADCC, the administration of NK cells along with tumor-targeting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), such as anti-GD2, cetuximab (anti-EGFR), or trastuzumab (anti-HER2), has also shown promising results in the treatment of recurrent and refractory neuroblastoma,42 advanced gastric and colorectal cancer,43 recurrent and metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma,44 and HER2-positive cancer.45 However, some of these studies also underlined the progressive downregulation of CD16 after infusion, which likely limits the immunotherapeutic efficacy that can be accomplished with mAb-based and NK cell-based immunotherapies.45

In order to accomplish a stronger and more durable NK cell-mediated anti-tumor response, different approaches have been tested by modulating activation, inhibition, and/or survival through different strategies.15,46 One of these approaches is the development of a fusion protein encompassing IL-15, the IL15Rα, and a modified human IgG that is unable to generate ADCC. This super-agonist, IL-15 N-803 (formerly ALT-803), has been shown to increase circulating NK cells in treated patients with solid cancer (NCT01727076). Although no clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy of combining NK cell infusion (autologous or allogeneic) with N-803 in cancer patients, a pilot study recently noted that haploidentical NK cells and N-803 were safe and well tolerated in HIV-infected patients, resulting in a moderate decrease in HIV-producing cells (NCT03899480).47

Some approaches involve the improvement of manufacturing protocols for ex vivo NK cell expansion. These changes aim to increase the quantity and quality of NK cells administered for infusion using feeder cells modified to express membrane-bound (mb) cytokines (IL-15, IL-12, IL-21) and/or NK cell ligands (41BBL). In non-HSCT settings, the administration of mbIL-15 activated NK cells demonstrated a higher in vivo proliferation in high-risk relapsed multiple myeloma patients without serious treatment-related adverse events (trAEs) and a partial response in one out of seven patients.48 Recently, a phase two clinical trial attempting to establish the infusion of haploidentical ex vivo mbIL-15-41BBL activated NK cells as a consolidation therapy for pediatric AML in first complete remission after chemotherapy. After 33 months, six of the seven patients stayed in complete disease remission (NCT02763475).49 Similarly, infusion of mbIL-21 ex vivo expanded NK cells into 12 patients with refractory AML settings improved the one-year overall survival without trAEs.50

Strategies that combine activated NK cell infusion with neutralization of immune checkpoints or inhibitory receptors are being evaluated in clinical trials based on promising preclinical studies that demonstrated a positive impact on the efficacy of NK cells.15,46,51 Some examples include the use of antibodies against PD-L1, such as pembrolizumab, or monalizumab, a first-in-class IgG4 humanized mAb neutralizing NKG2A.52,53 Although the use of monalizumab with NK cell therapy is yet to be explored in the clinic, a preclinical study showed a reduction in tumor growth when this combination was given intratumorally.54

A most recent approach involved the use of NK cell engagers (NCE) to enhance tumor targeting, NK-cell activation, and immunological synapses between the tumors and the NK cells themselves. Perhaps the most advanced NCE to date is a tri-specific (Tri-KE) against CD33+ tumors that incorporates an anti-CD16 scFV linked to an IL-15 moiety termed GTB-3550, which is being evaluated in an open-label phase I/II clinical trial for high-risk hematological malignances (NCT03214666).46,55

The introduction of new techniques that allow for genetically engineered immune cells has opened a new avenue for modulating NK cell activity, suppression, and survival, which brings up a new level of complexity and allows for the tailoring of NK cells that can potentially result in stronger and more durable response for a wide variety of cancers.

Genetic engineering of NK cells

Despite the great potential of NK cells for use in immunotherapy, the immunosuppressive TME and the difficulties arising from poor trafficking and infiltration to the tumor site usually limit their anti-cancer function in solid tumors. To overcome these resistances and augment their cytotoxic potential, molecular engineering and gene editing strategies are currently being used.56

Understanding the molecular mechanism by which NK cells function as effective killer cells is crucial for implementing appropriate genetic modifications to enhance their capacity to target and eliminate tumor cells. Over the last few years, attempts have been made to improve the effector function of NK cells using different strategies. In order to increasing the cytotoxic capacity, the balance of activating and inhibitory signals in NK cells can be tilted toward activation by transducing the cells with activating receptors. An example is overexpression of a chimeric activating receptor consisting of NKG2D associated with CD3ζ and DAP10 which specifically activates NK cells.57 Activating signals can also be delivered by CARs that recognize antigens expressed by tumor cells.58 Conversely, activation can be promoted by the disruption of inhibitory receptors. This can be achieved by deleting the gene encoding the receptor of interest by gene editing methods or by using protein expression blockers. For example, the disruption of the immune checkpoint molecule pair HLA-E:CD94-NKG2A by blockade of NKG2A enhances NK-mediated tumor cell killing in vitro and prevents tumor metastasis in vivo.59 In addition to improving effector function, approaches have also focused on improving NK cells persistence in vivo by reprogramming them to produce cytokines, such as IL-2, IL-15 and IL-21. This autocrine support has drawn great interest and CAR-engineered NK cells are emerging armed with supplemental cytokine signaling.60,61 Finally, enhancing NK cells migration toward the tumor site is essential for optimal anti-tumor efficacy. Studies have shown that inducing NK cells to express the chemokine receptor CXCR4 improve bone marrow homing.62 As another example, co-expression of CCR7 with high-affinity CD16 improved NK cell migration toward the lymph node-associated chemokine CCL19 in vitro.63

However, one of the main challenges in this field is that NK cells are especially difficult to genetically modify and display resistance to many genetic manipulation methods that work well in T cells. Moreover, the success of genetic modifications is highly dependent on the vector that is used, which should present a certain degree of safety and efficacy, facilitating the entry into the target cell.

Furthermore, the source of NK cells impact the efficiency of genetic modification methods used and have their respective advantages and disadvantages (Table S1). Peripheral blood-derived NK cells offer several benefits, such as easy accessibility, a mature phenotype, and strong cytotoxic activity. However, they also present some drawbacks like donor variability, which affects genetic engineering efficiency, and limited cell numbers requiring ex vivo expansion. Conversely, cord blood-derived NK cells typically show a naïve phenotype and high proliferative capacity, yet they can also display variability.10,64 NK cells can also be obtained from cord blood hematopoietic progenitor CD34+ cells.65 This process yields a higher expression of CXCR4 on NK cells that mediates bone marrow homing but also yields a lower expression of KIRs that may impact their tumor-targeting ability.66,67 Given the heterogeneous nature of these sources of primary NK cells, their genetic modification remains highly variable. More recently, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have been successfully used to generate NK cells. iPSC-derived NK cells offer high proliferative potential during differentiation and more homogeneous cell populations. These cells often have an immature phenotype and require extended in vitro culture conditions. Nonetheless, a growing number of genetically engineered iPSC-NK cell candidates are emerging in preclinical trials.51,68 Although the cancer-derived NK cell line NK-92 not represent the same challenges as primary NK cells, they can overcome the limitations above; however, safety requirements mandating mitotic inactivation by irradiation impose important drawbacks for potential patient use.69,70 Today, the main cell source used as starting material for the manufacturing of engineered NK cell products is the NK-92 cell line, followed by peripheral blood-derived NK cells.71

NK cell viral transduction methods

Transduction refers to the incorporation of genetic material into the host genome through viral vectors. These vectors are used as vehicles to introduce genes into different cell types, such as NK cells, resulting in highly efficient and permanent expression of these genes. The most commonly used viral vectors are lentiviral and retroviral vectors. Among them, lentiviral vectors have shown several advantages, including the possibility of transducing both dividing and non-dividing cells, the capacity to incorporate larger transgenes into the host genome (up to 9kb) and a lower risk of inducing insertional mutations or intrinsic genotoxicity. In contrast, retroviral vectors are restricted to proliferating cells and display a higher level of insertional mutagenesis due to their preferential integration into transcriptional start sites and their affinities toward oncogenes.72,73 In fact, it has been reported that four patients treated with retrovirus-transduced CD34+ cells developed leukemia because of insertional mutagenesis.74 Although in general the transduction efficiency of retroviral vectors seems to be higher than that of lentiviral vectors, it highly varies depending on the strategy used, and in some cases, multiple rounds of transduction are required.75 Owing to the innate characteristics of NK cells, it is difficult to obtain a high level of transduction. The presence of restriction factors and innate immune pathways within NK cells can hinder the ability of viral vectors to successfully integrate into the genome of the cells and express their genetic cargo. In addition, the efficiency of transduction often requires activating stimuli that can lead to NK cell exhaustion.76 However, efforts are being made to optimize the methodology to reverse this situation and avoid the NK cell resistance. Recent data indicate that the use of feeder cells and different cytokines promotes cell activation and vector entry, respectively. Furthermore, altering the antiviral mechanisms of cells or changing the envelope of the vector (pseudotyping) can improve the transduction efficiency. Finally, the use of different transduction agents, also known as enhancers, is currently being studied, including LentiBOOSTTM, Protamine sulfate, Retronectin and Vectofusin-1, to facilitate the internalization of the vector into the cell.69,77

Currently, viral-based genetic engineering strategies are the performant approach used for the delivery of CARs into immune cells. In one study, peripheral and cord blood-derived NK cells transduced with anti-CD19 CAR reached approximately 40% transduction efficacy in both sources when a lentiviral vector was used.78 In addition, in a recent study, allogeneic NK cells transduced with anti-CD19 CAR using retroviral vectors obtained approximately 70% CAR transduction.79 Similarly, cord blood-derived NK cells were transduced with retroviral vectors targeting prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) for pancreatic cancer, resulting in 67% transduction.

NK cell non-viral transfection strategies

Transfection is a potent tool for engineering cells that allows the delivery of exogenous transgenes or genome-editing reagents for gene modification.80 Transfection methodologies result in fast, although temporary, expression of the transgene and are safer than viral transduction methods.81,82 Furthermore, because of the non-integrative nature of the transfection process, the regulatory pathway used to validate its clinical implementation is less restricted. Non-viral vectors allow the delivery of a wide range of nucleic acids, including mRNA, small interfering RNA (siRNA), RNA guide (gRNA), and even supercoiled DNA.83 Among them, transfection with RNA confers several advantages over DNA delivery. RNA is moved efficiently to the cytoplasm, resulting in higher transfection rates. Moreover, unlike DNA the expression of RNA is not limited by the cell cycle because it does not need to enter the nucleus, enabling it to be expressed in both dividing and non-dividing cells. However, this approach has limitations, as higher quantities of mRNA are needed for clinical purposes, and multiple infusions are required to maintain the temporary expression of proteins. This transitory expression can also be a strength because it allows control of the risk of an excessive response by the administration of diverse doses and infusion schemes.84

Thanks to their safer clinical profile, high transfection efficiency, and cost-effective manufacturing process of clinical grade for non-viral vectors, the number of publications based on non-viral transfection of NK cells has dramatically increased in recent years. In most of these studies, the main transfection method used was electroporation, followed by nanoparticles, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR)-associated caspase 9 (Cas9) CRISPR/Cas9, and the transposon-based systems. Electroporation

Electroporation is one of the earliest and most frequently used non-viral transfection strategy. This method involves the application of a short electrical pulse to induce the formation of transient pores in the cell membrane, boosting the introduction of nucleic acids into cells.85,86 As against other techniques, cell membrane permeabilization caused by electroporation frequently results in high cell death.77,87,88 However, in recent years, electroporation systems have been refined to be more efficient and less disruptive to cells, leading to higher transfection efficiencies and better survival rates. DNA electroporation requires dividing cells in the exponential growth phase because their active state enhances cell membrane permeability, increasing the chances of DNA entering the cell and reaching the nucleus,89 but the nucleofection technology created by Amaxa® allows the transfection process also in non-dividing cells through the combination of optimized pulse parameters and cell type-specific reagents.90 Furthermore, some companies, such as MaxCyte and Miltenyi, have good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant electroporation equipment for clinical use, which has facilitated the introduction of this technique in clinics.

Due to the careful optimization of electroporation methodology, several authors have explored its potential for genetically modifying NK cells. One of the first studies carried out by Boissel et al. revealed that the nature of the nucleic acid used in the electroporation influences both, the transfection efficiency and cell viability. They electroporated the NK-92 cell line with a CD19-CAR mRNA or pDNA, and their results showed 47% CAR expression when it was delivered as mRNA while only 5% as pDNA. In addition, electroporation with pDNA is more toxic.91 Given its higher transfection efficacy and better cell survival rates, mRNA electroporation has gained a lot of interest in NK cell-based therapies. Carlsten et al. electroporated PB-derived NK cells with mRNA coding for the chemokine receptor CCR7 to increase NK cell lymph node homing toward CCL19 ligand. Furthermore, this modification was accompanied by the transfection of mRNA coding for a high-affinity CD16 receptor to improve the ADCC-mediated cytotoxicity of NK cells against lymphoma.63 This group also modified NK cells by electroporation of a CXCR4-coding mRNA to enable migration and in vivo bone marrow homing, which could be beneficial against hematological malignances.62

Electroporation of NK cells with mRNA has also shown promising results compared to lentiviral and retroviral transduction. For example, the NK-92 cell line electroporated with CD19 and CD20 CAR mRNA exhibited greater transfection efficiency than the same cells transduced with a lentiviral vector.82 In the same way, electroporation of expanded NK cells with anti-CD19 CAR mRNA resulted in higher transfection rates compared to retroviral transduction.58 In both studies, NK cell viability after electroporation was greater than 80%. However, CAR expression in electroporated cells using mRNA is transitory and usually lasts three to seven days with a peak of expression at 24–48 hours after electroporation to decline progressively afterward. In contrast, lentiviral and retroviral transduction achieved the maximum level a week after, and the expression of CAR remained stable.82 Owing to this limitation of the mRNA electroporation system, some authors have focused on improving DNA electroporation. Ingegnere et al. optimized different transfection factors, such as the number of cells, buffer conditions, the amount and size of plasmid DNA, voltage, pulses, and target cells. These improvements have allowed the engineering of NK cells with anti-CD19 and CCR7 CAR pDNA. Despite DNA transfection efficiency and cell viability were lower than those obtained with mRNA electroporation, CAR expression could be detected for up to 15 days.92

Although most electroporation studies on NK cells have focused on the treatment of hematological cancers, engineered NK cells by electroporation also have shown great results in the treatment of solid tumors. In this way, Liu et al. modified NK-92 cells with HER2-CAR pDNA, enhancing the ability of these cells to in vitro kill the human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-453 and SKBr3 and reducing the tumor size in a BALB/C mouse tumor model.93

Transposon systems

In the field of genetic modification techniques, transposable elements (TEs) or transposons represent a unique non-viral approach suitable for NK cell engineering. Transposons are prokaryotic or eukaryotic DNA sequences that can change their position in the genome, altering the gene expression pattern and contributing to genomic evolution. The transposon complex is commonly composed of a mobile transposon vector and a transposase enzyme that removes and reintegrates the vector into a new location in the target DNA. Given these characteristics, transposons have been widely used as mutagenic and transgenic tools, and more recently, for engineering human cells for cellular therapy, including human iPSCs and T cells.94,95 However, the genetic editing of NK cells using transposons is still in its early years, and few studies are focused on such promising tool.80

Currently, three main transposon systems are used for genetic engineering, which include Sleeping Beauty (SB), PiggyBac (PB), and Tol2.96 SB consists of a synthetic element reconstructed from the Tc1/mariner transposon superfamily of salmonid fish, and is optimized through the generation of hyperactive variants and transposon donor vectors. Interestingly, the particular composition of the SB sequence, together with its precise mechanism and its specific recognition of inverted repeat segments and TA dinucleotides, results in a close-to-random integration profile into the genome, reducing the probability of disrupting essential genes or regulatory elements.97 Thus, SB represents a secure, efficient, and cost-effective gene-editing tool with great applicability in the clinical context involving NK cells. Indeed, Bexte et al. demonstrated the safety and efficacy of the SB system to generate stable CAR-NK cells obtained from healthy donors for the treatment of leukemia.98,99 Also, Batchu et al. were able to improve the engraftment of a mesothelin-CAR in NK-92MI cells using SB into a minicircle vector.100 Another recent published study revealed the successful use of the SB method to design antigen-presenting cells to improve human primary NK cell expansion.101 Apart from SB, the PB system is a similar available integrative vector derived from the cabbage looper moth that has been improved to enhance its efficiency. Today, applications concerning gene modification in primary NK cells mediated by PB are also related to the optimization of CAR-NK therapies against leukemia.102,103 However, this field requires further investigation. Notably, some clinical studies have reported the development of lymphomas associated with the use of PB-modified CD19 CAR-T cells.104,105 Finally, Tol2 was the first vertebrate transposable element discovered in medaka fish and it has been extensively used to generate transgenic animals, especially in zebrafish model.96 Nonetheless, although Tol2 has demonstrated its efficacy for CAR-T cell manufacturing,106 such type of transposon has not yet been tested in NK cells. In addition to the three transposon systems mentioned above, Skeate et al., have recently used the TcBuster transposon system for engineering primary human NK cells with a CD19-CAR.107 TcBuster belongs to the hAT superfamily of transposases and is derived from the red flour beetle (Tribolium castaneum).

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated strategies

In addition to gene transfer strategies for transposon-based NK cell engineering, novel genomic modification technologies have emerged as innovative procedures for targeting specific genes. The CRISPR/Cas9 system was discovered in 2012108 and is a gene editing tool conformed by a customizable RNA-guide (sgRNA) that directs the Cas9 nuclease to a specific region of the genome to induce a double-strand break. Then, the desired genetic sequence is repaired through endogenous DNA mechanisms involving non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) or homologous recombination (HR).109–112 During this process, genome editing efficiency relies on the delivery format (plasmid DNA, mRNA, or sgRNA-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein), transfection method (viral or non-viral), and cell type.113,114 With respect to the delivery system, sgRNA-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein offers significant advantages, including the reduction of possible off-target events, toxicity, and immunogenicity.115 Regarding the transfection approach, while lentiviral and retroviral methods have shown good gene editing efficiency, both the encapsulating capacity and the risks associated with the integration of the viral sequence into the host genome make it necessary to improve non-viral alternatives.116,117 For these reasons, sgRNA-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein electroporation is the main non-viral mechanism to introduce CRISPR/Cas9 machinery into NK cells.118,119 Nevertheless, CRISPR editing of NK cells has been challenging; thus, many studies have focused on optimizing electroporation steps to obtain competent CRISPR/Cas9 delivery.118–120 In fact, the most recent studies related to CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in primary human NK cells through electroporation revealed a stable gene editing efficiency greater than 80%.119,121–123 A recent study carried out by Gong et al. demonstrated the efficacy of CRISPR/Cas9 to Knockout the NKG2A-encoding KLRC1 gene to prevent NK cell immunosuppression.124 In addition, another study showed the capacity of CRISPR/Cas9 to disrupt the transcription factor hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α to improve the anti-tumor function of NK cells under hypoxic conditions.125

Nanoparticles-mediated delivery

The generation of different types of nanoparticles designed as non-viral carriers has the potential to address the drawbacks associated with physical methods and viral vectors, such as poor cell viability, restricted loading capacity, and insufficient targeting capabilities. Nanoparticles (NPs) range in size from 20 to 100 nm and form complexes with cargo molecules, protecting them from degradation and facilitating the transfection process.126 They may be internalized via endocytosis followed by endosomal escape; however, some NPs can directly fuse with the cell membrane and deliver the cargo into the cytosol. NPs are composed of synthetic or natural biocompatible materials of which polymeric, polypeptidic, and lipid-based systems (or combinations of them), gold nanoparticles and dendrimers have shown better biocompatibility and biodegradability properties.127 In fact, since SARS-CoV-2 emergence, research on the application of NPs for immunotherapy has been promoted. Specifically, Pfizer-BioNTech´128 and Moderna´s129–131 vaccines have demonstrated the excellent efficacy of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as delivery vectors for COVID-19 mRNA.132

Despite the fact that LNPs have been mostly used for T cell engineering, there are a few studies on NK cells with encouraging results, especially those focused on the delivery of siRNA133 and the expression of CAR.134 Nakamura et al. developed a YSK12-LNP loaded system to deliver siRNA to induce gene silencing in several cell lines including NK-92 cells. The use of this approach to introduce an siRNA against GAPDH led to 75% silencing efficiency, but they also found a significant decrease in cell viability.135 To solve this issue, they introduced a core formed by protamine that interacts electrostatically with siRNA, condensing it. Thus, they recovered cell viability without affecting the gene silencing.136 Similarly, Douka et al. optimized the formulation of LNPs with the ionizable Lipid-5 for the delivery of mRNA into cord blood-derived NK cells, obtaining 75% transfection efficiency and more than 95% cell viability.137 Recently, mRNA-LNP technology was used to generate functional CD19-CAR and BCMA-CAR NK cells with 78% and 95% CAR expression, respectively.134

Other studies have focused not only on improving the transfection of NK cells through different design systems but also on facilitating their nanomolecular visualization into the tumor site. For instance, NPs can be developed with a magnetic core that allows them to be tracked in vivo using magnetic resonance imaging. Additionally, NPs can be stained with fluorescent molecules for visualization using optical imaging systems. Zhuo et al. transfected NK-92 cells with the human ferritin heavy chain gene (hFTH1) using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified dendrimers containing gold nanoparticles (Au DENPs). Their results showed 80% transfection efficiency and the capacity of these NPs to guide NK cells to efficiently target breast cancer cells.138 In another report by Kim et al., the NK92-MI cell line was genetically engineered with polydopamine (PDA)-coated NPs encoding pDNA EGFR-CAR and labeled with near-infrared fluorescent molecules to visualize NK cell trafficking.139

As an example of another approach to engineer NK cells with nanomaterials, Wilk et al. reported a novel charge-altering releasable transporter (CARTs) to deliver mRNA into NK cells. CARTs are cationic nanocarriers that can penetrate immune cells through the cell membrane. Under physiological pH conditions, CARTs turn neutral and biodegrade into small and nontoxic molecules in the cytosol, facilitating the release of the anionic cargo. Using CARTs, they successfully generated cytotoxic primary anti-CD19 CAR-NK cells without altering their phenotype and function.140

Clinical applications of engineered CAR-NK cells

Despite CAR-T cells have revolutionized immunotherapy in the field of oncology, NK cells have emerged as a potential alternative with a safer profile, opening the possibility of an “off-the shelf” therapy and promoting the rapid translation of the therapy into the clinic (Figure 3C,D).

Currently, most clinical trials based on CAR-NK cells are focused on the treatment of hematological cancers (Table 1), and in most cases, viral methods have been used for the production of the cell therapy. In 2016, the first phase I trial was released using the CD33-transduced NK-92 cell line for relapsed/refractory CD33+ AML, and the safety profile of the therapy was demonstrated (NCT02944162).141 Since then, the number of clinical trials using cord-blood- or PB-derived NK cells or NK cell lines has drastically increased. In 2020, Liu et al. published a phase I and II trial in which 11 patients with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma or chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) were treated with umbilical cord blood-derived allogeneic anti-CD19 CAR-NK cells. In this study, a retroviral vector was used to transduce cells with anti-CD19 CAR, IL-15, and inducible caspase 9, resulting in 49% transduction. A complete response was obtained in seven of eight patients, and no severe adverse events were associated with the treatment (NCT03056339).61 Furthermore, the same group conducted a phase I and II trial based on a study of the safety and efficacy of cord blood-derived anti-CD19 CAR-NK cells (CAR19/IL-15) for treating CD19+ B cell malignancies. In 37 patients infused with different doses of CAR-NK cells, the 30-day overall response rate was 48%, with no toxicities reported.142 Similar to this study, a cryopreserved off-the-shelf allogeneic PB-derived NK cell product (NKX019) expressing mbIL-15 and targeting CD19+ tumor cells is currently being evaluated in a phase I clinical trial for B-cell malignances (NCT05020678).143 Although further studies are needed to increase the sample size, these results are highly promising and advocate the use of engineered CAR-NK cells for cancer treatment.

The success observed in hematological tumors has driven the use of this therapy not only in solid tumors (Table 2A), but also in other diseases, such as viral infections and autoimmune diseases (Table 2B). In 2020, Lu et al. demonstrated that chimeric costimulatory converting receptor-modified NK92 (CCCR-NK92) cells, which involve a chimeric costimulatory/switch receptor with PD-1 linked to NKG2D, had a higher anti-tumor capacity against human lung cancer in vitro and in vivo.144 These results prompted an evaluation of the safety of CCCR-NK92 cells in a phase I clinical trial against NSCLC (NCT03656705). Moreover, advances in genetic engineering have encouraged the development of more complex CAR-NK cell designs. Li et al. generated an endogenous CD16-expressing NK cell line (termed oNK) conjugated with trastuzumab and an anti-HER2 antibody (ACE-oNK-HER2/ACE1702) to fight against metastatic HER2-expressing solid tumors, which is currently being evaluated for safety and tolerability in a phase I clinical trial (NCT04319757).145

In recent years, the use of non-viral approaches for CAR-NK cell engineering, such as electroporation, LNPs delivery, and CRISPR/Cas9 technology, has gained momentum because of safety concerns, high manufacturing costs, and vector capacity constraints that entail viral methods. A testament to this is all the recent preclinical studies that have been carried out looking for better transduction efficacy and anti-tumor responses, as previously mentioned. To date, there have been three clinical trials based on non-viral engineered CAR-NK cells that used electroporation of mRNA (two studies) or CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing (one study) as transfection methods (Table 3). In an interventional phase I trial, anti-CD19 engineered NK cells were produced by mRNA electroporation of haploidentical NK cells with anti-CD19 CAR (NKCARCD19) to treat B-lineage ALL patients. However, no information has been published and its current status is suspended (NCT01974479). In a pilot study, NKG2D-CAR NK (NCR-NK-01) cells were generated through mRNA electroporation of autologous or haploidentical NK cells with NKG2D-CAR and locally infused into the peritoneal cavity to treat three patients with metastatic colorectal cancer. Results published by Xiao et al. showed significant tumor remission in three patients without serious trAEs (NCT03415100).146 However, the status of this trial is unknown. Lastly, CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology was used to engineer hiPSC line-derived allogeneic NK cells with anti-BCMA-CAR (FT576) for the treatment of patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma. In this study, they confirmed the safety and tolerability of the therapy as a single-dose monotherapy and in combination with the anti-CD38 mAb daratumumab (NCT05182073).147

Conclusion remarks

The innate ability of NK cells to specifically kill malignant cells without prior encounter with the antigen and the possibility of an “off-the-shelf” product make them excellent candidates for ACT. Early trials using autologous or allogeneic donor-derived NK cells demonstrated their safety profile and minimal toxicity, overcoming some of the drawbacks of CAR-T cell therapies. Infusion of unmodified allogeneic NK cells in both HSCT and non-HSCT settings has shown complete hematologic remission in patients with AML and myeloid malignancies but not in solid tumors. Although additional strategies have been explored to accomplish a stronger and more durable NK cell-mediated anti-tumor response, the real breakthrough came with advances in genetic engineering technologies, especially the introduction of CAR to redirect NK cells specifically against cancer cells.

One of the main challenges in this field is that NK cells are especially difficult to genetically modify, and display resistance to many genetic manipulation methods that work well in T cells. Moreover, the success of genetic modifications is highly dependent on the vector that is used, which should present a certain degree of safety and efficacy, facilitating the entry into the target cell. Fortunately, technological advancements such as enhanced cell expansion, more efficient CAR, CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing, and improved viral transduction and electroporation methods, are making it possible to overcome these obstacles. In fact, the use of non-viral methodologies to transfect NK cells is experiencing immense growth and rapid advancement in preclinical studies. For example, since SARS-CoV-2 emergence, LNPs-based delivery systems have emerged as promising carriers for the delivery of nucleic acids to NK cells. In addition, thanks to the versatility of NPs, some studies are investigating their use in encapsulating the CRISPR/Cas9 complex and mediating its entry into cells. This methodology has displayed a certain in vivo potential in several cancer models, but its success in the transfection of NK cells is still limited. Despite advancements in non-viral strategies, to date, there have been only three clinical trials based on non-viral engineered CAR-NK cells, one of which has been suspended. Two of these trials used electroporation of mRNA and the other used CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing as a transfection method.

Research to achieve a stronger and more durable NK cell-mediated anti-tumor response is rapidly progressing, and we expect that novel and/or refined delivery technologies and gene editing tools will emerge to improve NK cell therapies in the near future.
