In Memoriam: Joseph D. Brain
Authors: Douglas W Dockery, Nancy Long Sieber
Published: 2024

Joseph D. Brain, a leading light in environmental and occupational health at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, passed away on 29 January 2024, just 9 days after his 84th birthday. His career, spanning more than 60 years, was marked by his passion for environmental health, his dedication to innovative research, and a positive spirit that inspired countless colleagues and students.

Born in Paterson, New Jersey, in 1940, Joe graduated summa cum laude with a BS in Physics from Taylor University in 1961. Next, he started his academic journey at Harvard, receiving an SM in Applied Physics from the Division of Engineering and Applied Physics in 1962.

Joseph D. Brain, 1940–2024. Image: Suzanne Camarata.

Joe then moved across the Charles River to the Harvard School of Public Health, earning an SM in radiological hygiene in 1963 and an SD in physiology in 1966. In 1968, he joined the Harvard School of Public Health faculty as an assistant professor of physiology and moved up the ranks to become a professor of physiology in 1978. He was named Cecil K. and Philip Drinker Professor of Environmental Physiology in 1987.

Pioneering Research and Human Connections

Joe’s research was groundbreaking, particularly in understanding how air pollutants are delivered and cleared from the lungs. Joe’s doctoral thesis was titled Clearance of Particles from the Lungs: Alveolar Macrophages and Mucus Transport. It portended his research career in the deposition and clearance of aerosol particles in the lungs, the effects of aerosols in environmental and occupational lung disease, and their use in drug delivery.

His scientific achievements in lung biology made him a magnet for hundreds of trainees and collaborators who passed through his lab. Beyond providing research opportunities, Joe had a gift for facilitating connections between people. He had an uncanny ability to find what two people might have in common—a shared research interest, children of the same age, or perhaps a love of music. He would introduce them, and, more often than not, a lifelong bond was forged. In this way Joe nurtured a vibrant network of fellow scientists and friends across the globe. This attentiveness to the lives of the people around him reflects Joe’s humanity, which played out in all aspects of his life.

A Lifetime of Teaching and Impact

Joe’s commitment to teaching was evident throughout his career, even during its most demanding phases. He was a devoted instructor at the Harvard School of Public Health and Harvard Medical School, inspiring graduate students to understand and address the effects of the environment on both individual and public health. However, his most significant contribution to education was “The Human Organism,” a course he passionately taught to Harvard undergraduates for more than 40 years. Joe believed public health education should be a cornerstone of a Harvard education, enabling students from any field to consider the health implications of their decisions, be it in government, their workplace, or in their personal lives. Nothing gave him more pleasure than hearing how his course had affected former students’ lives and, in many cases, their careers.

A Collaborative Force in Academia and Beyond

Joe’s greatest legacy may be his exemplary service as chair of the Department of Environmental Health between 1990 and 2005, along with directing the Harvard–NIEHS Center for Environmental Health between 1998 and 2008. Under his leadership, the department and center thrived and became global leaders in multidisciplinary research and education. Joe fostered collaboration across disciplines and welcomed diverse perspectives. With his warm and inclusive approach, he built a collegial community that continues today. He was open to new ideas and respectful of differing views. Even in difficult discussions around priorities or budgets, Joe maintained a calm and solution-focused demeanor. All who knew Joe appreciated his commitment to equitable and respectful dialogue. His example of open and uniting leadership enabled the department and center to achieve their mission. His philosophy and leadership style continue to serve as the bedrock to our department’s vision and leadership.

His remarkable talent for bringing people together and finding common ground led to Joe being called to serve on numerous academic committees across the university and school. He also contributed to scientific advisory committees for a broad array of governmental bodies, including the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute; the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences; the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health; the Environmental Protection Agency; and the National Council on Radiation Protection and Measurements. His expertise was sought by nongovernmental organizations such as the Health Effects Institute, the American Thoracic Society, the American Lung Association, and private foundations such as the Francis Family Foundation and the Lown Cardiovascular Research Foundation. Joe’s achievements were recognized with numerous awards, including being named a fellow of the American Association for the Advancement of Science and receiving a National Institutes of Health MERIT Award.

In recent years, Joe dedicated himself to documenting and preserving the history of his department and school. In 2020, he coauthored The Education of Alice Hamilton,1 a book that paid tribute to an occupational health pioneer and Harvard’s first female faculty member. His contributions to the history of health sciences were acknowledged by the Librarians, Archivists, and Museum Professionals in the History of the Health Sciences, which awarded him its Recognition of Merit in 2022.

A Lasting Legacy

Although his scientific achievements are numerous, those who knew Joe best remember his deep humanity—his kindness, humility, and ability to forge strong connections with people from all walks of life. Joe lived his life with a quiet purpose, placing high value on family, faith, community service, and friendships. His life’s work was a testament to his belief in the significance of creating a better environment for healthier lives.

Joe’s visionary outlook transcended traditional environmental concerns. At a tribute marking his 50 years of service to Harvard, Joe reflected on his enduring commitment: “What is constant for me? I believe in the importance of the environment. An improved environment enables longer, healthier lives. We have expanded our understanding of what constitutes the environment. Yes, nutritious food, safe water and air, and access to health care are basic. And so are equal access to education, wealth, and opportunity. To continue progress we must change lifestyles and the policies and ecosystems which determine health.”

Although we deeply feel the loss of Joe’s enduring presence, his influence continues to resonate through the countless lives he touched. Joe Brain was indeed a gift to public health and to Harvard. He lived his life fully in service to others, and his legacy challenges us all to adapt our lifestyles and reshape our policies to further public health.
