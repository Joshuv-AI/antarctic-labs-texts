Radiotherapy strategies for nonagenarian cancer patients at a tertiary cancer center
Authors: Kae Okuma, Ayaka Nagao, Koji Inaba, Tairo Kashihara, Kana Takahashi, Madoka Sakuramachi, Akane Yoshiba, Akikazu Kobori, Shoki Nakamura, Hiroshi Igaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWith increasing life expectancy, the clinical role of radiotherapy in cancer patients aged ≥ 90 years warrants systematic evaluation. This study aimed to assess treatment patterns, safety, and outcomes of radiotherapy in the super-elderly at a tertiary cancer center.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 119 patients aged ≥ 90 years who underwent radiotherapy between 2007 and 2025. Patient characteristics, treatment modalities, adverse events, and survival were evaluated. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify prognostic factors.ResultsThe median age was 91 years, and 71% of patients had ECOG performance status (PS) 0–1. Radiotherapy was delivered with curative intent in 69% of cases. Advanced techniques such as stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and brachytherapy (BT) were used in 59% of patients. Hypofractionated schedules (≤ 10 fractions) were applied in 66% of cases. Treatment completion rate was 95%, with grade ≥ 3 adverse events observed in only 3% of patients. Median overall survival was 31.2 months. On multivariate analysis, PS was the only independent prognostic factor for survival.ConclusionRadiotherapy for patients aged ≥ 90 years can be delivered safely and effectively in appropriately selected individuals, particularly when utilizing tailored approaches such as SRT, IMRT, or BT. Even among patients treated with palliative intent, meaningful local control and symptom relief were achievable. These findings suggest that chronological age alone should not preclude the use of radiotherapy; however, clinical decisions should be guided by individual patient factors such as performance status, functional reserve, and overall clinical context.Keywords: Super-elderly, Radiation therapy, Nonagenarian, Stereotactic radiotherapy, Brachytherapy, Treatment outcome

Introduction

Japan has undergone a dramatic demographic shift in recent years, becoming a super-aged society. As a result, the number of cancer patients aged 90 years or older requiring medical intervention is steadily increasing. According to a 2024 report by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the remaining life expectancy at age 90 is 4.3 years for men and 5.6 years for women [1]. Given that cancer incidence increases with age, it is not uncommon for nonagenarian patients to seek curative treatment at cancer centers, including radiation therapy. Among the three main cancer treatment modalities—surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy—the proportion of patients receiving no active treatment increases with age. This trend is particularly pronounced in individuals aged 85 years and older, regardless of cancer type [2]. Radiation therapy, being non-invasive and generally well-tolerated, offers a valuable option for elderly patients who are medically inoperable or unable to undergo systemic chemotherapy due to frailty, comorbidities, or personal preference. However, patients aged ≥ 90 years often present with compromised performance status (PS), cognitive decline, and multiple comorbid conditions. These factors necessitate highly individualized treatment planning and often complicate decision-making. Evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of radiotherapy in this age group remains limited. To date, Katano et al. [3] have reported a retrospective analysis of 124 patients aged 90 or older, and a few other studies have been published [4–7]. Nevertheless, more detailed investigations are warranted, particularly those evaluating outcomes stratified by radiation technique and treatment intent, treatment completion rates, and their associations with prognosis.

In this retrospective study, we analyzed clinical data from cancer patients aged ≥ 90 years who underwent radiotherapy at our tertiary cancer center over an 18-year period. Importantly, this study focuses on patients who were considered appropriate candidates for radiotherapy in routine clinical practice, rather than the entire nonagenarian population. We aimed to explore how clinical characteristics—including treatment modality, treatment intent, performance status, cognitive function, and primary disease site—affected treatment patterns and overall survival. By presenting real-world data, we hope to support evidence-based clinical decision-making and improve the treatment landscape for nonagenarian cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective, single-institution cohort study included patients aged 90 years or older who received radiotherapy at a tertiary cancer center between April 2007 and March 2025. Eligible patients were defined as those who were aged ≥ 90 at the initiation of radiotherapy. For individuals who underwent multiple courses after turning 90, only the first course was included in the analysis.

Data collection

Clinical and treatment-related data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records and the institutional radiotherapy planning system. Variables included age, sex, and primary tumor site, which was classified as lung, gastrointestinal, urological, gynecological, cutaneous, or other. Performance status (PS) was assessed using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale. The presence of dementia was determined by chart review, including either a documented clinical diagnosis or consistent cognitive symptoms noted during clinical interactions.

Treatment intent was categorized as either curative or palliative. Radiotherapy techniques were grouped into stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), or other modalities, including intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and brachytherapy. Radiation dose and fractionation schedules were also recorded.

Treatment completion was evaluated by determining whether the full prescribed course of radiotherapy was delivered without interruption or modification. Treatment-related toxicities were assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0), with grade ≥ 2 events considered clinically significant. Overall survival status and cause of death were recorded when available and classified as cancer-related, senescence-related, or other, with senescence-related death defined as death due to age-related physiological decline without active cancer progression, based on physician judgment and chart documentation.

Endpoints and statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to the date of death or last follow-up. For patients with known causes of death, disease-specific survival (DSS) was also analyzed by distinguishing deaths due to the primary malignancy from those due to other causes.

Treatment completion rates and the incidence of adverse events were analyzed as secondary endpoints. Treatment completion was defined as receiving the entire prescribed dose and all planned fractions without interruption or early termination. Patients who discontinued or modified treatment due to clinical deterioration were categorized as non-completers. Adverse events of any grade were recorded if they occurred during or after radiotherapy. Late adverse events were assessed only in patients with at least 3 months of follow-up after treatment completion. Toxicities were graded according to CTCAE v5.0.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the log-rank test. Prognostic factors for OS were evaluated using both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables that showed statistical significance in univariate analysis or were considered clinically relevant were included in the multivariate model. To avoid overfitting due to the limited number of death events (n = 66), the number of variables entered into the multivariate model was intentionally restricted to those considered most clinically essential. There were no missing data for performance status, dementia, toxicity, or cause of death in this cohort. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 119 patients aged 90 years or older who underwent radiotherapy at our institution between April 2007 and March 2025 were included. The median age was 91 years (range, 90–99), with 67 males (56%) and 52 females (44%). The most common primary tumor site was the lung (27 patients, 23%), followed by cutaneous malignancies (17, 14%), soft tissue sarcomas (9, 8%), gynecological cancers (8, 7%), and esophageal and prostate cancers (5 each, 4%). ECOG performance status (PS) was 0–1 in 85 patients (71%) and ≥ 2 in 34 (29%). Dementia was documented in 14 patients (12%) (Table 1).

The number of fractions was ≤ 10 in 79 cases (66%) and ≥ 11 in 40 cases (34%).

The overall treatment completion rate was high: 113 patients (95.0%) completed the planned radiotherapy course, while 6 patients (5.0%) discontinued treatment. Radiotherapy was delivered as monotherapy in principle, and no patients received concurrent chemotherapy during the treatment period.

Adverse events

Acute adverse events of grade ≥ 2 were observed in 23 patients (19%). The most common toxicity was radiation dermatitis (27 patients, 23%), followed by fatigue and nausea (14 patients, 12%) and radiation pneumonitis (9 patients, 8%). Grade ≥ 3 toxicities occurred in 3 patients (3%), including grade 3 dermatitis (2 cases, 2%) and grade 5 radiation pneumonitis (1 case, 1%).

The fatal pneumonitis case involved a 90-year-old man with recurrent left upper lobe lung cancer after radiofrequency ablation. He received 54 Gy in 18 fractions using VMAT for local and mediastinal recurrence. Despite an ECOG PS of 0 and an active lifestyle, he chose hypofractionation due to scheduling constraints. He developed dyspnea 51 days post-treatment, was hospitalized, and died despite steroid pulse therapy. Pre-existing interstitial pneumonia was suspected.

Late adverse events were evaluated in 103 patients (87%) with follow-up of at least three months. Radiation pneumonitis of grade ≥ 1 was observed in 8 patients (10%): grade 1 in 4, grade 2 in 3, and grade 3 in 1. No grade ≥ 4 late toxicity was reported. Other late adverse events included one case each of grade 2 cataract, grade 3 bacterial pneumonia, grade 3 radiation proctitis, rib fracture, and osteonecrosis (Table 3).

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up period was 12.9 months (range, 0.1–110.9 months). During this time, 66 patients died. Among them, 14 patients (21%) had well-controlled cancer and died of non-cancer causes, mainly age-related senescence.

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a median overall survival (OS) of 31.2 months (95% CI 27.5–35.0) and a mean OS of 41.8 months (95% CI 30.8–52.8). The 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates were 73.7%, 62.1%, and 41.6%, respectively (Fig. 1). When stratified by treatment intent, patients who underwent curative-intent radiotherapy showed significantly better OS than those treated with palliative-intent radiotherapy (median OS, 31.9 vs. 18.5 months; p = 0.009, log-rank test) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study analyzed the real-world clinical practice and outcomes of radiation therapy in cancer patients aged 90 years or older, based on retrospective data from a single institution. As the population continues to age, the management of cancer in nonagenarians is becoming increasingly important from both clinical and societal perspectives. However, large-scale reports focusing specifically on radiation therapy in this age group remain scarce in Japan. Our findings demonstrate that, even in this super-elderly population, radiation therapy can be appropriately selected and safely delivered in a specialized cancer center, with a high treatment completion rate and meaningful therapeutic outcomes.

Treatment completion and safety

The treatment completion rate among patients aged ≥ 90 years was 95%, and the incidence of severe acute treatment-related adverse events (≥ Grade 3) was only 3%, with just one treatment-related death (1%). These findings underscore that radiation therapy can be safely administered to this age group with appropriate patient selection and meticulous planning, reinforcing its feasibility even in the super-elderly population. The recent widespread adoption of stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) and image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) has made it possible to deliver highly precise treatment while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. Our study suggests that such advancements are facilitating the expansion of indications for radiation therapy even in super-elderly patients.

Overall survival and prognostic factors

In this cohort, the median overall survival (OS) was 31.2 months, with 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates of 73.7%, 62.1%, and 41.6%, respectively. According to the 2021 national life table in Japan, the average remaining life expectancy at age 90 is approximately 4.4 years for men and 5.7 years for women [1]. Our data show that a considerable proportion of patients aged ≥ 90 years who underwent radiation therapy achieved multi-year survival, suggesting that treatment intervention may offer survival benefit even in the context of limited life expectancy. In particular, patients receiving curative-intent radiation tended to have better survival outcomes. Multivariate analysis identified good ECOG performance status (PS 0–1) as an independent favorable prognostic factor, highlighting the importance of functional reserve in determining treatment eligibility and survival. Treatment decisions in this population should be based not solely on chronological age, but on a comprehensive assessment of functional status and patient motivation. In this extremely elderly population, overall survival serves as a pragmatic global endpoint that reflects real-world clinical benefit, particularly given the substantial impact of competing risks and non–cancer-related mortality across diverse disease settings.

Comparison with previous studies

Compared with previous studies (Table 8), our findings provide additional context regarding real-world treatment patterns and outcomes in nonagenarian patients, rather than serving as direct comparative evidence. For example, Katano et al. [3] reported on 124 nonagenarian patients in Japan, with 51% undergoing curative-intent radiation. In contrast, our study reported a higher proportion of patients receiving curative treatment (69%). This may reflect our institution’s status as a cancer referral center, where patients are more likely to seek and be considered for aggressive treatment. The low rate of incomplete treatments in our cohort also supports the feasibility of radiation therapy in this age group, provided that patient selection and treatment protocols are appropriately individualized.

In contrast to several European reports [5–7], which primarily focused on palliative radiation, our study featured a higher proportion of curative-intent therapy, suggesting regional differences in treatment strategy. Japan’s healthcare environment, which facilitates the use of high-precision techniques such as IMRT, SBRT, and brachytherapy under public insurance, may contribute to the broader application of definitive radiation therapy in the elderly. In terms of treatment outcomes, our cohort showed favorable overall survival, with 1-year OS of 69% and 2-year OS of 54%, among the highest reported across the selected studies. Notably, the 2-year OS exceeded that of Kocik et al. (2019, ~ 30%) [7] and Oguchi et al. (1998, 30%) [8], and was comparable to Katano et al. (2023, 3-year OS of 38%) [3]. These differences may partly stem from differences in patient selection, treatment intent, and the availability of advanced radiation techniques.

Moreover, the high treatment completion rate (94%) in our study is consistent with or superior to that reported in previous cohorts, ranging from 80 to 97%, further emphasizing the feasibility of radiotherapy in nonagenarians when carefully selected.

Trends in modality selection

SRT was preferentially selected for patients with favorable performance status and was frequently applied to lung lesions and other localized tumors. Given its highly targeted delivery and minimal systemic burden, SRT was particularly appropriate for patients with limited disease and preserved general condition. Notably, hypofractionated schedules of ≤ 10 fractions were employed in 66% of cases, reflecting a clinical preference for shorter courses that reduce physical and logistical strain on very elderly patients. Although a 10-fraction regimen is commonly used for standard palliative irradiation (e.g., 30 Gy in 10 fractions), it was included in the ≤ 10-fraction category in this study to reflect overall treatment duration and patient burden, rather than to imply ultra-short-course therapy. The feasibility and safety of SRT in the super-elderly population have also been documented in previous reports [9–11]. Brachytherapy (BT) was mainly applied to gynecologic and skin tumors, with some cases treated with BT alone and others combined with external beam radiation. The inherent advantage of BT—delivering high local doses in a small number of fractions—makes it particularly suitable for elderly patients [12–16]. Although the cohort included a wide range of primary diseases (lung, gynecologic, prostate, skin, anal canal, and urinary tract cancers), this heterogeneity reflects the diversity of real-world practice in geriatric oncology, although it may also limit the generalizability of the findings to broader cancer populations.

Clinical implications

Our results suggest that even in patients aged 90 and above, curative-intent radiation therapy is feasible and potentially beneficial, particularly in those with preserved performance status. Radiation therapy remains a viable treatment option in the elderly, not only for curative purposes but also for symptom palliation, including SRT or short-course regimens tailored to the patient’s condition and preferences.

In addition, cost-effectiveness is an important consideration when applying advanced radiotherapy techniques in super-elderly patients. In Japan, high-precision radiotherapy such as IMRT, SRT, and brachytherapy is widely accessible under the public insurance system, which differs from the healthcare environments in many European countries. Although formal cost-effectiveness analyses were beyond the scope of this study, the use of highly conformal techniques may reduce treatment-related toxicity, shorten overall treatment duration through hypofractionation, and decrease the need for hospitalization or supportive care. These factors may contribute to an acceptable balance between treatment burden and clinical benefit in carefully selected nonagenarian patients.

Limitations and future directions

To our knowledge, this represents one of the largest single-institution series focusing exclusively on nonagenarian patients undergoing radiotherapy in the modern era, encompassing various advanced techniques including SRT, IMRT, and brachytherapy. Nevertheless, this study has several important limitations.

First, this was a retrospective single-institution analysis and is therefore subject to inherent selection bias. Treatment decisions were influenced by physician judgment and family preferences, and our cohort likely represents a selected population. Patients with severe frailty, profound cognitive impairment, limited caregiver support, or family reluctance may not have been referred to our tertiary center or considered suitable candidates for radiotherapy. Moreover, as a designated cancer center, our institution may attract relatively motivated elderly patients or families seeking aggressive care, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings.

Second, cognitive function, comorbidity burden, frailty, and broader geriatric domains were not comprehensively assessed. Comprehensive geriatric assessment metrics such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index, frailty indices, nutritional status, and socioeconomic factors were not available. In addition, broader outcome measures such as quality of life (QOL), functional decline, caregiver burden, and patient or family satisfaction were not evaluated in this study.

Nevertheless, our analyses did include treatment completion rates and adverse event profiles, as well as exploratory evaluations of causes of treatment interruption and toxicity. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive geriatric assessments and patient-centered outcomes are warranted to better define optimal radiotherapy strategies for super-elderly patients.

Conclusion

Radiotherapy for cancer patients aged ≥ 90 years can be safely and effectively delivered with a high treatment completion rate when applied to appropriately selected individuals, particularly when advanced modalities such as SBRT, IMRT, and brachytherapy are utilized. In a tertiary cancer center setting, these technologies enable individualized treatment strategies that minimize patient burden while achieving meaningful therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, even in palliative settings, modalities such as brachytherapy may contribute to local tumor control and substantial symptom relief, offering tangible benefits beyond conventional supportive care.

Treatment decisions should not rely solely on chronological age but rather be guided by a comprehensive assessment of functional status, comorbidities, cognitive function, frailty, and patient preferences. As the population continues to age, radiotherapy will remain an important component of multidisciplinary cancer management in the super-elderly. Our findings indicate that radiotherapy can be feasible and beneficial for carefully selected nonagenarian patients with preserved functional reserve; however, treatment should always be individualized, with careful consideration of caregiver support and overall patient context.
