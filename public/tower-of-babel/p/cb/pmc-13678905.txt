The probabilistic dependency patterns between interpersonal emotion regulation and prosocial behavior in Chinese adolescents: insights from Gaussian Graphical and Bayesian Network Models
Authors: Mengqi Xiao, Xinyi Huang, Chuyu Yuan, Jiahua Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterpersonal emotion regulation (IER) and prosocial behavior are critical constructs in adolescent socio-emotional development, yet their complex interplay remains poorly understood. This study employed Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM) and Bayesian network analysis to characterize the network structure and probabilistic dependency patterns among four IER strategies and six prosocial behavior tendencies in a sample of Chinese vocational school adolescents. A cross-sectional sample of 2,842 adolescents (Mage = 15.49 years, SD = 1.13; 57.8% female) from 40 vocational schools completed the Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Questionnaire and the Prosocial Behavior Tendencies Measure. GGM identified a regularized network of 10 nodes and 33 edges, in which enhancement of positive affect exhibited the highest bridge centrality, descriptively serving as the most prominent hub connecting IER to emotional and anonymous prosocial behavior tendencies, while perspective-taking and social modeling were negatively associated with anonymous prosocial behavior. Bayesian network analysis identified probabilistic dependency patterns consistent with conditional dependencies between IER and prosocial behavior, including a probable upstream role of social modeling for anonymous prosocial behavior and convergent dependencies from prosocial tendencies onto enhancement of positive affect. Given the cross-sectional design, these patterns are hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Overall, IER and prosocial behavior are closely interconnected at the level of statistical dependence, with enhancement of positive affect functioning as the most prominent descriptive bridge node. Affect-centered strategies represent a plausible hypothesis for promoting adolescent prosocial development and should be tested in future experimental and longitudinal work.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05007-1.Keywords: Interpersonal emotion regulation, Prosocial behavior, Gaussian graphical model, Bayesian network, Adolescents

Introduction

Prosocial behavior — any voluntary action aimed at benefiting others — is foundational to social cooperation and group cohesion [10, 29]. Beyond its societal value, prosocial behavior is consistently linked to enhanced subjective well-being and psychological health in the actor [23, 36]. Adolescence represents a sensitive period for the consolidation of prosocial behavior tendencies, with long-lasting implications for social adaptation [11]. Understanding the psychological mechanisms that underpin prosocial behavior during this developmental window therefore carries both theoretical and practical significance.

Among the many antecedents, emotional processes occupy a central role in prosocial behavior. Interpersonal emotion regulation (IER) — the use of social interactions to regulate one's own or others' emotions — has gained increasing attention. To provide a parsimonious theoretical framework, we draw on Social Baseline Theory (SBT; [7]), which posits that the human brain treats social proximity as a bioenergetic resource, reducing the perceived cost and effort of goal‑directed actions. From this perspective, IER strategies that amplify positive affect through social connection should lower the psychological “effort” required to engage in prosocial acts, making such strategies more central than cognitively demanding strategies (e.g., perspective‑taking or social modeling). Specifically, we hypothesize that: (H1) Enhancement of positive affect (A1) will exhibit the strongest bridge centrality connecting IER and prosocial behavior, because it directly supplies a relational resource that reduces the metabolic and psychological cost of helping. (H2) In contrast, perspective‑taking (A2) and social modeling (A4), which rely on external standards and cognitive evaluation, will show negative or null associations with anonymous prosocial behavior (D4), given that anonymous helping lacks the social visibility required for such strategies to be rewarding.

Although IER and prosocial behavior are closely related, they are not conceptually interchangeable. IER refers specifically to attempts to influence another person’s emotional state through interpersonal processes, whereas prosocial behavior refers more broadly to actions intended to benefit others. Thus, some prosocial acts may function as forms of extrinsic IER when enacted to alter others’ feelings, but not all prosocial behavior is primarily emotion‑regulatory, and not all IER is prosocial in motive or outcome [25]. Moreover, interpersonal emotion regulation is not inherently altruistic. Regulatory efforts directed toward others may be used to comfort and support, but they may also be deployed for instrumental, self‑serving, or socially manipulative purposes, consistent with the “Jekyll and Hyde” perspective on emotion‑regulation knowledge [8]. Theoretically, IER and prosocial behavior share a close and potentially bidirectional relationship. Effective IER facilitates emotional stability in the face of others’ distress, thereby enabling constructive helping responses [23]. Conversely, some prosocial acts may function as forms of extrinsic IER when their proximal goal is to alter another person’s emotional state, although prosocial behavior as a broader construct is not reducible to interpersonal emotion regulation [37]. Moreover, research on “helping as self-regulation” demonstrates that prosocial behavior enhances the actor’s positive affect and physiological well-being, suggesting a pattern of statistical interdependence between the two constructs. However, whether this pattern reflects mutually reinforcing dynamics over time remains a hypothesis that requires longitudinal or experimental confirmation [20].

Despite these theoretical linkages, two gaps remain. First, prevailing regression-based approaches assume unidirectional linear relationships and are ill-suited to capture the complex, multidimensional network structure that likely characterizes the IER — prosocial behavior interface. Second, probabilistic dependency structures and patterns suggestive of interdependent associations between these constructs have rarely been examined, limiting understanding of their dynamic interplay.

To address these gaps, we adopted a network-analytic framework combining Gaussian Graphical Modeling (GGM) and Bayesian network analysis. GGM identifies unique pairwise associations among variables while controlling for all others and characterizes nodal influence through centrality indices [13]. Bayesian network analysis extends this by inferring probabilistic conditional-dependency structures that, under appropriate assumptions, may be consistent with directional pathways among variables [24]. Importantly, in the absence of temporal ordering or experimental intervention, Bayesian networks estimated from cross-sectional data identify probabilistic dependencies rather than causal directionality [31]. Together, these methods afford a principled, data-driven approach to modeling complex psychological systems.

This study aimed to: (1) map the unique pairwise associations among four IER strategies and six prosocial behavior tendencies in Chinese vocational school adolescents; (2) identify which IER strategies serve as bridge nodes linking the two construct domains; and (3) characterize probabilistic dependency structures and infer plausible (but not causal) directional patterns using Bayesian networks. Findings are expected to provide nuanced, theoretically grounded insights into the IER–prosocial behavior system and to generate testable hypotheses for evidence-based potential intervention approaches targeting adolescent socio-emotional development.

Methods

Participants

Using convenience sampling, data were collected from adolescents enrolled in 40 vocational secondary schools in Guangdong Province, China, between September 1 and September 30, 2025. This sampling focused on vocational school students because they represent a large adolescent subpopulation in China, and the research team had established partnerships with local vocational education authorities. The final analytic sample comprised 2,842 participants (1,198 male, 1,644 female; age range: 13–18 years; Mage = 15.49 years, SD = 1.13). The overall item-level missing rate was low (1.84% across all items; range: 0.6%–3.2% per item). Little’s MCAR test was non-significant (χ2 = 1283.41, df = 1219, p = 0.093), supporting the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption underlying multiple imputation. Because GGM-based network analysis cannot accommodate missing data [12], sporadic missing values were imputed using the mice package in R [34] with predictive mean matching for continuous items, m = 20 imputations, and 50 iterations per imputation; convergence was verified by visual inspection of trace plots. Networks were estimated on each imputed dataset and pooled at the matrix level (see Sect. "Data analysis"). The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Guangdong Polytechnic Normal University (Approval No: 20250801), and informed consent was obtained from participants, their parents, and teachers. However, because the sample was drawn exclusively from vocational schools in a single Chinese province (Guangdong), the findings may not generalize to Chinese adolescents attending general secondary schools or to adolescents in other regions or cultural contexts. Generalizations about Chinese adolescent socio-emotional development should therefore be made with caution.

Measures

Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (IERQ)

The IERQ [18] assesses the tendency and efficacy of using interpersonal interactions to regulate one’s emotions. The 20-item scale comprises four subscales: enhancement of positive affect (5 items; node A1), perspective-taking (5 items; A2), soothing (5 items; A3), and social modeling (5 items; A4). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = completely). In the present sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.916 for the total scale and ranged from 0.731 to 0.760 across subscales. Although these subscale reliabilities were acceptable, they were lower than those originally reported by Hofmann et al. ([18]; α = 0.85–0.92), suggesting that the four IER strategies may be less psychometrically distinct in this Chinese vocational-school adolescent sample than in the original validation samples. The implications of this for interpreting intra-IER network structure are discussed in Sect. "Integrating a broader social and intergroup perspective".

Prosocial Behavior Tendencies Measure (PBTM)

The Chinese-adapted PBTM [6] assesses six types of prosocial behavior: emotional (4 items; D1), compliant (2 items; D2), altruistic (5 items; D3), anonymous (5 items; D4), public (4 items; D5), and urgent (3 items; D6). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all like me, 5 = very much like me). Cronbach’s α was 0.946 for the total scale and ranged from 0.732 to 0.830 across subscales. Importantly, the IERQ and PBTM assess different functional constructs: the IERQ indexes adolescents’ habitual use of interpersonal strategies to regulate emotion, whereas the PBTM indexes tendencies to engage in helping behavior across prosocial contexts. Accordingly, the observed network associations are between empirically distinguishable constructs.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed in SPSS 31.0. Network analyses were performed in R 4.5.3, following Epskamp et al.’s [13] standardized pipeline encompassing estimation, visualization, centrality measurement, and accuracy/stability testing.

Network estimation was performed on each of the 20 imputed datasets. Because regularized GGM and Bayesian network estimation cannot be analytically pooled using Rubin’s rules in the same way as parametric estimators, we followed common practice in psychological network research: for the GGM, we averaged partial-correlation matrices across imputations to produce the final network, and conducted bootstrap inference on the averaged matrix; for the Bayesian network, structure learning was repeated within each imputed dataset, and an edge was retained in the final DAG only if it met the within-imputation inclusion criterion in ≥ 80% of the 20 imputations.

Gaussian graphical model network analysis

A regularized partial correlation network was estimated using the qgraph package. The GGM approach, combined with LASSO regularization, identifies unique pairwise associations while removing non-significant edges to enhance model parsimony and robustness [12, 33]. Networks were visualized using the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm; edge thickness reflects association strength, and edge color indicates direction (blue = positive, red = negative).

Node influence was quantified using the Expected Influence (EI) index, with higher EI indicating greater centrality within the overall network. Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) was used to assess the cross-community influence of each node, reflecting its role in connecting the IER and prosocial behavior domains [19]. Following recent methodological critiques [5, 17], we interpret centrality and bridge centrality as descriptive features of the estimated network — reflecting statistical patterns of conditional dependence — rather than as direct indicators of causal leverage or intervention efficacy. Robustness was evaluated via non-parametric bootstrapping (n = 1,000) for edge accuracy (95% CIs) and case-dropping bootstrapping (n = 1,000) for stability of centrality indices, quantified by the CS-coefficient (CS > 0.25 acceptable; CS > 0.50 good). Bootstrapped difference tests (α = 0.05) were used to assess significant differences among edge weights and centrality indices.

Bayesian network analysis

A Bayesian network with a directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure was estimated using the hill-climbing algorithm in the bnlearn package, optimized via the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC; [24, 30]). To ensure structural stability, 1,000 bootstrap resamples were generated [1]. Because the hill-climbing search is conducted on a single cross-sectional snapshot of data collected in September 2025, the directed edges it returns must be interpreted as probabilistic conditional dependencies rather than confirmed causal relationships; identifying causal direction would additionally require temporal ordering, intervention, or strong prior knowledge [26, 31]. Edge retention followed a two-stage procedure [24, 32]. In Stage 1 (edge inclusion), we computed the empirical bootstrap appearance frequency of each candidate edge and applied the optimal-cutpoint method of Scutari and Nagarajan [32], which identifies the data-driven threshold maximizing the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. The empirical optimum in our data was 42.90%; edges appearing in fewer than 42.90% of bootstrap networks were excluded. In Stage 2 (direction assignment), among edges retained in Stage 1, we assigned a direction X → Y to an edge only when its bootstrapped directional proportion exceeded 0.50 (operationalized in R as bootnet&strength > Stage-1 threshold AND bootnet&direction > 0.50).

Throughout, we emphasize that DAG directionality estimated from cross-sectional observational data identifies probabilistic conditional-dependency asymmetries, not causal precedence; valid causal interpretation would require temporal ordering, intervention, or strong prior knowledge [26, 31]. The directions reported here should therefore be read as probabilistic dependency patterns consistent with one factorization within a Markov equivalence class, and as hypotheses to be tested with longitudinal or experimental data. Furthermore, because the Bayesian network framework as implemented here yields directional probabilities and BIC contributions but not signed coefficients, we additionally fitted the linear-Gaussian parameters of the retained DAG using bn.fit() in bnlearn to obtain standardized partial regression coefficients (with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals), which we report alongside the directional probabilities to clarify the sign of each directed edge (see Table 2). In addition, by the acyclicity property of DAGs, the present analysis cannot, even in principle, recover within-dyad feedback cycles: between any pair of nodes the DAG admits at most one directed edge. What it can recover is an asymmetric pattern of conditional dependencies in which different edges across the two construct domains may run in different directions. We therefore use the term “probabilistic dependencies between the IER and prosocial behavior domains” rather than “causal feedback loops between pairs of variables.”

Results

Common method bias

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to examine common method bias. An unrotated exploratory factor analysis of all items indicated that the first factor accounted for 25.48% of the variance, well below the 40% threshold. Because Harman’s test is widely recognized as a relatively weak diagnostic [27, 28], we additionally conducted a confirmatory factor-analytic unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) test. We compared a baseline measurement model (substantive factors only) with a model adding a common method factor on which all items loaded. Adding the method factor produced only a small improvement in fit (ΔCFI = 0.018, ΔRMSEA = −0.004), below the ΔCFI = 0.05 heuristic threshold suggesting serious CMB. Taken together, these two converging — but not definitive — diagnostics suggest that common method bias is unlikely to fully account for the observed associations. Nevertheless, because all measures relied on a single source of self-report and the constructs assessed are socially desirable, we cannot definitively rule out shared-method variance; this limitation is discussed in Sect. "Integrating a broader social and intergroup perspective".

GGM network analysis

Network Structure

The regularized network comprised 10 nodes and 33 non-zero edges, of which 12 were cross-community edges (6 positive, 6 negative; Fig. 1). The strongest positive cross-community edge linked A1 (enhancement of positive affect) to D1 (emotional prosocial behavior; edge weight = 0.06), followed by A1–D4 (anonymous; weight = 0.04). The strongest negative cross-community edges connected A2 (perspective-taking)–D4 and A4 (social modeling)–D4 (both weight = − 0.03). These cross-community partial correlations, though statistically stable in this large sample, are small in absolute magnitude (|r| ≈ 0.03–0.06) and should be interpreted as candidate associations of limited practical magnitude rather than as strong effects (see Sect. "Cross-community network associations"). In particular, the negative partial correlations involving perspective‑taking (A2) and social modeling (A4) with anonymous prosocial behavior (D4) are especially susceptible to suppression effects or shared‑variance artifacts, and should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct negative psychological process without further replication. Following 1,000 case-dropping bootstrap iterations, the CS-coefficients for both EI and BEI were 0.75, indicating excellent stability (see Supplementary Figure S1). The directional probability graph (Fig. 3) revealed that the strongest cross-community directed edge was A4 (social modeling) → D4 (anonymous prosocial behavior; directional probability = 0.96; standardized β = −0.04, 95% CI [−0.06, −0.02]). The negative sign — consistent with the corresponding GGM partial correlation (r = −0.03) — was not estimated by the directional-probability output of the Bayesian network itself but obtained from the linear-Gaussian DAG fit (bn.fit), and is therefore best characterized as a probabilistically negative dependency pattern rather than a directly estimated negative effect. The next strongest cross-community edges were D1 (emotional) → A1 (enhancement of positive affect; directional probability = 0.94; standardized β = 0.05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.08]) and D4 → A1 (directional probability = 0.93; standardized β = 0.04, 95% CI [0.01, 0.07]), both positive, suggesting a probabilistic dependency pattern in which emotional and anonymous prosocial behavior tendencies co-vary with subsequent endorsement of enhancement of positive affect, consistent with — but not establishing — a mutually reinforcing IER–prosocial process.

Discussion

This study is the first to integrate GGM and Bayesian network analysis to systematically characterize the interplay between IER strategies and prosocial behavior tendencies in adolescents. Within the inferential limits of cross-sectional self-report data, our findings collectively portray a network of mutually entangled statistical dependencies rather than a simple linear causal chain, advancing understanding of the IER–prosocial behavior system in several important respects. Beyond replicating the well — established broad association between emotion regulation and prosocial behavior, the network approach yields three substantive theoretical insights that traditional regression methods cannot provide. First, the GGM reveals a differentiated pattern of strategy — specific associations: enhancement of positive affect uniquely bridges the two domains, whereas perspective — taking and social modeling show negative (or null) partial correlations with anonymous prosocial behavior — a finding that challenges the undifferentiated view that all IER strategies uniformly promote prosociality. Second, bridge centrality analysis moves beyond bivariate correlations to identify enhancement of positive affect as the most prominent descriptive hub, suggesting that affect — centered strategies may be statistically more entangled with prosocial tendencies than cognitively effortful strategies. Third, Bayesian network analysis uncovers asymmetric probabilistic dependencies (e.g., D1 → A1, D4 → A1) that are consistent with — though not proving — a directional pattern in which prosocial behavior is conditionally associated with greater endorsement of affect — enhancing regulation, generating novel hypotheses about the possible asymmetric interplay between the two domains. This challenges a purely unidirectional view (regulation → behavior) and suggests that prosocial tendencies may also statistically anticipate reliance on affect‑centered regulatory strategies.

Cross-community network associations

GGM analysis identified a differentiated pattern of cross-community associations. Enhancement of positive affect (A1) exhibited the strongest positive links with emotional (D1) and anonymous (D4) prosocial behavior tendencies. It is important to note at the outset that these cross-community edges, while statistically stable in our large sample, are small in absolute magnitude (|r| ≈ 0.03–0.06 after LASSO regularization). Such magnitudes are consistent with typical cross-domain partial correlations in psychological networks [12] but should not be interpreted as strong evidence of intervention leverage. Throughout the discussion below, we therefore frame these patterns as candidate associations whose practical significance must be established by future experimental or longitudinal work.

Consistent with SBT, the positive A1–D1 and A1–D4 connections suggest that individuals who habitually use positive affect enhancement perceive helping as less costly in terms of metabolic and psychological effort, because the social interaction itself supplies bioenergetic resources [7]. This mechanism uniquely explains why A1 — rather than other IER strategies — serves as the primary bridge node: it directly reduces the effort of prosocial action.

The positive A1–D4 association is theoretically noteworthy because anonymous helping, driven by intrinsic motivation and uncoupled from social rewards [9], might be expected to be less susceptible to social affective processes. Its positive link with A1 suggests that individuals who derive well-being from positive social experiences may accumulate psychological resources that enable other-oriented concern even absent external reinforcement [36].

In contrast, the cognitive-regulatory strategies — perspective-taking (A2) and social modeling (A4) — were negatively associated with anonymous prosocial behavior, although the partial correlations were small in absolute magnitude (|r| ≈ 0.03). Two complementary interpretations of this small negative partial association should be considered. First, the pattern is consistent with a classical statistical suppression effect [22]: because LASSO partial correlations control for shared variance with all other prosocial subscales — in particular the closely related altruistic (D3) and emotional (D1) tendencies — the residualised association between cognitive-regulatory IER strategies and anonymous helping isolates only the component of anonymous-helping variance not shared with general prosociality. Under this interpretation, the negative sign should not be read as evidence that perspective-taking or social modeling “reduces” anonymous helping, but as a within-network artefact of partialling out a highly intercorrelated set of prosocial tendencies. Second, to the extent that the negative partial correlations also reflect a substantive (if very small) inverse relationship. From an SBT perspective, perspective‑taking and social modeling are cognitively effortful strategies that do not directly reduce the bioenergetic cost of helping; instead, they rely on external social standards. Anonymous helping, which offers no audience or reputational reward, may therefore fail to engage these strategies. This interpretation is consistent with the negative partial correlations observed, although the small effect size cautions against over‑interpretation. However, two alternative explanations warrant consideration. First, this negative partial correlation may be a statistical artifact of the LASSO-regularized partial correlation network, where cognitive-regulatory strategies “compete” with other prosocial subscales (e.g., altruistic D3, emotional D1) for shared variance, producing a suppression effect rather than reflecting a genuine psychological inhibition [22]. This possibility is particularly relevant given that perspective‑taking is traditionally viewed as a driver of altruistic motivation [11]; the observed negative association therefore appears counterintuitive and may be better explained by statistical suppression than by a direct inhibitory effect. Second, the finding may be specific to the present vocational-school sample, whose peer norms and social evaluation pressures could differ from general adolescent populations. Indeed, social modeling inherently creates dependency on external validation [25], and anonymous helping removes that audience, which might be particularly aversive in a context where social approval is a salient motivator. Given the very small absolute magnitudes involved, the present data cannot empirically arbitrate between a substantive psychological mechanism, a suppression artifact, or sample‑specific characteristics; future replication across diverse adolescent samples is needed.We offer an integrative interpretation that best fits the evidence: the negative partial correlations (|r| ≈ 0.03) are most parsimoniously explained as a statistical suppression effect, emerging only after controlling for shared variance with other prosocial subscales. If a genuine negative relationship exists, it would likely reflect that cognitive‑regulatory strategies (perspective‑taking, social modeling) depend on external audience feedback — absent in anonymous helping — whereas affect‑centered strategies do not. Critically, the very small effect sizes argue against any strong claim of “inhibition”; at most, these cognitive strategies are simply not engaged in anonymous helping contexts. Future research should test whether these negative partial correlations replicate in non‑vocational samples and, if so, whether they are fully accounted for by suppression or reflect a context‑dependent motivational mechanism. A conceptual boundary condition should be noted. The present findings highlight positive associations between IER and prosocial behavior, but interpersonal emotion regulation is not inherently prosocial. Drawing on the “Jekyll and Hyde” perspective [8], emotion-regulation knowledge can facilitate both prosocial and interpersonally deviant behavior depending on the actor’s motives. In the present adolescent sample, we did not assess regulatory goals; thus, the observed positive associations may partly reflect contexts where IER is used for affiliation rather than manipulation, and future research should distinguish altruistic from self‑serving regulatory aims.

Bridge centrality: enhancement of positive affect as the key hub

Bridge centrality analysis confirmed enhancement of positive affect (A1) as the most prominent descriptive bridge connecting IER and prosocial behavior. This positions A1 as the most likely descriptive conduit through which emotion regulation co-varies with prosocial motivation. Mechanistically, positive affect broadens attentional scope and social approach motivation, which may facilitate prosocial engagement [16]. Consistent with Social Baseline Theory [7], individuals who efficiently obtain positive affect through social interaction may perceive the effort of helping others as less costly, potentially reducing the regulatory burden of prosocial action.

We emphasize that high bridge centrality of A1 does not by itself imply that targeting A1 is a causally efficacious intervention target. Centrality indices can be driven by measurement structure (e.g., shared item content across subscales), by partial-correlation artifacts arising in cross-sectional data, and by patterns of statistical dependency that need not reflect causal influence [5, 17]. The potential implications for practice discussed below should therefore be regarded as hypotheses to be tested experimentally, not as conclusions derivable from the present network estimates.

These findings generate hypotheses with potential practical implications. Interventions oriented toward moral reasoning or cognitive empathy training may be insufficient — and may inadvertently suppress certain prosocial forms — unless they also cultivate the capacity for positive social affect. Whether focusing on A1 — for example, through programs designed to foster positive social experiences, humor, or expressive social skills — is related to greater or more robust prosocial outcomes remains an exploratory hypothesis, which the present descriptive findings can only motivate, not answer. This hypothesis aligns with the positive psychology perspective that fostering positive experiences is a potent lever for promoting prosocial character and behavior.

Bayesian network: probabilistic dependency patterns

Before interpreting individual directed edges, we emphasize three inferential constraints. First, the present data are cross-sectional; the directional probabilities reported by the Bayesian network reflect statistical asymmetries in conditional dependence rather than temporal precedence or causal direction. Without temporal ordering, intervention, or strong prior knowledge, Bayesian network search can only recover a member of the Markov equivalence class of DAGs [26, 31]. The interpretations below are therefore offered as hypotheses about underlying dynamics, to be tested through longitudinal or experimental designs. Second, the 23 retained edges differ markedly in directional stability (range = 0.51–0.96; see Table 2). We focus interpretive emphasis only on the eight Tier-1 (Robust; directional probability ≥ 0.85) edges; Tier-2 (Moderate) edges are reported with appropriate hedging, and Tier-3 (Tentative; 0.50–0.64) edges — for which the data are nearly indifferent between two directions — are noted for completeness but should not bear interpretive weight. Third, the directional probabilities themselves do not encode the sign of an effect. The directionally negative interpretation of A4 → D4, and the directionally positive interpretation of D1 → A1 and D4 → A1, are based on the standardized partial regression coefficients obtained from bn.fit() applied to the retained DAG (see Table 2), together with the corresponding GGM partial correlations.

Bayesian network analysis provided a probabilistic dependency structure complementing the GGM’s undirected associations. The strongest cross-community directed edge, A4 → D4 (directional probability = 0.96; standardized β = −0.04), suggests that social modeling is probabilistically positioned upstream of, and negatively associated with, anonymous prosocial behavior. Because social modeling is inherently oriented toward external behavioral standards, it may be associated with reduced engagement in behaviors that bypass social visibility. From a motivational perspective, adolescents who rely on social modeling may perceive anonymous helping as less rewarding or even aversive, because it offers no opportunity for social validation or reputational gain — key reinforcers for this regulatory strategy. This does not imply that social modeling actively inhibits prosocial motivation; rather, it suggests that the perceived value of helping decreases when external audience feedback is removed.

Critically, the Bayesian analysis revealed two Tier-1 directionally robust pathways from prosocial behavior to IER: D1 → A1 (directional probability = 0.94; standardized β = 0.05) and D4 → A1 (directional probability = 0.93; standardized β = 0.04). These pathways indicate that the probabilistic dependency structure consistently places both emotional and anonymous prosocial behavior tendencies upstream of enhancement of positive affect as an IER strategy. Although the cross-sectional design precludes claims of causal direction or feedback, this dependency pattern is consistent with SBT’s prediction of asymmetric conditional associations: prosocial behavior may be conditionally associated with increased use of affect‑enhancing IER strategies, possibly reflecting probabilistic dependencies that warrant further investigation in longitudinal research.

Together, GGM and Bayesian results converge on a coherent descriptive picture: affect-centered IER co-varies with prosocial behavior, which in turn statistically anticipates greater reliance on that same regulatory capacity, a dependency pattern consistent with — though not establishing — an asymmetric pattern of probabilistic dependencies or exploratory directional structures spanning both the IER and prosocial behavior domains. We emphasize that, by the acyclicity property of DAGs, these identified exploratory directional patterns do not, and cannot, represent feedback loops. We emphasise that, by the acyclicity property of DAGs, this domain-level bidirectional pattern does not, and cannot, represent within-dyad feedback. This pattern of statistical interdependence suggests that IER and prosocial behavior tendencies exhibit asymmetric conditional dependencies that are consistent with — but do not constitute — mutually shaping processes across development. We refer to this as a ‘bidirectional dependency pattern’ rather than a ‘feedback loop’, because the latter implies causal directionality and temporal sequencing that cross-sectional data cannot support. Causal claims about feedback dynamics require longitudinal or experimental confirmation.

A clearer conceptual distinction between IER and prosocial behavior is also necessary when interpreting these findings. In the present study, we do not treat the two domains as interchangeable. Rather, IER is conceptualized as a process involving attempts to shape another person’s affective state, whereas prosocial behavior is conceptualized as a broader class of other-benefiting actions. This means that some behaviors may fall into both domains under specific functional conditions, but other prosocial actions may not primarily regulate emotion, and some forms of IER may not be prosocial at all. This distinction helps ensure that the observed network associations are interpreted as substantive relations between partially overlapping constructs rather than as mere definitional redundancy.

This distinction also points to an important boundary condition. Interpersonal regulation of emotion may support altruism and social connection, but it may also be used for impression management, compliance-gaining, or other self-serving interpersonal goals. In line with the “Jekyll and Hyde” account, emotion-regulation capacities may therefore amplify both socially desirable and socially undesirable tendencies depending on the motives with which they are deployed [8]. Future research should test these possibilities more directly by distinguishing regulatory goal, prosocial intent, and emotional target outcome at the measurement level.

Integrating a broader social and intergroup perspective

Our network analysis focused on individual‑level dependencies, but prosocial behavior is also shaped by intergroup perceptions, norms, and perceived injustice. For example, Borinca, Tankır, Barqa, and Cakal [3] found that empathy and perceived injustice drive solidarity — based responses to civilian suffering; Borinca, Tropp, and Ofosu [4] showed that meta — humanization moderates reactions to cross‑group prosocial behavior; and Borinca, Andrighetto, Valsecchi, and Berent [2] demonstrated that ingroup norms influence the acceptance of outgroup help. These findings suggest that our observed links between IER and prosocial tendencies — such as the negative association between cognitive‑regulatory strategies and anonymous prosocial behavior — may be moderated by group boundaries, normative expectations, or helper motives. Future research should therefore test how intergroup bias and social norms interact with the probabilistic dependency patterns identified here.

The socio-emotional context of chinese vocational school adolescents

In China, vocational education is recognized as an education pathway of equal importance to general education, with student selection driven by vocational interests, skill development, and family socioeconomic background rather than merely academic performance (1). Empirical studies indicate that vocational students generally exhibit higher levels of shyness, loneliness, internet addiction, and lower overall self-esteem compared to general high school students (2), although some research reports higher global self-esteem but lower “above-average” effects, suggesting more conservative self-enhancement (3). Perceived stigma and self-identity are typically moderate to low, with stigma higher among students from restructured families, and self-stigma negatively related to professional identity (7); vocational students also report lower self-identity and more mental health concerns than their peers (8). Additionally, self-esteem mediates the link between family socioeconomic status and peer acceptance (5), and significant group differences are observed in academic resilience (9). Notably, no direct empirical studies have addressed the mechanisms linking cognitive regulation strategies (e.g., perspective taking, social modeling) and anonymous prosocial behavior in this population, representing a critical research gap. We propose that the stronger dependence on external social feedback seen in vocational students—possibly due to higher perceived stigma and weaker self-identity—may mean that the absence of immediate social reinforcement in anonymous prosocial acts fails to meet their socio-emotional needs, resulting in a negative association between cognitive regulation and anonymous prosocial behavior. This hypothesis merits further empirical testing.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and constrains the interpretation of Bayesian network directionality, as detailed in Sect. "Bayesian network: probabilistic dependency patterns". Although Bayesian networks can characterize probabilistic dependency structures, their identifiability as causal models requires temporal ordering, experimental intervention, or strong external assumptions [26]. Future work should employ longitudinal network analysis or experimental designs, potentially including ecological momentary assessment of within-person IER–prosocial dynamics, to test the directional hypotheses generated by the present analysis. Second, exclusive reliance on self-report measures introduces social desirability bias; this is particularly concerning given that both IER and prosocial behavior are socially desirable constructs, and that the two CMB diagnostics we report (Harman’s single-factor test and the ULMC approach) are themselves widely recognized as limited [27, 28]. Triangulation with behavioral observation, peer nomination [21], dyadic-interaction designs, behavioral economic paradigms (e.g., dictator and trust games), or neuroimaging [35] would enhance ecological validity and substantially mitigate single-source bias. Third, the sample was drawn exclusively from Chinese vocational schools in a single province, substantially limiting generalizability. Vocational school adolescents may differ systematically from general secondary school populations in socio-economic background, educational pathways, peer ecology, and opportunities for prosocial engagement. Moreover, the study did not collect data on socioeconomic status or academic achievement—variables known to influence emotion regulation and prosocial behavior—so our network estimates may partly reflect unmeasured confounding. Cross-cultural and cross-educational-track replication is therefore warranted, and claims about Chinese adolescents or adolescent socio-emotional development more broadly must be treated as tentative until such replication is conducted. Fourth, network analysis was conducted at the subscale level, which assumes within-construct homogeneity that may not hold for multidimensional scales such as the PBTM. Item-level or latent-variable network approaches (e.g., Latent Network Models; [14]) may reveal finer-grained structure and represent a valuable direction for future research. Fifth, the cross-community partial correlations identified by the GGM are small in absolute magnitude (|r| ≈ 0.03–0.06), and centrality/bridge-centrality indices are descriptive features of the estimated network rather than direct indicators of intervention efficacy [5]. Although we used SBT as the unifying framework, the cross‑sectional design prevents us from testing its core mechanism (i.e., reduction of metabolic cost). Future studies should experimentally manipulate social proximity or use ecological momentary assessment to directly test the effort‑saving hypothesis. The clinical and educational implications discussed above should therefore be regarded as hypotheses to be tested experimentally. Sixth, IERQ subscale reliabilities in the present sample (α = 0.731–0.760) were modest and lower than those originally reported by Hofmann et al. ([18]; α = 0.85–0.92), and the Bayesian network revealed substantially stronger intra-IER dependencies (e.g., A2 → A3, β = 0.41; A2 → A1, β = 0.32; A3 → A4, β = 0.30) than cross-community dependencies. This pattern raises the possibility that the four IER strategies are not fully psychometrically distinct in Chinese vocational-school adolescents, and that some of the intra-IER network structure may reflect content overlap among regulatory strategies rather than separable mechanisms. Replication using item-level or bifactor network analyses, or culturally adapted IER instruments validated in Chinese adolescent samples, is therefore warranted before strong conclusions are drawn about the internal architecture of the IER domain. Finally, future studies should incorporate additional individual difference variables — such as personality traits, attachment style, and parenting practices — to construct more comprehensive models of adolescent socio-emotional development [15].

Conclusion

Combining GGM and Bayesian network analysis, this study provides novel descriptive evidence that IER and prosocial behavior form a network of closely interconnected statistical dependencies in Chinese vocational school adolescents Enhancement of positive affect emerged as the most prominent descriptive bridge linking emotion regulation to prosocial motivation in our network, and the probabilistic dependency structure was consistent patterns of statistical interdependence between IER and prosocial behavior. At the same time, these findings should not be taken to imply that IER and prosocial behavior are identical constructs or that interpersonal regulation is inherently prosocial. Conceptually, IER concerns the regulation of others’ emotions, whereas prosocial behavior concerns actions intended to benefit others more broadly; the two domains therefore overlap only partially. Likewise, interpersonal emotion regulation may serve supportive and affiliative ends, but it may also be used instrumentally or strategically, consistent with evidence that emotion-regulation knowledge can facilitate both prosocial and interpersonally deviant behavior depending on the actor’s motivational orientation [8, 25]. Because the present data are cross-sectional and self-reported, these patterns do not by themselves establish causal feedback. Nevertheless, they motivate affect-centered strategies as a promising hypothesis to be tested in future experimental and longitudinal work on adolescent prosocial development in this population, while noting that interpersonal emotion regulation is not reducible to prosocial behavior and may serve self‑protective orinstrumental functions depending on the actor’s motives [8].
