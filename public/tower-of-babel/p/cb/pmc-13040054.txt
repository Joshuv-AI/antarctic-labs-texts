A deep learning-based IoT malware detection approach for electric vehicle charging stations
Authors: Lin Xia, Yuanhe Chen, Lin Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As Electric Vehicle (EV) charging stations become increasingly intelligent, more Internet of Things (IoT) devices are being integrated to improve operational efficiency. Efficient and accurate detection of IoT malware is crucial to ensuring the stable and reliable operation of these charging systems. However, existing research in this field still exhibits three major limitations. First, most existing research lack unified adaptability across multiple CPU architectures. They are typically designed for a single architecture, such as ARM or MIPS, which limits their applicability in heterogeneous IoT environments. Second, the feature extraction dimensions in most approaches are limited, making it difficult to comprehensively capture both the structural and behavioral characteristics of malware. Third, existing multimodal feature fusion methods lack dynamic weighting mechanisms and rely on simplistic fusion strategies, making it difficult to achieve deep integration and effective analysis of multimodal features. To address the aforementioned limitations, this paper proposes a deep learning-based IoT malware detection approach for EV charging stations. The approach first incorporates a decompilation tool to convert opcodes from multiple CPU architectures into a unified intermediate representation, PCode, enabling consistent feature modeling across heterogeneous architectures. To enhance the comprehensiveness and discriminative power of feature representation, the approach integrates three types of features: global structural features derived from binary grayscale images, as well as statistical and semantic features extracted from PCode. Each feature type is processed using a dedicated deep-learning algorithm for its characteristics. Finally, a multimodal feature fusion model is designed, using a dynamic weighting mechanism and a multi-layer encoder to achieve deep integration and interactive modeling of the three feature types. Experiments conducted on the public IoT malware datasets demonstrate that the proposed approach outperforms existing mainstream approaches in metrics such as F1, achieving an improvement of 1.37%, which validates its effectiveness in IoT malware detection area.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of information technologies, IoT devices have been increasingly adopted in EV charging stations, significantly enhancing operational efficiency and intelligence1–7. The integration of devices such as smart meters, temperature sensors, and relays has made the charging process more efficient, convenient, and precise. For instance, smart meters8 can monitor the real-time charging status of EVs and cooperate with back-end systems to optimize power distribution by automatically adjusting charging power, thereby enabling dynamic load management. Additionally, temperature sensors monitor thermal changes at charging equipment and cable connection points in real time, enabling early detection of temperature rise risks caused by overloads, malfunctions, or environmental anomalies.

However, alongside the enhanced intelligence brought by these advanced IoT devices, new security vulnerabilities have inevitably emerged9–15. Attackers may exploit hardware or software vulnerabilities in IoT devices, thereby launching attacks on the core systems of charging stations or even implanting malware16–18. Such attacks may intercept communication data, alter transmitted content, or implant backdoor programs to steal users’ personal information or payment data, posing serious threats to user privacy and the economic security of the charging infrastructure19,20. More critically, attackers who gain control over the power dispatching system of a charging station via malware can manipulate charging loads, leading to imbalanced power distribution. This may result in grid overload or equipment failure, severely disrupting the stable operation of the power system and threatening societal stability21. In recent years, the global power industry has witnessed multiple cyberattacks triggered by IoT malware. For instance, in 2022, IoT devices embedded in wind turbines manufactured by the German company Enercon were compromised by malware deployed by hackers22, resulting in the disconnection of over 6,000 turbines and severely disrupting normal operations. Similarly, hackers infiltrated the corporate intranet of an Indian nuclear power plant by implanting malware into IoT devices, ultimately gaining control privileges and posing a serious threat to the plant’s operational security23. Therefore, the ability to detect malware within IoT devices at EV charging stations accurately has become a key challenge in advancing the intelligent development of EV charging infrastructure.

In the field of IoT malware detection, existing methods are generally categorized into two major types: static detection and dynamic detection. Static detection is performed before an executable is deployed to an IoT device, where static features are extracted from the file and evaluated by a detection model to determine whether it is benign. Only executables classified as benign are allowed to be installed on the device. Because static detection does not rely on runtime behavior, offers fast processing, and is relatively easy to deploy, it is the most common approach in practical applications. Moreover, static detection does not require deploying the model on resource-constrained edge devices, which reduces demands on memory usage, transmission latency, and computational complexity. In contrast, dynamic detection monitors software behavior in real time on IoT edge devices by collecting behavioral features such as system calls, memory activity, or network interactions. When abnormal or malicious behaviors are detected, an alert is immediately sent to the cloud or control center. Although dynamic detection can capture richer behavioral signals and identify more sophisticated attacks, it requires the detection model to operate continuously on the device, thus imposing significantly higher demands on computational resources, energy consumption, and network conditions. As a result, its deployment cost is substantially higher. This paper focuses on a static, centralized detection scenario for IoT malware. In this setting, feature analysis and maliciousness assessment are performed in a computation-rich centralized environment prior to software deployment, ensuring that only safe executables are distributed to IoT devices.

To address the limitation (1), this paper incorporates the decompilation tool Ghidra28 to uniformly process IoT executable files originating from various CPU architectures, including ARM, x86, AArch64, Intel 80386, and MIPS. Ghidra offers cross-platform decompilation capabilities, enabling the translation of binary code from diverse instruction set architectures into an intermediate representation language known as Pcode. Pcode is a platform-independent intermediate language that preserves the core semantics and logic of program instructions while aligning syntax across CPU architectures, enabling consistent representation of malicious behavior regardless of the underlying instruction set.

To address limitation (2), this paper proposes a multi-perspective feature extraction method aimed at comprehensively enhancing the representational and discriminative capabilities of the model for IoT malware features. The proposed method extracts three key types of features, enabling modeling and analysis from the perspectives of global structural patterns, Pcode statistical characteristics, and Pcode semantic behavioral information. First, the binary file of the malware is read byte by byte and converted into a grayscale image. A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is then employed to extract spatial distribution patterns and global structural features from the image. These features effectively capture the overall instruction layout of the malware and are particularly useful for identifying malicious samples with distinctive structural patterns. Second, this paper models opcode sequences based on the platform-independent intermediate language Pcode, using the Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) algorithm for vectorization to extract frequency distribution features. A Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) is then constructed to learn and analyze the resulting statistical feature vectors. Third, a redundancy elimination algorithm is applied to preprocess the Pcode sequence, mitigating the impact of redundant information on feature extraction. Subsequently, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is used to perform sequential modeling on the refined Pcode, capturing contextual dependencies and deep semantic features between instructions. By modeling these three feature types, the approach broadens feature coverage and improves characterization of complex malware, laying a solid data foundation for accurate malware classification.

To address the limitation (3), this paper proposes a multimodal feature analysis model by embedding a 1-Dimensional CNN (1DCNN) into the Transformer Encoder architecture. In this model, the multi-head attention mechanism dynamically allocates weights to the input multimodal feature vectors, adaptively adjusting their contributions based on their importance in malware samples. The embedded 1DCNN module performs local convolution on the serialized feature vectors to capture latent local patterns, enhancing the model’s ability to identify critical behavioral features. The multi-layer encoder structure further models the fused features at a deeper level, strengthening inter-feature information exchange and multimodal collaborative analysis, thereby improving malware detection accuracy.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed IoT malware detection approach, this paper adopts the publicly available datasets that are widely recognized and authoritative within the cybersecurity research community: the malware dataset provided by Yokohama National University (Japan)29, and the well-known malware sample sharing platform VirusShare30. In particular, to validate the proposed approach’s generalizability and adaptability across heterogeneous environments, the experiments focus on five mainstream CPU architectures commonly found in IoT terminals of EV charging stations: AArch64, ARM, Intel 80386, MIPS, and x86. Experimental results show that the proposed approach outperforms mainstream detection approaches, achieving a 1.37% improvement in overall F1, confirming its effectiveness and superiority in IoT malware detection. Related work

In the field of IoT malware detection, some researchers have begun addressing the issue of CPU architecture adaptability. HaddadPajouh et al.31 proposed a behavior-based IoT malware detection method, but their research was limited exclusively to ARM-based devices. Similarly, Dovom et al.32 focused their research on malware analysis under the ARM architecture, lacking support for compatibility with other mainstream platforms. Phu et al.33 concentrated on malware detection for the MIPS architecture, and their approach also exhibited strong architecture dependency. Although Hamad et al.34 considered malware samples from ARM, MIPS, and PowerPC architectures in their research, their detection process involved separately modeling and processing each architecture, rather than proposing a unified cross-architecture detection framework. This limited the generalizability of their method and hindered its practical deployment in heterogeneous IoT environments. Alandjani et al.35 conducted separate analyses for ARM and x64 architectures, but the range of architectures did not adequately reflect the diversity of processor platforms commonly found in IoT systems. Some studies have attempted to construct architecture-agnostic detection methods. For instance, Zhao et al.36 proposed a method for multi-architecture IoT malware analysis that uses the B2R2 framework37 to convert binary instructions from ARM, Intel 80386, MIPS, and x86 architectures into a unified intermediate language. However, the B2R2 tool has limited translation capabilities, often producing complex and redundant intermediate representations that hinder feature extraction and reduce detection effectiveness in complex scenarios.

In the field of feature extraction for IoT malware, numerous researchers have explored various feature dimensions and proposed a range of feature-based detection methods. Su et al.38 converted malware binary files directly into grayscale images and employed lightweight CNN for classification, aiming to identify structural patterns of malware from a visual perspective. Similarly, Panda et al.39 also analyzed executable file grayscale images, leveraging deep learning techniques to extract structural features. While such approaches can reflect the global distribution patterns of malware instructions to some extent, they rely solely on the macro-level structural information provided by grayscale images and struggle to capture behavioral logic and semantic features, resulting in limited detection capability. To enhance feature discriminability, Peters et al.40 abandoned the use of grayscale images and instead extracted opcodes from executable files using decompilation tools, modeling their statistical properties such as term frequency. Yewale et al.41 also adopted opcode frequency-based features for classification. These methods compensate for the lack of structural features through statistical modeling, but they overlook the contextual semantic relationships among opcodes, limiting the model’s ability to understand complex behavioral patterns. To address these limitations, Manoharan et al.42 introduced semantic modeling of opcodes to better capture instruction logic. Yan et al.43 employed LSTM networks to model opcode sequences and capture contextual dependencies between instructions. Although such methods offer advantages in extracting deep semantic features, relying solely on semantic information makes it difficult to capture critical structural and frequency-based behavioral characteristics of malware. Additionally, some studies have attempted to combine multiple feature types to improve the comprehensiveness of modeling. For example, Kumar et al.44 incorporated both header information and opcode features for malware detection. Mai et al.45 simultaneously transformed binary files and opcode sequences into grayscale images for joint analysis; and Lai et al.46 proposed an IoT malware detection method based on Control Flow Graphs (CFG), in which a CFG model is constructed by integrating opcodes with Program Structural Information (PSI), and feature extraction and classification are performed on the resulting graph. However, this approach primarily focuses on static modeling at the graph-structure level and fails to effectively capture the contextual semantic information embedded within the opcode sequences.

In the area of multimodal feature fusion, several researchers have explored combining different types of features to improve the accuracy of IoT malware detection. Mai et al.45 extracted grayscale images from both the binary file and opcode sequence of malware and merged them for subsequent feature extraction and classification using a multi-layer CNN. Although this method achieves structural-level information fusion, it fails to adequately account for the semantic correlations and representational differences between different feature modalities. This may lead to information redundancy or interference, hindering the model’s ability to focus on the most salient and discriminative features. Li et al.47 further extended this idea by converting three types of features–opcodes, binary files, and strings–into grayscale images and merging them into a single RGB image, which is then analyzed using a CNN. While this approach achieves a form of three-modal fusion, it still relies on static concatenation and does not model the varying importance or heterogeneous structure of different modalities, thus limiting the effectiveness of the fusion process. Jeon et al.48 proposed a multi-channel image fusion method specifically for IoT malware detection. They transformed five types of features–binary files, opcode sequences, Application Programming Interface (API) text, API statistical features, and strings–into image representations and used CNNs to extract feature vectors from each. These five vectors were then concatenated and passed to a FNN for classification. Although this method enhances feature diversity, it similarly lacks a weighting mechanism during the fusion stage. Without modeling the complementarity or redundancy among features, it struggles to emphasize critical information effectively, thereby constraining its overall detection performance.

Framework of the proposed IoT malware detection approach

This paper proposes a deep learning-based IoT malware detection approach for EV charging stations, aiming to enhance detection accuracy and robustness in multi-architecture environments. The approach consists of four key components. First, in the global structural feature analysis model, malware binaries are converted into gray-scale images, and spatial patterns are extracted using a CNN to generate global structural features. Second, the Pcode statistical feature analysis model uses the Ghidra decompiler to convert opcodes from different CPU architectures into the platform-independent intermediate representation Pcode. TF-IDF is then used to vectorize the Pcode, and a FNN models its statistical distribution. Third, in the Pcode semantic feature analysis model, redundant Pcode sequences are removed, and an LSTM network is applied to capture contextual dependencies and semantic relationships, producing semantic feature vectors. Finally, the multimodal feature analysis model fuses the three types of features, applies a multi-head attention mechanism for dynamic weighting, and uses a multi-layer encoder to enhance deep feature interaction and representation. The proposed detection approach can effectively capture structural, statistical, and semantic characteristics of malware, enabling accurate detection across heterogeneous IoT environments. Image process

After converting executable files into grayscale images, a CNN is employed to analyze the image representations. Convolutional layers are used to capture local spatial patterns, while pooling operations reduce dimensionality and retain salient structural information. The resulting feature vectors encode global structural characteristics of the executable and are subsequently used for malware classification. The proposed IoT malware detection approach

This paper proposes a static malware detection approach designed for IoT environments. The approach integrates four complementary components: a global structural feature analysis model, a Pcode statistical feature analysis model, a Pcode semantic feature analysis model, and a multimodal feature fusion model. The first component, the global structural feature analysis model, extracts binary information from the executable and converts it into a grayscale image to reveal the file’s overall structural pattern. A CNN is then used to learn deep structural representations from the grayscale image, producing feature vectors that capture global layout characteristics and providing the basis for subsequent multimodal analysis. The second component, the Pcode statistical feature analysis model, focuses on the intermediate representation generated during decompilation. By examining instruction frequencies and statistical patterns within the Pcode stream, the model constructs a feature vector through TF-IDF encoding based on a predefined vocabulary. A FNN is then employed to analyze these statistical patterns in greater depth, capturing behavioral tendencies reflected in the instruction distribution. To further enhance representational quality, the Pcode semantic feature analysis model removes redundant patterns from the instruction sequence before learning begins. This is achieved through a redundancy-elimination procedure that filters out frequently occurring but semantically uninformative fragments. An LSTM network is then applied to the cleaned instruction sequence to model its contextual dependencies and semantic relationships, yielding a semantic feature vector that reflects deeper behavioral characteristics of the executable. The final component, the multimodal feature analysis model, integrates the three previously obtained feature vectors. To achieve this, we employ a hybrid architecture combining Transformer Encoder with a 1DCNN. This module is designed to reconcile the differences among the structural, statistical, and semantic feature spaces and to learn a unified representation that captures their complementary strengths. Dataset

The IoT malware dataset used in this paper combines two publicly available sources: the dataset released by Yokohama National University29 and the dataset provided by VirusShare30. To evaluate the proposed approach across different hardware environments, we selected five CPU architectures commonly found in modern IoT devices: AArch64, ARM, Intel 80386 (I386), MIPS, and x86. Since neither dataset alone provides sufficient samples or coverage for all five architectures, we merged them into a more diverse dataset. Malware family labels were preserved according to the original annotations, and duplicate samples were removed based on file hash values. After construction, 100 samples were randomly selected and manually verified through VirusTotal, achieving 100% accuracy. In the final dataset, 32.55% of samples come from VirusShare, with the remainder from Yokohama National University. According to Yokohama National University’s labeling policy, the category “Other” denotes samples outside the eleven predefined malware families, including previously unknown or unclassified variants. The overall design of the detection framework in this paper is inspired by the principles of ensemble learning. Specifically, lightweight and task-focused subnetworks are first employed to extract the core features of each modality, after which a more sophisticated and expressive fusion model performs in-depth analysis and joint modeling of the resulting feature vectors. In the initial framework design, to balance semantic modeling capability with computational complexity, we selected a relatively simple LSTM as the base model for extracting semantic features from opcode sequences. LSTM effectively captures contextual dependencies within sequences while maintaining a manageable computational cost, aligning well with the framework’s “light-weight front end, heavy back end” design philosophy. Their methods do not fully exploit relationships among static features, which affects generalization across families. In addition, their fusion strategies remain relatively simple and struggle to combine global structural information with local semantic features, leading to fluctuating detection results. By comparison, the proposed approach consistently achieves higher accuracy across malware families, demonstrating the advantage of multi-dimensional feature modeling for IoT malware detection.

Conclusions

This paper proposes a deep learning-based IoT malware detection approach tailored for EV charging stations, with a focus on improving accuracy across diverse CPU architectures. The approach comprises four models. The first model, global structural feature analysis, transforms malware binaries into grayscale images, from which a CNN extracts spatial patterns to generate global structural feature vectors. The second model, Pcode statistical feature analysis, leverages the Ghidra decompiler to unify opcodes from various architectures into platform-independent Pcode representations. These are then vectorized using the TF-IDF algorithm, and a FNN is used to learn their statistical distribution. The third model, Pcode semantic feature analysis, removes redundant Pcode entries and applies a LSTM network to model contextual and semantic dependencies, yielding high-level semantic feature vectors. In the multimodal feature analysis model, the three feature types are fused using a multi-head attention mechanism for dynamic weighting, followed by deep interaction modeling through a multi-layer encoder. This integrated approach captures malware’s structural, statistical, and semantic features, enabling precise detection in heterogeneous IoT environments. Experiments conducted on the widely used public IoT malware datasets demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves an average improvement of 1.37% in F1 compared to existing mainstream detection approaches, highlighting its superior performance.

A limitation of this work is that the proposed approach relies only on static features. Its performance may therefore degrade when handling obfuscated, encrypted, or packed malware, as well as previously unseen samples. In future work, we intend to incorporate dynamic behavioral features extracted from sandbox execution and combine them with static representations. This integration may provide complementary information for detection. We also plan to evaluate the model on resource-constrained IoT edge devices by monitoring runtime behavior and measuring operational metrics such as memory usage and transmission latency. In addition, automated hyperparameter optimization will be explored to reduce reliance on empirically selected parameters.
