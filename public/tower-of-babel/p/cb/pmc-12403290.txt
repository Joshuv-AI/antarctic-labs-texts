Feasibility of a nurse-led online video intervention for mealtime assistance in dementia care: a quasi-experimental mixed-methods study
Authors: Dukyoo Jung, Leeho Yoo, Soogyung Shin, Sukyung Byeon, Hyein Seo, Eunju Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMealtime assistance is essential in long-term care for older adults with dementia, as it promotes autonomy, social interaction, and dignity. Flexible and accessible training for direct care workers (DCWs) is crucial in improving mealtime support. This study aimed to develop and evaluate the effectiveness of a YouTube-based mealtime assistance education program for dementia care based on the cognitive theory of multimedia learning. The intervention was designed to provide accessible and flexible training for DCWs in care facilities.MethodsA quasi-experimental, mixed-methods design was used. Twenty-four DCWs and five supervising nurses from two long-term care facilities participated in this study. To maximize participation, DCWs were non-randomly assigned to the intervention based on facility recommendations and individual work schedules. The effectiveness of the program was evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s four-level model. Reactions were measured by collecting satisfaction data from the DCWs through surveys and focus group interviews (FGIs). Learning outcomes were assessed using self-reported questionnaires to measure mealtime assistance knowledge with pre- and post-intervention comparisons. Behavioral changes were evaluated by supervising nurses using an observation-based questionnaire and analyzed using pre- and post-intervention data. Organizational results and the dissemination effect were explored through FGIs with nurses and thematically analyzed.ResultsThe median mealtime assistance knowledge score increased significantly (Pre: 91.67, Post: 100.00, p = .002, effect size = 0.45, interquartile range: 83.3–97.92). The median mealtime assistance behavior score showed significant improvement (Pre: 67.00, Post: 92.00, p < .001, effect size = 0.56, interquartile range: 82.33–92.00). Supervising nurses observed enhanced ethical attitudes and behavioral changes among DCWs during mealtime assistance.ConclusionYouTube-based multimedia learning interventions effectively improved the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors of DCWs in mealtime assistance for older adults with dementia. Its accessibility and practicality suggest that this approach can be a useful educational model in long-term care settings. Further studies are needed to explore the role of opinion leaders in sustaining and expanding these effects.

Introduction

With the acceleration of global aging, the number of individuals with dementia is steadily increasing and is projected to reach approximately 139 million by 2050 [1]. Older adults with dementia often experience physical dependence and eating difficulties due to cognitive decline [2, 3], which can lead to serious health problems such as weight loss, malnutrition, and dehydration [4].

Mealtimes in long-term care(LTC) facilities are a central aspect of daily life and play a vital role in maintaining the health and well-being of individuals with dementia [5]. It is a meaningful activity that promotes autonomy, facilitates social interaction, and preserves dignity among individuals [6, 7]. Accordingly, mealtime assistance should go beyond simply supporting food intake and adopt a person-centered approach that respects autonomy and dignity while also enhancing the overall quality of the experience, including interactions between direct care workers (DCWs) and residents with dementia [8].

According to the Alzheimer’s Association [1], approximately 48% of older adults with dementia reside in LTC facilities due to cognitive and physical impairments. In South Korea as well, it has been reported that about 49.9% of older adults with dementia live in LTC facilities [9]. Among those with dementia residing in such facilities, 71% require mealtime assistance [10]. The level of support needed can vary depending on the residents’ cognitive and physical function, disease progression, the texture or type of food provided, and the care provider’s style of assistance [11]. While some residents are capable of engaging in mealtime activities independently, others may require full support as the disease progresses [11, 12]. Therefore, mealtime assistance must be delivered through a structured, individualized approach that accounts for these diverse factors [8]. In this context, DCWs play a key role in supporting individuals with high levels of mealtime dependency [13]. However, mealtime assistance is often provided based on subjective judgment, such as limiting the type or amount of food or shortening mealtime rather than standardized knowledge or guidelines, which may lower its quality. This underscores the need for improved training approaches [14].

Previous interventions targeting DCWs have primarily focused on the technical aspects of feeding, such as feeding skills training programs [15, 16], web-based instructional programs [17], and mobile applications for mealtime support [18]. However, there is a notable lack of programs that promote positive caregiver–resident interactions and address the emotional and behavioral dimensions of dementia care [19, 20]. Moreover, many existing programs do not reflect the specific educational needs of DCWs, which reduces their effectiveness [21].

A recent needs assessment study reported that DCWs desire more education on topics such as assessing swallowing function, understanding food forgetfulness, multisensory stimulation, exercise, and available government support systems [22]. The nurses supervising these workers also stressed the importance of ethical attitudes toward understanding individuals with dementia. However, due to limited staffing and resources in care facilities, DCWs often have little time for in-person training [18], which highlights the need for accessible and flexible educational formats.

YouTube, a multimedia platform, offers unrestricted and self-directed access to learning materials, making it a viable tool for DCWs constrained by time and place [23]. According to Mayer’s cognitive theory of multimedia learning, learning is enhanced through the dual-channel processing of visual and verbal inputs, allowing learners to actively select, organize, and integrate information [24]. Features such as signaling (e.g., lyrics and visual labels), segmenting (microlearning units), and modality (narration paired with visuals) reduce extraneous cognitive load and increase germane processing, which promotes deeper learning and supports the transfer of knowledge into real-world contexts [25]. For instance, YouTube incorporates signaling principles using lyrics, screen labels, and visual elements to convey key information and reduce cognitive load [26]. In addition, microlearning techniques break down content into small units, allowing busy professionals to focus on and learn effectively without time or location constraints. This aligns with the segmenting principle, which advocates for the presentation of learning materials in manageable chunks [27]. Learners first select relevant words and images, organize them into coherent mental models, and finally integrate this new knowledge with their prior experiences [25], enabling DCWs to apply learned techniques to complex caregiving situations. Consequently, an intervention program based on the cognitive theory of multimedia learning using YouTube could promote effective learning among DCWs.

Traditional evaluations of DCW interventions rely primarily on self-report questionnaires to assess short-term understanding and effects [17, 28]. While useful for assessing knowledge and comprehension, this approach has limitations in evaluating actual behavioral changes or long-term outcomes. It is essential to use evaluation methods that reflect real behavioral changes and long-term outcomes to systematically measure the effectiveness of education. The Kirkpatrick educational evaluation model provides a systematic framework comprising four levels: reaction, learning, behavior, and results [29]. This model emphasizes the practical application of learned knowledge and skills and evaluates their long-term impact on individuals and organizations, facilitating a comprehensive analysis of educational effectiveness. In addition, the model effectively reduces the distance between education and practice [30].

In response to these challenges, a multimedia learning program to improve mealtime assistance for DCWs should be developed and evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s four-level model.

Aims

This study aimed to address challenges in dementia mealtime care by developing a nurse-led intervention for DCWs using YouTube. The intervention was grounded in Mayer’s cognitive theory of multimedia learning, and its effectiveness was evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s evaluation framework, focusing on DCWs’ satisfaction as well as changes in their knowledge and behavior related to mealtime assistance.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a quasi-experimental design using a mixed-methods approach. Effectiveness was evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s model. A mixed-methods approach combines quantitative and qualitative data from a single study for a more comprehensive understanding [31]. Specifically, an explanatory design was employed in which quantitative data collection and analysis were conducted first, followed by qualitative data collection to elaborate on the results [32]. In this study, surveys were conducted with participants for quantitative data collection. This was followed by focus group interviews(FGIs) for qualitative data collection. This approach connects measurable outcomes, such as changes in knowledge and behavior, with participants’ experiences for a deeper understanding of the impact of the intervention.

Participants

Quantitative phase

The inclusion criteria were DCWs in LTC facilities with a minimum of six months of experience. The required sample size for the quantitative phase was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.6 [33], resulting in a minimum of 19 participants (one-sample t-test, two-tailed, α = 0.05, effect size = 0.80, power = 0.90). Considering a 20% dropout rate, 24 participants were recruited through self-selection, based on facility referrals. Facility directors recommended eligible DCWs based on their availability and work schedule. All 24 participants completed the study, and no dropouts were reported.

Qualitative phase

The qualitative phase involved conducting FGIs with two groups: DCWs who participated in the intervention and the nurses who supervised them. Prior studies involving FGIs with DCWs or their supervisors have typically included four to seven participants per group [34, 35]. Based on these findings, a minimum of four participants per group was determined as necessary for this study, with an additional 20% added to account for potential dropouts, resulting in a final sample of five DCWs and five supervising nurses.

Since the purpose of the nurse FGI was to explore overall changes in the mealtime assistance behavior of DCWs before and after the intervention, supervising nurses were chosen from the same facilities to ensure familiarity with the DCWs’ work performance.

Data collection

We used convenience sampling by first contacting the directors of the facility and then sending recruitment materials via mail to those who agreed to participate. Two LTC facilities (Y and H) located in different cities in South Korea were selected. The selection criteria included the provision of dementia care services, geographical diversity, and adherence to national LTC facilities standards. Both facilities met government certification requirements, admitted residents with LTC grades, and housed 90–100 residents.

The researchers visited each facility in person to obtain consent and collect questionnaires. DCWs were selected for the study after being recommended by the facility directors based on their experience. All FGIs were conducted using Zoom to ensure scheduling flexibility. Data were collected between July 26, 2024, and August 16, 2024, after screening for eligibility. At facility H, 10 DCWs and two supervising nurses participated, whereas at facility Y, 14 DCWs and three supervising nurses were enrolled. The number of supervising nurses was determined based on the number of DCWs participating at each site.

Study process

Development of a nurse-led intervention

The nurse-led video intervention was developed through a collaboration between a research team, including a faculty member from a college of nursing, a gerontological nurse practitioner, and an educational video production company.

The intervention was designed based on Mayer’s Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) to promote a deep understanding and integration of educational content through scenario-based videos. Additional instructional elements, including lectures and interviews, were incorporated to reinforce knowledge transfer [24].

The intervention consisted of seven videos covering six topics selected based on the educational needs of DCWs in mealtime assistance [22]: (1) basic knowledge of mealtime assistance, (2) ethical considerations related to mealtime assistance, (3) assessment of swallowing function, (4) mealtime assistance techniques, (5) behaviors of older adults with dementia during mealtime, and (6) strategies for managing meal refusal. The videos had an average duration of 7 min and 13 s (range: 3:15–9:39), reflecting microlearning principles to enhance focus and learning effectiveness.

Topic (1) was delivered through a lecture by the first author, whereas topic (2) included interviews with two directors of LTC facilities. In topic (1), the lecturer briefly introduced dementia and the importance of mealtime and independent eating in older adults with dementia. Topic (2) addressed ethical considerations and highlighted the directors’ memories of exemplary mealtime assistance by DCWs. Topics (3) to (6) were developed by combining scenario-based dramas with instructional explanations. Each video presented inappropriate mealtime assistance, followed by an explanation of correct practices and a revised scenario applying the appropriate intervention. To enhance learners’ comprehension, visual cues such as lyrics, summary statements, and highlighted imagery were incorporated throughout the videos [24, 36].

Scripts of scenarios were developed by the research team based on guidance in mealtime assistance [37] and previous studies on the mealtime assistance of dementia patients [4, 38–41]. The scripts were further refined through consultations with five experienced nurses working in LTC facilities and through multiple discussions within the research team. These scenarios targeted residents who required partial or full assistance during meals. Interventions applicable only to individuals requiring full assistance were distinctly identified in the videos (e.g., neck and salivary gland massages for those unable to perform exercises, and the use of non-verbal cues when verbal prompts are ineffective). The detailed content of each topic is provided in (Table 1).

To ensure professional production and facilitate communication, we created a storyboard with related images and provided it to a video production company. Filming was conducted under the direction of a producer and involved two professional actors experienced in portraying DCWs and older adults. Members of the research team were present during the filming to offer guidance on the accuracy of the interventions and the content that should be emphasized. The videos underwent three rounds of editing, incorporating lyrics, additional explanations, and visual highlights based on feedback from the research team.

Implementation of the developed intervention

The participating DCWs watched one to two videos per day over the seven-day intervention period according to the curriculum. The videos were uploaded to YouTube, and the corresponding links were shared daily via a group chat to guide the participants on what to view. In addition, YouTube Shorts, each lasting less than one minute and summarizing the key content, were provided for review.

The intervention was delivered by three nurses from the research team and implemented simultaneously at two LTC facilities from July 29 to August 6, 2024. Given the online delivery format, we visited each facility prior to Day 1 to conduct an orientation session explaining the overall objectives and procedures of the program.

To encourage active participation and ensure intervention fidelity, we incorporated the following strategies into the intervention: (1) Checklists: participants documented their daily learning progress using a checklist included in the program manual; (2) Group Chats: participants shared their experiences and reflections in group chats, allowing researchers to monitor their engagement; and (3) Monetary Incentives: participants who completed the program through Day 3 received the first incentive, whereas those who completed the post-intervention survey were provided with a second incentive.

Effectiveness evaluation: Kirkpatrick’s model

The overall effectiveness of the intervention was evaluated using the Kirkpatrick model (Fig. 1).

Level 1: Reaction

Satisfaction with the intervention was assessed at the reaction level to capture the participants’ immediate perceptions of its usefulness and acceptability. DCWs completed a self-reported survey within three days of completing the program. The survey, developed by the research team, consisted of 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). The items assessed overall satisfaction, perceived usefulness of the content, comprehension, and willingness to recommend the program (Appendix 1). To gain deeper insights, FGIs were conducted with five direct care workers. The FGI questions were designed to expand and elaborate on the quantitative results by exploring participants’ experiences with the survey responses, thereby providing contextual insights. For example, if participants reported high satisfaction with “mealtime assistance skills acquisition,” the FGI aimed to capture specific experiences in applying the skill and perceived changes.

Level 2: Learning

For the learning level, DCWs’ mealtime assistance knowledge was assessed before the program and within seven days of its completion. We developed a questionnaire based on the content of the educational program and included 12 items with a three-point response format (“Yes,” “No,” or “Don’t know”), each with a clearly defined correct answer (Appendix 2). Eight items were selected and adapted from the Korean-translated version of the Formal Caregivers’ Knowledge of Feeding Dementia Patients Questionnaire [28], originally developed by Chang [15], to ensure alignment with the present intervention content. Additionally, three items related to swallowing assessment, consideration of food preferences, and use of resident names were added based on previous studies [12, 42], as these topics were not covered in the original tool. An additional item addressing the importance of ethical attitudes during mealtime assistance was also included. Correct responses were scored as 1 point and incorrect responses as 0, and the total score was converted to a 100-point scale. Content validity was confirmed by six experts (one nursing professor, one nurse manager, and four geriatric nurse specialists), with an I-CVI (item-level content validity index) of 1.0 for all items.

Level 3: Behavior

At the behavioral level, nurses observed changes in DCWs’ mealtime assistance behaviors. Since patient-centered behaviors are often under-recognized by providers themselves, observational methods offer a valid approach for capturing care practices [43]. Observations occurred twice for each DCW—before the program and within seven days after its completion—during one mealtime assistance session. Each supervising nurse was assigned to four to five DCWs and instructed to inform participants that their mealtime assistance behaviors would be observed.

The observation tool, developed by the research team based on the intervention content, included 20 items assessing actions such as preparation, assessment of swallowing function, mealtime assistance techniques, and handling refusal (Appendix 3). Each item was rated on a three-point scale (0 = not performed, 1 = sometimes performed, 2 = often performed), with a “not applicable” option (coded 9) excluded from the scoring. The total score was converted to a 100-point scale, with higher scores indicating better performance.

Fourteen items were selected from the Korean-translated version of the Formal Caregivers’ Behavior of Feeding Dementia Patients Questionnaire [28], originally developed by Chang [15]. To ensure alignment with the intervention content, the wording of items was revised (e.g., “responded appropriately to choking” was rephrased as “paused feeding when coughing or choking occurred”). Six additional items were developed based on previous studies of observation tools in LTC settings during mealtimes [44–46] to address domains not covered by the original tool, such as swallowing assessment, management of challenging behaviors, modeling, and ethical attitudes. To improve clarity and support accurate observations, each item was accompanied by operational definitions and concrete examples. Content validity was confirmed by the same expert panel, with all items receiving an I-CVI score of 1.0. The internal consistency of the tool was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.848.

Level 4: Results

For the results level, FGIs were conducted two weeks after program completion with five supervising nurses who had observed the DCWs’ behavior during this period. Themes explored included behavioral changes, program applicability, organizational effects, and feasibility of the intervention. The FGI questions were designed to elicit detailed accounts of nurses’ observations of DCWs’ behavioral changes, and organizational effects were explored through questions on the impact on residents, peer dynamics, and unit-level changes. To ensure accuracy, the nurses received observation logs in advance to record their observations and refer to them during the FGIs.

Data analysis

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed following the method outlined by Creswell [47], which describes qualitative data analysis for mixed methods research. We employed a reflexive thematic analysis, which proceeds through the process of initial coding, categorization, and theme development. All researchers involved in the analysis had completed master’s and doctoral courses related to qualitative research and had experience and publications in qualitative studies.

All FGIs were conducted and audio-recorded in Korean with the participants’ consent. Transcription was performed by a researcher who participated in each FGI. To ensure data accuracy and reliability, the entire research team cross-checked all transcripts against the original recordings and field notes.

During the data-coding phase, the data were organized by grouping them into segments, creating categories, and labeling them using Excel. The key concepts from the data were then categorized to derive overarching themes. All researchers participated in the coding and analysis process and engaged in repeated reading and iterative discussions. Discrepancies in coding or interpretation were addressed and resolved through thorough team dialogue until a consensus was reached. The content was extracted and described based on these themes.

After the final themes were derived in Korean, the results were collaboratively translated into English by the research team. The translated content was reviewed by a bilingual expert fluent in Korean and English and familiar with both cultural contexts to ensure accuracy and cultural fidelity. To ensure the fidelity of the analysis, we rigorously reviewed and revised the results with the help of fellow researchers with extensive experience in qualitative research and gerontological nursing.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ewha Woman’s University (ewha-202407-0007-01). All participants were provided with sufficient information about the study and were informed that they could withdraw at any time. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results of qualitative data from direct care workers and nurses

Theme 1. Changes in attitude toward meal assistance

DCWs reported positive changes in their attitudes toward assisting older adults with dementia during meals. Throughout the program, they recognized their own behaviors through dramatic examples of inappropriate practices and reflected on their routine mechanical approach to mealtime assistance. After completing the program, they expressed a renewed commitment to respect older adults with dementia and provide proper mealtime assistance. The program motivated them to adopt more ethical attitudes toward caregiving.

Level 4: “Result”

Analysis of the FGIs conducted with five supervising nurses revealed four themes, eight subcategories, and 20 meaningful statements, as summarized in Table 3. The identified themes included: Ethical attitudes in mealtime assistance, Changes in mealtime assistance behaviors, Organizational-level changes, and Practical challenges.

Theme 1. Ethical attitudes in mealtime assistance

Nurses observed significant improvements in the ethical attitudes of DCWs toward mealtime assistance. DCWs reflected on their previous habit of rushing through meals and began to recognize the importance of respecting the dignity of older adults with dementia and the significance of mealtimes.

They seem to have developed a new mindset. They’re now considering ethical aspects and the dignity of older adults while assisting with meals. (Nurse 4)

They used to rush through meal assistance, but I noticed they’ve reflected on this and are making efforts to change. (Nurse 1)

Theme 2. Changes in mealtime assistance behaviors

Nurses noted observable changes in DCWs’ behaviors during mealtime assistance. Techniques such as oral exercises, modeling, perioral stimulation, non-verbal cueing, and encouragement for self-feeding were applied. DCWs have also shifted from simultaneously assisting multiple residents to focusing on one resident at a time.

They’ve started trying oral exercises and following the training exactly, like stimulating the mouth with a spoon or guiding the residents to mimic mouth movements. They didn’t do that before. (Nurse 5)

Earlier, they’d give one resident a spoonful and then move to another. Now, I see them finishing one resident’s meal completely before moving to another. (Nurse 4)

I’ve noticed them doing things like giving the spoon to the residents so they can try eating on their own. (Nurse 5)

Theme 3. Organizational level changes

Nurses observed a “dissemination effect,” in which trained DCWs shared the program’s principles with their untrained colleagues. DCWs began to influence their peers, promoting a culture of patience and respect during mealtime.

For example, if someone says, “This resident isn’t eating,” a trained direct care worker will say, “Leave it. Let them try on their own.” That’s been really effective. (Nurse 2)

Theme 4. Difficulties of change

Despite training, nurses reported challenges in changing some DCWs’ behaviors. While DCWs understood the importance of taking time during meals, some continued to rush and complete meals within 10–20 min due to ingrained habits. Nurses identified this as a mindset rather than a staffing problem.

Some finish meals in just 10 min, even after the training. It’s not so much a staffing issue as it is a mindset problem—this idea of needing to rush through meals to get everything cleaned up quickly. (Nurse 4)

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and evaluate the effectiveness of an intervention to support the feeding of older adults with dementia based on the educational needs of care workers. The effectiveness was assessed according to Kirkpatrick’s four levels (Reaction, Learning, Behavior, Results).

Positive reception of educational interventions

In this study, satisfaction with the intervention was higher on average (4.61 ± 0.467) than in previous training for DCWs (4.17 ± 0.72) [48]. This high satisfaction level is noteworthy because, according to Kirkpatrick’s reaction evaluation, a positive evaluation at the reaction stage is essential for moving to the second stage of learning [29]. Participants reported that the program was structured to help them understand positive behaviors in older adults with dementia and made the educational content easy to comprehend. Liu et al. [12, 42] highlighted the importance of promoting independent eating attempts as a positive behavior in dementia care. For instance, hand movements or wiping actions during meals may sometimes be misinterpreted by DCWs as hygiene issues or abnormal behaviors [49], although these may actually signify attempts at self-feeding. Recognizing such behaviors as positive signals and supporting them toward independent eating aligns with current research that focuses on dining behaviors in dementia care.

Kirkpatrick defined the reaction stage as the perception of the effectiveness of training [29]. During FGIs, DCWs reflected on their mealtime assistance experiences using a dramatic educational format and recognized areas for improvement. They also noted that using easily accessible YouTube videos allowed them to view short segments repeatedly, making it easier for them to understand the content. This positive reception at the reaction stage suggests that the intervention was well regarded in terms of both content and delivery methods.

Enhancing knowledge and ethical attitudes through Care-based learning

The learning stage can be evaluated by including participants’ changed knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors [29]. Mealtime knowledge was measured before and after the intervention to evaluate the effectiveness of the educational learning stage. The results confirm the positive impact of the intervention on DCWs. In particular, there was a significant difference in knowledge scores related to “using friendly names rather than common names” and “adapting meals to reflect the food preferences of older adults with dementia.” This suggests that the training increased awareness of the importance of individualized mealtime assistance, which is closely linked to increased food intake in older adults with dementia, making it an important factor in improving the quality of care [50]. Premeal assessment is essential for individualized mealtime assistance. The FGIs reported that the case studies increased the participants’ knowledge by providing specific information on how to stimulate the mouth area before eating and how to assess the discomfort of older adults with dementia.

In addition, the knowledge scores for the “correlation between mealtime assistance and ethical attitudes” improved significantly. Ethical attitudes have a direct impact on the way DCWs provide care and on the quality of care [50]. While changing ethical attitudes is challenging, they can be improved with appropriate training interventions. Simulation training can be an effective way to provide clinical ethics education by helping standardized patients identify, resolve, and reflect on ethical issues [51]. Although the current intervention is based on case-based learning rather than simulation, it shares similarities with simulation training in that both approaches involve dealing with real-world ethical issues through concrete scenarios [52]. By presenting realistic situations and encouraging critical thinking, case-based learning provides a practical framework for addressing ethical dilemmas in care settings. Therefore, developing the cases from this study into structured simulation programs could better address ethical issues such as dignity and autonomy during mealtimes. Scenarios involving conflict between independence and safety can help caregivers navigate real-world ethical challenges.

In other words, in terms of the learning stage, the results indicate that the training increased not only the knowledge of correct mealtime assistance but also the ethical attitude toward providing care.

Behavior change through consistent and practical training

The third step in Kirkpatrick’s model evaluates how effectively participants apply the knowledge, skills, and abilities gained from training in their work settings [29]. Assessing behavioral change is essential, as learning that is not applied cannot contribute to an organization’s desired outcomes [53]. In this study, nurses observed significant improvements in DCWs’ feeding behavior after training with techniques such as mouth exercises, modeling, perioral stimulation, non-verbal cueing, and independent encouragement to eat. This increase in targeted behaviors is attributed to the foundation of training in the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, which emphasizes delivering concise information and integrating text with visuals to reduce cognitive load [24].

The training utilized short videos averaging seven minutes, breaking down the key content into manageable segments. Such micro-learning promotes the transfer of information from short- to long-term memory, facilitating behavioral change [54]. Consistent exposure to brief training videos enhances DCWs’ retention of mealtime assistance behaviors, ultimately leading to sustained behavioral improvements [55]. This approach demonstrates that concise, focused training can have a lasting impact on behavior, supporting the ongoing application of skills in caregiving practices.

Organizational impact and the dissemination effect

The fourth step in Kirkpatrick’s model evaluates the outcomes that occur as a result of the training and analyzes the impact of the job training on organizational effectiveness. In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of education from the organizational perspective of nurses who supervised DCWs rather than the DCWs themselves. We found that, after the training, DCWs’ attitudes toward assisted eating changed ethically, and they actively applied assisted eating techniques. We also found a “dissemination effect” in which the content of the training was passed on to fellow DCWs who had not participated in the training. This dissemination demonstrates that such training is required in the workplace.

The diffusion effect can be accelerated, especially by opinion leaders [56]. Similar to champion models, opinion leaders play an important role in the delivery and interpretation of information, actively promoting positive changes within an organization [40, 57]. If this nursing intervention is to be expanded in the future, it will be necessary to allow the DCWs who participated in this study to act as opinion leaders and contribute to shaping the correct model of mealtime assistance. Therefore, it is necessary to support opinion leaders in interpreting and applying the information provided through this training to real-life considerations in LTC facilities, such as staffing shortages [56]. This will ultimately improve the quality of the mealtime support provided to older adults with dementia.

Programs should address these limitations to strengthen their dissemination. Even after training, DCWs still feel pressured to feed residents quickly because of a heavy workload and the belief that faster feeding is more efficient. Furthermore, the need to assist multiple residents simultaneously often impedes the provision of person-centered care. To mitigate these challenges, it would be beneficial to involve opinion leaders, who can actively promote new practices among their peers. By implementing campaigns that promote patience and individualized assistance during meals, coupled with incentives for person-centered practices, opinion leaders can encourage other care workers to adopt these strategies.

In addition, assessing each resident’s mealtime difficulties [58] and strategically allocating care workers by combining those with low and high needs can balance the workload. Opinion leaders can also play a key role in advocating this structured approach and guiding their peers on how to efficiently implement person-centered care. By leveraging the influence of opinion leaders, the dissemination of these improved practices can be accelerated, fostering a person-centered care environment.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size for both quantitative and qualitative components was relatively small and limited to two facilities, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Second, while the intervention was designed to enhance learning and behavioral changes, the short training period (seven days) may not have been sufficient to evaluate long-term outcomes or sustained behavioral changes. Third, this study’s reliance on YouTube as the primary delivery platform may have excluded care workers with limited access to technology or digital literacy, potentially affecting the broader applicability of the intervention. Finally, the evaluation tool used to measure learning and behavioral changes was developed by the research team, which may have limited its reliability. To address this limitation, future research should consider using objective assessment tools and evaluating interrater reliability to ensure consistent and unbiased evaluations. Incorporating such methods can enhance the accuracy and credibility of findings. Further research with larger and more diverse samples, extended follow-up periods, and the use of valid tools is recommended to validate and expand these findings.

Conclusions

This study aims to systematically determine the effectiveness of a targeted educational intervention based on the Kirkpatrick evaluation model. Grounded in Mayer’s Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning and delivered on YouTube, the intervention significantly enhanced the DCWs’ knowledge, ethical attitudes, and practical skills regarding mealtime assistance for older adults with dementia. These findings highlight the importance of using structured, case-based, and easily accessible training methods to support care workers in effectively applying their skills. Additionally, the observed dissemination effect underscores the potential of such interventions to influence organizational culture and caregiving practices more broadly. Future efforts should focus on increasing the duration of the program and evaluating its long-term impact across diverse settings.
