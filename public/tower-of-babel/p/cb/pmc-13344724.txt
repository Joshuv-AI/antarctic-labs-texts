Mood disorders and peripartum cardiomyopathy
Authors: Blake Neuburg, Melissa Harris, Anna Palatnik, Rachel Harrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between mood disorders and peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM).MethodsRetrospective cohort study utilizing the National Inpatient Sample from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project, Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality of pregnant and postpartum patients from 2017 to 2019. Patients were separated into two groups based on ICD‐10 coding for the presence or absence of mood disorder (depression, bipolar depression, anxiety, or other mood diagnosis). The primary outcome was diagnosis of PPCM. Secondary outcomes included a composite of adverse cardiac events and maternal death. Groups were compared via t‐tests, chi‐squared analysis, and logistic regression that included all variables that differed between groups with p < 0.05.ResultsOver 2.2 million subjects were analyzed and approximately 168,000 (7.3%) had an ICD‐10 diagnosis of mood disorder. Those with mood disorders were more likely to be non‐Hispanic white, obese, tobacco users, publicly insured, have comorbidities, and deliver at a large, private, non‐profit hospital (all p < 0.05). They were delivered at an earlier gestational age and were also more likely to undergo cesarean (37.0 ± 4.6 vs. 37.8 ± 3.7 weeks and 35.7% vs. 31.8%, respectively, p < 0.001). The primary outcome of PPCM was identified twice as often in those with mood disorder (0.12% vs. 0.06%, p < 0.001). Composite cardiac events, consisting of incidence of acute myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, cardioversion, cardiac failure, and pulmonary edema, were more frequent among patients with mood disorders (0.35% vs. 0.14%, p < 0.001). After controlling for confounders, PPCM remained independently associated with diagnosis of mood disorders (aOR 1.37, 95%CI 1.04–1.81) as did the composite of adverse cardiac events (aOR 1.58, 95% CI 1.38–1.82).ConclusionMood disorders in pregnancy and postpartum are associated with increased rates of PPCM and other cardiac events.

INTRODUCTION

Mood disorders are common, affecting up to 1 in 8 women, and are linked to increased risks of development of a number of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including heart failure, ischemic heart disease, stroke, and chronic hypertension [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. A 2021 American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement on psychological health reported strong evidence linking psychological health and CVD risk, recommending the routine assessment of psychological well‐being in patients with or at risk of CVD [6]. The AHA statement describes the “mind‐heart connection” where mood disorders alter the hypothalamic pituitary axis, increasing baseline sympathetic tone and levels of chronic inflammation, resulting in endothelial dysfunction and end organ dysfunction [6, 7].

CVD risk factors specific to women are often focused on pregnancy‐associated diseases such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) and gestational diabetes; however, recent studies underscore the role of mood disorders, including perinatal depression, as independent predictors of future CVD risk [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Beyond influencing long‐term risk, mood disorders have also been shown to influence clinical outcomes and reintervention rates among individuals suffering from cardiac events [13]. Importantly, mood disorders represent a modifiable risk factor with effective treatment options such as cognitive behavioral therapy and pharmacotherapy [14]. Despite this, limited research has examined mood disorders as a risk factor for the development of peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) or its potential impact on maternal outcomes [15].

PPCM, defined as a new onset reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of < 45% within the last 3 months of gestation or 5 months postpartum, is a leading cause of maternal death in the United States and worldwide [16]. The incidence of PPCM diagnosis in the United States is 1 in 1000 to 4000 live births [17, 18]. Complications of PPCM frequently include severe cardiac morbidity and mortality, as PPCM is responsible for nearly 60% of cardiogenic shock cases in the peripartum period 19]. Currently, there is an incomplete understanding on the pathophysiology leading to the development of PPCM; however, genetic, infectious (viral), pregnancy maladaptation, and nutritional deficiency hypotheses have been proposed [15, 17, 18]. Given current knowledge gaps surrounding the clinical development of PPCM, few predictive insights exist and all rely on known risk factors to identify those at the highest risk for the development of PPCM [15, 18]. Mood disorders are highly prevalent in pregnancy and often become exacerbated postpartum, which is also the time period coinciding with 70%–80% of PPCM diagnoses. Considering mood disorders are a known risk factor for CVD outside of pregnancy, the additional impact on the peripartum period could be substantial [20]. The objective of this study was to examine the hypothesis that individuals with a diagnosis of a mood disorder have higher odds of PPCM and related adverse cardiac events in pregnancy and postpartum.

METHODS

This study was a retrospective cohort study utilizing the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project National Inpatient Sample (HCUP‐NIS), Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. The HCUP‐NIS database is the largest publicly available all‐payer inpatient database designed to produce national estimates of inpatient utilization, access, cost, quality, and outcomes with a stratified, single‐cluster sampling design [21]. It is designed to approximate a 20% stratified sample of inpatient hospital stays annually, representing more than 97% of the United States population per calendar year. We performed analysis on all HCUP‐NIS database pregnant and postpartum patients from 2017 to 2019. Exclusion criteria included male gender, age < 11 or > 50 years old, and non‐pregnant or non‐postpartum status. The HCUP‐NIS database was queried using International Classification of Diseases‐10 (ICD‐10) codes to identify a cohort of pregnant or postpartum persons (ICD‐10 codes as listed in Table S1). Patients with a history of mood disorder (depression, bipolar, anxiety, or other mood diagnosis, ICD‐10 codes as listed in Table S1) were compared to those without a history of mood disorder. A detailed list of additional ICD‐10 codes used is provided in Table S1. The primary outcome was diagnosis of PPCM, defined by ICD‐10 code. Secondary outcomes included death (as defined by the NIS database) and a composite of acute cardiac events: acute myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, cardiac conversion, cardiac failure/arrest, and pulmonary edema, defined based on ICD‐10 codes (Table S1). Demographic and clinical factors aligned with prior studies on PPCM were chosen and abstracted from the database and compared between patients with and without mood disorders including maternal age, race and ethnicity, obesity, insurance type, household income quartile, tobacco use, presence of diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, asthma, anemia, history of cesarean delivery, limited prenatal care (as ICD coded by provider), multifetal gestation, cesarean delivery, gestational age at delivery, hospital location, type (rural or urban, teaching or non‐teaching, private or government or non‐profit), hospital size (based on NIS categorization), and death based on either existing NIS data or ICD‐10 codes. Care was taken during this process to only include those ICD‐10 codes related to a diagnosis of a mood disorder, regardless of pregnancy status in order to avoid accidental inclusion of those with a mood disorder diagnosed after PPCM. Similarly, only a current diagnosis of PPCM (ICD‐10 diagnosis code O90.3) was included to avoid inclusion of those with a history of PPCM in a prior pregnancy.

All analyses were performed using Stata (Version 17.0, StataCorp LLC). Categorical variables were presented as proportions and compared between groups using the chi‐square test. All tests were two sided and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The association between mood disorders and PPCM was examined with univariable logistic regression. Adjusted multivariable analysis incorporated the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics that significantly differed between groups with a p < 0.05. The secondary outcome models evaluated maternal death, the cardiac SMM composite outcome, and each individual cardiac adverse outcome using univariable and multivariable logistic regressions. Results of multivariable analyses are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The NIS is supplied as an unidentified, publicly limited data set, as defined by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, this study was determined to be exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board.

RESULTS

Over 2.2 million hospitalizations were analyzed and 167,688 (7.3%) included an ICD‐10 code diagnosis of mood disorder. Those with mood disorders were more likely to identify as non‐Hispanic white; be obese; use tobacco; have a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, asthma, and anemia; be publicly insured; and deliver at a large, private, non‐profit hospital (all p < 0.001). Patients with mood disorders were also more likely to deliver earlier and undergo a cesarean (37.0 ± 4.6 vs. 37.8 ± 3.7 weeks and 35.7% vs. 31.8%, respectively, p < 0.001).

The primary outcome, incidence of PPCM, was higher in patients with a mood disorder (0.12% vs. 0.06%, p < 0.001), as were other cardiac events, including the composite of adverse cardiac events (0.35% vs. 0.14%, p < 0.001). Table 3 demonstrates the unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression analysis for the association between PPCM and cardiac morbidity with mood disorders. After adjusting for confounding factors, including age, race and ethnicity, obesity, tobacco use, diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, asthma, anemia, history of cesarean, limited prenatal care, multiple gestation, location, income, insurance, hospital location, type, ownership, and size, PPCM remained independently associated with mood disorders (aOR 1.37, 95% CI 1.04–1.81) as did the composite of adverse cardiac events (aOR 1.58, 95% CI 1.38–1.82) and pulmonary edema (aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.38–1.88). The other individual components of the composite cardiac morbidity outcome were not associated in the adjusted model.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study leveraging the NIS database, mood disorders were associated with increased rates of PPCM and composite adverse maternal cardiac events. Despite these associations, we did not find increased rates of mortality among pregnant individuals with a mood disorder compared to those without.

Previous studies have identified several risk factors for the development of PPCM, including advanced maternal age, African descent, multifetal pregnancies, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [16, 18, 22, 23]. The largest of these studies was by Mielniczuk et al., examining ∼52 million US births over a 20‐year period from 1990 to 2002 [16]. The authors described increasing incidence of PPCM over the 20‐year period from 1 case per 4350 live births in 1990–1993 to 1 case per 2289 live births in 2000–2002 and hypothesized that the primary risk factors driving increasing prevalence were advanced maternal age and increasing rate of multifetal gestations [16]. Kolte et al. published a more recent evaluation of the incidence of PPCM using the NIS database from 2004 to 2011 and noted continued increasing incidence to ∼1 per 968 live births [18]. The strongest associations for the development of PPCM in that cohort were patients with chronic hypertension, anemia, diabetes, and pre‐eclampsia in individuals identifying as African American [18]. Kolte et al. did not assess mood disorders as an independent risk factor specifically but did find higher incidence of depression among women with PPCM during the study period ranging from 5.4% to 7.2% [18]. On the other hand, Davis et al. published a validated risk predication model for PPCM and also found that women with mood disorders were at double the increased risk for PPCM [15]. Wolfe et al. evaluated maternal outcomes among patients with PPCM and found that women with PPCM had more depressive symptoms and had received medical treatment preconception for mood disorders [24]. Our study adds to this knowledge by showing that a diagnosis of mood disorders is significantly associated with PPCM, with an incidence rate of 1 in 827 among patients with mood disorders. This rate is higher than previously reported, aligns with historical trends, and supports the hypothesis that mood disorders are a significant risk factor for developing PPCM [15, 17, 18].

We found an association between mood disorders and other peripartum cardiac events. The predominant diagnosis contributing to cardiac SMM was pulmonary edema. Peripartum and postpartum periods are hallmarked by hemodynamic changes and fluid shifts, which, when coupled with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction of PPCM, can often result in pulmonary edema [25]. Additionally, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are frequently diagnosed or coincide with PPCM diagnosis and can result in or worsen pulmonary edema [17, 26]. US PPCM mortality estimate rates vary from 1‐year mortality of approximately 4% to 2‐year estimates ranging from 0% to 16% with higher rates generally observed over longer‐term follow‐up. Our overall mortality rates were lower than previously reported. The higher prevalence of composite cardiac outcomes in the mood disorder group may suggest that the differences in mortality from PPCM develop more remote from delivery and postpartum, not associated with the initial PPCM admission, consistent with prior studies [27, 28].

In both obstetric and non‐pregnant adult cardiac literature, mood disorders are associated with new CVD within 24 months postpartum, and are a sex specific, independent risk factor for the development of CVD [1, 2, 29]. Recently, a large national cohort study utilizing the Swedish Birth Register examined the association of approximately 878,000 women over a 15‐year period, concluding that women who experienced perinatal depression had a 36% higher risk of development of CVD [5]. Among the cohort, those with history of psychiatric disorders had the highest association for the development of CVD and the association was significant for all CVD subtypes including ischemic heart disease and heart failure. Furthermore, while CVD is the leading cause of death in the general population, individuals with significant mental health disorders are disproportionally affected by CVD [30, 31]. A large metanalysis examining the incidence of CVD and mortality in over 3.2 million patients with significant mental illness compared with 113 million controls found individuals with significant mental illness had an approximate odds ratio of 1.53 increased risk of CVD relative to controls across the 11 studies included in the analysis [21]. The mechanisms by which mood disorders have deleterious effects on the CVD are multifactorial in nature [32, 33]. The AHA describes mood disorders, such as depression, as impacting core aspects of cardiovascular health via increased likelihood of smoking, physical inactivity, poor diet, and obesity. Another supported hypothesis is mood disorders cause chronic hypothalamic dysregulation where mood disorder–related chemical imbalances act at the level of the hypothalamus, to increase cortisol levels and glucocorticoid receptor resistance, in turn increasing low‐level systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, leading to observed adverse CVD outcomes [34, 35, 36].

Our study findings suggest that further investigation is warranted to characterize the relationship between mood disorders and PPCM [30]. Mood disorders, unlike other factors associated with PPCM, are potentially modifiable with increased rates of referral for mental health services and optimization of pharmacotherapy and behavioral health therapy. Whether positive psychological well‐being initiatives may be protective against future CVD disease or improve outcomes following cardiac events is an area of active research [14, 33].

Our study has several strengths. It included a large sample size allowing the study of rare outcomes such as PPCM and other cardiac events. The weighted sampling design of the NIS database ensured diverse representation across insurance status, race, income strata, and both urban and rural hospital settings, lending to the generalizability of our findings to the broader US population [37]. Limitations of our study include the use of ICD‐10 codes for research purposes, relying on coding accuracy and limiting disease severity data; however, prior studies have demonstrated reasonable accuracy [18]. Additional limitations to any study utilizing the NIS dataset are that it is cross‐sectional data from single inpatient hospitalizations, thus, lacks the ability to discriminate temporally between conditions present on admission and new conditions acquired during the hospitalization, limiting the ability to assess causation over association and the chronicity of conditions. Finally, the NIS does not report readmission data or post‐discharge data precluding analysis of long‐term outcomes and cannot account for potential disparities in mental health screenings.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we found that perinatal mood disorders were associated with increased rates of PPCM and other cardiac events. These findings support mood disorders as a risk factor in pregnancy and postpartum for PPCM and adverse cardiac events.
