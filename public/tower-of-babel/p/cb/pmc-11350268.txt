Introduction

Gynecomastia is a benign enlargement of the male breast secondary to a proliferation of ductal, stromal, and/or fatty tissue. The term is derived from the Greek words ‘gyneka’ (woman) and ‘mastos’ (breast) [1]. This is the commonest male breast disorder with incidence ranging from 40 % to 55 % [2].

The primary aetiology appears to be an imbalance between the actions of estrogen and androgen on breast tissue. However, other causative factors can also give rise to gynecomastia such as drug use (spironolactone, calcium channel blockers) or pathological conditions such as cirrhosis, testicular neoplasms, and hypogonadism [3].

Three age distributions have been identified in relation to development of gynecomastia. The first peak is found in the neonatal period secondary to trans-placental passage of estrogen which usually regresses by the age of 1 year. The next age distribution is found during puberty due to hormonal imbalance and the final peak is found in older men with the most frequent causes being hypogonadism and pharmaceutical drug use [4,5].

Asymptomatic gynecomastia is quite prevalent and typically self-limiting. Reassurance and regular follow-up are advised in the absence of significant underlying pathology being discovered following proper work-up. Surgical treatment is generally recommended for patients with long-lasting gynecomastia, which does not regress spontaneously. Surgical intervention has also been shown to improve physical and psychosocial functioning significantly which is mostly pronounced in the adolescent population [6].

Several classification systems have been described in the literature to characterize the severity of male breast hypertrophy. [7,8]

These classifications assist the surgeon in deciding the type and most suitable surgery for a patient. The Simon classification system was described in 1973 and focused on a qualitative assessment of skin redundancy and breast volume [7]. (Table 1).

The goal of the surgery in gynecomastia is to restore the male thorax to its normal appearance with minimal scarring [9]. The choice of the surgical technique is mainly decided upon the size of the gynecomastia, the skin redundancy and correction of nipple position [10]. Standard surgery such as liposuction and subcutaneous mastectomy with peri-areolar incision and Benelli incision are suitable for grade I and II gynecomastia. However, in grade III gynecomastia with larger breast volumes, the usual methods of subcutaneous mastectomy will be inadequate as they do not correct excess skin redundancy (Fig. 1). Another aspect to consider especially in grade III gynecomastia is the degree of ptosis or the nipple to inframammary fold (IMF) distance. Higher the distance, the amount of tissue resection is larger and may compromise the blood supply to NAC.

Two patients with Grade III gynecomastia with excessive skin and deviation of nipple areolar complex.

Therefore, a variety of surgical techniques have been described in literature for grade III gynecomastia in order to remove excess skin and move the nipple areolar complex to a more anatomically acceptable position. These include minimally invasive techniques such as liposuction and endoscopic mastectomy to surgical approaches such as mastectomy with skin resection with superior or inferior subdermal pedicles or mastectomy through inframammary fold (IMF) approach with free nipple graft etc. Unfortunately, these procedures may need revision surgery, cause unsightly scars or may give rise to nipple loss. There is also no universally accepted algorithm regarding the ideal surgical approach given the description of a wide variety of surgical techniques. [11,12]

Thus, management of grade III gynecomastia is challenging. Herein, we report our experience in the use of the circular incision bi-pedicled vertical flap based mastectomy for grade III gynecomastia described by Mário Múcio Maia de Medeiros in 2012 as his personal modification to the current techniques [13]. This modified technique is named as circular incision with inferior, superior, lateral, and medial extensions and especially suited for patients who required a wider resection of breast tissue in the lower pole. The skin marking is based on the ideal position for the nipple-areola complex (NAC) with superior, inferior, medial, and lateral markings drawn to allow resection of excess skin as needed (flying saucer appearance). This avoids flaccidity upon removal of the glandular and fatty tissue and improves the NAC positioning. The vertical bipedicled de-epidermized flap is maintained to promote a proper blood flow through the NAC [14] (Fig. 2).

Method

This is a retrospective study conducted between January 2022 and April 2023 in selected two units in Sri Lanka. Patients who presented to the breast clinics with grade III gynecomastia were included in the study.

The severity of the gynecomastia and the suitability for the aforementioned surgical correction was decided based on the surgical experts' decision on pre-operative assessment with the Simon's classification, the need for skin resection and also the patients' preference. All surgeries were performed by the breast surgeon or the senior registrar. The resected specimens were not sent for histological analysis.

Socio-demographic and clinical data were recorded with age, weight, height, grade of gynecomastia according to Simon's classification, location (unilateral or bilateral), cause of gynecomastia, symptoms (aesthetics, palpable mass, pain).

Post-operative complications were documented such as hematoma, seroma, nipple numbness and loss. The aesthetic outcome was assessed by assessing patient satisfaction with an interviewer based questionnaire on overall satisfaction, breast contour, wound scars, improvement in self-confidence. This was a 10 point Likert scale questionnaire [16]. Surgical correction of gynecomastia in this study was performed using the aforementioned technique as described below.

Surgical procedure

Pre-operative markings were drawn in the upright sitting position to obtain the optimal NAC placement and to resect the excess skin (Fig. 3). The skin markings were based on the ideal position for the nipple-areola complex (NAC) with superior, inferior, medial, and lateral markings drawn to allow resection of excess skin as needed (flying saucer appearance).

Circular incision with superior, inferior, lateral and medial extensions.

All patients were operated under general anaesthesia. At first, the vertical bi-pedicled flap was de-epithelialized (Fig. 2). Then, the subcutaneous mastectomy was performed with removal of the excess skin. When the gland was completely free, it was delivered through the incision. Good haemostasis was achieved. The circular space for areolar was recreated by approximating the dermis of the superior and inferior incisions. Skin was sutured with absorbable polyglecaprone 3/0. Suction drain was inserted and brought out through the lateral mammary incision and compression dressing was applied (Fig. 4).

From left to right: Subcutaneous mastectomy with excision of excess skin, preservation of the pedicle to the NAC, resected specimen and approximation of dermis to recreate NAC.

Results

A total of 7 patients were included in this study with a median age of 24 years (range: 18–42 years). The mean BMI was 23.2 kg/m2 (19.2–25.6 kg/m2). All patients had bilateral grade 111 gynecomastia. All patients exhibited near symmetrical breasts and large areolar diameters of >3 cm. All patients presented to the clinic due to the physical and psychological dissatisfaction with their condition and sought an aesthetic correction. Two patients were diagnosed with Klinefelter syndrome and others were apparently healthy. All patients had pre-operative grade III gynecomastia according to Simon classification. All patients underwent ultrasound scan of the breasts which showed glandular breast tissue with or without adipose tissue.

The main complication was seroma formation (7/7). This was treated with aspiration of seroma until clinically resolved. No patients developed haematoma. Nipple hypothesia was noted in two patients which improved with time. The follow- up duration was 4–9 months. The survey of patient satisfaction showed 9 for contour, 8.6 for wound scars, 9.2 for overall satisfaction and 9.6 for improvement in self-confidence (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Our study consisted of seven patients who were either young or middle aged males. Grade III gynecomastia with excessive skin is relative uncommon compared to other grades. Similar studies show limited number of patients within a comparable age range [11,17]. This category of patients is more like to be significantly impacted psychologically and socially by the feminine appearance of the chest and will benefit greatly from a well-planned surgery as shown in our study.

However, this is the most challenging group to be surgically corrected. Poor decision making can lead to poor aesthetic outcomes. Excessive skin resection with peri-areolar skin incision for this type of gynecomastia will end up with unnatural widening of the NAC, a common complication following excessive skin resection [18]. The very fact that several surgical options are available for the treatment of severe gynecomastia suggests that no single option has been validated as being the best.

One drawback in this technique is the visibility of the scar. However, in comparison to other surgical approaches such as the inframammary fold mastectomy or wise pattern reduction this technique has a limited scar [19].

Liposuction is also another popular commonly preferred surgical modality as it is minimally invasive with minimal scarring and good aesthetic outcome. The most common complication with this technique when adopted alone for grade 3 gynecomastia is the residual sub-areolar gland tissue and excess skin resulting in an unacceptable appearance, warranting revision surgery. Therefore, liposuction assisted surgery is generally advocated as it results in minimal incision, which is one drawback of the described technique in comparison to liposuction. [20,21]

One advantage of this technique is it allows resection of excessive skin and placement of the NAC at the correct anatomical site. This procedure also leaves room to reduce the NAC diameter (2.5 -3 cm) to an appropriate size since large diameter NAC is a common occurrence with Grade III gynecomastia [22]. This was noted in our study group as all patients required reduction of the NAC size.

We did not observe any major surgical complications such as haematoma formation, wound dehiscence or nipple loss. Seroma formation was seen in all patients which is most likely due to the large volumes resected in Grade III gyenocomastia. Nipple sensation was also preserved which might not be achievable in surgical techniques that include free nipple grafting. Techniques based on pedicles usually preserve the tactile sensitivity of the NAC [12]. In our study, a bi-pedicled flap was preserved for the NAC. Further, this technique can be easily practised in resource limited hospitals.

Overall this surgical technique ticks most of the objectives of a gynecomastia surgery such as a contained aesthetic incision, correction of the NAC site and size and excision of redundant skin. The patients also reported satisfactory aesthetic outcome and improvement in confidence which is a major aim of successful treatment.

Limitations

Further studies with a larger sample size is recommended as our study has a limited sample size.

Conclusion

Surgical treatment for gynecomastia must be considered individually and treatment tailored accordingly given the availability of wide variety of surgical techniques.

We have described our experience in the use of this surgical technique for correction of severe grade III gynecomastia. This surgical technique allows the surgeon to achieve satisfactory aesthetic results with a limited scar and can be practiced in a resource limited hospital. This technique can be considered as a surgical option for grade III gynecomastia.
