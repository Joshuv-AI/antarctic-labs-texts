Mitogenome characterization and organellar genome evolution in achlorophyllous orchid Chamaegastrodia shikokiana Makino & F.Maek. (Orchidaceae)
Authors: Young-Kee Kim, Hyoung Tae Kim, Jung Sung Kim, Jun-Ho Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile plastome evolution in mycoheterotrophic plants is well documented, the mitogenome remains poorly characterized in these lineages—particularly within Orchidaceae, where no complete mitogenomes from the subfamily Orchidoideae have been reported in Genbank. Here, we present the first complete mitogenome of Chamaegastrodia shikokiana, a fully mycoheterotrophic orchid in Orchidoideae, to investigate its mitogenomic structure, gene content, and intracellular gene transfers.ResultsThe mitogenome of Chamaegastrodia shikokiana, consists of 19 contigs with a total length of 412,787 bp. Unlike its reduced plastome, the mitogenome retains almost the full complement of protein-coding genes except four genes (nad1 exon1, rpl2, rpl16, and rps19). Twenty-one plastome-derived homologous regions were detected in the mitogenome, including photosynthetic-related and housekeeping genes. Phylogenetic analysis of rbcL-like sequences indicated recurrent intracellular gene transfer events. Despite this, no clear correlation was found between transferred regions and elevated substitution rates or selection pressure. RELAX analysis revealed a mix of relaxed and intensified selection among plastome genes, but most genes under selection pressure were not associated with transferred sequences. Notably, conserved synteny between transferred genes and plastome-derived tRNAs suggests that tRNA-mediated recombination may have facilitated these integrations prior to severe plastome degradation.ConclusionIt is revealed that plastome-derived gene integration into the mitogenome in C. shikokiana does not coincide with accelerated evolutionary rates or altered selection pressure. This suggests that intracellular gene transfer (IGT) may occur independently of functional constraint or adaptive significance. Despite extensive plastome degradation, the conserved mitogenome gene content in C. shikokiana underscores the evolutionary resilience of mitochondrial genomes. This study establishes a foundational reference for understanding organellar genome evolution in mycoheterotrophic orchids and highlights the need for broader mitogenomic data across Orchidaceae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08437-6.

Background

Orchidaceae is one of the largest angiosperm families, comprising approximately 28,000 species across 736 genera [1, 2]. A distinguishing feature of this family is its minute seeds, which lack endosperm, rendering them obligately dependent on external nutrient sources during germination [3]. Consequently, all orchids exhibit an initial mycoheterotrophic stage, relying on fungal associations for carbon acquisition during early seedling development [4]. While many species develop photosynthetic capabilities as they mature, some retain this reliance partially or fully into adulthood [5]. Fully mycoheterotrophic orchids, in particular, have completely lost photosynthetic ability and rely entirely on mycorrhizal fungi for carbon uptake [6]. According to recent estimates, 235 species across 43 genera in Orchidaceae are putative full or nearly full mycoheterotrophs [6]. Notably, these mycoheterotrophic species are confined to three subfamilies: Vanilloideae, Orchidoideae, and Epidendroideae, whereas the subfamilies Apostasioideae and Cypripedioideae remain predominantly autotrophic [6].

Chloroplasts and mitochondria are essential for photosynthesis and cellular respiration, respectively [7, 8]. However, the loss of photosynthetic capability in mycoheterotrophs is associated with significant structural and functional alterations in the plastome, including reductions in genome size and gene content [9, 10]. Since the first complete plastome of a green plant, Nicotiana tabacum L., was reported [11], numerous plastome sequences have been published across angiosperms, such as Sesamum L. [12], Panax L. [13], and Phalaenopsis Blume [14]. Notably, these published plastome sequences include mycoheterotrophic species such as Cyrtosia Blume [15], Galeola Lour. [16], Lecanorchis Blume [17] within the subfamily Vanilloideae; Chamaegastrodia Makino & Maek. [17], Rhizanthella R.S.Rogers [18] within the Orchidoideae; and others within the Epidendroideae [19–27]. Comparative analyses of these sequenced plastomes revealed that fully mycoheterotrophic orchids often undergo substantial plastome degradation and structural rearrangement [9, 10, 15, 17, 19, 21–28].

In contrast, mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) have been relatively unexplored due to their structural complexity [29–31]. Plant mitogenomes exhibit substantial variation in both size and structure across species, largely attributable to the expansion of repetitive sequences, frequent genomic rearrangements, and insertions of foreign DNA [29–31]. Consequently, only 688 mitogenome sequences are available for Viridiplantae in the NCBI database, compared to the over 14,707 complete plastome sequences currently available (accessed July 2025, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/organelle/). Within Orchidaceae, complete mitogenomes have been characterized for only a few genera, such as Apostasia Blume [32] (Apostasioideae), Paphiopedilum Pfitzer [33] (Cypripedioideae), Galeola (Vanilloideae), and Cymbidium Sw. [34], Dendrobium Sw. [35], Gastrodia R.Br. [23, 36], and Phalaenopsis [37] (Epidendroideae). Notably, although whole genome shotgun data for Platanthera Rich. (Orchidoideae) have been generated [38], no complete mitogenome assembly has yet been reported for this subfamily. Current understanding of mitogenome evolution in mycoheterotrophs is heavily biased toward the subfamily Epidendroideae [23, 34–37]. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the structural complexity and gene conservation observed in taxa like Gastrodia represent a universal feature of mycoheterotrophy or lineage-specific traits restricted to Epidendroideae. To resolve this, comparative data from the distantly related subfamily Orchidoideae are essential.

Beyond structural variation, gene transfer events, including intracellular gene transfer (IGT) and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) among the nuclear genome, plastome and mitogenomes are well documented in angiosperms [32, 39–44]. Notably, HGT has been observed in the context of orchid-fungus symbiosis, which is central to the mycoheterotrophic lifestyle in orchids [45]. Such gene transfers have been reported in the mitogenomes of Aeginetia L. (Orobanchaceae) [46], Gastrodia [36], and Valeriana L. (Caprifoliaceae) [47]. However, the lack of complete mitogenomic data significantly limits our understanding to assess the impact of these transfer events in the subfamily Orchidoideae.

The genus Chamaegastrodia currently includes four species: Chamaegastrodia inverta (W.W.Sm.) Seidenf. [48], Chamaegastrodia shikokiana Makino & F.Maek. [49], Chamaegastrodia vaginata (Hook.f.) Seidenf. [48], and the recently described Chamaegastrodia guidongensis L.Wu, H.Z.Tian & C.Z.Huang [50]. C. shikokiana is a fully mycoheterotrophic orchid belonging to the subtribe Goodyerinae, tribe Cranichideae, subfamily Orchidoideae, within the family Orchidaceae [51]. C. shikokiana is distributed in South Korea, China, Japan, and India and is characterized by its complete dependence on mycorrhizal fungi, such as the members of Ceratobasidiaceae, for carbon acquisition [6, 51, 52]. Although plastome sequences of C. shikokiana and C. inverta have been reported [17, 53], and morphological and cytological analyses have been previously conducted [48, 50, 54], no complete mitogenome has yet been reported for either C. shikokiana or any member of the subfamily Orchidoideae. Due to the limited availability of mitogenomic data from the subfamily Orchidoideae, phylogenetic relationships within Orchidaceae remain insufficiently resolved at the mitochondrial level. In this context, C. shikokiana serves as a valuable model plant for elucidating organellar genome evolution and mitochondrial-based phylogenetic relationships in nonphotosynthetic Orchidoideae species.

Therefore, this study presents the complete mitogenome assembly of C. shikokiana, representing the first mitogenomic report for the subfamily Orchidoideae. The absence of mitogenomic data from this species-rich subfamily has previously hindered a comprehensive assessment of organellar genome evolution across the Orchidaceae. Specifically, our research objectives are to: (1) characterize the structural architecture and gene content of the C. shikokiana mitogenome to determine if reductive evolution extends beyond the plastome; (2) identify mechanisms of intracellular gene transfer (IGT), particularly focusing on tRNA-mediated recombination and the retention of plastome-derived sequences; and (3) establish the phylogenetic position of Chamaegastrodia within the Orchidoideae using a mitochondrial-based framework. This research will contribute to a deeper understanding of mitogenome evolution in fully mycoheterotrophic orchids and offer broader insights into the genomic consequences of trophic transition in plants.

Methods

Sampling, DNA extraction, sequencing, assembly and annotation

Whole plants of C. shikokiana, including the entire root system, were collected from Jeju Island, Republic of Korea. To minimize disturbance to the natural population, only five individuals were sampled (N33.3126512°, E126.4564136°). Sample was identified using morphological approaches and verified by Dr. Young-Kee Kim. The voucher-specimen was deposited in the Korea University Herbarium (KUS), Seoul, Republic of Korea, under the accession number of KUS2019-0841 (Fig. S1). Fresh tissues from flowers and floral bracts were collected from a single individual. Subsequently, the collected tissues were ground into fine powder using liquid nitrogen in a mortar. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the powdered tissue using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and integrity were assessed using UV-Vis spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted genomic DNA was deposited in the Plant DNA Bank in Korea (PDBK), Seoul, Republic of Korea, under the accession number of PDBK2019-0841.

The extracted genomic DNA of C. shikokiana was subjected to short-read sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, CA, US), generating a total of 781,446,152 raw reads. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea), following protocols for Illumina short read libraries. The quality of the sequencing data was assessed using FastQC v.0.11.98 [55]. Adapter trimming and quality filtering of the raw reads were performed using Trimmomatic v.0.89 [56] with the following parameters (ILLUMINACLIP: TruSeq3:2:30:10, LEADING:20, TRAILING:20, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:36).

Quality and adapter trimmed reads were initially subjected to de novo assembly using GetOrganelle v.1.7.5 [57], with the recommended parameter settings (-R 20 -k 21,45,65,85,105 -P 1000000). Preliminary gene annotations were subsequently conducted using Geneious v.10.2.6 [58] to identify and extract putative protein-coding genes. These trimmed reads, together with the extracted sequences, were used to perform NOVOPlasty v.4.3.1 [59], with a k-mer length of 105. To isolate mitochondrial contigs, local BLASTN searches were performed against a custom database of green plant mitogenomes previously constructed by Kim et al. (2023) [36, 60]. Screening was carried out on contigs derived from GetOrganelle and NOVOPlasty results, using the following BLASTN parameters (blastn -query -db -outfmt 6 -out).

Putative mitochondrial contigs were initially screened for gene content using the find annotation function in Geneious, based on Gastrodia pubilabiata Y.Sawa (subfamily Epidendroideae) mitogenome sequences (Genbank accession number of OR004100-OR004143). Because no mitochondrial genome sequences have yet been reported from the subfamily Orchidoideae, we used a reference genome from the closely related subfamily Epidendroideae, which also includes mycoheterotrophic orchids, for annotation. Protein-coding genes missing from the draft C. shikokiana were identified (nad1 exon1, rpl2, rpl16, and rps19) and the corresponding protein-coding genes were extracted from the G. pubilabiata mitogenome. These extracted sequences were compiled into a seed file for using in a second de novo assembly with NOVOPlasty. The aim of this reassembly was to verify the presence and integrity of the protein-coding genes in the C. shikokiana mitogenome.

Trimmed reads and filtered mitochondrial contigs were subjected to error correction and de novo assembly, using SPAdes v.3.13.1 [61], with the following parameters (spades − 1–2 -o --trusted-contigs -k 105). Mitochondrial contigs from the SPAdes output were further filtered through local BLASTN searches and BANDAGE v.0.8.0 [62]. The resulting putative mitochondrial contigs were manually assembled using the Geneious assembler. Sequencing coverage depth across putative mitochondrial contigs was calculated using BWA 0.7.19 [63] and SAMtools v.1.21 [64]. Due to the high frequency of repeat elements and structural recombination commonly observed in angiosperm mitogenomes [29–31], circularization of the mitochondrial genome was not attempted in this study. Instead, all putative mitochondrial contigs were treated as linear sequences. A total of 19 contigs were confirmed as mitogenome of C. shikokiana.

Mitochondrial genome contigs were annotated using a combination of tools, including BLASTN, tRNAscan-SE v.2.0 [65], ORF finder and find annotation function in Geneious. A total of 27 published orchid mitogenome sequences were retrieved from NCBI and used as reference data for the Geneious annotation process (see Table S1). Alternative start codons, ACG and TTG, were also considered during open reading frame (ORF) prediction. Determination of pseudogenes and gene absence was conducted according to criteria outlines in previous studies [17]. A linear representation of the mitogenome was generated using the OGDraw web server [66].

Genome characterization

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified using Phobos v.3.3.12 [67], with minimum repeat thresholds set at 10, 5, 3, 3, and 3 for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, and pentanucleotide motifs, respectively. Identified SSRs were summarized in Table S2. For comparative repeat analysis, the plastome sequence of C. shikokiana (NC046802) was retrieved from NCBI, reported by Kim et al. (2020) [17]. Both plastome and mitogenome sequences of C. shikokiana were analyzed using the repeat finder tool in Geneious, with the minimum repeat length set to 100 bp and no mismatches permitted. The results of this analysis are presented in Table S3.

A total of 28 publicly available orchid mitogenomes, along with the newly assembled mitogenome of C. shikokiana, were used to validate and compare gene content. Additionally, 109 orchid plastome sequences were retrieved from NCBI to assess plastid gene content. The accession numbers for all downloaded sequences are provided in Table S1. Gene content comparisons were visualized using the ggplot2 [68], dplyr [69], and readr [70] in R [71]. For C. shikokiana, graphical representations of protein-coding genes, SSRs, and repeats in both the plastome and mitogenome were generated using Circos [72].

Phylogenetic analysis

Fifty-three orchid plastome sequences were retrieved from NCBI for phylogenetic analysis (Table S1). Due to the highly reduced plastomes in some mycoheterotrophic orchid species, a subset of 38 conserved protein-coding genes was selected and extracted from the plastome sequences. Each region was individually aligned using MAFFT v.7.450 [73] with default parameters. The resulting alignments were trimmed using trimAl v.1.5.rev0 [74], under default parameters. For model selection, each trimmed alignment was analyzed with ModelFinder implemented in IQ-TREE2 v.2.3.6 [75, 76], and the selected models were summarized in Table S4. All trimmed alignments were then concatenated into a single datamatrix using AMAS [77], resulting in a total alignment length of 31,890 bp. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE2 with partitioned models and ultrafast bootstrap approximation (-s -p -m MFP+MERGE -B 1000) [76, 78]. The resulting tree (log-likelihood = -207693.6477) was visualized using TreeGraph v.2.15.0-887 [79].

Twenty-eight orchid mitochondrial genomes were retrieved from NCBI for phylogenetic analysis (Table S1). A total of 38 protein-coding genes were obtained from these genomes and analyzed with methods consistent with those used for the plastome dataset. The concatenated alignment comprised 39,623 bp, and the resulting phylogeny was inferred and visualized in TreeGraph2 (log-likelihood = -93,628.9159).

Putative gene transfer events survey

To investigate and characterize the evolutionary history of putative gene transfer events, two previously used datamatrices were utilized as reference datasets [36]. Taxonomic metadata were incorporated into the datamatrices to facilitate accurate inference of evolutionary patterns. Local BLASTN searches were conducted to assess sequence homology and identify the potential origin of the transferred regions, using the following parameters (-query -db -out -outfmt 6). The resulting BLASTN output files were processed using BEDTools [80] with merge function to consolidate overlapping hits. Each merged BLASTN dataset was visualized using the ggplot2 and dplyr packages in R. A summary of BLASTN results is provided in Table S5.

The consolidating and overlapping hits regions were extracted to investigate whether the region contain protein-coding genes or not. Only protein-coding genes were extracted from the previously used datamatrices, and then extracted sequences were used to construct protein-coding region datamatrix (makeblastdb) [36]. Local BLASTN was performed using the following parameters (-query -db -out -outfmt 6). The resulting BLASTN output file was summarized in Table S6.

To further explore the evolutionary dynamics of putative gene transfer, a total of 21 candidate regions were identified that showed sequence homology to plastome derived protein-coding genes. These homologous regions were categorized based on their corresponding plastome gene names, resulting in the identification of 18 unique protein-coding genes. These gene sequences were used to construct maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees to infer the potential origins of the intra or interspecific transferred genes.

For this phylogenetic analysis, 50 representative angiosperm plastome sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database (Table S1). The corresponding protein-coding genes were extracted from each plastome and aligned individually using MAFFT with default parameters. Each alignment was subsequently used to construct phylogenetic trees using IQ-TREE2 with the following command (-s -m MFP+MERGE -B 1000). Summary statistics for each alignment, including alignment length, best-fit model, and log-likelihood values, were presented in Table S7. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using TreeGraph2.

Evolutionary analyses and selection pressures

To investigate evolutionary rate variation within the Orchidaceae, a total of 112 orchid plastome sequences and 28 mitogenome sequences were analyzed. Thirty-seven plastome and 34 mitogenome protein-coding genes were extracted and aligned using the codon-aware aligner MACSE v.2.07 under default parameters [81]. Sequences containing premature stop codons or frameshift mutations were manually removed prior to downstream analyses. Each codon alignment was used to construct phylogenetic trees with IQ-TREE2 (-s -m MFP+MERGE -B 1000). Nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitution rates were estimated using the CODEML v.4.10.7 in PAML [82]. To assess differences in evolutionary rates between photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic orchids, both the free-ratio model (model = 2) and the one-ratio model (model = 0) were applied. Log-likelihood values (lnL) from each model were compared using likelihood ratio test (LRT), performed in R. Results are summarized in Table S8.

To reduce computation burden and focus on selection pressure within the subfamily Orchidoideae, a subset of the dataset was used: one representative plastome each from Apostasioideae, Cypripedioideae, Epidendroideae, and Vanilloideae, along with 49 plastomes from Orchidoideae. Protein-coding genes that showed statistically significant differences in the previous evolutionary rates analysis via CODEML were selected for further investigation. In total, 17 plastome and two mitochondrial protein-coding genes were included in the selection pressure analysis.

Each gene was aligned using MACSE, and sequences premature stop codons or frameshift mutations were manually excluded. The resulting alignments were analyzed using RELAX [83], implemented in HyPhy 2.5.33 [84] under the GTR substitution model. Analyses were performed separately to estimate selection intensity and substitution rates for (1) nonphotosynthetic, mycoheterotrophic orchids as the test group, and (2) the two Chamaegastrodia species. Due to the availability of only one Chamaegastrodia mitogenome, mitochondrial selection analyses were conducted only for the nonphotosynthetic test group. Output json files were parsed by in-house Python script to extract and summarize LRT results, p-values, and selection intensity parameters.

Results

Mitogenome characterization of C. shikokiana

The mitochondrial genome of C. shikokiana was assembled into 19 contigs, with a total length of 412,787 bp (Table 1; Fig. 1). The overall GC content was 27.2%, and the average sequencing coverage depth was 4,159.28x. Due to the high prevalence of AT-rich regions, structural rearrangements, and frequent recombination events, reconstruction of a complete, circular mitogenome was not feasible. Consequently, the mitochondrial genome was retained in a linear contig format. A total of 35 protein-coding genes, seven transfer RNA (tRNA), and two ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes were annotated. Comparative analysis revealed the absence of four commonly conserved genes (nad1 exon1, rpl2, rpl16, and rps19) against other reported orchid mitogenomes (Fig. 2). Notably, while nad1 exon1 was absent, all other exons of nad1 (exons 2–5) were identified within the mitochondrial contigs.

The plastome of C. shikokiana has been previously published and is available in the NCBI database [17]. The plastome spans 130,934 bp, with a GC content of 36.3% (Table 1). A total of 39 protein-coding genes, 30 tRNA genes, and four rRNA genes were identified. Compared to photosynthetic orchids, the plastome of C. shikokiana contains a substantially reduced number of protein-coding genes (Fig. 3), consistent with its fully mycoheterotrophic lifestyle.

A total of 103 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified within the mitogenome of C. shikokiana, including 15 mononucleotide, 24 dinucleotide, 16 trinucleotide, 44 tetranucleotide, and four pentanucleotide motifs (Table S2). To investigate repeat structure conservation across organellar genomes, a comparative repeat analysis was performed between the plastome and mitogenome. This analysis revealed a total of 42 repeat regions. Of these, 32 were unique to the mitogenome, one was exclusive to the plastome (inverted repeat regions), and nine were shared between both organellar genomes (Fig. 4, Table S2, and S3).

Phylogenetic relationship

In the mitogenome-based phylogenetic tree, C. shikokiana was positioned between Cypripedioideae and Epidendroideae, forming an isolated lineage. This isolated phylogenetic placement is likely attributable to the absence of other mitogenome sequences from Orchidoideae (Fig. 5). Notably, the mycoheterotrophic genera Galeola Lour., Gastrodia, and Chamaegastrodia exhibited long branch lengths. Most nodes in the mitogenome tree were also supported by bootstrap values ≥ 80%. In the maximum likelihood (ML) tree based on plastome protein-coding genes, C. shikokiana and C. inverta formed a strongly supported monophyletic clade (bootstrap support = 100), corroborating their close evolutionary relationship (Fig. 6). This clade was recovered as sister to the genus Odontochilus Blume, also with bootstrap support of 100.

A total of 106,631 BLASTN hits were retrieved. These results were aligned and screened to identify distinct regions of homology, yielding 153 candidate regions for putative gene transfer events (Fig. 7; Table 2, Table S5 and S6).

Evolutionary rate variation and selection pressure in organelles

Protein-coding genes from the plastome and mitogenome of C. shikokiana were analyzed to compare evolutionary rate between photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic orchid species. Among the 37 plastome protein-coding genes tested, 18 regions including accD, atpE, atpH, ndhK, psaJ, psbE, psbH, psbK, psbT, rpl14, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps11, rps12, rps14, and ycf1, showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05; Table S10). Of these, five genes (atpH, ndhK, psbE, rps11, and rps4) displayed highly significant variation (P < 0.00001). In contrast, analysis of the 34 mitochondrial protein-coding genes revealed only two genes (cox1 and nad6) with statistically significant rate differences between photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic taxa (P < 0.05), while the remaining 32 genes did not exhibit significant variation.

To assess shifts in selection pressure, 18 protein-coding genes from the plastome and mitogenome of C. shikokiana were analyzed using the RELAX framework. Two independent analyses were conducted to evaluate potential differences in selection intensity: (1) between photosynthetic and non-photosynthetic orchid species, and (2) between the two Chamaegastrodia species and the remaining taxa.

Of the 18 genes tested, 13 plastome genes and one mitogenome gene showed statistically significant shifts in selection pressure (P < 0.05; Table 3). Among them, five plastome genes (atpH, psbE, rps11, rps12, and rps2) yielded highly significant results (P < 0.00001). Three plastome genes-atpH, ndhK, and psbK were identified as being under intensified selection (K > 1), indicating an elevated selective pressure relative to the reference branches (photosynthetic species). Conversely, the remaining ten genes were inferred to be under relaxed selection (K < 1), suggesting a reduction in selective constraint.

Discussion

Structural evolution of organelle genomes

Plastome evolution in the genus Chamaegastrodia is characterized by distinct reductive trends and convergent structural rearrangements. Specifically, the plastome of C. shikokiana (NC046802) is moderately reduced in size (130,934 bp; Fig. S2), whereas that of its congeneric species, C. inverta (MW589502), exhibits a more extensive reduction (116,220 bp). Both species display distinct structural rearrangements characterized by the contraction of the SSC region and the expansion of the IR region. While IR expansion is typically associated with plastome structural stability via homologous recombination [85, 86], such expansion does not universally correlate with increased stability across certain orchid genera [87].

Notably, these structural alterations, particularly SSC contraction, are not unique to Chamaegastrodia, but appear to be convergent events occurring in three subfamilies: Cypripedioideae (Paphiopedilum) [87], Vanilloideae (Pogonia Juss. and Vanilla Plum. ex Mill.) [28] and Orchidoideae [88]. This contrasts with Apostasioideae and Epidendroideae, which generally retain the canonical SSC length. Within the Orchidoideae, genera such as Corybas Salisb., Eurystyles Wawra, Lankesterella Ames, and Odontochilus have also been reported to contain shortened SSC regions [88]. Interestingly, SSC length within Odontochilus is variable (9,821 bp of O. yakushimensis (Yamam.) T.Yukawa to 17,286 bp of O. tortus King & Pantl.). Furthermore, unlike mycoheterotrophic Vanilloideae (e.g., Cyrtosia, Galeola, and Lecanorchis) which display extensive genomic rearrangement, Chamaegastrodia maintains a relatively conserved gene order despite SSC contraction [89]. Collectively, this variability among closely related taxa (e.g., Odontochilus) reinforces the view that plastome structural evolution is driven by independent events rather than shared ancestry.

Unlike the plastome, identifying broad structural trends in orchid mitogenomes poses a significant challenge due to their extreme structural heterogeneity. Currently, Apostasia is the only orchid genus reported to maintain a predominantly single-circular mitogenome structure. However, even within this conserved architecture, comparative analysis reveals significant structural fluidity, including at least six large inversion events between congeneric species.

In contrast, other genera such as Dendrobium and Gastrodia exhibit complex multi-chromosomal architectures, preventing direct syntenic comparison. Notably, the number of chromosomes fluctuates wildly even within the same genus, ranging from 17 to 36 in Dendrobium and 16 to 73 in Gastrodia. Crucially, this extensive structural fragmentation manifests regardless of the trophic mode; similar multi-chromosomal patterns are observed in both the photosynthetic genus Dendrobium and the fully mycoheterotrophic genus Gastrodia, suggesting that structural complexity appears to be uncorrelated with the loss of photosynthesis.

Despite this extensive fragmentation and structural variation, the functional gene content remains remarkably conserved across these taxa (Fig. 2). This discrepancy suggests that the extreme structural plasticity of orchid mitogenomes is driven by frequent recombination and the accumulation of repetitive sequences [29–31], rather than by shared evolutionary history. Consequently, unlike plastome structural patterns which may reflect lineage-specific trends, mitogenome structural rearrangements likely represent stochastic, independent events, limiting their utility as phylogenetic markers at the structural level. However, given the currently limited number of complete mitogenome assemblies available, further comprehensive sampling across diverse subfamilies is essential to verify whether this lack of correlation between structural complexity and trophic mode is a generalized pattern across the entire Orchidaceae family.

Divergent evolution of organelle gene content

Consistent with the reduction in genome size, the plastome of C. shikokiana exhibits extensive gene loss, particularly in photosynthesis-related genes (Fig. 3). Based on the previously established plastome degradation stages [9, 10], the plastome of Chamaegastrodia can be classified at an intermediate stage between stages 3 and 4 (Fig. 3). However, the overall plastome size of Chamaegastrodia remains relatively large compared to fully reduced plastomes in other orchid genera such as Degranvillea Determann [90], Rhizanthella [18], and Silvorchis J.J.Sm [91]. This discrepancy suggests that the degradation process in Chamaegastrodia may be relatively recent in evolutionary terms. Consequently, these observations reinforce the hypothesis that gene reduction represents lineage specific and independent evolutionary events within the Orchidaceae, consistent with patterns observed in other subfamilies [28].

In contrast to the extensive gene loss in the plastome, the mitogenome of C. shikokiana shows no significant reduction or alteration in gene content (Fig. 2). Furthermore, a comparison across the five subfamilies of Orchidaceae reveals no notable reduction or modification in mitochondrial gene content (Fig. 2). These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that, unlike plastomes, mitogenomic gene composition is generally retained across trophic transitions, even in fully mycoheterotrophic lineages, likely due to the indispensability of mitochondrial respiration [36].

Despite this prevailing conservation, specific localized gene losses or modifications were identified. A few genes (e.g., nad1 exon 1, rpl2, rpl16, and rps19) appear to be absent from the mitogenome of C. shikokiana. Although reference-guided assembly was attempted using homologous genes from closely related taxa, no corresponding sequences were recovered. The absence of nad1 exon 1 is particularly intriguing given that exons 2–5 were clearly identified. This suggests a localized loss or pseudogenization event rather than a whole-gene deletion. Alternatively, this may reflect gene loss associated with RNA editing or functional transfer to the nuclear genome (IGT). Verification of these possibilities requires additional phylogenomic investigations, particularly incorporating RNA sequencing.

Broadly, the conservation of mitochondrial genes in mycoheterotrophic orchids [32–36] contrasts sharply with the substantial gene reduction documented in some parasitic plants, such as Viscum scurruloideum (Viscaceae), which retains only 19 protein-coding genes [92]. Although broader taxon sampling is needed to fully resolve these evolutionary patterns, this study fills a significant gap in orchid mitogenomes. By extending the observation of structural complexity and gene contents to the Orchidoideae, our findings suggest that such features may be widespread across derived lineages [32–36], establishing a critical baseline for future comparative analyses.

Evolutionary dynamics of putative transferred genes

In angiosperms, intracellular gene transfer (IGT) from the plastome to the mitogenome is a pervasive evolutionary process [31, 93, 94]. In C. shikokiana, a total of 21 plastome-derived regions were identified, the majority of which (11 regions) correspond to photosynthesis-related genes (e.g., ndh, psa, and psb families; Table 2). Crucially, 11 of these 21 plastome-derived regions are absent from the current plastome (Fig. 3). This pattern indicates that the transfer events likely occurred prior to the severe reduction of the plastome. Comparative analysis with other mycoheterotrophic orchids, such as Gastrodia and Epipogium, reveals shared transferred genes (e.g., psbA), yet the retention patterns vary significantly. For instance, while rps4 is retained in the Gastrodia plastome, it is absent in Epipogium. These discrepancies suggest that IGT reflects the stochastic nature of DNA uptake and the varying degrees of plastome degradation across lineages. Phylogenetic analysis further supports this, as most of the transferred regions form well-supported monophyletic clades within Orchidaceae, pointing to intra-lineage transfer events (Fig. S3).

Beyond simple transfer events, the evolutionary history of IGT in C. shikokiana is further complicated by evidence of recurrent transfer events, as exemplified by the rbcL gene (Fig. S4). Two distinct rbcL-homologous regions and sequence identities were identified with differing lengths (3,359 bp vs. 423 bp) (Fig. S4). Phylogenetic analysis places these two fragments at distinct positions within the tree (Fig. S3), suggesting they originated from separate transfer events. The shorter fragment likely represents an ancient transfer, consistent with findings in Gastrodia [36, 94], whereas the longer fragment may reflect a more recent event. Given that Chamaegastrodia diverged relatively recently (approx. 30 mya) [17], the coexistence of both ancient and recent rbcL-like sequences reinforces the hypothesis that IGT is not a singular historical accident but a continuous, dynamic process involving multiple independent events.

Mechanistically, bioinformatic analyses identify tRNA-mediated recombination as a pivotal driver of these gene transfers [95]. A total of seven tRNA genes were identified in the mitogenome, four of which (trnM-CAU, trnN-GUU, trnQ-UUG, and trnR-UCU) show homology to plastome-encoded tRNAs. Notably, conserved synteny between these tRNAs and protein-coding genes was observed. For example, the trnR-UCU gene in the mitogenome is located near an atpA-like sequence. In typical photosynthetic orchids with conserved plastomes, atpA is naturally adjacent to trnR-UCU. Since atpA has been lost in the current C. shikokiana plastome, its presence alongside trnR-UCU in the mitogenome strongly suggests that a block of plastid DNA containing both genes was co-transferred prior to the degradation of the plastome. This implies that tRNA genes, known hotspots for structural rearrangements [95], acted as anchors or vehicles, facilitating the integration of adjacent functional genes into the mitogenome before they were lost from the plastome.

Elevated evolutionary rates and selection pressure

Comparative studies in parasitic and mycoheterotrophic plants have demonstrated that the loss of photosynthetic function is often accompanied by elevated substitution rates, particularly within the plastome [10, 28]. In contrast, plant mitogenomes tend to evolve more slowly at the sequence level, despite exhibiting rapid structural rearrangements [96]. To evaluate whether similar evolutionary trends occur in C. shikokiana, substitution rate differences in protein-coding genes between photosynthetic and non-photosynthetic orchid species were examined. While these comparisons represent relative rate assessments rather than absolute measures of dN or dS, the results align with previous findings: the mitogenome of C. shikokiana generally exhibits lower substitution rates compared to its plastome [92, 96]. Specifically, statistically significant rate shifts were identified in several plastid-encoded genes, spanning both housekeeping functions (atpE, atpH, and rps3) and photosynthesis-related roles (ndhK, psbE, and psbT).

Notably, none of these genes showing intensified selection pressure correspond to the homologous plastome-derived regions (IGTs) detected in the mitogenome. However, an ndhK-like sequence was previously reported in the mitogenome of Gastrodia pubilabiata [36], suggesting possible lineage-specific variation in gene transfer events. Although four plastome-derived regions (ndhA, psbA, psbB, and rpoB) were identified in the mycoheterotrophic orchid, Epipogium roseum, evolutionary rates of those four genes were not measured because of the drastically reduced plastome of E. roseum [97].

Regarding the plastome-derived regions (IGTs) identified within the C. shikokiana mitogenome (Table 2), none displayed significant signatures of selection intensification or elevated substitution rates compared to native mitochondrial genes. This lack of correlation suggests that the integration and retention of plastome-like sequences in the mitogenome may not be driven by ongoing molecular evolutionary pressures. Future research incorporating broader taxonomic sampling, along with functional and expression analyses of transferred regions, will be essential to clarify whether particular classes of transferred genes exhibit distinct evolutionary trajectories.

Phylogenetic relationship

Phylogenetic analyses based on organellar genomes have been extensively applied to elucidate evolutionary relationships within the Orchidaceae [17, 98]. In this study, plastome-based phylogenetic reconstruction strongly supported the placement of C. shikokiana within the Orchidoideae (Fig. 6), consistent with previous studies [17, 98]. In the mitogenome-based phylogeny, C. shikokiana was positioned as a sister group to the Epidendroideae (Fig. 5). While this topology is broadly concordant with the subfamily-level relationships, the precise phylogenetic placement within the Orchidoideae remains unresolved because no other mitogenomes from this subfamily have been reported. Consequently, further mitogenomic investigations with broader sampling across the Orchidoideae are essential to validate and refine the evolutionary position of C. shikokiana.

Conclusions

This study presents the first complete mitochondrial genome assembly of C. shikokiana, a fully mycoheterotrophic orchid from the subfamily Orchidoideae. As the first representative from this subfamily, this assembly fills a critical phylogenetic gap, enabling a comprehensive comparison of mitochondrial evolutionary trends across diverse trophic modes in Orchidaceae.

Our comparative analysis highlights a fundamental divergence in organellar evolution: unlike the plastome, which undergoes convergent reduction following the loss of photosynthesis, the mitogenome exhibits no distinct structural dichotomy between mycoheterotrophic and autotrophic orchids. We observed that extensive structural complexity and high gene conservation are shared traits across the family, driven by lineage-specific stochasticity rather than trophic strategy. Additionally, phylogenetic analyses of homologous sequences, including two distinct rbcL-like regions, suggest both ancient and recent IGT events. Furthermore, the proximity of certain transferred genes to plastome-derived tRNAs implies that tRNA-mediated recombination facilitated these transfers prior to the severe reduction of the plastome.

Collectively, these findings indicate that mitochondrial structural evolution in C. shikokiana is decoupled from the loss of photosynthesis, demonstrating that the reductive evolution characteristic of plastomes does not extend to mitogenome. The data presented here provide a foundational basis for future evolutionary and genomic studies of mycoheterotrophic lineages.
