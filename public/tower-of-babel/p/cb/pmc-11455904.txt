Association between self-care activities and glycemic control among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus in Northwest Ethiopia general hospitals : a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Samuel Agegnew Wondm, Tirsit Ketsela Zeleke, Samuel Berihun Dagnew, Tilaye Arega Moges, Getachew Yitayew Tarekegn, Eyayaw Ashete Belachew, Fasil Bayafers Tamene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes self-care activities are essential for achieving optimal glycemic control. However, little investigation has been conducted in Ethiopia to evaluate the relationship between the rate glycemic controland self-care activities among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Therefore, this study was conducted to assess self -care activities and their association with glycemic control among patients with T2DM in Northwest Ethiopia general hospitals. This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in Northwest Ethiopia general hospitals diabetic clinics. Diabetes self-care activities were measured using the Amharic version of the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA-Amharic). Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were used to assess the rate of glycemic control. A linear regression model was used to identify predictors of self-care activities and glycemic control. P-value of < 0.05 at 95% confidence interval (CI) was considerd as statistically significant. Of 413 participants included in the final analysis, two-thirds (66.3%) had poor glycemic control, with a mean HbA1c of 7.94% (SD = 1.75). Blood glucose testing was the most important self-care activity domain for predicting better glycemic control [β=-0.36, 95% CI (-0.48, -0.24); P = 0.0001] followed by diet [β=-0.29, 95% CI (-0.39, -0.083); P = 0.0001], foot-care [β=-0.28, 95% CI (-0.3, -0.061); P = 0.003], and physical activity [β=-0.27, 95% CI (-0.29, -0.056); P = 0.004], respectively. Moreover, unable to read and write [β = 0.72, 95% CI (0.57, 3.8); P = 0.037], overweight [β = 0.32, 95% CI (0.011, 0.62); P = 0.042], obesity [β = 0.67, 95% CI (0.39, 0.94); P = 0.0001], and low level of medication adherence [β = 0.7, 95% CI (0.39, 1.1); P = 0.0001] were significant predictors of poor glycemic control. Previous diabetes education [β=-0.88, 95% CI (-1.2, -0.57); P=0.0001] was a significant predictor of good glycemic control. The prevalence of poor glycemic control and poor self-care activities were high among patients with T2DM. Self-care activities were independent predictors of glycemic control among patients with T2DM. Therefore, management interventions for patients with T2DM should focus on improving self-care activities and other predictor variables.Keywords: Self-care activities, Glycemic control, Association, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, EthiopiaSubject terms: Endocrinology, Medical research

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disease with a variety of etiologies that is distinguished by persistent hyperglycemia and abnormalities in the metabolism of fat, proteins, and carbohydrates. It is caused by a malfunction in the production of insulin, the action of insulin, or both, and over time, it causes major damage to several organs1. Diabetes mellitus is expected to increase from 9.3% in 2019 to 10.2% in 2030 and 10.9% in 2045, of which 79.4% live in low- and middle-income countries2.In Ethiopia, 6.5% of the population had T2DM in 20213. An increased risk of complications, including diabetic nephropathy, diabetic neuropathy, heart disease, stroke, amputation of a leg, and death, can result from poorly treated type 2 diabetes4.Delaying the progression of diabetes can lead to improved health and economic outcomes for patients, society, and the healthcare system5.

A number of studies have shown a clinically significant correlation between self-care activities and glycemic management, which is important for attaining diabetes-related beneficial health outcomes6, 7. To maintain optimal glycemic control, patients must adhere to a healthy diet, regular physical activity, blood glucose monitoring, and foot care and treatment regimen. These diabetes self-care activities are crucial for efficient type 2 diabetes management8. Research indicates that in nations like Ethiopia, where resources are scarce and medical expenses are constantly rising, self-care activities are a more beneficial strategy with restorative and affordable outcomes9, 10. Achieving glucose control is difficult and complex because it is affected by several clinical, psychological, and sociodemographic variables. Therefore, factors associated with poor glycemic control should be identified by combining such factors, including time spent on self-care activities.

Self-care activities significantly affect glycemic control11–14. In addition, the level of glycemic control was significantly affected by various sociodemographic, clinical, psychological, and medication-related characteristics11, 14. Poor glycemic control is associated with low income15, low educational level, unemployed16, obesity, and longer duration of diabetes17, 18. High psychological distress was significantly associated with poor glycemic control12, 15 and inadequate self-care activities19, 20.

The Ethiopian literature has examined the degree of self-care activities9, 10 and the level of glycemic control21–23. However, the relationship between self-care practices and glycemic control in Ethiopian patients with T2DM remains unclear. Furthermore, whereas earlier research on self-care activities employed validated tools from outside Ethiopia, the current study used locally validated tools for measuring self-care activities of T2DM. Therefore, this study evaluated the rate of glycemic control and diabetes self-care activities and the independent significance of each diabetes self-care activity on glycemic control among patients with T2DM in selected Northwest Ethiopia general hospitals. Identifying the contribution of each self-care activity toward glycemic control among patients with T2DM is especially useful in policymaking and prioritizing diabetes self- care interventions to enhance glycemic control.

Methods

Study design and area

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted at selected government general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia, which were selected using the lottery method among other hospitals in the area. The selected hospitals were Finote-Selam General Hospital (FSGH), Injibara General Hospital (IJH), and Shegaw Motta General Hospital (SMGH). The study was conducted from January 5 to April 10, 2023. The FSGH is located in Finote Selam town, which is 176 km from Bahir Dar and 387 km from Addis Ababa. IJH is located in Injibara, which is 118 km from Bahir Dar and 435 km from Addis Ababa. SMGH is located in Motta, which is 175 km from Bahir Dar and 450 km from Addis Ababa. In total, approximately 3.6 million people were treated in the catchment area. The selected hospitals have chronic outpatient clinics, including clinics for patients with diabetes. General hospitals serve as referral centers for cases that cannot be managed by health centers, including those with diabetes mellitus.

Population, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The source population included all patients with T2DM who were receiving follow-up care at selected general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. The study population consisted of randomly selected patients with T2DM who underwent diabetic follow-up at selected hospitals during the study period. All T2DM adult patients (18 years and above) who were regularly followed up for at least 3 months were included in the study. Patients who could not communicate, were severely ill, were newly diagnosed with T2DM, or had incomplete medical records were excluded from this study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula.Because no studies have specifically examined the subjects in our study, we used a 50% prevalence rate in the sample size calculation. Thus, , where n is the desired sample size, Z is the typical normal distribution set at 1.96 (which corresponds to 95% CI), the p-value signifies that positive prevalence was utilized in calculating the optimal sample size, and W is the degree of accuracy 0.05 required (a marginal error is 0.05).Then, computed for n = 1.962*0.5 (1-0.5)/0.052, n = 384. By adding a potential non-response rate of 10% to the interview and/or missing or lost of pertinent data on the patient’s medical record, 422 patients were approached in the study. According to each hospital’s statistics, 456, 542, and 577 patients with T2DM received follow-up care at the FSGH, IJH, and SMGH in the previous three months, respectively. As a result, 1575/422 = 4 was the sampling fraction. The random start among the first 4 attendants was determined using the lottery method. The proportional allocation of samples to the total population of each hospital was applied using the following formula: n = nxNi/N, where n = required sample size, Ni = total number of patients at each hospital, and N = total number of patients at the selected hospitals. Consequently, we considered 122, 145 and 155 samples from FSGH, IJH, and SMGH, respectively. The participants were interviewed, and concurrently reviewed medical charts every 4 intervals until the total sample size was reached.

Data collection instruments and procedures

Data were collected using an interviewer who administered a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire contains information about socio-demographicfactors (age, gender, residence, religion, maritalstatus, educationastatus, occupation, wealth index), personal factors (including knowledge of diabetes, diabetes self- efficacy, having a glucometer at home, social support, depression, previous DM education), clinical factors (comorbidity, family history of DM, BMI, duration of diabetes, and family history), polypharmacy status, medication adherence, and SDSCA-Amharic version.

According to the American Diabetes Association guidelines, HbA1c levels were classified as good glycemic control when HbA1c < HbA1c < 7% (< 53mmol/mol) and poor glycemic control when HbA1c ≥ 7.0% (≥ 53mmol/mol)24.

We used a 4-factor structure with a 9-item SDSCA-Amharic instrument which is validated in Ethiopia.The overall internal consistency is 0.8525. The SDSCA-Amharic has four components of diabetic self-care management (diet, physical activity, blood glucose testing and foot care). The respondents were asked to rate how many days during the past 7 day did they perform specific self-care activities. The scale ranges from 0 to 7, where higher scores indicate greater diabetes management activities. The mean score was calculated for each domain (diet, physical activity, blood glucose testing and foot care). The overall mean score was calculated by summing the mean scores of each diet, physical activity, blood glucose test, and foot care item divided by four26. After calculating the overall mean score, patients were classified as having good self -care activities if patient scored ≥ 4 or poor self-care activities if patient scored < 426.

The Revised Brief Diabetes Knowledge Test (DKT2) was used to measure diabetes knowledge27. The DKT2 is a 23-item multiple-choice test designed to assess knowledge regarding diet, exercise, blood glucose levels and testing. Each item has three or four multiple choices with only one correct answer. Owing to lack of relevance in the Ethiopian context and because they are not recommended for non-US patients, items 1, 2, 3, 4, and 8 were omitted from this study. The first 14 items were designed for all adults with Type 2 diabetes, while 15–23 apply only to those taking insulin27. The DKT2 scores were computed for each participant. Scores were determined by dividing the number of correct answers by the total number of questions (18 for patient taking insulin and 9 oral hypoglycemic agents). Participants who scored equal to or above the mean in the DKT2 were classified as having better diabetes knowledge, whereas those who scored below the mean were considered to have less knowledge28. The Diabetes Empowerment Scale Short form (DES-SF) was used to measure self-efficacy for diabetes self-care. The DES-SF is an 8-item scale and is scored by averaging the scores of all completed items that have response options ranging from strongly disagree = 1 to strongly agree = 5. It had a coefficient alpha reliability coefficient of 0.8429. In this study, participants who scored equal to or above the mean in the DES-SF were classified as having better self-efficacy for diabetes self-care28.

Social support was assessed using the Oslo-3 social support scale (OSS-3). The OSS − 3 scores ranged from 3 to 14 with scores of 3–8 = poor social support; 9–11 = moderate social support; and 12–14 = strong social support30.Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). The PHQ-9 scores ranged from 0 to 27. Each of the 9 items was scored from 0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day. A PHQ-9 score 0–4 no depression, 5–9 indicates mild depression, 10–14 indicates moderate depression, 15–19 moderately severe depression, and a score of 20 to 27 indicates severe depression31. Moreover, the PHQ-9 has been validated in the Ethiopian healthcare context with specificity and sensitivity of 67% and 86%, respectively32.

Body mass index (BMI) was categorized according to World Health Organization classification as underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25-29.9), or obese (≥ 30)33. Patients with any chronic disease that coexisted with their diabetes were considered a comorbidity34. Polypharmacy was defined as the use of a combination of ≥ 5 medications34. Wealth index was generated using principal component analysis. Score 18 selected groups of assets and utilities, and the first factor that explained most of the variation was used to group study subjects into 4 ranked wealth groups (poorest, poor, medium and rich).

For medication adherence (MA) assessment, we used the medication adherence reporting scale-5 (MARS-5) which is a validated tool that has been used in Ethiopia to assess medication adherence for different chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus35. The MARS-5 has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77 for measuring medication adherence36. It contains 5 items and each item is rated on a 5-point likert scale, ranging from 1 (always) to 5 (never).The range of MARS-5 tool total score is between 5 and 25. The adherence level was measured by taking the summation of the response of 5 questions and was categorized as poor or good if the total score was less than the mean score and greater than or equal to the mean score, respectively35.

The data were collected by 3 clinical oriented pharmacists and 3 BSc nurses from the selected hospitals. Once the medical record identification numbers of the participants were entered into Microsoft Excel 2016 and checked for repetition, data were extracted from the medical records simultaneously, and the patients were interviewed.

Data quality control measures

Before the data collection period, the data collection instrument was pretested in 5% (20 participants) of the participants at SMGH to determine its consistency, clarity, and ease of collection. The data from the pre-tested participants were excluded from the final analysis. After some modifications to the questionnaire, actual data collection was performed. The data collectors received a 1-day training session regarding the purpose of the study, nature of the data collection instruments, and ethical considerations for the data collection procedures. On each day of the data collection period, the completed questionnaires were checked for completeness, clarity, and cleanliness by principal investigator.

Data processing and analysis

Data quality, completeness, consistency, and clarity were checked before entry. We used Epi data version 4.6.2 for data entry and exported the data to STATA version 17 for analysis. Categorical variables were described by percentage and frequency. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation. In addition, the independent sample t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the bivariable association between HbA1c levels and self- care activities with sociodemographic, clinical, and psychological behavioral characteristics. A linear regression model was performed to examine the relationship between independent and dependent variables (self-care level and glycemic control). Variables with a P- value of ≤ 0.2 in the simple linear regression analysis were further analyzed using multivariable analysis to identify factors potentially related to the level of self-care activities and glycemic control. Preliminary analysis were run to ensure that the assumptions of normality, linearity, and multicollinearity were not violated. Shapiro-Wilk tests and P-P plot was used to assess normality and linearity, respectively. Multicollinearity was checked, and the maximum variance inflation factor (VIF)was less than 5. The regression analysis results were expressed as unstandardized coefficients. A p –value of < 0.05 at the 95% CI was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Out of 422 eligible participants, 413 responded and completed the interviews, resulting in a response rate of (97.9%). The mean age of the patient was 50.62 (± 14.4) years, with more than one-third of patients 162 (39.2%) were 41–59 years. More than half of the participants 234 (56.7%) were female. More than three-quarter of patients 316 (76.5%) could not read and write. Around two-thirds of patients 260 (62.9%) were users of health insurance. Nearly one-third of patients 134 (32.45%) developed diabetes complications. The mean BMI (±SD) was 24.82 (±5.9) with 63 (15.3%) and 131 (31.7%) patients being overweight and obese, respectively. The mean DM duration was 5.4 years (± 4.32 years), with 44.6% participants had 6–10 years. Regarding the DM treatment type, 148 (35.8%), 128 (31%), and 137 (33.2%) patients received insulin, OHA, or both insulin and OHA, respectively. Nearly two-thirds of patients 257 (62.2%) had low diabetes knowledge. Nearly two- thirds of patients 262 (63.4%) had low diabetes self-efficacy. Nearly two-thirds of patients 267 (64.7%) received poor social support. More than one-third of patients 147 (35.6%) had severe depression. Nearly two-thirds of patients 268 (64.9%) did not receive diabetes education from healthcare providers. Two-thirds of patients 274 (66.3%) had poor glycemic control (Table 1).

Correlation between self-care activities and glycemic control

The overall mean score for SDSCA was 3.43 (± 1.1) days, indicating poor self-care practice, with 87.9% of participants having poor self-care activities. Physical activity was the top-rated domain of SDSCA, with a mean score of 4.42 (± 1.8) which was the only satisfactory domain of SDSCA. Blood glucose testing was the bottom-rated domain of SDSCA, with a mean of 2.26 (± 0.98)). Lower HbA1c levels were significantly associated with greater compliance in self-care activities, including diet (r=-0.55, P < 0.001), foot care (r=-0.69, P < 0.05) , physical activity (r=-0.33, P < 0.001), and blood glucose testing (r=-0.68, P < 0.001). The independent sample t-test also showed that higher self-care activities, including diet (p = 0.038), foot care (p = 0.0001), physical activity (p = 0.0001), and blood glucose testing (p = 0.026) were significantly associated with good glycemic control. Similarly, the total SDSCA score (p = 0.00014) was also significantly associated with good glycemic control (Table 2).

Factors associated with self-care activities among patients with type 2 diabetes

The linear regression results indicate that the model was explained by an adjusted R2 of 40.74%, variance inflation factor < 5 and normally fitted (P = 0.298).In the simple linear regression analysis varaibles with a p-value of < 0.2 was further considered for multivaraible anlysis to reduce counfouders for self care activities (Supplementray Table 1). Multivaraible linear regression analysis showed that diabetes self-care practice was significantly associated with educational status, wealth index, diabetes self-efficacy, diabetes knowledge, and previous diabetes education. Discussion

The cornerstones of T2DM therapy are lifestyle modification and self-care activities37. In order to reduce the course of the illness and its accompanying problems, glycemic control is a necessary goal that requires efficient treatment. The purpose of this study was to assess the rate of glycemic control and its relationship with self-care activities among patients with T2DM in selected general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia.

Two-thirds of the patients 66.3% (95% CI: 61.6, 70.9) had inadequate glycemic control, necessitating adherence to American Diabetes association (ADA) guidelines24.When DM is diagnosed, the ADA advises that patients seek self-care management38. To enhance self-care management habits, this study suggests that healthcare providers should step up their interventions, particularly for patients with type 2 diabetes in Ethiopia who lack DM education. For patients with type 2 diabetes, Ethiopian medical professionals may offer a program that will improve and encourage self-care management, particularly with regard to food, exercise, foot care, and blood glucose monitoring11, 14. The prevalence of poor glycemic control in our study is comparable with previously reported rates in Libya 69.6%39, Jordan 65.1%40, and Ethiopia16 but lower than the study conducted in Jordan 83.8%41 and Pakistan 83%11. In contrast, poor glycemic control was higher in Saudi Arabia 49.4%42, Oman 54%43, and Jordan 43.5%44. The high prevalence of poor glycemic control among the participants is alarming given that poor glycemic control among patients with T2DM has serious adverse health and financial consequences45 and requires immediate intervention.

The multivariable linear regression analysis revealed that the following variables were significantly correlated with self-care activities: wealth index, educational status, diabetes self-efficacy, diabetes knowledge, and prior DM education. According to our study, engaging in appropriate self-care activities was substantially correlated with higher levels of diabetes knowledge and self-efficacy. This outcome is consistent with a prior Ethiopian research28. On the other hand, low levels of diabetes awareness were strongly linked to low levels of self-care in Ethiopia46 and Bangladesh47. This may be explained by the fact that patients with low knowledge about diabetes are less likely to take their medicine as prescribed and to take care of themselves, which leads to inadequate glycemic control43, 48.

To prevent complications in type 2 diabetes, self-efficacy is essential, and higher levels of self-efficacy correlate with higher levels of self-care activities49. Factors linked to effective diabetes treatment were knowledge of type 2 diabetes and patient self-efficacy for self-care in managing symptoms and psychosocial consequences49. Self-care practices have an impact on HbA1c. Therefore, it is more likely that variables influencing self-care habits would alter HbA1c, and these factors must be taken into account for this particular patient population.

Poor and poorest wealth index were significantly associated with poor self-care activities. This result agreed with a previous study in Nekemte, Ethiopia, which discovered a significant relationship between suitable self-care behaviors and a high monthly income50. It should be made clear that diabetes self-care activities, including monitoring blood glucose levels at home, getting regular laboratory blood tests, eating healthy meals, and taking prescription drugs on time, depend on financial means. Accordingly, people with T2DM engage in less self-care activities when their income and confidence in their ability to take care of themselves are low51.

Most of participants 87.9% (95% CI: 84.5, 91) reported poor compliance with diabetic self-care activities, with an average of less than four days per week. This was in line with findings from Northern Jordan14, Saudi Arabia52, and Libya39. Conversely, research employing the same evaluation method in Jordan found that good compliance with diabetic self-care behaviors was observed53. Some of the possible explanations include differences in sociodemographic and literacy levels, study subjects on follow-up, absence of structured diabetes education practices, media involvement in raising awareness, and other barriers that hinder participants from following recommended self-care practices in the study setting54.

The results of the multivariable linear regression analysis in our study showed a strong correlation between excellent glycemic control and good compliance with self-care practices, including diet, physical activity, foot care, and blood glucose testing. Blood glucose level was dramatically lowered by exercise, blood glucose monitoring, and foot care, according to a Chinese research12. According to a Jordanian research, blood glucose monitoring, diet, and physical activity were all strongly correlated with decreased HbA1c levels14. A Pakistani investigation discovered a substantial correlation between excellent glycemic control and physical activity, dietary control, and glucose management11. According to a Canadian study, self-monitoring blood glucose, medication usage, physical activity, and a balanced diet were all strongly linked to decreased HbA1c levels13. Our study revealed that blood glucose testing was the strongest predictor of good glycemic control in the domain of self-care activities. This result was consistent with research conducted in Pakistan11 and Saudi Arabia55. A longitudinal Canadian study reported self-monitoring of blood glucose was associated with a 12-month improvement in HbA1c13.

According to our research, patients with adequate glucose control had considerably higher physical activity ratings than patients with inadequate glucose control56. Physical activity can improve insulin sensitivity in T2DM patients and help lower elevated glucose levels back into the normal range57. For certain people with or at risk of diabetes, physical exercise may be a treatment approach that helps postpone the onset of complications from T2DM57, 58. Increased physical activity was shown to significantly lower HbA1c levels11, 14. Completely stopping exercise after being physically active at baseline was associated with an increase in HbA1c levels at 6-month follow-up13. This implies that exercise should be performed regularly.

Glycemic control was significantly predicted by a healthy diet. The results were consistent with some other investigations11, 13, 14. In order to regulate blood glucose levels and illness course, diet is crucial. Although the patients did not score high for a heathy diet for their diabetes, those who scored high had good glycemic control. Nutritional education programs are good for glycemic management, according to an interventional investigation59. High intake of sugar, carbohydrate, and salt and low polyunsaturated fatty acid intake are associated with poor glycemic control60. As no standard diet plan works for all patients, healthcare practitioners must provide patients with individualized diet plans that take into account their financial situation, as well as their cultural and personal preferences.

Higher foot care adherence was significantly associated with better glycemic control. This study was in agreement with a previous study conducted in Jordan14. In addition, participants’ adherence to foot care was satisfactory, which is likely to be frequently performed by Muslims as a result of religious practice14 whereas our study was unsatisfactory because more than half of our study participants (56.7%) was orthodox Christian.

When considering confounding variables and diabetes self-care practices at the same time, higher BMI demonstrated an independent predictive power for poor glycemic control. According to earlier research14, 61, 62, individuals with higher BMIs (obesity and overweight) were more likely to have poor glycemic control than those with normal BMIs. This finding may be explained by the development of insulin resistance caused by higher body mass index and visceral adiposity, which impair insulin sensitivity in individuals with higher BMIs. Hence, regular exercise and dietary changes may be recommended for individuals with diabetes to lower their BMI to the normal range. Sedentary lifestyle is associated with increased risk and complications of T2DM in Ethiopia. Therefore, patients with T2DM who have a higher BMI can engage in optimum daily physical activity, healthy diet, and healthy lifestyle to lower higher body mass index in order to prevent poor glycemic control.

Poor glycemic control was significantly associated with low medication adherence. Only 33.2% of patients exhibited high medication adherence, whereas 66.8% exhibited low medication adherence. Adherence to medication increases the benefits of pharmaceutical treatments. Poor glycemic control was significantly more common in patients with low medication adherence than in those with excellent medication adherence to prescription antidiabetic drugs. Non-adherence has been associated with higher HbA1c levels and unfavorable outcomes, such as frequent hospitalization and mortality; therefore, adherence to antidiabetic medication is essential for attaining metabolic control63.

Previous diabetes education was significantly associated with good self-care activities and good glycemic control. This outcome has also been observed in Ethiopia64, 65. This result was in agreement with a study conducted in Sudan66, Jordan14, and a multi-national survey67. One explanation might be that diabetes education provided by medical experts improves the knowledge and practices associated with self-care. In order to effectively manage diabetes, it is critical to educate patients about the disease’s symptoms and signs, associated complications, risk factors for complications, and significance of maintaining appropriate glycemic control, particularly in relation to the management and prevention of diabetic complications68. Nearly two- thirds of participants (64.9%) reported to have never attended a diabetes education session. Higher education levels and participation in diabetes education programs have been identified as important determinants of self-care activities. They have also been linked to increased self-efficacy and diabetes care abilities66, 67. One of the biggest obstacles to receiving diabetes care services, healthy diets, prescription drugs, and supplies is financial problems67, 69. Non-compliance with diabetes self-care activities may result in poor health outcomes, problems, mortality, and higher medical expenses. Effective diabetic self-management education programs should be designed and implemented by healthcare professionals to encourage patient adherence to healthy lifestyles and self-care activities. Enhancing diabetes self-care education is crucial for emerging nations like Ethiopia, where the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus is increasing rapidly.

Educational status was an independent predictor of self-care practice and glycemic control. Good glycemic control and higher self-care activitiesscore were significantly associated with secondary school and above education level. This result is in line with earlier research conducted in Ethiopia, Dire Dawa70. Similar patterns were noted in Pakistan, where a strong correlation was found between higher education levels and healthy self-care activities11. One explanation might be that individuals with higher educational attainment are more inclined to practice self-care because they better comprehend health information and medical expert advice. Their increasing access to healthcare and health information from various sources is another factor that likely affects their practice of self-care. On the other hand, a study conducted in Ethiopia revealed that respondents with lower educational levels exhibited poorer self-care activities71. According to our research, patients with low glycemic control were more likely to be illiterate. Comparable patterns were noted in Jordan, where improved glycemic control was linked to higher educational attainment14.

In general, this study focused on the degree of self-care activities and their correlation with glycemic control in patients with T2DM living in a country with limited resources. The results have consequences for managing type 2 diabetes. In developing nations, especially Ethiopia, the prevalence and burden of diabetes mellitus have increased rapidly. Most patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus were not well informed about their condition. Therefore, changes may necessitate quick action to meet glycemic objectives and achieve self-care adherence. To assist in making informed treatment and adjustment decisions, this study provides background information on patients and healthcare providers.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Social desirability bias is justified because the current study’s data collection approach relied on patient self-reported data. Furthermore, because of its cross-sectional form, this study failed to draw conclusions about cause and effect. Consequently, a long-term investigation is required to confirm and broaden our results. Despite these limitations, this study has the following strength. First, self-care activities were measured using a locally validated tool. Second, this was the first multicenter cross-sectional study to evaluate the association between self-care activities and glycemic control in Ethiopia. Third. the sources of error were minimized using a systematic random sampling technique.

Conclusion

The prevalence of poor glycemic control and lower level of self-care activities were high among patients with T2DM. Secondary school and above educational level, better diabetes self-efficacy, better diabetes knowledge, and previous diabetes education were significantly associated with better level self-care activities. Poor wealth index and poorest wealth index were significantly associated with lower level of self-care activities. Blood glucose testing was the most important self-care activity domain for predicting better glycemic control followed by diet, foot-care, and physical activity. Unable to read and write, overweight, obesity, and low level of medication adherence were significant predictors of poor glycemic control. On the other hand, previous diabetes education was significant predictor of good glycemic control. Good self-care activities were independent predictors of good glycemic control among patients with T2DM. Therefore, management interventions for patients with T2DM should focus on improving self-care activities and other predictor variables.
