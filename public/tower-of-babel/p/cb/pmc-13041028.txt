DNA topological regulation in RNA polymerase II transcription
Authors: Heeyoun Bunch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA polymerase II (Pol II) is the main enzyme that synthesizes protein-coding messenger RNA and a subset of nonprotein coding RNA molecules based on the DNA sequences harboring genetic information in eukaryotes. Pol II engagement with and dissociation from genes and its catalytic rate and polymerization processivity are modulated by diverse transcriptional elements. These factors control Pol II directly or modulate transcriptional microenvironments, chromatin, and nucleic acid structures. Classical and recent studies have reported multifaceted, important functions of DNA topology and structure and DNA topological regulators including DNA topoisomerases (TOPs), for controlled Pol II transcription. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated intriguing crosstalk among transcriptional factors, TOP2, and DNA damage response/repair factors in transcription, in particular, in the transcriptional initiation and elongation steps. This review updates and discusses these important findings regarding DNA topological modulations and the molecular mechanisms of TOP2 regulation in Pol II transcription.Keywords: DNA topology, Transcription, RNA polymerase II, Topoisomerase II, DNA repair factors, Pol II promoter-proximal pausing, Stress-inducible genes

Introduction

Transcription is the first cellular process involved in gene expression and comprises multiple regulatory steps. In the transcriptional initiation step, RNA polymerase II (Pol II) is engaged with the promoter, forms the open (DNA) complex, begins to polymerize the nascent RNA chain at the transcription start site (TSS), and escapes from the promoter, using the stress energy acquired through a round of abortive initiation [1, 2]. In the transcriptional elongation step, Pol II processively polymerizes the RNA chain to extend it to the gene end. In the transcriptional termination step, Pol II and the fully synthesized nascent RNA chain are released from the DNA template [3] (Fig. 1A). Throughout these three major steps, Pol II is constantly modulated by transcription factors and nucleosomal and nucleic acid architectural elements. These elements regulate the catalytic activities and kinetic dynamics of Pol II. For instance, Pol II pausing is an important precursory position in transcriptional regulation. Pol II pausing occurring in the early elongation step, called Pol II promoter-proximal pausing, enables Pol II either to be equipped for processive elongation or to be prematually dissociated from the DNA template, depending on transcriptional signals and their associated elements [4–8] (Fig. 1A). In addition, Pol II is paused or slowed down during the processive elongation and transcriptional termination steps; DNA sequences and nucleosomal and transcription factors control Pol II pausing and processivity [9–12].

Gene expression regulation is essential for organismal development, survival and adaptation, homeostasis, and proper response to internal and environmental signals. In contrast to house-keeping genes, stimulus- or stress-inducible genes are often tightly and systematically regulated for expression as their gene products, proteins, or mRNAs, have drastic, potent impacts on cellular and organismal physiology, growth, and survival/death [7, 13, 14]. Representative stimulus-inducible genes include sex hormone/hormonal receptor-inducible genes, activity-inducible genes, heat-shock genes, and hypoxia-inducible genes [15–21]. In stress-inducible genes, Pol II is tightly controlled by transcription factors, from binding to the promoter and polymerizing RNA molecules, to dissociating from genes. Notably, many stress-inducible genes harbor Pol II in the promoter-proximal site, whether the genes are active or resting [4] (Fig. 1A). Short RNA analysis showed that Pol II in the promoter-proximal site generates and is engaged with nascent, immature RNAs [5]. Pol II pausing in the promoter-proximal site has been demonstrated in different subsets of stress-inducible genes, including the ones listed above, before their activation, waiting for the reception of activating signals. More precisely, a paused Pol II is terminated without activation, and the Pol II turnover rate is reportedly approximately 2 min in humans [22, 23]. Pol II promoter-proximal pausing and pause release have been recognized as a notable characteristic and a major regulatory step of stress-inducible gene regulation.

Transcriptional regulatory elements can be categorized into three functional groups: 1. Factors controlling the mechanical and catalytic activities of Pol II, the main enzyme of transcription; 2. Factors regulating the accessibility and distribution of nucleosomes; 3. Factors modulating the structure and geometry of nucleic acids, RNA or DNA [7, 24–26] (Fig. 1B). Several protein-based transcriptional factors have been identified for their roles to control Pol II through protein–protein interactions and covalent posttranslational modifications. Chromatin structure/organization modulators and epigenetic regulators on the histone proteins in nucleosomes, so called nucleosome remodelers and modifiers, determine the accessibility of Pol II and transcription factors to the DNA strand and also condition the road for Pol II to slowdown, to pause, or to run. The DNA sequence has the intrinsic potential to dictate the stability of the RNA–DNA hybrid within the Pol II elongation complex, which is crucial for Pol II processivity [27, 28]. The spatial arrangement of DNA strands, called DNA topology, is essentially modified in DNA metabolisms including transcription. For Pol II pausing, pausing-inducing protein factors, obstructing nucleosomes, and accumulated DNA torsional stress are known to induce Pol II to stall; these must be reversed or alleviated for Pol II to resume transcription [8, 18, 26, 29]. In addition, DNA topology, such as DNA negative/positive supercoiling, replacement-loop (R-loop), and G-quadruplex (G4), influences Pol II kinetics throughout transcriptional initiation, elongation, and termination. In recent years, a few important studies including ours, have revealed the roles of DNA topological strain and regulation/resolution by topoisomerases as important transcriptional regulatory mechanisms [15–17, 19, 21, 30–36]. This review summarizes and discusses the DNA topological regulation in Pol II transcription, including recent breakthrough findings.

DNA topology as a regulatory element in transcription

A canonical B-form DNA structure comprises right-handed double helices. Owing to the nature of this helical structure, DNA metabolisms that are accompanied by denaturing or twisting DNA strands impose torque, introducing DNA supercoiling [37–39]. In vitro biophysical study has shown that bacterial RNA polymerase (RNAP) can sustain torque to certain levels; however, excess torsion, compensated by either negative or positive supercoiling, stalls it [29]. A recent study with ape genomes indicated that noncanonical DNA structures occupy approximately 9–38% of chromosomes, and G4s and left-handed, Z-form DNAs are abundant in promoters and enhancers [40]. Another study reported that transcriptional burst induces negative supercoiling and, if not relieved, it can inhibit transcription of a neighboring gene [41]. Negative supercoiling facilitates the binding of the general transcription factor (TF) IID and Pol II to the promoter, which is important for transcriptional initiation and activation [42, 43]. DNA underwinding promotes negative supercoiling, and the melting of the promoter DNA by general and gene specific transcription factors (e.g. TBP, TFIIH, and DNA topoisomerases) that are required for transcriptional initiation, and the assembly of transcription factors on the DNA presumably facilitate and fortify negative supercoiling formation in the TSS (Fig. 2). On the other hand, stable R-loop and G4 formation in the promoter appear to relieve DNA supercoiling to suppress transcription although G4 has also been reported to activate transcription [43–47].

DNA topology and transcription. Chromatin looping generated by protein and DNA elements including cohesin, insulator, and CTCF is important for the enhancer–promoter interaction and the separation of the transcriptionally active region (within the loop) from the transcriptionally inactive region (outer side of the loop). Both negative and positive supercoiling in excess may interfere with transcription, although negative supercoiling in the TSS favors active transcription. The beneficial and adverse effects of positive supercoiling on transcription have been reported. For instance, positive supercoiling ahead of Pol II hinders Pol II translocation, while it may destabilize and dissociate downstream nucleosomes. Overall, the R-loop is known to dampen transcription and is involved in DNA repair. G4 has multifaceted functions, both as an inhibitor and activator of transcription, depending on the context. Positive supercoiling of DNA, a topology prompted by DNA overwinding, is generated ahead of transcribing RNAP and replicating DNA polymerase and in nucleosome formation [3, 37, 39, 48]. Positive supercoiling is believed to hinder these polymerases from translocating and thus needs to be relaxed for the processive translocation of polymerizing enzymes. On the other hand, positive supercoiling modulates DNA compaction and looping, which are important for DNA–protein interaction. Some studies have reported that positive supercoiling enhances RNAP elongation and that the positive supercoiling ahead of elongating Pol II destabilizes the downstream nucleosomes in vitro [49, 50]. Furthermore, it is speculated that positive supercoiling may cause the DNA bases to protrude and be exposed for certain transcription factors to be engaged [51] (Fig. 2).

Beyond DNA supercoiling, three-dimensional (3D) genome structures have recently been of interest and intensely studied. Proteins, including cohesin, condensin, WAPL, and CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), regulate DNA looping, spatial arrangements, and functional contacts that play crucial roles in genomic folding and transcriptional regulation. For instance, cohesin, a protein complex that connects sister-chromatins during cell division, is reportedly important for the binding of transcription factors to the DNA [52]. Another study has consistently demonstrated that cohesin facilitates the formation of DNA negative supercoiling [53]. Conversely, condensin, which is responsible for chromosome compaction during cell division, stimulates the formation of DNA positive supercoiling [54]. A recent study also suggested that condensin drives negative supercoiling formation at physiological concentrations, which suggests that condensin may control DNA superhelicity depending on its local concentrations [55]. CTCF and cohesin generate DNA looping, and CTCF functions to modulate the loop extrusion and define the loop boundary (Fig. 2). Recent studies have indicated the functional and physical interaction between CTCF and type II topoisomerase (TOP2)[56, 57]. CTCF, cohesin, and TOP2 are thought to be the major protein factors that mediate the 3D genomic organization of topologically-associated domains (TADs). TADs play important roles in gene regulation; in particular, TADs determine the enhancer–target gene interactions, whose deregulation results in abnormal gene expression [58, 59].

DNA topoisomerases involved in transcriptional regulation

Type I, II, and III topoisomerases (TOP1, TOP2, and TOP3) have been reported and characterized in humans. TOP1 is a DNA-nicking enzyme that does not require ATP for catalysis and that mediates single strand DNA (ssDNA) break, relaxes both negative and positive supercoiling, and rejoins the broken sites [60, 61]. The function of TOP1 in replication, recombination, and transcription has been reported. Although knocking-out TOP1 is not lethal to in vitro cell lines or mice, it prepositions genomic instability and neurodegeneration [62]. TOP1 catalytic activity is reportedly required for enhancer RNA (eRNA) transcription and activation [30] and rRNA transcription [35]. TOP1 interacts with transcription factors, including BRD4, MYC, ARF, and p53, Pol II, and mRNAs. Studies have indicated that inhibiting the interaction of TOP1 with Pol II hinders Pol II elongation [34], and BRD4–Pol II–TOP1 dynamics regulate R-loop formation and R-loop-mediated genomic instability [63–65]. Furthermore, a recent study has shown that TOP1 is an RNA binding protein that particularly interacts with mRNAs, and RNAs regulate TOP1 activities [66].

In humans, TOP2 consists of two isoforms of TOP2A and TOP2B. Both TOP1 and TOP2 proteins utilize a tyrosine residue to mediate a nucleophilic catalysis of the phosphodiester bond of the DNA chain and form a temporary DNA–enzyme intermediate. TOP1 functions as a monomer and interacts with and nicks a single DNA strand to relieve DNA supercoiling. On the other hand, TOP2 forms a homodimer, which interacts with both DNA strands and mediates reversible double strand DNA break (DSB), resolves positive or negative supercoiling and catenation, relaxes the torsional stresses, and rejoins the broken DNA ends [67, 68]. TOP2 catalysis requires ATP consumption [69]. TOP2A and TOP2B are approximately 68% identical in the protein primary structure, with distinctive N- and C-terminal domains [70]. Multiple studies have shown that TOP2 plays an important role in DNA replication. For instance, inhibiting TOP2 by small chemical, catalytic inhibitors such as etoposide and ICRF193 or the ectopic expression of catalytically inactive TOP2B arrested cells in the G2–M stage in the cell cycle [71, 72]. TOP2A is crucial for cell division and proliferation as the depletion of functional TOP2A prevents the cells from completing chromosome segregation in mitosis, which cannot be compensated by TOP2B [73]. The expression patterns of TOP2A and TOP2B are also distinctive in that TOP2A is expressed in proliferating cells, while TOP2B is expressed in both proliferating and postmitotic cells [74, 75].

Studies have indicated the important role of TOP2B in the transcription of human estrogen- and androgen-receptor inducible genes and long genes in yeast and humans [16, 17, 76–78]. Our previous work unexpectedly identified DNA damage response (DDR) signaling during Pol II pause release at human heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) and immediate early genes (IEGs)[18, 79]. Consequently, TOP2B was speculated as a potential cause of the DDR, because of the TOP2B role in estrogen- and androgen-receptor inducible gene activation (Fig. 3). Inhibiting TOP2B using ICRF193, a catalytic inhibitor of TOP2, interfered with Pol II pause release and dampened DDR signaling [3, 19, 33]. Similar phenomena, such as TOP2B-mediated DSB and gene activation, were shown in neuronal activity-induced genes in neurons [15, 80]. The regulatory roles of TOP2B have also been reported in retinoic acid-inducible genes [81], hypoxia-inducible genes [21], and key genes involved in fatty acid biosynthesis, such as FASN and ACACA in humans [82, 83].

The roles of TOP2A in transcription have recently emerged as important. A previous study reported that TOP2A is responsible for DNA break at G4 structures in transcription [84]. The repressive function of TOP2A in IEGs has also been reported, whereas another study suggested that the TOP2A–MYC–TOP1 complex, called topoisome, stimulates transcription in proliferating cells [36, 85]. In the case of human hypoxia-inducible genes in SH-SY5Y cells, TOP2A and TOP2B appear to function similarly, primarily suppressing their expression under normoxic conditions [21]. Under hypoxic stresses, TOP2A and TOP2B are released from representative hypoxia-inducible genes, including p21, ALDOC, GLUTs, and IL1β [21] Fig. 3. Some challenges in understanding the mutual and distinctive roles of TOP2A and TOP2B are their significant sequential and structural homologies and the lack of catalytic chemical inhibitors to selectively inhibit one over the other. The commonly used inhibitors, such as etoposide, merbarone, and ICRFs, are rarely reliable in discriminating them. However, a chemical inhibitor selectively targeting a novel druggable site of TOP2B has been developed recently [86]. While both TOP2A and TOP2B can cause DNA break and genomic instability in DNA metabolic processes such as replication and transcription [15, 16, 19, 87–89], their mutual and distinctive roles, perhaps depending on their general and gene-specific binding partners, and the underlying mechanisms need to be further understood.

TOP1 and TOP3 mediate transient ssDNA breaks and rotate and rejoin the broken DNA ends to relieve DNA torsional stress. TOP1 reportedly plays important roles in active Pol II transcription. TOP1 is required for effective transcription in diverse subsets of genes, including eRNAs, IEGs, and pathogen-inducible, inflammatory genes [30, 90, 91]. The affinity of TOP1 with RNAs has been reported, in which RNAs suppress the catalytic activity of TOP1 [66]. Interestingly, TOP1 also functions as a kinase that phosphorylates metazoan splicing factors. In this study, camptothecin, a catalytic inhibitor of TOP1, was shown to inhibit the kinase activity of TOP1 [92]. In addition to its function in resolving DNA positive or negative supercoiling, the binding affinity of TOP1 to Pol II, RNAs, and its functional interaction with splicing factors indicate that TOP1 is an essential transcriptional and posttranscriptional regulator [66, 93–95]. Similar to TOP1, TOP3 catalysis does not require ATP consumption and utilizes an reactive tyrosine residue to nick and rejoin a single DNA strand. TOP3 has two isoforms, TOP3A and TOP3B, and is implicated in relieving transcription–replication conflict and chromosome segregation [96, 97]. TOP3A functions in DNA replication and recombination as a decatenase to resolve Holliday junctions and homologous recombination intermediates, suppressing genomic crossovers [98, 99]. TOP3B relaxes both DNA and RNA, known as the only RNA topoisomerase to date, and resolves the R-loop [100]. Recently, the structure of TOP3B has been determined, elucidating the catalytic steps and the mechanical differences between TOP3A and TOP3B for their substrate specificities [101]. In addition, TOP3 interacts with DNA repair factors, such as p53 and BRCA1, and is involved in DNA repair and the maintenance of genomic stability [102].

DNA topoisomerase II in transcription-coupled DNA break and repair

It has been debatable whether TOP2 catalysis, which relieves DNA torsional stress in Pol II transcription, is programed and/or fortuitous, although several studies support that TOP2-induced DNA break increases genomic instability [16, 57, 67, 103, 104]. The dependence of active transcription on the catalytic activity of TOP2, more precisely TOP2B, was first reported in estrogen- and androgen-receptor target genes [16, 17]. In the study with estrogen-receptor target genes, DNA break in the promoter of a representative gene was visualized and mapped with a nucleotide-precision [16]. TOP2B and DNA repair factors’ involvement in these gene activations were also reported: the gene activation of these sex-hormonal receptor genes along with other subsets of genes listed below provokes DDR signaling, suggesting that DNA break by TOP2B might be rather persistent than transient [105]. In addition, although DDR signaling was not validated, TOP2B and DNA-PK were shown to be recruited to the FASN gene in transcriptional activation [82, 83].

TOP2B engagement and DDR signaling in the transcriptional activation of IEGs have been reported in human kidney and neuronal cell lines [15, 19]. Initially, our group identified that DDR signaling and DNA repair effort might be coupled with Pol II pause release in human HSP70 and IEGs in HEK293 cells [3, 7, 18, 19, 31, 79] (Fig. 4). In 2014, we first found and reported tripartite motif-containing 28 (TRIM28) binding specifically to the promoter-proximal Pol II pausing site of the human HSP70 gene [18]. Consecutively, it was verified that promoter-proximally bound TRIM28 stabilized Pol II pausing, whereas its phosphorylation at S824 by DNA-PK and ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) was also required for effective Pol II pause release and active transcription at human HSP70 and IEGs [19, 31]. Strikingly, the chemical inhibition of DNA-PK kinase activity potently interfered with transcriptional activation to a degree comparable with that of P-TEFb inhibition, suggesting that DDR signaling is crucial for productive Pol II transcription in these stress-inducible genes [3, 7, 19].

While phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-like kinases, ATM, and DNA-PK activation and phospho-TRIM28 and γH2AX accumulation indicated DSB occurrence, the mechanisms behind the phenomenon, and in particular, its causes, were unclear. We investigated the involvement of TOP2B for the lessons previously learned from estrogen- and androgen-receptor gene activation and found that the catalytic function of TOP2B is responsible for the DDR signaling and required for efficient Pol II pause release [19, 33] (Fig. 4). Another study reported similar phenomena: gene activation-induced DDR signaling and the role of TOP2B-mediated DSB and DDR in the activity-induced transcription of the early response genes in neurons [106–108].

Transcription activation contributes to DNA mutations and genomic instability accumulation. Classically, the R-loop and transcription–replication collision were considered the major causes of transcription-mediated genomic instability or chromosome fragility [109, 110]. In fact, the transcription–replication collision may be incidental and limited to the S phase. Conversely, the R-loop is frequently accompanied by transcriptional activation, typically occurring in the TSS and transcription termination site (TTS)[64, 111, 112]. The R-loop is resolved by TOP1, TOP2B, and TOP3B [100, 112, 113]. Interestingly, the R-loop and G4 are notably colocalized in the human genome, and their formation is cooperative [43, 111, 114]. The R-loop and G4 are involved in DNA repair, and these noncanonical nucleic acid structures coincide with mutational hot spots [115–117]. Transcription factor binding and DNA unwinding drive negative supercoiling in the TSS during transcriptional activation. In turn, the negative supercoiling may be compensated by the formation of R-loop and/or G4 [43, 118, 119], in which TOP2 (also TOP1 and TOP3B) resolves these structures to maintain active transcription (Fig. 4). TOP1 and TOP2B prevent/remove R-loop/G4 by relaxing negative supercoiling of DNA in transcription [64, 120]. TOP3B, along with a helicase DDX5, binds to existing R-loop and relaxes it through its decatenase activity [100]. Conversely, it is speculated that the catalysis of TOP2 to remove negative supercoiling can be required to suppress gene expression if certain genomic architectures are prone to unwind DNA even before gene activation. In this case, the suppressive role of TOP2 must be reversed for active transcription. To understand the function of TOP2 in gene-specific contexts, in vivo genome topologies and 3D structures of genes need to be monitored.

Whether the DSB caused by TOP2 is an accidental byproduct of transcriptional activation or a deliberate requisite, built-in gadget is debatable. It should be noted that etoposide-induced TOP2 poisoning, allowing the DSB formation without rejoining the broken ends, upregulates IEG transcription, whereas ICRF193 downregulates it [19, 21, 33]. In addition, a DNA break artificially caused by a restriction enzyme at a targeted DNA site recruits Pol II and activates transcription [121–124]. Although some studies have reported that DSB represses transcription [125, 126], others have suggested that DSB stimulates transcription and that several DNA repair factors function as transcription factors, transcriptional repressors or activators [17–20, 30, 31, 79, 127, 128]. TOP2 recognizes the DNA lesion and binds to it, and TOP2–DNA cleavage complex (TOP2cc) is not mutually bound by the DNA-PK–Ku70–Ku80 complex. In our recent study, TOP2 was shown to relax negative supercoiling in the TSS of human hypoxia-inducible genes and suppress gene activation under normoxic conditions [21]. TOP2 release or depletion increased DNA-PK and HIF1α occupancies and activation in these genes. Previous studies from our group and others did not observe γH2AX accumulation under hypoxic stresses [20, 21], in which TOP2 dissociates from hypoxia-inducible genes. In contrast, TOP2B recruitment and activation upon transcriptional activation coincide with γH2AX accumulation in other stress-inducible genes, discussed above. These observations reinforce the coupling of DSB formation with TOP2 activity in transcriptional regulation. It is also possible that the DNA repair factors that are accumulated during transcriptional activation, in the case of HSP70, IEGs, and eRNA and neurotransmitter-, sex hormonal receptor-, and hypoxia-inducible genes, may exercise uncharacterized, noncanonical functions, rather than DNA repair. The interplay and function of TOP2B, TOP2B-mediated DNA break, and DNA repair factors in transcriptional activation have not been completely understood and await to be elucidated in the future.

RNAs play regulatory roles in Pol II transcription and DNA break and repair. The important roles of both small and long RNAs and endoribonucleases, such as RNase H, DICER, and DROSHA, have been reported in effective DNA repair [121, 122, 124, 129, 130]. For instance, small RNAs are processed from short TSS RNAs by DICER at Pol II pausing sites, which is similar to DNA damage-induced RNAs [121, 129]. These observations raise the interesting question of whether a DNA single or double strand break may be present in Pol II pausing rather than being generated upon transcriptional activation (Fig. 4). In fact, TOP2s and DNA repair factors, including TRIM28, DNA-PK, and BRCA1, are abundantly occupied in Pol II-paused, resting HSP70 and IEGs [7, 18, 19, 31, 32, 79]. Therefore, it seems plausible that the DNA broken ends are concealed in the Pol II pausing state and that transcriptional activation recruits and mobilizes TOP2 to expose them to activate/phosphorylate DNA repair factors and provoke DDR signaling. Although these hypotheses have not been tested or proven by experimental evidence, small RNA accumulation and DNA break evidenced in the promoter and TSS of uninduced HSP70 gene before transcriptional activation suggest these possibilities [5, 18, 19, 32], which needs to be evaluated in future studies.

Gene-specific DNA topological regulation in transcription

Genomic landscape and topology determine DNA accessibility and the contact between the gene regulatory regions and promoters. TADs have been studied in different cell lines and organisms, and have indicated the chromosome folding enabling the tissue-specific interactions between enhancers and their target gene promoters [131, 132]. In addition to the spatial organization and 3D genome configuration in the nucleus, DNA superhelicity and sequence determine DNA accessibility and reactivity with other molecules. For instance, negatively supercoiled DNA drives R-loop formation in G-C skewed regions such as the TSS [133]. In addition, the genomic structure of a gene is likely to be affected by the activities on the gene and on its neighboring genes [41, 134–136]. A large number of genes are located divergently from other genes and/or have other genes embedded or overlapped within them. The catalytic and interactive activities on a gene can cause overwinding or underwinding of the DNA in the neighboring genes [41, 137]. Both the activity and topology of the genes in spatial proximity are closely related and must be coordinated for adequate gene expression. The mechanisms regulating gene activity and DNA topology in compact gene loci await future investigations.

Interestingly, in the case of TOP2B, some genes require its catalytic activity for productive transcription, whereas other genes require it to suppress transcription. The former has been known for HSP70, FASN, estrogen- and androgen-receptor genes, neurotransmitter-inducible genes, autism-related genes, long genes, and IEGs [15–17, 19, 31, 33, 76, 78, 82, 83, 138]. The latter has been recently shown in hypoxia-inducible genes [21]. The DNA of the hypoxia-inducible genes becomes more underwound and accessible in the TOP2B KO neuroblastoma cells than in the WT cells, which is consistent with more transcriptional activities at these genes [21]. This finding suggests that the major function of TOP2B for hypoxia-inducible genes is to maintain the transcriptional resting state (Fig. 5). Without TOP2B, Pol II machinery is permitted for leaky transcription of hypoxia-inducible genes under normoxia, which could have devastating effects on the cells. In contrast, the catalytic activity of TOP2B is required for the activation of other gene subsets listed above. It is important to address whether these two types of genes have distinctive topological architectures and whether the differences intrinsically wire the genes with distinctive topological requirements for gene activation. For example, resolving positive supercoiling or R-loop, which requires TOP2B catalysis, may be crucial for gene activation in HSP70, FASN, estrogen- and androgen-receptor genes, neurotransmitter-inducible genes, autism-related genes, and IEGs. Conversely, the topological predisposition to unwind the promoter in hypoxia-inducible genes requires TOP2B catalysis to relax the DNA under normoxia, as a critical mechanism to maintain inactive transcription. Understanding the topological nature and mechanism underlying these gene-specific requirements awaits future research.

A mechanism that enables the distinctive functions of TOP2 on the basis of the gene-specific topological context is through TOP2 posttranslational modifications by its regulators. Gene-specific, DNA-binding transcriptional activators interact with the Mediator complex and recruit histone/nucleosome modifiers and major transcriptional activators such as P-TEFb, provoking drastic changes in the molecular interactomes and gene architectures [139–141]. This indicates that the interaction between TOP2 and transcriptional activators/repressors may hold the key to understanding how TOP2 is regulated in gene activation/repression. For instance, TOP2B is extensively modified at numerous residues through phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, sumoylation, and methylation (https://www.phosphosite.org/proteinAction.action?id=5866&showAllSites=true). However, the functions of these modifications have not been studied until recently and are poorly understood.

In IEG transcription, ERK1 and ERK2 interact and phosphorylate the C-terminal domain of TOP2B (Fig. 4). Biochemical analyses showed that ERK1 and ERK2 phosphorylate the mutual and distinctive residues of TOP2 and exert differential effects on TOP2B catalysis [33]. The in vitro immobilized template assay and EMSA indicated the association of TOP2B with dsDNA, not ssDNA, corresponding to the TSS sequences of EGR1, a representative IEG [31–33]. It is noted that TOP2B binding to the dsDNA appears rarely sequence-specific, and TOP2B, dependent on the energy from ATP hydrolysis, instantaneously resolves DNA supercoiling without accessory factors [31, 33]. Interestingly, while both ERK1 and ERK2 enhance TOP2 catalysis of positive DNA supercoiling, ERK2 delays TOP2B catalysis of negative DNA supercoiling [33]. Presumably, the negative supercoiling in the TSS facilitates transcription that ERK2 retards TOP2B to relax it to assist transcriptional burst. Conversely, ERK2 enhances the relaxation of positive supercoiling ahead of paused Pol II to increase the processivity and enzymatic rate of Pol II (Fig. 4).

We also reported the interaction between TOP2B and the BRCA1–BARD1 complex in IEGs. The BRCA1–BARD1 complex, as an E3 ligase, ubiquitinates TOP2B, and in the in vitro biochemical analyses, the ubiquitination was shown to be long- or short-chained, such as mono-, di-, and tri-ubiquitination, depending on the E2 enzyme partners [31]. Importantly, TOP2B ubiquitination controls TOP2B–DNA binding: ubiquitinated TOP2B has a much stronger affinity to the EGR1 TSS DNA segments than ubiquitin-stripped TOP2B [31](Fig. 4). In this study, we found that BRCA1 is phosphorylated at S1524 by ATM/ATR upon transcriptional activation. Interestingly, the phospho-null mutant, S1524A BRCA1 ubiquitinates TOP2B more effectively than the WT, and the phosphomimetic mutant S1524D BRCA1 is incapable of ubiquitinating TOP2B [31]. It is speculated that TOP2B binding to the EGR1 TSS is ensured by the interaction with the BRCA1–BARD1 complex and ubiquitination in the resting state of transcription, whereas the TOP2B mobility and catalytic boundary become flexible and increased by the absence of BRCA1–BARD1-mediated ubiquitination upon transcriptional activation, in which BRCA1 is phosphorylated at S1524 [31]. These results, along with the ERK data described above, provide important evidence that the substrate binding and catalysis of TOP2B are regulated by its transcription factors and posttranslational modifications by the factors.

We speculate that various transcription factors modulate TOP2 and that context-dependent topological regulation by TOP2 is possible through gene-specific transcription factors. Recently, it was observed that HIF1α and DNA-PK are mutually exclusive to interact with TOP2B [21]. TOP2B interacts with and is phosphorylated at the C-terminal domain including T1403 by DNA-PK, an event important for TOP2B to relax the DNA negative supercoiling/underwinding in the TSS of hypoxia-inducible genes and suppress its building-up in the inactive state of transcription. In addition, it is noted that DNA-PK KO increases mRNA production in a majority of hypoxia-inducible genes in HCT116 cells. Interestingly, the absence of DNA-PK apparently reverses the function of TOP2B from a suppressor to an activator of hypoxia-inducible genes [21]. In WT cells, hypoxic stresses release TOP2B from the hypoxia-inducible genes, resulting in DNA underwinding in the TSS and gene body, provoking a transcriptional burst (Fig. 5). Consistently, depleting TOP2B or catalytic inhibition of TOP2B induces increased mRNA production in these genes [21]. Together, protein–protein interactions involving posttranslational modifications of TOP2B by gene specific transcription factors (e.g. HIF1α-DNA-PK, ERK2, and the BRCA1–BARD1 complex) determine the DNA topological changes to turn on or off and tune the transcription of stress-inducible genes.

Perspectives

Past and present studies have shown that genome topology and topological regulation are important elements in gene expression regulation. Topological functions and mechanisms are likely gene-specific and coupled to temporal molecular arrangements for the DNA region to be more permissive and flexible or restricted and rigid (Fig. 5). For instance, DNA topology can directly influence the DNA-protein/DNA-nucleic acid interaction and DNA melting and looping, thereby affecting a variety of molecular events in transcription, including Pol II processivity and the enhancer–promoter interaction. Thus, the functions of topoisomerases are essential for DNA metabolisms owing to the nature of the DNA structure, which is inclined to form supercoils, when torsional stresses occur. In humans, TOP1, TOP2A/2B, and TOP3 have mutual and distinctive functions to one another. TOP1 and TOP2 play important roles in the transcriptional regulation of numerous genes. DSB caused by TOP2B in the resolution of negative or positive supercoiling is implicated in gene activation and genomic instability [105, 142]. Furthermore, overlapping and distinctive functions of TOP2A and TOP2B in transcription have been reported and await further understanding.

In addition, the precise roles and regulatory mechanisms of TOP2B-mediated DSB in transcription need to be understood in the future. The functions of DNA repair enzymes, such as DNA-PK, TRIM28, and the BRCA1–BARD1 complex, in the transcriptional activation of stress-inducible genes are also incompletely understood. It seems reasonable to conjecture that these factors and events are not only important for gene regulation but are also closely related to the preservation of genomic integrity, which is prone to be compromised by transcription. The functional interaction and regulatory mechanisms between transcription factors/activators and DNA topological and repair factors should be further understood at the molecular level (Fig. 6). Transcribing regions of the genome reportedly accumulate more mutations than nontranscribing regions, and certain mutations in DNA repair factors are implicated in tumorigenesis and aging-related diseases [143–147]. The dynamics among three DNA metabolic processes, transcription, DNA topological regulation, and DNA repair regulate gene expression and genomic stability (Fig. 6). It should be noted that this interdisciplinary research topic has become more intensively studied and recognized in recent years. Therefore, this review might have omitted some new crucial findings and concepts, and certain current understandings discussed here could be subject to change or modification along with future findings. In spite of these limitations, it is anticipated that further revelation of the relations, interactions, and molecular mechanisms among transcription, DNA topological regulation, and DNA repair will be important to obtain a complete picture of stress-inducible gene regulation and genome biology in the future.

Relationship of transcription, DNA repair, and DNA topological regulations. Gene activation triggers the actions of transcriptional activators. They control Pol II activities, nucleosome remodelers, topoisomerases and nucleic acid modifiers, and DNA repair factors. Pol II transcription is the first step in gene expression and the major source of genomic instability. The close functional relationship between genomic topological regulation and DNA repair has emerged as an important key to maintaining genomic stability in stress-inducible Pol II transcription.
