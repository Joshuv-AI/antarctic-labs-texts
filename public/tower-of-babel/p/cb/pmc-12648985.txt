Interpretable machine learning of clinical MALDI-TOF spectra discriminates carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae while revealing phylogenetic heterogeneity that limits model generalizability
Authors: Chuangye Cai, Mengxue Zou, Mingxiao Chen, Peibo Yuan, Zhencheng Fang, Lanlan Zhong, Dingqiang Chen, Hongwei Zhou, Nianyi Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ML classifiers using MALDI-TOF MS data perfectly discriminate KPC from non-KPC.•NDM and CSKP show minimal spectral divergence due to close phylogenetic relatedness.•SHAP analysis identifies the 4521.91 m/z peak as a KPC-specific biomarker.•NDM and CSKP intermix in evolutionary clusters, limiting MALDI-based differentiation.•Locally informed model training recommended.Keywords: MALDI-TOF MS, CRKP, Interpretable machine learning, SHAP value, Phylogenetic analysis

Introduction

The rise of antibiotic-resistant organisms represents a growing and formidable challenge to modern healthcare systems [[1], [2], [3]]. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) have been classified as critical priority pathogens by the World Health Organization (WHO), with carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) ranking foremost [4]. Given the substantial clinical and economic impacts of antimicrobial resistance, including elevated morbidity and mortality, timely initiation of appropriate antimicrobial treatment is paramount [5,6]. In China, approximately 90 % of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) isolates carry carbapenemase genes, predominantly KPC (88.4 %) and NDM (9.2 %) [7]. KPC enzymes (class A serine β-lactamases) generally remain susceptible to ceftazidime-avibactam (CAZ-AVI), whereas NDM metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs) confer resistance to this combination [[8], [9], [10]]. Given this resistance profile, aztreonam plus CAZ-AVI has been proposed as a preferred regimen for NDM-producing CRKP [11,12]. Therefore, rapid detection of CRE infections, including precise resistance mechanism identification, is critical for optimizing antimicrobial therapy. Concurrently, immediate reporting to infection control teams is vital to limit transmission of resistant strains. The optimal selection of antibiotics is a cornerstone of antimicrobial stewardship, particularly critical in the era of globally spreading resistant pathogens. Currently, the CLSI-recommended CRE typing method requires an extended 18–24-hour culture step [13], significantly delaying targeted therapy. Rapid resistance profiling would not only improve patient outcomes but also advance broader stewardship goals. Thus, accelerating the diagnosis of CRKP and its resistance mechanisms remains an urgent unmet need in clinical microbiology.

Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) enables acquisition of bacterial protein fingerprints within minutes, offering significant advantages in speed, throughput, and cost-effectiveness for clinical microbial identification [14]. Beyond species identification, this technology has demonstrated potential for antimicrobial resistance detection, virulence assessment, and strain typing across diverse microbiological applications [[15], [16], [17]]. Notably, Weis et al. pioneered the integration of machine learning with MALDI-TOF spectral data to predict antimicrobial resistance patterns, showcasing the platform's capability to expedite resistance profiling [18]. Unlike PCR-based, phenotypic, or immunochromatographic methods, this approach provides concurrent microbial identification and resistance data without requiring additional instrumentation, reagents, or specialized labor [[19], [20], [21], [22]].

While several studies have successfully employed MALDI-TOF MS for CRKP detection [[23], [24], [25], [26]], several challenges impede clinical implementation. Key limitations include inter-platform reproducibility issues, insufficient external validation cohorts, and geographical restrictions in strain applicability [[27], [28], [29]].

To address these challenges, we collected CRKP strains from multiple institutes across China and developed a classification model based on clinical mass spectrometry data. The model's robustness was rigorously validated using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometers from different manufacturers and an independent strain set. Furthermore, we employed Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values [30] and bacterial genomic evolution analysis to interpret the model's classification performance and uncover biologically meaningful features.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

This study analyzed 1,050 clinical Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates collected from Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University between January 2018 and July 2024, which were preserved in 20 % glycerol broth at −80 °C. For external validation, we included 482 strains from three other institutions in China: 389 Klebsiella pneumoniae from Guangzhou Medical University (2023–2024, Guangzhou), 70 isolates from Sun Yat-sen University (2023–2024, Guangzhou) with confirmed sequence type (ST) and K locus (KL) genotyping data, and 23 strains from Zhejiang University (2023, Hangzhou). All strains underwent resuscitation on 5 % sheep blood-enriched Columbia agar (35 ± 2 °C) with 24 h subculturing prior to testing.

All clinical bacterial strains used in this study were isolated and purified from completely anonymous patient samples that were not obtained specifically for use in this study, therefore neither informed consent nor IRB review were required.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were determined using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France) with GN335 cards according to manufacturer protocols. Bacterial suspensions were standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity following CLSI M100-ED33 guidelines. For quality control, all isolates were concurrently evaluated using the disk diffusion assay on Mueller–Hinton agar plates (Guangzhou Dijing Microbial Technology Co., Ltd., China) with 0.5 McFarland standardized inocula. Carbapenem disks (imipenem/ertapenem/meropenem; 10 μg, Oxoid, UK) were applied, and plates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 24 h. Zone diameters were measured using calibrated digital calipers (Mitutoyo, Japan) and interpreted according to CLSI breakpoints [13]. Discordant results between automated and manual methods were further tested with broth microdilution inhibition testing to confirm the results.

Phenotypic carbapenemase detection

Carbapenemase production was assessed using a modified inhibitor-enhanced disk diffusion assay. Four Imipenem (10 μg) disks (Oxoid, UK) were tested concurrently: (Ⅰ) imipenem alone; (Ⅱ) imipenem plus 300 μg aminophenylboronic acid (APB; Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemases [KPC] inhibitor); (Ⅲ) imipenem with 292 μg EDTA (metallo-β-lactamases [MBLs] inhibitor); and (Ⅳ) imipenem with both APB and EDTA. Mueller-Hinton agar plates were inoculated with 0.5 McFarland standard suspensions and incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 24 h. A ≥ 5 mm increase in zone diameter around inhibitor-containing disks compared to imipenem alone, measured with digital calipers, confirmed carbapenemase activitiy according to established interpretive criteria in the referenced methodology [31,32].

qPCR detection of carbapenemase genes

Primers targeting blaKPC, blaNDM, blaIMP, blaVIM, and blaOXA-48 (See Supplementary Table 1) were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST and synthesized by AcroImmune BioTech. Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Primer specificity was validated through melt curve analysis (95 °C to 60 °C gradient) with positive and negative control strains prior to experimental use.

Genomic DNA was extracted using thermal lysis (100 °C, 10 min) and quantified spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop One, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with purity criteria (OD260/280 ≥ 1.8). DNA aliquots were stored at −20 °C until use.

MALDI-TOF MS identification

Microbial identification was performed using three MALDI-TOF MS platforms:

(Ⅰ) QuanTOF System (QuanID, version 1.0.59375.5983; Intelligene Biosystems Co., Ltd, Qingdao, China): Fresh colonies were smeared on the target plate with 2 μl 70 % formic acid and overlaid with 1.5 μl α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA; 10 mg/mL). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was applied for daily calibration (mass targets: 3,128 Da, 4,365.4 Da, 5,096.8 Da, 5,381.4 Da, 6,241.4 Da, 6,255.4 Da, 6,316.2 Da, 6,411.2 Da, 7,158.8 Da, 7,274.5 Da, 7,872.1 Da, 9,536 Da and 10,300 Da), with species identification requiring spectrum scores of 800–2000.

(Ⅱ) Autof ms1000 (Autof Acquirer, version 2.0.157; Autobio, Zhengzhou, China): Direct smear preparation with 1 μl CHCA matrix (10 mg/mL) using the manufacturer's Bacterial Test Standard (BTS; calibration mass targets: 3,637.772 Da, 4,365.343 Da, 5,096.776 Da, 5,381.446 Da, 6,255.444 Da, 7,274.467 Da, 10,300.032 Da, 13,683.173 Da and 16,952.332 Da). Identification confidence thresholds were set at 9.0–10.0 (arbitrary units per manufacturer specifications).

(Ⅲ) Bruker Biotyper (MBT Compass, version 4.1; Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Germany): IVD-mode analysis with MBT Subtyping Module enabled for KPC-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae prediction. Using the manufacturer's BTS standard for calibration, direct smear preparation with 1 μl of CHCA matrix (10 mg/mL) covered a mass range of 3637.8–16952.3 Da (including specific masses at 3,637.8 Da, 5,096.8 Da, 5,381.4 Da, 6,255.4 Da, 7,274.5 Da, 10,300.1 Da, 13,683.2 Da and 16,952.3 Da), with high-confidence identification defined as scores 2.00–3.00.

All systems utilized manufacturer-specified protocols for colony processing, matrix application, and air-drying (ambient temperature). Spectral acquisition parameters followed instrument-specific recommendations with daily mass axis calibration verification.

Results

Workflow

As shown in Fig. 1, the present study developed and validated a robust machine learning model for classifying CRKP strains using MALDI-TOF MS clinical microbial identification data. We collected 1,532 clinical isolates (Table 1) from four institutes in China, implementing rigorous exclusion criteria to ensure data quality (Fig. 1A). Following the exclusion of strains producing IMP/VIM/OXA-48-like enzymes, isolates with discordant phenotypic-genotypic correlations, and those demonstrating unreliable MALDI-TOF MS identification, the final curated dataset comprised three distinct strain categories: KPC-type carbapenemase producer, NDM-type metallo-β-lactamase producer, and CSKP. We conducted comprehensive molecular profiling of selected strains, encompassing multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) for sequence types (STs) and K locus type (KL) analysis, with detailed results provided in Tables S2 and S3. We employed a balanced training set of QuanTOF platform (30 isolates per category) to avoid bias in feature selection and model building. At the same time, we tested three different strategies to determine the optimal classification process (Fig. 1B). The random forest (RF) algorithm was identified as the optimal classifier. In contrast, the evaluation used imbalanced data sets mirroring real-world epidemiology to ensure clinically relevant assessment. External validation performed across three distinct MALDI-TOF MS platforms (QuanTOF, Autof MS1000, and Bruker Biotyper) and geographically diverse clinical isolates on a single system (n = 439) confirmed the model's robust generalizability (Fig. 1C). By integrating SHAP interpretability analysis with maximum likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction, we systematically elucidated the feature contribution profiles underlying the model's decision-making mechanism and the genetic determinants driving divergent classification performance across distinct strain subtypes (Fig. 1D-E). To enhance the model's discriminatory capacity between NDM producers and CSKP (Fig. 1F), we assessed the impact of advanced feature selection strategies and extensive training dataset expansion. The training dataset was approximately tripled in size, and three distinct feature selection methods (Boruta, LASSO, and RFECV) were rigorously evaluated. None of these interventions, however, yielded a significant improvement in model efficacy. This result suggests that the original model had likely reached a performance ceiling with the available feature architecture, indicating that the primary constraint lies in the explanatory power of the features themselves rather than the model's capacity or feature selection strategy.

Differential MALDI-TOF MS profiles across Klebsiella phenotypes of drug resistance enable RF-based discrimination

We leveraged raw MALDI-TOF MS spectral data (m/z 2,000–20,000 range) from clinical isolates, utilizing full-spectrum feature extraction for comparative evaluation of five supervised learning algorithms: decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), naïve Bayes (NB), and logistic regression (LR). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices and PERMANOVA testing demonstrated significant intergroup spectral divergence (P = 0.001, R2 = 0.07; Supplementary Fig. 1A), particularly in KPC vs NDM (P = 0.001, R2 = 0.08; Supplementary Fig. 1B), KPC vs CSKP (P = 0.002, R2 = 0.06; Supplementary Fig. 1C), and KPC vs non-KPC comparisons (P = 0.001, R2 = 0.06; Supplementary Fig. 1D), revealed significant differentiation among strains stratified by their carbapenem resistance phenotypes. Notably, tree-based algorithms achieved perfect discriminative capacity for KPC and NDM differentiation (AUC = 1.00, 95 % CI:1.00–1.00, 5-fold cross-validation; Supplementary Fig. 2A), whereas PCoA showed non-significant spectral difference in NDM and CSKP (P = 0.143, R2 = 0.03; Supplementary Fig. 1E) with correspondingly poor classifier performance (NB: AUC = 0.687, 95 % CI:0.478–0.895; RF: AUC = 0.673, 95 % CI:0.547–0.799; Supplementary Fig. 2B). Among the five evaluated machine learning algorithms, the RF classifier exhibited superior discriminatory capacity across phenotypic classification tasks. RF achieved perfect discriminative performance in distinguishing KPC-producing from non-KPC strains (AUC = 1.000, 95 % CI:0.987–1.000; Supplementary Fig. 2C) and demonstrated robust classification efficacy between CRKP and CSKP isolates (AUC = 0.809, 95 % CI: 0.659–0.961; Supplementary Fig. 2D).

Robust KPC strain discrimination across analytical pipelines contrasts with NDM-CSKP classification discordance

Building upon these findings, we systematically developed three classification frameworks to optimize the multiclass differentiation of KPC, NDM and CSKP strains: (Ⅰ) Model A employed direct three-class classification using all spectral features; (Ⅱ) Model B adopted two-tiered modeling with initial CRKP and CSKP stratification followed by CRKP subtyping (KPC vs NDM); and (Ⅲ) Model C utilized sequential cascade classification implementing KPC and non-KPC discrimination followed by NDM identification within the non-KPC subgroup. These architectures were specifically designed to improve diagnostic precision while maintaining clinical interpretability by mirroring the hierarchical reasoning process of clinical clinicians: first determining resistance presence, then identifying the specific resistance mechanism.

The diagnostic performance of the classification models was evaluated using the area under the ROC curve and confusion matrix analysis. In the three-class paradigm (Model A), KPC producers demonstrated exceptional discriminative capacity with AUC values spanning 0.89–1.00 across development datasets, maintaining > 0.80 accuracy in external validation. Conversely, NDM classification exhibited moderate diagnostic reliability (AUC = 0.67–0.87, ΔAUC = 0.20 between platforms) with confusion matrices revealing stark contrasts: KPC prediction accuracy reached 82.9 % (95 % CI:81.0–84.7 %) versus 38.5 % (95 % CI:32.4–45.0 %) for NDM isolates (Fig. 2A-F, Supplementary Fig. 3 A-F, Supplementary Table 2). Binary classifier analysis further underscored the divergence in model reliability. Model B's phased approach created diagnostic asymmetry. CRKP vs CSKP differentiation achieved moderate performance (AUC = 0.64–0.88), while KPC vs NDM discrimination maintained strong instrument-agnostic accuracy (AUC > 0.95 across three different platforms, 0.85 in multicenter validation; Fig. 2G-L). Notably, Model C's cascade architecture demonstrated distinct performance stratification. The KPC vs non-KPC model exhibited near-perfect classification (AUC ≥ 0.94), contrasting sharply with inconsistent NDM and CSKP differentiation (AUC = 0.50–0.86; Fig. 2M-R). Stratified performance metrics revealed sustained KPC prediction fidelity (KPC vs NDM: Recall = 0.739–1.0, KPC vs Non-KPC: Recall = 0.83–1.0) and variable NDM accuracy improvements (56.5–96.7 %) accompanied by elevated CSKP misclassification rates (Supplementary Fig. 3 G-R, Supplementary Table 2). These findings establish that KPC-specific modeling achieves diagnostic stability, whereas composite CRKP classification is fundamentally constrained by phylogenetic confounding between NDM producers and CSKP lineages.

Persistent NDM-CSKP classification challenges reveal fundamental limits of MALDI-TOF profiling for metallo-β-lactamase DISCRIMINATION

To address the suboptimal classification performance of NDM across different models and workflows, we explored multiple optimization strategies. Three distinct feature selection methodologies were evaluated: (1) the Boruta algorithm, which identified three significant variables, achieving a training set AUC of 0.78, but a testing set AUC of only 0.63; (2) LASSO regression, which selected 16 features with a training set AUC of 0.77 and a testing set AUC of 0.69; and (3) SVM-based recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV), which selected seven variables, yielding a training set AUC of 0.71 and a testing set AUC of 0.74. Additionally, combining features from all three methods (20 variables in total) improved the training set AUC to 0.8, but the testing set AUC dropped to 0.61, suggesting potential overfitting or data distribution inconsistencies (Supplementary Fig. 4). Next, we increased the training sample size to 93 NDM and 94 CSKP strains, ensuring data were collected within the same day to minimize instrument variability. While the training set AUC improved to 0.86 (Supplementary Fig. 5A), performance on the internal testing set (11 NDM and 17 CSKP strains) and external validation set (48 NDM and 35 CSKP strains) remained poor, with AUC values of 0.45 and 0.60, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5B-C). Furthermore, even after harmonizing peak extraction methods between training and testing sets, the model achieved an AUC of only 0.55 on the testing set (Supplementary Fig. 5 D-F). These results indicate that the intrinsic spectral differences between NDM and CSKP strains are minimal, and despite employing diverse feature selection techniques and expanding the training dataset, significant improvements in classification performance remain elusive.

Explainable machine learning unveils CRKP typing disparities: Coexistence of KPC-specific biomarkers and NDM classification bottlenecks

To elucidate the decision-making process of the classification models, we employed SHAP values, a game theory-based approach, to quantify the contribution of spectral features to model predictions. The superior performance of the model in classifying KPC strains was primarily attributed to the prominent role of the 4521.91 m/z peak. In both three-class and binary classification models, the 4521.91 m/z peak was identified as the most significant feature, with its mean SHAP value substantially higher than any other features. Bee swarm plots further confirmed that a higher intensity of this peak correlated with a greater probability of KPC prediction, underscoring its critical role in KPC classification (Fig. 3A, C-D). However, when KPC and NDM were modeled collectively as CRKP, the contribution of the 4521.91 peak was moderate (Fig. 3B), as it was predominantly expressed in local KPC strains, but rarely in other types, reducing its specificity. Notably, the previously reported 11109 Da P019 protein, a known KPC biomarker, was absent in local KPC strains due to the lack of the p019 gene, resulting in the failure of Bruker’s KPC module to predict any local KPC strains (Supplementary Fig. 6 A). Additionally, the unique 4521.91 peak in the local (Guangdong region) KPC strains showed no absolute association with ST11 or KL64 serotypes, but was exclusively linked to KPC strains (Supplementary Fig. 6B-D). However, the presence of the 4521.91 peak in some NDM strains from the Zhejiang region led to misclassification (Supplementary Fig. 6 E). The suboptimal classification of NDM strains stemmed from the absence of highly contributory spectral features. In the three-class model, the 4521.91 peak remained the most important feature for predicting NDM or CSKP, but its role was limited to excluding KPC predictions, as NDM and CSKP strains either lacked or exhibited low expression of this peak. The remaining features contributed minimally to NDM classification, with low mean SHAP values (Fig. 4A-B). Similarly, the NDM and CSKP binary classification model lacked dominant features, with all features showing comparable and low contributions (Fig. 4C). This indicated that, unlike KPC strains, NDM and CSKP strains shared highly similar spectral profiles without distinct, universal biomarkers for classification. Phylogenetic analysis in Fig. 4D provides a genetic context for the model’s performance. Local KPC strains formed a distinct clade, while NDM and CSKP strains were intermixed. Furthermore, analysis of the feature intensity heatmaps revealed a strong, conserved spectral peak at 4521.91 Da within the KPC clade, which was conspicuously absent in most non-KPC strains. Conversely, features associated with NDM exhibited no consistent phylogenetic pattern, highlighting the lack of universal biomarkers for NDM classification (evident in the outer heatmap). The genetic similarity and evolutionary complexity between NDM and CSKP strains likely explain the challenges in identifying effective spectral features for their differentiation.

Discussion

In this study, we established random forest models to classify KPC-type CRKP, NDM-type CRKP, and CSKP strains using MALDI-TOF spectral profiles. The classification of KPC strains demonstrated strong performance across different analytical workflows, instruments, and strain sources. However, NDM and CSKP strains were more easily confused, with significant variations in model performance across different datasets. SHAP values were used to interpret the differences in model performance. The KPC model incorporated a characteristic peak at 4521.91 Da, which is commonly observed in KPC strains and significantly contributes to the prediction. In contrast, the NDM model exhibited minimal contributions from individual peaks, making it difficult to identify distinguishing features. From an evolutionary perspective, KPC strains tend to cluster separately earlier, suggesting unique evolutionary characteristics. In contrast, NDM and CSKP strains have complex and diverse genetic backgrounds, with overlapping evolutionary branches, leading us to speculate that the potential differences in MALDI-TOF profiles may be associated with evolutionary divergence among strains.

In this study, we tested different classification workflows and validated the models on various datasets. The classification of KPC strains was stable and effective across different instruments and sources of strains, consistent with previous studies demonstrating the potential of MALDI-TOF profiles to accelerate antimicrobial resistance detection, such as in MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) [33] and CRKP studies [34]. Similar studies have also reported a characteristic peak close to 4521.91 Da in KPC strain profiles [23,24,35]. However, these studies had limited sample sizes. Since ST11 is the predominant KPC type in China, this peak is easily associated with MLST. In our study, we explored the correlation between ST typing and KL typing using local strains and found that the peak at 4521.91 Da was only present in KPC strains, regardless of the ST or KL type.

When NDM and KPC strains were modeled collectively as CRKP, the classification of KPC was adversely affected by the high misclassification rate between NDM and CSKP. In contrast, modeling KPC as a distinct group resulted in consistently high and stable classification performance across different instruments and strain sources. These findings highlight the importance of tailored modeling strategies to optimize the classification of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae strains in clinical settings.

The primary clinical value of our classifier lies in its potential for rapid screening, enabling faster initiation of targeted therapy and infection control measures for CRKP, long before phenotype or genotype results are available. A positive prediction would signal the urgent need for intervention, not the cessation of further testing. However, this approach also has certain limitations. The fundamental challenge in constructing a robust classification model lies in the objective differences among strains that can be detected. First, differences among resistant strains of various genetic subtypes may affect the selection of characteristic peaks. A Korean team used MALDI-TOF to detect carbapenemases in the 12,000–32,000 Da range for CRE classification with high accuracy [36]. Their study set a larger error range for NDM enzyme detection compared to other carbapenemases, at 26,743.53 ± 33. The 10 K. pneumoniae strains included in their study were all NDM-1, with detection values ranging from 26,742.1 to 26,751.7 Da, showing minimal variation. In contrast, three subtypes (NDM-1, NDM-5, and NDM-9) included in the detection of Escherichia coli had a broader range of 26,724.1 to 26,771.1 Da, indicating inherent differences among strains of different genetic subtypes. Second, strains from different regions have distinct epidemiological characteristics, limiting the cross-regional application of models. Although our KPC model performed well across datasets, its predominance also underscores potential limitations: the risk of misclassification based on a single, highly weighted feature and geographic discrepancy. The misclassification of NDM strains from Zhejiang Province as KPC-positive, which exhibited peaks at the 4521.91 Da region, unlike strains from Guangdong Province, exemplifies a geographic spectral discrepancy critical for clinical application. A study suggested that this peak might be associated with the genetic environment of IS26-ISKpn27-bla KPC-2 −ΔISKpn6-Tn1721, where 4521.91 Da could be related to the presence of KPC resistance genes [23]. Like previous reports of the 11,109 Da peak as a characteristic of KPC strains, the association between this protein-coding gene and KPC is not absolute. The 11,109 Da peak is linked to the p019 protein, whose encoded gene is frequently co-located with blaKPC on specific plasmids that are common in other regions, but are rarely found in China [37]. This fundamental epidemiological difference renders Bruker’s module less effective for our local isolates. This highlights that linking characteristic peaks from MALDI-TOF profiles directly to antimicrobial resistance requires caution and consideration of regional epidemiological differences. In our NDM model, the lack of representative differences between NDM and CSKP profiles led to poor model performance. From an evolutionary standpoint, the genetic similarity between NDM and CSKP strains is higher than that with KPC strains. Thus, our findings strongly support the development of localized, region-specific models and the implementation of dynamic, continuous-learning frameworks to ensure the robustness and accuracy of MALDI-TOF-based predictions across geographically diverse and temporally evolving bacterial strains.

Conclusion

In summary, this study established models for classifying KPC, NDM, and CSKP strains and analyzed the differences in model performance. Our results demonstrate that machine learning combined with MALDI-TOF profiles can effectively classify KPC strains, validated across different instruments and external datasets, highlighting the potential for accelerating bacterial resistance detection. Compared with KPC strains, the close evolutionary relationship between NDM and CSKP strains may result in minimal differences in their MALDI-TOF profiles, leading to misclassification. Effective classification requires the objective presence of detectable and analyzable differences among strains. Therefore, when developing models, it is essential to account for regional variations in strain characteristics and to construct local resistance classification models based on regional epidemiology, with regular updates informed by current epidemiological data.
