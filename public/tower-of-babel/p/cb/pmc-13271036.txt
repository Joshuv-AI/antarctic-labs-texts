Transformation of ALK-translocated lung adenocarcinoma to LCNEC under alectinib treatment – a case report and mini-review
Authors: Josef Kim, Lars Uwe Stephan, Konrad Steinestel, Yasir Arafat Tamal, Annette Arndt, Thomas Barck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe histologic transformation of non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) alterations remains a rare phenomenon and a challenge for treatment with ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI). We present a seldom-reported case of transformation of lung adenocarcinoma into large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) during treatment with alectinib, a second-generation ALK inhibitor, aiming to evaluate diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. The underlying mechanisms driving this transformation—likely due to a secondary resistance mechanism acquired during treatment—remain poorly understood but may involve genetic alterations, changes in the tumor microenvironment, and the emergence of additional oncogenic mutations. This case adds to the limited body of evidence and underscores the importance of recognizing neuroendocrine differentiation in ALK-positive lung cancer cases that show clinical progression. Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular drivers of this transformation and to optimize treatment strategies for affected patients.

Introduction

After the identification of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene rearrangements in 2007 — most commonly the echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like 4 (EML4)-ALK fusion gene–as oncogenic drivers in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), targeted therapy with ALK inhibitors has become a cornerstone of treatment, significantly improving both progression-free survival and overall survival.1,2 Alectinib, a second-generation ALK inhibitor, has demonstrated clinical efficacy and is widely used as a first-line treatment in this patient population. However, despite initial therapeutic success, most patients eventually experience disease progression due to acquired resistance mechanisms. While secondary ALK mutations and activation of bypass signaling pathways are well-established resistance mechanisms, histologic transformation has emerged as a less common but increasingly recognized phenomenon.3 Histologic transformation has been identified as a relatively rare resistance mechanism to ALK-TKI, occurring in fewer than 5% of cases. Evidence from various NSCLC molecular subtypes indicates that this transformation is more likely driven by transcriptional reprogramming rather than by newly acquired genetic mutations while retaining the original ALK fusion.4,5 We report a case of histologic transformation from ALK-rearranged lung adenocarcinoma to LCNEC under first-line treatment with alectinib.

Patient story

In April 2021, a 44-year-old former light smoker (4 pack years) male patient presented with a persistent dry cough and left-sided pleural effusion evident on chest X-ray. The computed tomography (CT) demonstrated mediastinal and retrocrural lymphadenopathy without a discernible primary lung lesion (cTx cN2 cM1a, stage IVa according to AJCC 8th edition). Histologic confirmation was obtained from a mediastinal lymph node and showed a glandular neoplasm without microscopic evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation. Immunohistochemistry was positive for thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1), consistent with pulmonary adenocarcinoma. CK7 staining was not performed, as its diagnostic utility is limited in this setting due to frequent expression in both adenocarcinoma and LCNEC. Molecular profiling, including next-generation sequencing (Oncomine Focus panel, Thermo Fisher, USA), identified an echinoderm microtubule–associated protein-like 4 (EML4)-ALK fusion. No additional oncogenic driver mutations or metastases were detected. Subsequently, targeted therapy with alectinib was initiated, followed by a response to treatment.

In January 2025, the patient’s restaging revealed new suspicious masses in the dorsolateral pleura, while mediastinal lymphadenopathy remained regressive. A subsequent PET-CT scan confirmed hypermetabolic activity and a solid lesion in the left upper lobe (Figure 1).

Molecular tumor board

Histologic examination of the pleural mass revealed infiltrates of a poorly differentiated large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), immunohistochemically positive for TTF-1 and synaptophysin (Figure 2). Although the confirmatory ALK-FISH turned out borderline negative (14%), the comprehensive genetic profiling (Illumina TSO500 Panel, Illumina, USA) data suggested persistence of the translocation. An oncogenic sequence variant in TP53 (p.Tyr220Cys) (AF 90.85%), along with likely oncogenic mutations in CREBBP (AF 17.35%) and SPEN (AF 18.18%), was identified, accompanied by a low tumor mutational burden (TMB) of 4.0 mutations per megabase. No further therapeutically relevant mutations or resistance mutations were detected. However, a genome-wide analysis regarding copy number alterations suggested a biallelic loss of RB1. Additionally, liquid biopsy analysis confirmed the inactivating TP53 mutation and identified an additional alteration in the ATM gene. Due to the limited amount of circulating tumor DNA, the presence of additional mutations could not be ruled out.

In the absence of detectable ALK resistance mutations or off-target mechanisms, neuroendocrine transdifferentiation is considered the most likely resistance mechanism.6 Immunohistochemically, synaptophysin expression was absent at initial analysis, whereas ALK expression persisted in both specimens (Figure 2). Although the findings support histologic transformation, coexistence of a neuroendocrine component at initial diagnosis cannot be fully excluded given the limited mediastinal biopsy sampling and potential intratumoral heterogeneity. Additional Napsin A staining could not be performed due to insufficient remaining tissue. Ki-67 indices were not available for both specimens and may be of limited interpretability given the broad overlap reported between lung adenocarcinoma and LCNEC. Placing this case into context, Table 1 summarizes all published cases of transformation to LCNEC reported by Fares et al. (2020) and Ambrosini et al. (2025). Across these reports, patients (4 women, 1 man; age range 43-74 years) were predominantly never-smokers and presented with stage IV disease and heterogeneous metastatic patterns. All patients received ALK inhibitors (eg, alectinib, crizotinib, and ceritinib), with progression-free survival ranging from 12 to 45 months. LCNEC transformation occurred 12 to 38 months after initial diagnosis. Frequently observed resistance alterations included ALK mutations such as G1202R and L1196M as well as TP53 mutations. Subsequent treatment strategies comprised next-generation ALK inhibitors (eg, lorlatinib or brigatinib) and platinum-based chemotherapy. Reported overall survival ranged from 34 to 81 months, with post-transformation survival of 7 to 24 months. Collectively, these data underscore LCNEC transformation as a clinically relevant resistance mechanism in ALK-positive NSCLC.5,7

Therapeutic management of LCNEC remains challenging, largely due to the absence of standardized treatment algorithms and the extrapolation of evidence from SCLC and NSCLC. In advanced disease, platinum-based doublet chemotherapy, often combined with PD-L1 inhibitors, is commonly used, although the role of immunotherapy remains uncertain given the exclusion of patients with LCNEC from key trials. First-line strategies are typically guided by SCLC-like versus NSCLC-like approaches, whereas second-line options such as fluoropyrimidines combined with temozolomide or dual immunotherapy (nivolumab plus ipilimumab) are considered, albeit with limited evidence.8

From a molecular perspective, LCNEC can be subdivided into three entities. The SCLC-like subtype is characterized by inactivating alterations in RB1 and TP53, frequently accompanied by changes in CREBBP, EP300, and MLL. The NSCLC-like subtype shows mutations in the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway and may harbor targetable alterations such as EGFR, ERBB2, or FGFR1. In contrast, the carcinoid-like subtype is associated with MEN1 mutations.8 In the present case, the tumor was classified as SCLC-like according to this framework; however, it demonstrated a better response to an NSCLC-based treatment regimen. No clear association between molecular subtype and therapeutic response was observed, underscoring the difficulty of treatment selection in LCNEC. The low tumor mutational burden (TMB) observed in this case is consistent with previous findings in NSCLC with oncogenic driver alterations such as ALK rearrangements. ALK rearrangements have also been associated with increased PD-L1 expression on tumor cells. Nevertheless, responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors are often limited, possibly because high PD-L1 expression and strong immune cell infiltration rarely co-occur in NSCLC with EGFR or ALK alterations.9–11 Additionally, Fares et al. described two cases of ALK-rearranged NSCLC with LCNEC transformation, differing in RB1 status and outcome, suggesting a possible link between RB1 wild-type status and a less aggressive phenotype despite ALK-resistance.5 By contrast, TP53 mutations in ALK-positive NSCLC are consistently associated with poorer overall survival and shorter duration of response to targeted therapy, regardless of whether they are present at baseline or acquired during disease progression.12 Finally, in metastatic NSCLC with TKI resistance, re-biopsy is recommended to identify resistance mechanisms.13 However, a cohort study of 148 patients with EGFR or ALK/ROS1 alterations showed that only half underwent re-biopsy, resistance mechanisms were identified in only 20% of cases with ALK rearrangements and overall survival was not improved. These findings suggest a limited impact of routine re-biopsy on survival, although it remains valuable for guiding individualized treatment strategies, particularly when combined with next-generation sequencing approaches.14

Patient update

In summary, we report the case of a male patient with ALK-rearranged NSCLC who initially responded well to alectinib but developed histologic transformation to LCNEC after nearly four years. Molecular analyses at the time of transformation confirmed persistence of the ALK fusion and revealed additional oncogenic alterations, including an inactivating TP53 mutation, likely pathogenic variants in CREBBP and SPEN, RB1 loss with along a low tumor mutational burden. Taken together, these co-occurring genetic alterations may have contributed to histologic transformation and therapeutic resistance despite persistent ALK rearrangement.

Treatment was switched to lorlatinib during repeat histopathological evaluation, with an initial pleural response observed on sonography. After 5 months, disease progression occurred, with worsening dyspnea due to a large left-sided pleural effusion. Following histological transformation, systemic therapy with carboplatin (AUC 4), etoposide, and durvalumab was initiated. Despite therapy, clinical progression was observed, consistent with re-staging findings and rising neuron specific enolase (NSE) levels. Treatment was subsequently changed to carboplatin (AUC 4), pemetrexed, and pembrolizumab, resulting in clinical improvement, reduced liver metastases and pleural carcinosis, resolution of pleural effusion, and normalization of NSE levels. A partial response was maintained for 5 months. After further progression, second-line therapy with docetaxel and ramucirumab was initiated, followed by transition to best supportive care in February 2026 after subsequent progression. The patient died in April 2026.
