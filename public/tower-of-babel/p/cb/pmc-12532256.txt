Humidification in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit: What We Know, What We Need
Authors: Mounika Reddy
Published: 2025

Invasive mechanical ventilation is one of the most powerful yet potentially harmful interventions in critical care. It bypasses natural airway defense mechanisms, depriving the distal airways of warmth and humidity that are normally supplied by the upper respiratory tract. Inadequate humidification of inspired gases can impair mucociliary clearance, cause epithelial damage, and increase the risk of secretion plugging and atelectasis – events that may contribute to ventilator-related adverse events (VAEs), including ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and artificial airway occlusion (AAO).1–3

Humidification is an essential component of safe and effective mechanical ventilation. Two broad approaches exist: Active humidification using heated humidifiers (HHs) and passive humidification using heat and moisture exchangers (HMEs). While HHs offer precise control over temperature and humidity, they are resource-intensive and costly, require close monitoring, and increase the risk of condensate formation. HMEs, conversely, are simpler, disposable, portable, and cost-effective but add dead space and resistance to the circuit.2 Balancing these trade-offs is particularly challenging in children, whose smaller airways and variable physiology heighten the risks associated with both under- and over-humidification. According to the American Association for Respiratory Care guidelines, active humidification should deliver 33–44 mg/L of water vapor at a gas temperature of 34–41°C with 100% relative humidity at the circuit Y-piece, whereas passive devices should provide at least 30 mg/L.3 Achieving these targets is technically feasible with both methods, but whether this translates into meaningful differences in patient outcomes, especially in children, has remained unclear.

Against this backdrop, the study by Sivamurukan et al.,4 published in this issue of the Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine, makes an important contribution. This pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT), conducted in a tertiary care pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) in South India, compared VAEs between children ventilated with HMEs and those with HHs. Sixty children aged 2–15 years, expected to require mechanical ventilation for more than 48 hours, were randomized, stratified by age, and followed for 7 ventilator days. The incidence of VAEs was measured as a composite of VAP (CDC-defined and physician-diagnosed) and AAO. The investigators found no statistically significant difference in VAEs between the two groups, though the numerical rate was higher in the HME arm (28.36 vs 12.98 per 1,000 ventilator days). Six VAEs occurred – two in the HH group and four in the HME group. None were airway occlusions; all were VAP. Secondary outcomes, including suction frequency, peak inspiratory pressures, ventilator days, and mortality, were also comparable between groups. Importantly, no patient in the HME group required “rescue” to HH due to ventilatory difficulties. Taken together, these findings suggest that HMEs may be a feasible alternative to HH in children older than two years, at least for the duration studied.

Several features underscore the significance of this trial. It is the first pediatric RCT to directly compare humidification strategies in an ICU setting, in critically ill children requiring prolonged ventilation, a population notably absent from previous research. Previous evidence has largely been derived from adult studies and extrapolated to children, an approach fraught with limitations given pediatric differences in airway anatomy, physiology, and disease patterns. Second, the investigators employed a clinically relevant composite outcome that captured both VAP and airway occlusion, acknowledging that these are the real-world complications that matter most to clinicians and families. Finally, the study was conducted in a resource-limited setting, where cost and ease of use are particularly salient considerations.

The findings align with prior systematic reviews in adults, which have generally shown no clear superiority of either method. A 2017 Cochrane review, including 34 trials and nearly 3,000 patients (only three studies with data for infants and children), reported no significant difference in VAP, tube occlusion, or mortality between HMEs and HHs; however, the overall quality of evidence was low.5 Similarly, Vargas et al.6 found no difference in AAO, pneumonia, or mortality. A trend favoring HMEs was found with studies including a high percentage of patients with a pneumonia diagnosis at admission and those with prolonged mechanical ventilation. Pediatric data, however, are sparse and outdated, largely limited to neonates or perioperative populations.7 Infant crossover studies and bench evaluations demonstrate that HMEs can meet humidity targets in ideal conditions, but performance may degrade with leakage – a concern particularly in pediatric ventilation; long-term performance is not known.8,9 The present study extends this knowledge to older children requiring prolonged ventilation and demonstrates that HMEs can be used without obvious increases in adverse outcomes, though caution remains warranted.

The trial represents an important step in addressing a long-standing evidence gap in pediatric critical care. Like any pilot RCT, the study's strengths lie in feasibility and hypothesis generation rather than definitive answers. However, several limitations temper interpretation. With only 60 children, the study was underpowered to detect modest but clinically important differences. Exclusion of children with airway pathology and severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) limits applicability to higher-risk groups who are more vulnerable to inadequate humidification. A single HME type was used across ages 2–15 years, potentially mismatching device dead space with child size. Delivered humidity levels at the Y-piece were not measured; the study inferred adequacy of humidification from device specifications, and outcomes such as work of breathing or leak fraction were not evaluated. Outcome assessment for VAP was not blinded, raising potential observer bias. Outcomes were assessed up to seven ventilator days, but many children in the PICU may remain ventilated longer, during which risks of infection and tube occlusion may evolve. These limitations do not detract from the study's contribution but highlight the need for cautious interpretation. Nevertheless, the study offers a valuable proof of concept and highlights key methodological challenges for future trials.

Should clinicians change their practice based on these findings? Probably not yet – but the study provides reassurance. In the absence of clear evidence of harm or benefit, the choice between HME and HH should be guided by context. In resource-limited settings, where HH systems may be unavailable or unaffordable, the absence of major safety signals with HMEs is encouraging. In this trial, HMEs were well tolerated, required no rescue interventions, and did not increase suctioning needs. However, the numerical trend toward higher VAEs with HME warrants caution. Until more definitive evidence emerges, clinicians should individualize humidification strategies, balancing device availability, patient characteristics, and local expertise. Heat and moisture exchangers may be considered a reasonable alternative in selected children. Extra caution is needed in younger children, patients with high ventilatory demands, prolonged ventilation, airway pathology, or copious secretions. Importantly, VAP prevention bundles, oral care, head elevation, subglottic suctioning, and judicious antibiotic use likely impact on outcomes than humidification modality alone. Humidification method is but one piece of the complex puzzle of safe mechanical ventilation in children.

This pilot study lays the groundwork for larger, multicenter RCTs. Future studies should aim to increase sample size to detect differences in clinically meaningful outcomes, use appropriately sized HMEs to minimize dead space effects, measure physiologic parameters such as delivered humidity at the Y-piece, work of breathing, dead space ventilation, and gas flows to link device performance with clinical outcomes, and include higher-risk populations, such as children with ARDS, airway pathology, or prolonged ventilation. Assessing cost-effectiveness, balancing device costs against infection-related expenditures and staff workload, and exploring long-term outcomes, including duration of ventilation, PICU stay, and neurodevelopmental sequelae, would add value. Research is also needed to compare different types of HME and HH.10 Collaborative, multicenter pediatric critical care research networks in India and globally are ideally positioned to take up this agenda.
