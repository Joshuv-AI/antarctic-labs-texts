Influence of carbon ionization increment by adding ne on the bonding, electrical, and tribological properties of carbon thin films deposited by HiPIMS
Authors: César D Rivera Tello, F J Flores-Ruiz, J Pérez Alvarez, J A Guerrero de León, I Farías, L Flores-Cova, M Flores
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we use mass quadrupole spectroscopy to analyze the ion energy distribution function for C+ ions from different gas composition discharges (20, 40, 60, 80, and 90% Ne) + Ar in a plasma sputtering process. Carbon films were obtained for each gas composition discharge. The carbon bonding structure of films was analyzed by Raman spectroscopy using deconvolution fitting of the G and D Raman peaks. The C-sp3 content was correlated with the electrical and tribological properties of the carbon films. Our results further corroborate the enhancement of carbon ionization in HiPIMS processes by adding neon in conventional argon gas during the deposition process. Furthermore, we found that excessive levels of carbon ionization were detrimental in the formation of C-sp3 decreasing the resistivity, and indicating the decrement of the elastic modulus of the samples. In addition, the use of neon in the gas working mixture increased the deposition rate significantly compared to argon-only processes from 1.7 to 3.22 nm/min for the highest deposition rate cases. Tribology showed that an intermediate C-sp3 content in the carbon films developed desirable tribological behaviors with lower friction coefficients and wear rates, revealing that higher values of C-sp3 content are not necessarily for robust solid lubricious and wear resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73446-0.Keywords: Ion-energy-distribution-function, Plasma-sputtering, Carbon-film, HiPIMS, C-sp3Subject terms: Materials science, Condensed-matter physics, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

During the last 70 years of the development of carbon films with high sp3 bonding content, better known as diamond-like carbon (DLC) coatings, extraordinary properties such as high hardness, low friction coefficient, low wear rate, low coefficient of thermal expansion, and semiconductor behavior have been reported1–5. Consequently, DLC coatings have attracted attention in several industrial applications, such as protective coatings and micromechanical, optical, and semiconductor devices6–9. These outstanding properties are related to the carbon structure. DLC coatings are a type of amorphous carbon film, in which hybrid sp3 and sp2 orbitals constituting diamond and graphite, respectively, are always present in the carbon bonding. The sp3 sites are interlinked by strong covalent bonds without any weak chain elements, which explains the hardness of diamond. In graphite, the carbon atoms reside in sp2 sites and are interlinked by a stronger bond within the dense plane. The fourth electron of the outer carbon atom’s electron orbital contributes to a weak binding energy between planes, explaining why it is much softer and somewhat brittle10. Therefore, the mechanical properties of DLC are widely associated with diamond sp3 (κ bonds) and the friction coefficient tends to be related to sp2 hybridization (ι bonds).

Moreover, the deposition technique plays an essential role in sp3 and sp2 bonding11,12. The model proposed by Lifshitz et al.13 supports the ion trajectory applied on carbon ions with sufficient energy to be accumulated by surface implantation with a thermal spike for the growth of diamond-like carbon films. In addition, Robertson14 considered an underlying mechanism in which implantation promoted atomic hybridization to adjust the local density, favoring sp2 formation in low-density regions and sp3 binding formation in high-density regions. Furthermore, he reported a ternary phase diagram15, suggesting that ions of higher energies would not promote sp3 hybridization because excessive local heating allows active relaxation in excess density. He also suggested the presence of an optimal ion energy for a maximum sp3 content of around 100 eV.

One of the first systems proposed for the fabrication of DLC coatings by carbon ion beam was reported by Aisenberg et al.16, where Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) and Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD)17 techniques have attracted attention as promising methodologies for growing DLC coatings with control of the carbon-bonding structure using the implantation mechanism. Variations in DLC growth routes include plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD)18 and Direct Current Magnetron Sputtering (DCMS), which are commonly used to obtain DLC coatings in a wide range of applications. However, the low degree of ionization of the DCMS process leads to a low sp3 hybridization fraction as a result of the neutral species generated by sputtering, achieving DLC coatings with low hardness and low density compared to other synthesis routes, such as pulsed laser deposition19 and cathodic arc evaporation20. Hence, several avenues and development strategies have been proposed to promote electron impact ionization in magnetron sputtering discharges with carbon ions. Gas Injection Magnetron Sputtering (GIMS) is one approach, where the gas injection frequency is reduced, and gases with ionization energy higher than that of argon, or a mixture of these gases, are used. In the GIMS process, argon-helium, neon-helium, and neon-argon have been reported to exhibit a higher sp3/sp2 ratio21–23. Another promising alternative to increase carbon ionization is the High-power Impulse Magnetron Sputtering (HiPIMS) technique, which generates discharges with relatively high plasma densities and exhibits high ionization energies in metal targets. For HiPIMS plasma produced in pure argon, the ionization fraction in nonmetals such as carbon is meager24. Materials and methods

Carbon films with five different neon contents (% Ne) in the working gas Ar + Ne mixture of the PVD-HiPIMS plasma deposition process were deposited on steel 316 L biomedical alloy and silicon (001) wafers. The Ne content of the Ar + Ne mixture was controlled and varied using an MKS Mass flow controller (247 C) as follows: 20, 40, 60, 80, and 90% Ne. Table 1 shows its labeling and the main parameters for each carbon film. A carbon target of 50.8 mm (two inches) was used, and the power to the cathode was applied in the form of unipolar pulses with a source and pulser IONAUTICS-Hipster. The frequency of the pulses was 300 Hz with a width of 30 µs for all the coatings. The setup of the deposition process was schematically illustrated in our previous work29. The Ne and Ar gas atoms were introduced in the same input; and the applied peak discharge current density (Jpk) remained constant (5.67 ± 0.096 A/cm2).

The ion energy distribution function (IEDF) curves of C+, C2+, Ar+, Ar2+, Ne+, and Ne2+ in the discharge plasma were obtained by mimicking tests of the deposition process (MTDP) after having finished the carbon films, as described in our previous work29, where the Jpk and voltage curve values were very similar to those used in the deposition processes. The morphology and thickness of the carbon thin films were analyzed from cross-sectional images using a field-emission scattering electronic microscope (FESEM) Tescan MIRA 3 LMU system. The bonding microstructure of the pristine surfaces of each sample and of the wear track obtained after the tribology tests were analyzed by Raman spectroscopy with a Thermo Scientific DXR confocal Raman microscope using a wavelength of 532 nm. Additionally, the spectral analysis of the Raman data was obtained by Lorentz and Breit-Wigner-Fano (BWF) deconvolution into the D and G bands, respectively30. The electrical resistivity of the five carbon films deposited on silicon was obtained from electrical measurements on the surface films by using the Four-Point probe technique (Keithley, 2450), for details of this method see in ref31. The tribological behavior of the carbon ultra-thin films was obtained by wear tests with a reciprocating ball-on-plate configuration, using a 10 mm alumina ball as counterpart using a tribometer CETR-UMT2 and applying a load of 2 N to the reciprocal-sliding movement of 10 mm stroke length at a frequency of 1 Hz for 1800 s. Three tests were performed for each carbon thin film to obtain average friction coefficient data. After sliding, the wear tracks and wear debris generated were analyzed using FESEM images and Raman spectroscopy.

Results and discussion

Ion Energy distribution function (IEDF)

The IEDF curves of the C+ and C2+ ions are shown in Fig. 1(a) and (b), respectively. As expected, there was higher carbon ionization (higher number of C+ and C2+ species and more energetic) when the % Ne was increased. This was corroborated by the IEDF curve of the 90% Ne of the film process, which showed the highest count level of the energy tail (from 40 to 100 eV). The remaining energy tails decreased as the % Ne decreased. Furthermore, none of the energy tails of the IEDF curves reach zero counts, which is due to the energy-ion limit of our mass spectrometer (100 eV). Therefore, this result indicates that the carbon ion energy exceeds 100 eV for all the deposition processes. A similar behavior was observed in our previous work29, where the Jpk of the deposition process was increased to obtain more carbon ionization. Furthermore, the insertion of neon as the working gas slightly increased the ionization level for all Ne percentages compared to the ionization of the plasma processes that only used argon seen in this previous work. Figure 2(a)–(d) show the IEDF curves for Ne+, Ne2+, Ar+, and Ar2+ plasma ions, respectively. The ionization levels of Ne+ were significantly higher than those of Ar+ [see Fig. 2 (a) and (c)] and showed that the Ne+ ionization was proportional to the C+ ionization. The IEDF curves of Ar2+ and Ne2+ [see Fig. 2(b) and (d)] showed a similar behavior because a higher Ne content in the Ar + Ne mixture increased Ne2+ and Ar2+ ionization. Owing to the decrease in the Ar species, when the Ne gas atoms increased, the Ar+ ionization decreased. This is corroborated by the IEDF Ar+ curve [see Fig. 2(c)]. The IEDF curves are typically divided into (i) low-energy peaks, corresponding to the ions that lost their initial kinetic energy owing to collisions, a phenomenon called thermalization, and (ii) high-energy tail, representing the probability function of the electron impact to produce ionization29,33 (see Figs. 1 and 2). In this sense, it was clear that the carbon ionization followed a similar Ne+ ionization behavior since processes with 20, 40, 60 and 80% Ne showed an intense low energy-peak between 0 and 2 eV. In contrast, no low-energy peaks were detected for the 90% Ne, this occurred for C+ and Ne+ IEDF curves [see Fig. 1(a) and 2(a)]. In addition, the rest of the ionized species followed this Ne+ behavior, where only the 90% Ne process showed no low peak energy. The ascent side of this low-energy peak represents the quick neon and argon ionization produced at the cathode surface owing to the large number of electrons impacting the cathode surface. The descending side shows thermalization owing to the loss of kinetic energy. Therefore, the large number of highly Ne ionized species at 90% Ne in the Ar + Ne gas working mixture generated many ionized species during the initial ionization process, and the mass detector used did not perceive the thermalization decay in the IEDF curves. The similarity in the energy tail behavior of C+ and Ne+ IEDF curves was less evident because the C+ energy tail positions [ETP, see Fig. 1 (a) and 2 (a)] were tighter, except for the 90% Ne process that was above, although it is possible to visualize that C+ energy tail position slightly increases with % Ne in the deposition process. The less energetic ionized species shown in both high- and low-energy peaks suggest a larger number of low-ionized species that could have decreased the probability function of the electron impact to produce ionization (energy tail) in the deposition process. In other words, an increase in the high- and low-energy peaks decreases the energy tail intensity, generating slightly lower ionization. This effect was larger for Ne+ than for Ar+ ionization.

Growth and thickness evaluation

The FESEM characterization of the carbon films revealed a columnar growth for all the samples (Fig. 3 shows such growth taken from the CF-Ne20 sample), and the thicknesses were obtained as follows: 480 nm (2.66 nm/min), 520 nm (2.89 nm/min), 440 nm (2.44 nm/min), 440 nm (2.44 nm/min), and 580 nm (3.22 nm/min) for CF-Ne20 to CF-Ne90, respectively. Such values show that the samples CF-Ne60 and CF-Ne80, which had the lowest deposition rates, did not follow the expected upward trend in thickness. This behavior could be related mainly to the number C+ ions with low energy (less than 50 eV) content in the plasma and then reach the growing film. Figure 4 shows the similarities between the C+/Ar+ and thickness evolution for the different neon percentages (%), where show similar curves between them from 20 to 80 Ne (%), for the case of Ne 90% the highest thickness and lower C+/Ar+ ratio can be attributed to the significantly higher number of C+ showed in the IEDF curves [see Fig. 2 (a)]. From these observations, it seems that the number C+ ion species with lower energy levels significantly influenced on the deposition rate. Additionally, the Ar+ ions, which have lower ionization energy than the neon, can promote these lower energetic ions into the plasma. The number of higher energetic C+ ions could also contribute to the deposition rate with less influence, as observed in the CF-Ne90 sample. Figure 2 (a) shows that the slopes of the energy tails of the Ne+ ions seem to follow the same thickness growth behavior, where the thicknesses increased with the energy tails slopes, although this observation is less evident. In addition, it is worth noting that the deposition rate increased considerably compared to that of the samples obtained in our previous work29 under an Ar atmosphere (1.25 to 1.7 nm/min).

Raman spectroscopy

Figure 5 (a)–(e) show the Raman spectra from CF-Ne20 to CF-Ne90. Each spectrum was deconvoluted to obtain the Raman semiquantitative parameters listed in Table 2. Details of the meaning of such parameters for obtaining a semi-quantitative estimation of sp3 bonding have been described in our previous works34,35. The sp3 content of the carbon films was estimated by the sp3 equation from the work of E. C. Talibouya Ba and coworkers36. The CF-Ne80 and CF-Ne90 samples exhibited a significant decrease in the sp3 bonding compared to the other three carbon films because of their higher and lower values of ID/IG and FWHM, respectively (see Table 2; Fig. 6a). Owing to the opposite behavior of these two parameters, the CF-Ne60 film exhibited the highest sp3 content, followed by CF-Ne40 and CF-Ne20. These Raman results demonstrated that the sp3 content increased until it reached a maximum (Ne-60%). After this point, the sp3 content decreased significantly. The G-peak position showed a similar behavior, because samples CF-Ne40 and CF-Ne60 obtained the lowest peak position values, indicating the highest sp3 content for these samples. The sp3 estimation corroborates these Raman parameters showing the highest sp3 for Ne-60% and the lowest for Ne-90%.

These behaviors could be correlated with the deposition mechanism described by Robertson, where the density and sp3 content of the carbon films increase with the C+ ions energy until reach a maximum37. On the other hand, the initial growth of carbide phases occurs in the carbon ion penetration into the metallic substrate (M) forcing the bonding M-C, after this point the increment on the ion fluence generates higher local carbon concentration forming a 2D layer (graphite). As the carbon concentrations increase, they could form 3D bonding similar to graphite and diamond38. In this sense, the type and amount of sp2 and sp3 bonding will depend on the carbon atoms concentration on the forming film and on the energy level of the carbon ions that reach the surface. Higher carbon concentrations and higher carbon ion energies will promote deeper subplantation into the forming carbon film. In addition, for the cases where the working gas has heavier species like neon, their effects on the growing carbon film should be considered. The energy transfer of the noble ions plays a crucial role since they can promote noble ion subpplantation and knock-on processes that can alter the bonding of the film. According to B. Akhavan and coworkers39 the noble ion energy transfer to individual carbon atoms of the growing film is more effective than the carbon ion energy, generating compressive stresses as the carbon atoms are struck by the incident noble ions and subsequently can reach deeper into the carbon films. On the other hand, when the energy levels into the carbon growing film are exceeded, the bonding net starts to generate thermalization and subsequently relaxation effects that decrease the dense and disorder bonding (sp3 bonds) by the effects of the effective subplantation and knock-on40. After this point the carbon atoms search its natural sp2 bonding, decreasing the sp3 bonds. In this sense, the C+/Ne+ at 50 and 100 eV ratios shown in Fig. 6(b) show considerable lower carbon ions into the plasma for CF-Ne80 and CF-Ne90 (specially for 50 eV values) showing a predominant neon ions arriving and colliding the growing film, indicating higher energy absorption compared to the rest of the carbon films and suggesting that on this two samples the thermalization and relaxations effects where the highest, decreasing the sp3 content as the Raman results showed. The C+/Ne+ ratios of the other three samples were higher suggesting that such neon effects in the growing films were lower, therefore the sp3 content increased due to the lower thermalization and relaxation effects due to lower high energetic neon that arrived to the growing film. Worth is nothing that the distinctly semi-separated G and D peak positions of the samples with 80 and 90 Ne% indicate nanocrystalline (NC) graphite41, which is a net with greater hexagons that improve the mechanical properties and wear resistance of the carbon films compared to the common graphite net40,42.

Electrical properties

The electrical resistivity measurements of the samples performed using the four-point method are shown in Fig. 7. Films deposited at 20–60 Ne% in the mixed atmosphere showed electric resistivity values of approximately 8 × 105 Ω·cm, which is in agreement with the literature reports on carbon films43,44. However, for CF-Ne80 and CF-Ne90, the electrical resistivity decreased significantly at 60 Ω·cm and 70 Ω·cm, respectively. This behavior indicated a significant change in the sp3/sp2 ratio of the deposited films. The sp2 has a trigonal form with three electrons in a plane (σ bond), and a fourth electron, perpendicularly arranged on the z-axis, lies in the pπ orbital45. The electrical properties of DLC are dependent on the carrier electrons of the sp2 bonding structure (graphite-like) through a discontinuous hopping transport mechanism46. The electrical resistivity decreases with an increase in the number of sp2 bonded, regarding sp3 bonded (diamond-like), due to the increase in localized states of unsaturated orbitals44,45,47. These results are in accordance with the sp3 estimations obtained in the Raman section, since carbon films with 80 and 80 Ne (%) decrease by around 10% their sp3 content. On the other hand, it must also be considered the nanocrystalline structures of these two films, because of their grain boundaries could have acted as a rapid path for transferred electrons, influencing the abrupt resistivity drop on both films48; grain boundaries contribute to the resistance of the material49.

Tribological behaviors

Figure 8 shows the friction coefficient (µ) evolution obtained from the tribological tests. Table 3 shows the average friction coefficients and wear rate values obtained for all carbon films and substrates. CF-Ne80 and CF-Ne90 showed a complete rupture of the films at the beginning of the tests (200 s and 550 s for CF-Ne80 and CF-Ne90, respectively); therefore, their friction coefficient values were a combination of the carbon film debris and the substrate surface, reducing the µ compared to the one seen in the substrate 316 L alloy. This rupture also resulted in the highest wear rates of all carbon samples (see Table 3). On the other hand, CF-Ne20 and CF-Ne40 showed similar tribological behaviors, as both at the beginning of the tests reached the highest µ values (from 0 to 400s). After this point, the µ values gradually decreased until they reached the most stable and lowest µ values (~ 0.12) after 400 s and 600 s for CF-Ne20 and CF-Ne40, respectively. In the case of CF-Ne60, its behavior was distinctive because the µ values did not show significant variation throughout the test (see Fig. 8), obtaining an average µ value of 0.330.

Figure 11 (a) and (b) show the Raman spectra taken from the wear tracks of the samples deposited with 20 and 40 Ne %, where the graphitization processes are shown through the Raman spectra shape modification compared to those shown from the pristine surface films. Such modifications resulted in significant differences in the Raman parameters and on the sp3 estimation (see Table 4). The increment in the sp2 content promotes the graphitization processes that can reduce the friction coefficient35,50, for these two films. The graphitization process during sliding tests is associated with the stress relaxation of sp2 bonds, leading to their re-hybridization into sp2 bonds51. Furthermore, for carbon films with lower sp3 content exposed to sliding tests is common to find a carbon transferred layer on the ball-counterpart after the tests, that acts like a solid lubricant that promotes low friction coefficients29. In the case of the Raman spectrum obtained from the CF-Ne60 wear track surface, see Fig. 11(c), it showed a non-typical behavior because there was no significant Raman spectrum modification before and after the tribological tests, conserving similar sp3 content before and after of the sliding tests (see Tables 2 and 4), evidencing a lack of graphitization processes that could have decreased µ values during the reciprocating test. Therefore, µ values remained unchanged during all the evolution of the sliding test. This behavior could be explained from the typical hard coatings film properties, where harder surfaces avoid local plastic deformations that could increase the friction coefficient52. However, local asperities can more easily to increase the µ values due to the higher resistance of them to be flattened by the applied load, generating lower wear rates and slight increments in friction. In addition, carbon films with higher sp3 content no show transferred layer to the ball-counterpart29, influencing the friction increments on this film with the highest sp3 content compared to the films with lower sp3.

Conclusions

The influence of neon in the gas-working Ar + Ne mixture of the HiPIMS deposition process on the growth, bonding structure, and tribological carbon film properties through carbon ionization was carefully analyzed. According to our observations and following the deposition model of Robertson, the deposition rate primarily depended on the number of C + ions with lower energetic levels that were promoted for the argon and neon ionization, however, argon ionization determined the small thickness variation, because the C+/Ar+ and thickness vs. Ne (%) curves followed similar behaviors. The bonding characterization by Raman spectroscopy revealed that the sp3 content increased with increasing %Ne until 60% in the gas-working mixture. The samples above this value showed a significant drop in the sp3 content. This behavior was attributed mainly to the highly energetic Ne+ ion species that can generate effective subplantation and knock-on displacement into the growing films that promote disorder bonds like the sp3 ones. On the other hand, in samples above the Ne 60% and according to the sp3 estimation and C+/Ne+ ratio, the neon and carbon ionized species that reach the highest energy impregnation levels that generated an energy excess that promote thermalization and relaxation effects that decrease the sp3 content of the carbon films. The influence of the transferred energy to the growing film from the neon species seems that was a decisive role in the sp3 content. Samples with intermediate sp3 contents showed the best tribological behavior because they obtained the lowest friction coefficients and wear rates, showing slight superficial deformation. In the case of the sample with the highest sp3 content, it was shown that the friction coefficient increased owing to the lack of superficial deformation or difficult flattening owing to the strong bonding of the high sp3 content.
