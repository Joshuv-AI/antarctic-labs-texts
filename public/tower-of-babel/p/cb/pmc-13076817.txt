Response to “Treatment Outcomes with Lanadelumab Every Two Weeks and Garadacimab Are Very Similar in Patients with Hereditary Angioedema”
Authors: Imtiaz A. Samjoo
Published: 2026

Following the letter to the editor by Andresen et al., titled “Treatment Outcomes with Lanadelumab Every Two Weeks and Garadacimab Are Very Similar in Patients with Hereditary Angioedema” [1], we appreciate the opportunity to clarify the main concerns raised and provide context on our methodology. We address the following key topics herein.

Use of Q4W Dosing

The purpose of a network meta-analysis (NMA) is to synthesize randomized evidence while maintaining internal validity. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for indirect comparisons because randomization minimizes bias and confounding, and RCTs are preferred globally by health technology assessment agencies. Excluding evidence because it does not reflect clinical practice or regulatory guidance introduces selective interpretation and undermines the scientific purpose of evidence synthesis. The only randomized evidence for dosing every 4 weeks (Q4W) comes from the HELP study, where Q4W was tested as a starting dose, and the only maintenance dose studied in clinical trials for lanadelumab is every 2 weeks (Q2W) in HELP-OLE.

Combining Studies with Different Follow-Up Times

Andresen et al. suggested that combining data from a 12-week, phase II study and a 6-month phase III trial favored garadacimab for non-time-normalized endpoints. This critique is unclear because our model uses time offsets to normalize exposure in rate models (Poisson/negative binomial) and cumulative risk models (cloglog link with log-exposure offset), which is a standard approach that adjusts for differential follow-up times [2]. We nevertheless acknowledge the importance of having homogeneous follow-up times and made all efforts possible to minimize this potential bias.

Definition of Attack Severity

Andresen et al. misquoted the HELP definition of mild attacks as requiring “no medical intervention.” The actual definition was: “Transient or mild discomfort (<48 hours); no medical intervention/therapy required.” In clinical research, ‘therapy’ typically refers to disease-specific medication (e.g., IV C1-inhibitors, bradykinin B2 receptor antagonists) used to treat hereditary angioedema (HAE) attacks. In particular, analgesics are not considered disease-specific therapy; they alleviate pain but do not treat the underlying condition. In HAE trials, analgesics are usually categorized as concomitant medication (CM), which is often under-reported [3].

We acknowledge minor differences in definitions of mild and moderate attacks between HELP and VANGUARD, which may contribute to cross-trial heterogeneity. However, such heterogeneity is not uncommon when synthesizing clinical data from several trials, and does not necessarily invalidate the evidence synthesis [4], considering that the clinical impact of these differences is limited. It is also reasonable to assume that, given the size of the treatment effects in favor of garadacimab versus lanadelumab Q2W and Q4W for the time-normalized number of moderate or severe attacks, our conclusions are robust to this type of heterogeneity.

Fixed-Effect Versus Random-Effects Models

It is our position that the application of a Bayesian framework was appropriate in the present context to allow for a coherent and explicit representation of uncertainty in sparse networks with limited information, where frequentist approaches have their own limitations (e.g., unstable estimators of heterogeneity, questionable use of asymptotics) and degree of subjectivity (with assumptions embedded in the estimator).

Andresen et al. noted that random-effects models should have been used when fit statistics favored them and highlighted the differences in the statistically significant findings versus the fixed-effects models. The apparent disagreement between models reflects well known challenges in Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis with vague priors for between-trial heterogeneity [2]. Vague priors are chosen to allow results to be driven primarily by data; however, in sparse networks, vague priors dominate the posteriors, producing unstable estimates. As shown in our supplementary tables, the between-trial standard deviation estimates were nearly identical to the vague prior (uniform(0,5)), which implies implausible uncertainty, leading to posterior distributions with limited clinical interpretability (e.g., rate ratios ranging from 3 to > 75,000) [5]. In this regard, the wide intervals reported in the supplementary random-effects model might reflect prior-driven uncertainty inflation rather than genuine evidence of large heterogeneity [5].

Empirical evidence suggests that heterogeneity parameters in randomized controlled trials tend to be small [6], and that informative priors could provide a more plausible representation of between-study variability than very diffuse priors. When such empirically grounded priors are used, random-effects estimates typically align more closely with fixed-effect results, suggesting that the conclusions of the manuscript may be less sensitive to model choice than implied by analyses based on vague priors.

We agree that SUCRA and p-best should not be interpreted in isolation. Rankings simplify the reporting of the results based on the relative ordering of treatments, since they are less affected by variations of the baseline risk, and they should complement effect size estimates for clinical decision making. The observation that mean monthly attack rates for garadacimab and lanadelumab Q2W are similar aligns with our interpretation, though the analyses reported in this paper confirm cross-trials comparisons should not be made naively.

In summary, our manuscript provides a rigorous synthesis of randomized evidence for long-term prophylaxis in HAE. Our NMA methodology has been externally validated by experts and HTA agencies. The findings remain robust under reasonable assumptions and align with independent analyses [7].
