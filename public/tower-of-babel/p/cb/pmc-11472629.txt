Biomarker trajectory for earlier detection of lung cancer
Authors: Ehsan Irajizad, Johannes F Fahrmann, Iakovos Toumazis, Jody Vykoukal, Jennifer B Dennison, Yu Shen, Kim-Anh Do, Edwin J Ostrin, Ziding Feng, Samir Hanash
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundTo determine whether an algorithm based on repeated measurements of a panel of four circulating protein biomarkers (4 MP) for lung cancer risk assessment results in improved performance over a single time measurement.MethodsWe conducted data analysis of the 4 MP consisting of the precursor form of surfactant protein B, cancer antigen 125, carcinoembryonic antigen, and cytokeratin-19 fragment in pre-diagnostic sera from 2483 ever-smoker participants (389 cases and 2094 randomly selected non-cases) in the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, Ovarian (PLCO) Study who had at least two sequential blood collections over 6 years. Research in context

Evidence before this study

To-date, there has been a paucity of studies that assessed the performance of longitudinally collected blood-based biomarkers for risk prediction of lung cancer and other cancer types. Trajectory evaluation of biomarkers has the potential to provide better assessment of cancer risk compared to a single-time measurement.

Added value of this study

We conducted data analysis of the pre-established 4-marker panel (4 MP) consisting of prosurfactant protein B, CA125, CEA and CYFRA in serum samples collected from 2483 ever-smoker participants (389 cases and 2094 randomly selected non-cases) in the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, Ovarian (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial who had at least two sequential blood collections. We demonstrated that repeat measurements of the 4 MP improves sensitivity without loss of specificity and lead-time detection of lung cancer compared to a single-time point measurement.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings demonstrate that an adaptive testing algorithm based on serial biomarker would better select individuals who would benefit from low-dose CT screening, thereby potentially reducing lung cancer mortality.

Introduction

There is currently substantial evidence that supports the use of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) for lung cancer screening for high-risk individuals. The first randomized-controlled evidence of a mortality benefit from lung cancer screening by LDCT was as a result of the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) that showed a reduction of lung cancer death by ∼20% compared to chest radiography in those with a significant history of cigarette smoking.1,2 The NLST findings were validated in other trials including the European NELSON trial,3 the Multicentric Italian Lung Detection (MILD),4 the German Lung cancer Screening Intervention (LUSI),5 and the UK Lung Cancer Screening (UKLS) Trial.6 In March 2021, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) broadened screening to include individuals who are between 50 and 80 years old, have ≥20 pack-years (PYs) of smoking, and are either currently smoking or formerly smoked with ≤15 years since quitting.

A concern has been that a sizable portion of lung cancers occur outside of the adopted eligibility criteria.7, 8, 9 Additionally, the uptake rate of LDCT has been modest (<6%) within screening eligible populations, partially due to concerns related to high false positive rates and barriers related to accessibility.7, 8, 9, 10, 11

Blood-based biomarkers have the potential to better individualize the risk of lung cancer and inform on the need for lung cancer screening.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 To this end, we have recently demonstrated that a 4-marker protein panel (4 MP) consisting of the precursor form of surfactant protein B (Pro-SFTPB), cancer antigen 125 (CA125), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and cytokeratin-19 fragment (CYFRA 21-1), coupled with the PLCOm2012 risk prediction model, better identified individuals with a smoking history of ≥10 pack-years (PYs) who are at high risk of lethal lung cancer that would benefit from LDCT screening compared to USPSTF screening criteria.17,18 However, these studies considered 4 MP measurements at a singular time point. A fundamental question remains whether changes in biomarker trajectories from repeat measurements yields further improvements in lung cancer risk prediction. Advantages of repeat testing compared to a single time point measurement have been evidenced in the context of ovarian cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, wherein respective rises in CA125 or AFP provided improved lead-time and diagnostic performance.19, 20, 21

In the current study, we assessed whether repeated measurements of the 4 MP would increase sensitivity while maintaining specificity at thresholds corresponding to 1.0% and 1.7% 6-year risk thresholds, which align with the current and former USPSTF screening criteria, respectively, and would result in an earlier detection compared to a single threshold approach.17,18

Methods

PLCO specimen set

The PLCO Cancer Screening Trial is a randomized multicenter trial in the United States which aimed at evaluating the impact of early detection procedures for prostate, lung, colorectal, and ovarian cancer on disease-specific mortality. Detailed information regarding the PLCO cohort is provided in our prior publications.17,18 Study participants completed a baseline questionnaire at study entry that included demographic, personal, and medical information. Reporting of cancer status was based on annual questionnaires. Medical records were obtained to document diagnostic follow-up and characteristics of any diagnosed lung cancers. A biorepository was created for blood specimens were collected from consented, intervention group participants, at baseline entry and annually up until the time of a clinical lung cancer diagnosis or until the end of the 6 year study follow-up period.22 There were 42,450 individuals who have ever smoked in the intervention arm; 85% of the participants in the intervention arm had at least one collection. Sex was self-reported by study participants at baseline.

All histologically confirmed lung cancers from the ever-smoked subjects in the intervention arm that were diagnosed within six years of study entry (n = 552 case participants) with pre-diagnostic sera available were considered for the current study. Non-case participants who have ever smoked were randomly selected in a 4:1 ratio with cases (n = 2193 non-cases). The random selection of non-case participant was to allow approximate representation of the population. We selected non-cases at random among ever-smoking study participants who remained cancer free during the PLCO study period and the 13 years of study follow-up. Risk-set sampling was not considered as to ensure that non-case individuals remain cancer-free during study follow-up.

Of the 552 lung cancer cases and 2193 non-case, 389 (70.5%) and 2094 (95.4%) had 2 or more serial samples available, respectively (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Table S1). Of the 389 lung cancer cases with 2 or more available blood draws, 338 (86.9%) had at least one sample collected within 2 years of a lung cancer diagnosis (Supplementary Table S2). 163 lung cancer cases and 99 non-case controls had only one specimen available and were excluded from subsequent analyses as this precludes assessment of performance of repeat biomarker testing.

Statistics

Data for the 4 MP from cases and non-cases PLCO participants were from our previous publications.17,18 Information regarding immunoassays is also provided in the Supplemental Materials. We used single-threshold (ST) method that considers the value of the panel at a single time point vs a parametric empirical Bayes (PEB) algorithm, which consider all existing measurements of the panel and adjusts the biomarker threshold to reflect participant history and detailed information is provided in the Supplemental Materials.19 Briefly, biomarker mean value (μ) and variability of measurements within and across non-case participants were estimated and used to center and scale biomarker measurements. These parameters were estimated from non-case participants with ≥10 PYs smoking history as the target population for blood-based biomarker screening.17,18 In PEB algorithm, the current measurement compared against a weighted average of μ and the participant's previous biomarker values. However, the ST method compares the current biomarker measurement against only the μ value. False-positive rate (FPR) was estimated at the screening level, defined as the proportion of positive results among all the screenings conducted in the control group. The screening-level specificity was defined as 1-FPR. We estimated the true-positive rate (TPR) or sensitivity at the patient level, which was defined as the proportion of lung cancer cases with at least 1 positive biomarker test, consistent with previous applications of this method.21,23 For the ROC curve, we used the full range of specificities and estimated the sensitivities and then calculate the area under the curve (AUC). In PLCO cohort, patient information (e.g. smoking history) was only collected at the time of trial entry (baseline), precluding inclusion of patient information in models that consider longitudinal data. Therefore, both PEB and ST used only 4 MP scores derived from our previously developed logistic regression model as covariate. 12 For our analysis, we set the specificity at 45.4% and 63.2% which are the specificities of 4 MP at 1.0% and 1.7% 6-year lung cancer risk established in our prior publications, respectively.17,18 1.0% 6-year risk represents the same number of screening exams as the current 2021 USPSTF guideline and 1.7% 6-year risk represents the same number of screening exams as the former 2013 USPSTF guideline.

To account for multiple measurements per participant, 95% confidence interval were calculated as percentiles of the sampling distribution estimated from 1000 resamples using a stratified and clustered bootstrap method.24 Prevalence adjusted Positive Predictive Value (PPV) and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) were estimated using pre-established Bayes’ rule.25 Analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing).26 All P-values were estimated from bootstrapping approach.

We additionally performed a training and testing approach to evaluate the robustness of the model. The training set consisted of participants with two or more serial samples collected from five of the ten PLCO study centers. The five centers used for validation had a similarly broad distribution across the US as the centers used to train the longitudinal model.

Results

Amongst ever-smoker individuals, the PEB algorithm yielded an area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.82–0.89), which was improved compared to the ST approach (AUC: 0.81 (95% CI: 0.78–0.84); comparison of AUCs P-value:0.009) (Supplementary Figure S2). To assess for stability and reliability of PEB, we divided the specimen cohort into training (N = 163 cases and 913 non-cases) and testing (N = 226 cases and 1181 non-cases) sets. The PEB parameters were developed in the training set and evaluated in the testing set. Consistent with our findings in the entire PLCO specimen set, the PEB approach improved the AUC by 4% AUC compared ST (AUCPEB: 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.92) vs AUCST: 0.83 (95% CI: 0.80–0.87); comparison of AUCs P-value: 0.01) in the testing set (Supplementary Fig. S7 and S8), thus providing confidence that the PEB model is not overfitted.

When considering individuals with ≥10 PYs in the entire PLCO specimen set, the PEB algorithm yielded an additional 5% improvement in AUC compared to the ST approach (AUCPEB: 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.88) vs AUCST: 0.81 (95% CI: 0.79–0.84); P-value: 0.001) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables S17). Benefit of the PEB algorithm was observed among cases stratified into those that were clinically diagnosed with early-stage (I + II) or late-stage (III-IV) lung cancer with respective AUC improvements of 0.06 (AUCPEB: 0.83 (95% CI: 0.80–0.87) vs AUCST: 0.77 (95% CI: 0.73–0.81); P-value: 0.02) and 0.04 (AUCPEB: 0.89 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92) vs AUCST: 0.85 (95% CI: 0.82–0.89); P-value:0.04) (Supplementary Tables S3–S5, S18 and S19; Supplementary Fig. S2–S6, S12).

ROC curves depicting the performance of the 4 MP using the PEB and ST approaches amongst participants with ≥10 PY smoking history.

At a pre-established specificity threshold of 63.2%, corresponding to the specificity of the 4 MP at 1.7% 6-year risk threshold,17 PEB improved the positive predictive value (PPV) by 0.53% (PPVPEB: 5.18% vs PPVST: 4.65%; P-value: 3.2e-10) and sensitivity by 9.8% (SenPEB: 90.7% vs SenST:80.9; P-value: 1.7e-12) (Table 2; Supplementary Tables S6–S9). Improvements in sensitivity from PEB were most pronounced among individuals in the high- and medium-risk strata (7.2% and 14.5% increase in sensitivity compared to ST, respectively) (Supplementary Tables S7).

Of the 324 cases with ≥10 PYs, 297 (92.6%) were identified by PEB and/or ST. 247 of the 297 (81.1%) were captured with PEB/ST at the same time, of which 222 (74.7%) were identified based on the first biomarker measurement (Supplementary Fig. S9A-B, Supplementary Table S16). Of the 50 individuals that were identified by either PEB or ST, the PEB algorithm identified 35 individuals that remained ST negative, at an average of 1.19 years (interquartile range (IQR): 0.81–1.92) before diagnosis. Of those, 17 (48.6%) were non-eligible for LDCT screening based on USPSTF2013 eligibility criteria (Supplementary Table S10). Ten individuals that were captured by ST at an average of 0.90 years (IQR: 0.46–1.48) prior to diagnosis were also identified by PEB at an average of 3.35 years (IQR: 2.81–3.67) before diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. S9C, S10; Supplementary Table S10, 13–S15).

At a specificity threshold of 45.4%, which corresponds to specificity of 4 MP at 1.0% 6-year risk of lung cancer,17 PEB improved the PPV by 0.20 (PPVPEB: 3.76% vs PPVST: 3.57%; P-value: 9.1e-5) and sensitivity by 5.3% (SenPEB: 96.3% vs SenST: 91.0; P-value: 6.7e-3), corresponding to 41.1% of the cases that were missed by ST (Table 2; Supplementary Tables S6–S9). Improvements in sensitivity from PEB came from identifying more lung cancer cases among high-risk individuals (Supplementary Table S7).

Of the 324 cases with ≥10 PYs, 312 (96.3%) were identified by PEB and/or ST. 284 of the 312 (91.0%) were captured with PEB/ST at the same time, of which 261 (83.6%) were identified by PEB/ST on the first blood draw (Supplementary Fig. S11A and B, Supplementary Table S16). Of the 28 individuals that were captured by either PEB or ST, the PEB algorithm identified 17 individuals at an average of 1.26 years (IQR: 0.87–2.15) before diagnosis while ST remained negative (Supplementary Table S10). 6 of the 17 (35.3%) were non-eligible for LDCT screening based on USPSTF2021 criteria. Ten individuals identified by ST at an average of 1.03 years (IQR: 0.27–1.69) before diagnosis were captured by PEB at an average of 2.70 years (IQR: 2.02–3.54) prior to diagnosis (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S11C; Supplementary Tables S10–S12).

Discussion

This study introduces an adaptive testing algorithm on serial biomarker measurements intended for risk prediction of lung cancer. We demonstrated that, compared to a ST method, PEB yields improvements in sensitivity (without loss of specificity) and in lead time for risk assessment of lung cancer.

In our prior studies, we demonstrated that the 4 MP combined with the PLCOm2012 risk model better identifies individuals at high-risk of a lethal lung cancer that would benefit from lung cancer screening compared to current USPSTF criteria.17,18 Periodical testing of the 4 MP would be useful for individuals who are currently eligible for LDCT screening as well as identifying those with a ≥10 PY smoking history who have an equivalent lung cancer risk that would imply benefit from LDCT-based screening. Individuals with an initial negative test would be recommended for annual repeat testing. In this scenario, the application of the PEB algorithm may provide additional information regarding lung cancer risk that may not be captured with a single timepoint measurement. Patients that are PEB and/or ST positive at the repeat test would be recommended for LDCT-based screening whereas patients PEB and ST negative would be advised for continued repeat biomarker testing.

Dissemination of biomarkers into peripheral circulation is, in part, a function of initial tumor size and Volume Doubling Time (VDT), defined as the number of days in which a malignant nodule doubles its volume. VDT is a clinically important metric in lung cancer screening with a general range of 20 days to <590 days for malignant nodules.27,28 Tumors with a short VDT would be anticipated to have a more exponential rise in circulation biomarker levels whereas slower growing tumors may exhibit a more modest rise in biomarker levels. Thus, a modest or rapid rise in biomarker values over time may inform upon presence of occult disease, even if the absolute value of the biomarker score has not yet reached the threshold. The advantage of PEB is that it factors in incremental changes in biomarker values from prior readouts.

In our study, at a 1% 6-year risk threshold, representing an equivalent risk as the 2021 US screening criteria, we demonstrated that the PEB algorithm captured 60.7% of lung cancer cases that were negative by the ST approach, and led to a significant extension in lead time, which is anticipated to result in a shift towards detection of earlier stage disease. Similar results were observed at the 1.7% 6-year risk threshold, which corresponds to the former 2013 US lung screening guidelines.

We note some limitations to our study. In the PLCO cohort, age limitation was an entry criterion.29 Therefore, the benefit of serial sampling over single threshold outside of this age range could not be determined. Information regarding changes in smoking status during study enrollment was also not reported, thus preventing assessment of cancer risk attributed to potential changes in smoking behavior. Though this study allows the full natural history of lung cancer to be observed which provides an accurate estimation of potential lead time, a further prospective study on a LDCT screening cohort will provide tumor stage-shift and positive predictive value information necessary for clinical adoption. Lead time estimates of a positive PEB or ST test are under the assumption that this would have led to an imminent diagnosis. We also note that the major benefit of PEB over ST method at 1.0% 6-year risk threshold comes from the high-risk group however at 1.7% 6-year risk the benefit was observed across all risk strata. Nevertheless, the findings in this study complement our previous work to provide additional evidence for the utility of the 4 MP for identifying individuals who are at high-risk of lung cancer and who may benefit from enrollment into lung cancer screening programs. Although the PEB algorithm has not been externally validated, we emphasize the challenges associated with acquisition of serially collected pre-diagnostic specimens with adequate follow-up. To this end, the PLCO specimen set included two or more serial blood draws from 2483 participants, which enabled us to calculate the model parameters for PEB and therefore accurately determine detection rate of the algorithm amongst the case and non-case participants. Additionally, we emphasize that the PEB algorithm used only data from the non-case control participants to calculate the model parameters and the same model parameters were used to scale both PEB and ST methods. Thus, sensitivity and lead-time estimates were not affected by overfitting problem (if any) since there were estimated from case participants only. Nonetheless, to address this limitation, we additionally applied a training and testing approach which demonstrated improvement of PEB over ST. Additionally, our studies excluded non-cases with previous or subsequent cancer diagnoses. Training and externally evaluating the ST and PEB 4 MP-algorithms in appropriately designed studies with non-cases who are at risk will be needed prior to implementation into clinical practice.

In conclusion, this study provides important proof-of-concept in the potential of using repeat protein biomarker measurements to improve risk assessment of lung cancer.
