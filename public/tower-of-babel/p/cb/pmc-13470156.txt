Understanding the influence of training schedules on short-term load forecasting via deep residual networks: An empirical study
Authors: Junchen Liu, Faisul Arif Ahmad, Khairulmizam Samsudin, Fazirulhisyam Hashim, Mohd Zainal Abidin Ab Kadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern electrical systems rely on short-term load forecasting (STLF) for reliable and efficient operation. Although deep residual networks (DRNs) have achieved strong forecasting performance in STLF, most existing studies mainly emphasize architectural modifications, while the influence of training strategies has received relatively limited attention. In particular, the effect of training schedule design, especially epoch allocation, remains insufficiently studied. This work examines the influence of epoch allocation on DRN-based STLF under a unified multi-stage snapshot ensemble framework. By keeping the model architecture and optimization settings unchanged, the analysis focuses specifically on the effect of different epoch allocation strategies on convergence behavior and generalization performance. Experiments are conducted using two real-world datasets, namely the New England Independent System Operator (ISO-NE) dataset and the Malaysia Petaling Jaya (MyPJ) dataset, which exhibit different temporal characteristics. The experimental results show that the optimal training schedule depends strongly on dataset properties. The ISO-NE dataset benefits from longer training durations and later snapshot extraction stages, whereas the MyPJ dataset achieves better forecasting performance at earlier training stages because of stronger short-term variability. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA)-based feature representation improves forecasting stability and reduces forecasting error by alleviating redundancy among meteorological variables. Statistical analysis further confirms that these improvements are statistically significant. Overall, the results demonstrate that suitable training schedule design can improve DRN-based STLF performance without increasing model complexity.Keywords: Deep Residual Networks, Short-Term Load Forecasting, Snapshot Ensemble Learning, Training Schedule, Principal Component AnalysisSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Modern power systems rely heavily on accurate short-term load forecasting (STLF) to maintain stable and efficient operation1. STLF provides demand estimates over short forecasting horizons, typically ranging from 24 h to one week ahead, and supports important operational tasks such as generation scheduling, unit commitment, and energy trading2. Improved forecasting accuracy can reduce operating costs while enhancing grid reliability, planning efficiency, and overall system operation3. However, the rapid growth in energy consumption and the increasing uncertainty of electricity demand patterns have made STLF more challenging and important in recent years4,5. From an economic perspective, even small improvements in forecasting accuracy may lead to considerable financial benefits. Previous studies have reported that a 1% improvement in forecasting accuracy can save large-scale utilities millions of dollars annually through better resource allocation and reduced reserve requirements6. Therefore, improving forecasting performance remains an important research objective in modern power systems.

STLF techniques can be broadly categorized into traditional statistical methods and data-driven approaches, including machine learning and deep learning (DL) techniques. Early research mostly employed statistical models and conventional machine learning methods7, which gave crucial insights into power consumption behavior and laid the groundwork for the development of STLF. Nevertheless, these approaches may exhibit limitations in capturing highly complex and nonlinear load patterns under certain conditions, particularly when dealing with high-dimensional and dynamic data. Their dependence on simplistic assumptions about core consumer characteristics is the reason for their incapacity to adapt to the unpredictability of the actual world. Furthermore, the chance of overfitting increases as dimensionality increases since the generalization effectiveness of input features often decreases8,9.

The increasing use of artificial neural networks (ANNs) has further advanced data-driven approaches for STLF. DL techniques, as an extension of ANNs, provide enhanced capability for modeling complex nonlinear relationships in load data10. However, increasing the number of input features, hidden neurons, or network layers may also increase the risk of overfitting, thereby reducing the generalization capability of the model. To alleviate this issue, various improved ANN-based architectures have been developed and applied in STLF studies11. Despite these developments, the capacity of early ANNs and their variations, which are usually characterized by relatively shallow architectures, to represent highly complex load patterns remains limited. Deep neural networks (DNNs) with multiple hidden layers have attracted increasing attention because of their strong nonlinear modeling capability and ability to learn hierarchical feature representations. Compared with conventional ANN-based models, DNN-based frameworks are more effective in capturing complex temporal dependencies and nonlinear relationships in load data. Consequently, advanced DL architectures have been increasingly adopted in STLF research due to their superior flexibility and forecasting performance12.

In STLF research, shallow models have increasingly given way to deeper neural network architectures with richer input representations. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are effective in extracting localized temporal features, but their ability to capture long-term dependencies remains limited, particularly in deeper structures13,14. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) partially alleviate this issue through the use of dilated and causal convolutions, which enlarge the receptive field without substantially increasing computational cost. Nevertheless, TCN performance is still highly dependent on architectural configuration, especially when handling long sequential data15,16. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, can model temporal dependencies effectively through memory mechanisms; however, their sequential nature may reduce computational efficiency when handling long input sequences, depending on the specific implementation and application context17,18. Transformer-based models improve the modeling of long-range temporal dependencies through self-attention mechanisms and parallel computation. However, deep Transformer architectures may introduce substantial computational overhead and unstable training behavior19–21. To improve forecasting performance further, recent studies have explored hybrid architectures that combine convolutional, recurrent, and attention-based components. Representative examples include Transformer–LSTM22 and CNN–LSTM with multi-modal attention (CNN–LSTM–MMA)23, which integrate different feature extraction strategies within a unified framework. Despite their promising forecasting performance, these hybrid models are often computationally intensive and structurally complex. Such challenges motivate continued research into more efficient and stable DL frameworks for STLF.

Residual learning, implemented through identity shortcut connections, was originally introduced in the residual network (ResNet) architecture to alleviate vanishing gradients and improve the training stability of DNNs24. Based on this idea, Chen et al.25 introduced the Deep Residual Network (DRN) for STLF and demonstrated that an appropriate balance among network depth, training stability, and representational capability can provide accurate and reliable forecasting performance. Since then, various improvements to DRN-based frameworks have been explored, including residual structures specifically designed for STLF tasks26, ensemble strategies such as snapshot learning and residual ensembles27,28, and adaptive feature selection methods29. Other studies have incorporated convolutional residual blocks and inception-style modules to enhance local feature extraction and representation learning30–32. Hybrid DRN architectures integrating recurrent networks or attention mechanisms have also been investigated to better capture temporal dependencies33–35. In addition, the Principal Component Analysis–Deep Residual Network (PCA–DRN) framework was proposed to handle high-dimensional meteorological inputs through PCA-based dimensionality reduction prior to model training36.

Although previous DRN-based STLF studies have reported promising results, most of them mainly emphasize architectural modifications, feature engineering, and ensemble strategies26–36. In contrast, the influence of the training process itself has received relatively limited attention. In many existing studies, training configurations are selected empirically or kept fixed throughout the experiments, while the effect of training dynamics on forecasting performance is rarely analyzed in detail.

Among the training-related settings, the allocation of training epochs is an important factor that remains insufficiently investigated. In DL models, the number of training epochs directly affects the optimization process and influences both convergence and generalization performance37–39. Training with too few epochs may result in underfitting, whereas excessive training may increase the risk of overfitting. Moreover, besides the total number of epochs, the distribution of epochs across different training stages may also affect model learning behavior40,41.

This issue is especially relevant in multi-stage training frameworks such as snapshot ensemble learning, which has been adopted in several DRN-based STLF studies to improve forecasting stability and robustness25,42. In these frameworks, model snapshots are collected at different stages of training and combined as an implicit ensemble. As a result, the allocation of training epochs influences not only the quality of individual snapshot models but also the diversity among ensemble members43,44. Nevertheless, training schedules in existing studies are usually determined empirically, and systematic investigations of epoch allocation or snapshot timing remain limited.

Therefore, this study investigates how different training schedule designs influence the forecasting performance of DRN-based STLF models. The analysis focuses on the temporal distribution of training epochs within a unified multi-stage training framework and examines its effect on convergence behavior and model generalization. Experiments conducted on real-world datasets are used to evaluate the effectiveness of different training strategies and to provide practical observations for DRN-based STLF research.

Background and problem formulation

This section reviews recent DL-based approaches for STLF and discusses the main research gaps in the existing literature. The growing complexity and uncertainty of electricity consumption patterns have made accurate STLF increasingly challenging. Traditional statistical methods and shallow learning models often have limited ability to capture the nonlinear relationships and temporal dependencies contained in load data. In recent years, DL approaches, particularly DNN-based models, have received considerable attention because of their strong nonlinear modeling capability and effectiveness in learning temporal features from large-scale datasets.

Modern data-driven forecasting approaches, particularly those based on DL, can be broadly classified into four frameworks: convolution-based, recurrent-based, attention-driven, and hybrid. Different DL frameworks focus on different characteristics of load time-series data. CNN-based models are effective in extracting localized temporal features. For example, Li et al.13 transformed load sequences into image-like representations to exploit spatial correlations, although this increased the structural complexity of the model. Similarly, Jurado et al.14 proposed an encoder–decoder CNN framework with probabilistic forecasting capability to improve prediction accuracy, but its performance deteriorated under high-load conditions. Building upon conventional CNN architectures, TCNs employ dilated and causal convolutions to improve the modeling of long-range temporal dependencies. Tang et al.15 incorporated channel-wise and temporal attention mechanisms to further improve forecasting accuracy, although the resulting architecture became more complex. Liu et al.16 introduced a depthwise separable convolutional structure to improve computational efficiency. Nevertheless, because these approaches remain primarily convolution-based, their ability to capture highly complex temporal dynamics may still be limited.

Recurrent architectures, particularly LSTM-based models, have been widely applied in sequential forecasting tasks. Narayan and Hipel17 demonstrated that LSTM models can achieve satisfactory forecasting performance when sufficient historical data are available, although their effectiveness may decrease when external influencing factors are not adequately considered. Bento et al.18 further improved prediction accuracy through metaheuristic-based hyperparameter optimization, but this approach also increased computational cost.

Transformer- and attention-based frameworks have recently attracted considerable attention because of their ability to model long-range temporal dependencies. Jiang et al.19 enlarged the attention receptive field to improve forecasting accuracy, although this required higher computational and memory resources. Ran et al.20 combined Transformer with empirical mode decomposition to improve feature extraction capability. In addition, Li et al.21 proposed the TS2ARCformer framework, which integrates hierarchical attention with autoregressive components to improve forecasting efficiency, but at the cost of increased architectural complexity.

Hybrid DL frameworks have also been investigated to address the limitations of single-structure models. Chen et al.22 proposed a Transformer–LSTM hybrid architecture to simultaneously capture global and sequential dependencies, whereas Guo et al.23 developed a CNN–LSTM framework with multi-modal attention for feature fusion. Although these hybrid approaches improve forecasting performance, they generally require more complex hyperparameter tuning and higher computational cost.

Although convolutional, recurrent, attention-based, and hybrid DL models have achieved considerable improvements in forecasting performance, increasing model depth and structural complexity may also introduce training instability, gradient degradation, and performance saturation. These challenges make stable optimization an important issue in deep forecasting models. In this context, DRNs have attracted attention because residual connections facilitate gradient propagation and enable the construction of deeper network architectures.

DRN-based models have shown strong potential for modeling complex nonlinear load patterns in STLF applications. Early studies mainly focused on validating the effectiveness of residual learning frameworks and comparing them with conventional DL models. Chen et al.25 combined residual learning with ensemble strategies to improve robustness and generalization performance, although this increased computational cost. Kondaiah et al.26 proposed a task-specific DRN architecture and demonstrated that customized residual structures can effectively model nonlinear load characteristics across different datasets. However, these studies mainly emphasized overall forecasting accuracy, while less attention was given to highly fluctuating or high-frequency load behavior.

To improve forecasting stability and generalization capability, several ensemble-enhanced DRN frameworks were subsequently developed. Xu et al.27 proposed an ensemble residual network (ERN) based on snapshot ensemble learning to improve robustness without independently training multiple models. Chen et al.28 further extended this approach by incorporating multi-scale feature extraction and snapshot ensemble strategies into a ResNet-based framework. Although ensemble-based DRN models generally provide better generalization performance, they often require higher computational resources, longer training time, and careful design of snapshot intervals, which may limit their practical deployment in real-time applications.

At the same time, several studies explored data-driven strategies to improve the learning stability of DRN-based models. Kondaiah et al.29 developed a Deep-ResNet framework with adaptive feature selection to capture important load characteristics under different operating conditions. Although this approach improved forecasting consistency, its performance remained sensitive to changes in data distribution, which may reduce its adaptability across different power systems. Structural modifications have also been introduced to improve feature representation capability. Sheng et al.30 proposed a convolutional residual network (CRN) by redesigning convolutional ResBlocks for high-resolution load forecasting. Liu et al.31 incorporated CNN modules into the feature extraction stage to strengthen feature learning, while Ding et al.32 integrated inception-inspired structures to support multi-scale feature extraction. Although these approaches improve representation capability, they also increase architectural complexity and make hyperparameter configuration more challenging.

In addition, several DRN-based studies incorporated recurrent or attention mechanisms to enhance temporal dependency modeling. Tian et al.33 combined residual learning with sequential modeling in a ResNetPlus–LSTM framework, whereas Li et al.34 introduced attention mechanisms to emphasize important temporal information. Sheng et al.35 further proposed a Residual LSTM Plus architecture integrating ResBlocks with recurrent layers. Despite the forecasting improvements achieved by these hybrid frameworks, combining residual, recurrent, and attention-based components substantially increases model depth and computational cost, which may limit scalability in large-scale or real-time STLF applications.

Besides architectural and ensemble-based improvements, previous DRN studies have also explored the integration of richer meteorological information at the input level. Liu et al.36 proposed the PCA-DRN framework, which applies PCA to combine multiple meteorological variables before model training. Although PCA-DRN reduces feature redundancy and input dimensionality, the use of linear transformation may reduce the interpretability of meteorological features.

Although these approaches have improved forecasting accuracy and feature representation capability, most existing studies mainly concentrate on architectural modifications and feature engineering. As a result, the role of training dynamics, particularly optimization behavior and convergence patterns, remains insufficiently explored in DRN-based STLF models. In particular, training-related factors such as epoch allocation and model update timing have rarely been analyzed systematically, even though they can substantially affect model convergence and generalization performance.

In multi-stage training frameworks such as snapshot ensemble learning, which have been incorporated into DRN-based STLF models to improve forecasting stability and generalization performance25, multiple model states are collected during a single training process to form an implicit ensemble. This strategy was introduced in early DRN-based STLF studies and later adopted in several residual learning frameworks42. In these approaches, snapshots are extracted at different stages of training according to a predefined schedule45. As a result, the allocation of training epochs directly affects snapshot extraction timing and may influence both convergence quality and ensemble diversity. Nevertheless, in most existing studies, epoch distribution and snapshot timing are usually selected empirically, while their effects on convergence behavior and forecasting performance are rarely investigated in detail.

In DL, the number of training epochs is an important factor affecting model optimization and generalization performance37. An epoch corresponds to one complete pass through the training dataset, and increasing the number of epochs allows model parameters to be updated progressively during training38. When the number of training epochs is insufficient, the model may fail to learn underlying data patterns effectively, resulting in underfitting. In contrast, excessive training may increase the risk of overfitting and reduce generalization capability39.

Besides the total number of epochs, the distribution of training epochs across different stages of the learning process may also influence optimization behavior40. Previous studies have shown that forecasting performance is sensitive to training duration, and an appropriate balance between insufficient and excessive training is necessary for stable model performance41. In multi-stage frameworks such as snapshot ensemble learning, epoch allocation determines the stages at which model snapshots are collected43. Snapshots extracted too early may correspond to undertrained models, whereas snapshots extracted too late may reduce ensemble diversity as different models gradually converge toward similar solutions44. Therefore, epoch allocation may substantially influence both convergence quality and generalization performance.

Nevertheless, most existing studies treat epoch configuration as a fixed training setting and rarely analyze its influence on model performance systematically. In DRN-based STLF models, where deep architectures and snapshot ensemble strategies are widely adopted, the effect of epoch allocation on training dynamics and forecasting performance has received relatively limited attention.

Therefore, this study investigates the influence of epoch allocation within a fixed multi-stage training framework. By keeping the number of training stages and optimization settings unchanged, the analysis focuses specifically on the effect of epoch distribution during training. The study further evaluates how snapshot extraction timing affects forecasting accuracy, convergence behavior, and model stability in DRN-based STLF models. To the authors’ knowledge, existing DRN-based STLF studies have rarely provided a detailed investigation of epoch allocation in snapshot ensemble training.

Methodology and experimental protocol

Datasets for experiments

Real-world power system data are often affected by issues such as incomplete records, missing values, noise interference, and heterogeneous data formats, all of which may significantly degrade forecasting accuracy if not properly addressed46. Therefore, effective data preprocessing is essential to ensure the robustness and reliability of STLF models47. To maintain consistency with prior activation function evaluations and enable a fair and systematic comparison, two representative real-world datasets are employed in this study, namely the ISO-NE dataset and the MyPJ dataset. The ISO-NE dataset corresponds to a temperate climate and spans from March 2003 to December 2006, whereas the MyPJ dataset represents a tropical climate and covers the period from January 2020 to December 2022. These datasets provide a comprehensive basis for evaluating model performance under diverse climatic conditions and load-driving mechanisms.

The ISO-NE dataset covers the New England region of the United States and includes hourly electricity load and temperature data. It exhibits pronounced seasonal and interannual variations, primarily driven by heating demand in winter and cooling demand in summer, and has been widely adopted as a benchmark in STLF research due to its high data quality and completeness. In contrast, the MyPJ dataset comprises hourly load data obtained from Tenaga Nasional Berhad’s Malaysian Grid System Operator, together with daily meteorological observations collected in Petaling Jaya by the Malaysian Meteorological Department. The weather variables include rainfall, mean temperature, minimum temperature, maximum temperature, mean wind speed, wind direction, and maximum wind speed. Compared with temperate regions, load patterns in this dataset show weaker seasonal variation, while short-term weather fluctuations play a more dominant role. A small proportion of missing meteorological values (approximately 0.59%) is addressed through linear interpolation, ensuring data continuity while preserving the underlying temporal characteristics.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, distinct load characteristics are observed across the two datasets under different environmental conditions. Exhibiting pronounced variability, the ISO-NE dataset shows load values typically ranging from 10,000 to 27,500 MW, reflecting strong seasonal fluctuations. In contrast, comparatively stable patterns are observed in the MyPJ dataset, where load values generally fall between 10,000 and 18,000 MW, with minimal seasonal variation. Such differences highlight the varying impact of climatic conditions on electricity demand, with consumption in temperate regions largely driven by seasonal factors, whereas in tropical regions, it is more strongly influenced by short-term weather dynamics.

Additional preprocessing procedures were applied to both datasets to ensure consistency during model training and evaluation. In particular, normalization was performed using statistical parameters calculated only from the training set to avoid information leakage. The same normalization parameters were subsequently applied to the test set. This strategy provides a more reliable evaluation of forecasting performance under practical operating conditions.

Method of study

This study employs two representative residual-based forecasting frameworks, namely the original DRN25 and PCA-DRN36, to investigate how training schedule design, particularly epoch allocation, influences training behavior and forecasting performance. Unlike previous DRN-based studies that mainly emphasize architectural modifications for improving forecasting accuracy, the present work focuses on the effect of training configuration while maintaining the same residual learning structure. The two frameworks provide a consistent basis for comparing different input representations and training strategies.

The architecture of the original DRN model for the ISO-NE dataset is illustrated in Fig. 2, while the DRN and PCA-DRN structures used for the MyPJ dataset are presented in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. The PCA-DRN framework is applied only to the MyPJ dataset because it contains multiple meteorological variables that may introduce redundancy in the input representation. In contrast, the ISO-NE dataset contains only a single weather-related variable, and therefore PCA-based dimensionality reduction is unnecessary. Despite differences in input processing, both frameworks share a similar overall structure consisting of two major components, namely a basic structure and a ResNetPlus module.

The input variables include historical load data, temporal indicators, and meteorological features. These inputs are first processed by the basic structure to generate preliminary feature representations of future electricity demand. The resulting representations are subsequently refined through the ResNetPlus module, which extends the conventional ResNet architecture using deep residual learning to improve 24-hour-ahead forecasting performance.

Although the original DRN framework demonstrates strong forecasting capability, it considers only a limited number of meteorological variables. In practical STLF applications, electricity demand is often influenced by multiple weather-related factors that may exhibit strong correlations and redundancy. To address this issue, the PCA-DRN framework extends the original DRN by incorporating additional meteorological variables together with PCA-based dimensionality reduction during feature preparation. This process reduces input redundancy while preserving the same residual learning structure.

For day-ahead forecasting, both DRN and PCA-DRN employ multiple hour-specific sub-models, where each sub-model predicts the load demand for a particular future hour. The outputs generated by previous sub-models are recursively fed back as inputs to capture short-term inter-hour dependencies. The final 24-hour forecasting profile is then obtained through the ResNetPlus module. In both frameworks, the Scaled Exponential Linear Unit (SELU) is used as the activation function for all hidden layers, while the output layer adopts a linear activation function.

In the original DRN architecture, day-ahead STLF is first performed through the basic structure, which consists of a sequence of fully connected (FC) layers for generating preliminary 24-hour-ahead load predictions. This component integrates historical load information from multiple time horizons to capture multi-scale temporal dependencies.

A modified DRN framework, referred to as PCA-DRN, is adopted for the MyPJ dataset to address the limitations associated with a limited set of meteorological inputs, where a PCA-based feature preprocessing scheme is introduced prior to model training to enhance the representation of weather-related information. Within this framework, the first component corresponding to the basic structure retains the same architectural design as the original DRN, while the representation of meteorological variables is redefined; instead of directly utilizing raw weather variables, PCA is applied to project multiple meteorological features into a lower-dimensional space, thereby reducing redundancy while preserving the dominant patterns that collectively account for of the cumulative variance. ResNetPlus serves as the second major component in both the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks. It is designed as an enhanced residual learning module based on the conventional ResNet architecture with several modifications for STLF tasks. The module consists of multiple stacked building units, where each unit contains a hidden layer with 20 SELU-activated neurons followed by a linear projection layer for residual aggregation and dimensional consistency. By stacking these units repeatedly, ResNetPlus forms a deep residual structure capable of progressively refining feature representations. In the adopted configuration, four units are grouped into a single ResBlock, and the overall framework contains ten stacked residual levels. Shortcut connections are incorporated throughout the network to support stable optimization and efficient gradient propagation. Compared with the standard ResNet structure, ResNetPlus reorganizes the internal block composition while retaining the original hyperparameter settings, enabling residual learning to be more suitable for DRN-based STLF applications.

A unified training objective is adopted in both the original DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks across all datasets to simultaneously preserve the structural consistency of daily load profiles and improve forecasting accuracy. Within the adopted modeling framework, training is performed using a multi-stage strategy that has been widely used in DRN-based STLF studies. In this approach, model states are collected at different stages of optimization, allowing the training process to retain both early-stage diversity and later-stage convergence characteristics. As a result, an implicit ensemble effect can be achieved within a single training procedure without independently training multiple models.

The allocation of training epochs directly influences the optimization process. It affects not only the convergence level of individual snapshot models but also the diversity among them. Snapshots extracted during early training stages may correspond to undertrained model states, whereas snapshots collected during later stages may become increasingly similar because of model convergence. Consequently, the effectiveness of the multi-stage training strategy depends on maintaining a balance between convergence quality and ensemble diversity.

Nevertheless, in most existing DRN-based studies, training schedules are usually selected empirically and treated as fixed training settings. The relationship between epoch allocation, optimization behavior, and forecasting performance has therefore received relatively limited attention, particularly under a unified experimental framework.

To address this issue, the present study treats epoch allocation as a controllable factor within the training process. By systematically adjusting the distribution of training epochs while keeping the model architecture and optimization mechanism unchanged, the effect of training dynamics can be examined more directly. This framework allows the influence of epoch allocation on convergence behavior, ensemble characteristics, and generalization performance in DRN-based STLF models to be evaluated systematically. Detailed training strategies and experimental settings are described in the following section.

Training configuration

This study utilizes real-world electricity load datasets from the ISO-NE and MyPJ systems, representing temperate and tropical climatic conditions, respectively. For the ISO-NE dataset, 24,888 samples from March 2003 to December 2005 are used for training, while 8760 samples from January 2006 to December 2006 are used for testing. For the MyPJ dataset, 17,544 samples from January 2020 to December 2021 are used for training, and 8760 samples from January 2022 to December 2022 are reserved for testing.

To evaluate the influence of training schedules on forecasting performance, the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks are implemented using the default architectural configurations reported in previous studies without additional structural modification. The snapshot ensemble strategy also follows the original DRN setting, where three snapshots are extracted at fixed 50-epoch intervals. Under this framework, multiple epoch allocation strategies are designed by varying only the starting epoch of the snapshot sequence, while keeping both the number of snapshots and the extraction interval unchanged. Specifically, ten training configurations (TC1–TC10) are constructed with starting epochs ranging from 100 to 1000. Among them, the configuration using snapshots at 600, 650, and 700 epochs corresponds to the default setting adopted in previous DRN and PCA-DRN studies and is therefore selected as the reference configuration. The remaining configurations are designed to investigate earlier and later snapshot extraction stages relative to this reference setting. In particular, TC10 extends the analysis to a more prolonged training stage to further examine model behavior under extended optimization. To ensure fair comparison among configurations, all other experimental settings are kept unchanged, including the model structure, input features, loss function, optimizer, learning rate, batch size, and normalization strategy. To provide benchmark comparisons, several widely used DL models for STLF are selected as baseline methods. These include convolution-based models such as CNN and TCN, the recurrent-based LSTM model, the attention-based Transformer model, and two representative hybrid frameworks, namely Transformer–LSTM22 and CNN–LSTM–MMA23. Together, these models represent the major DL paradigms commonly applied in STLF research. The purpose of introducing these baselines is not to perform a comprehensive comparison of network architectures, but to evaluate the effectiveness of the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks under different training schedule configurations. For fair comparison, all baseline models are trained using the same total number of epochs corresponding to the optimal epoch allocation identified for each DRN or PCA-DRN setting. Other training settings, including optimizer, learning rate, batch size, and data preprocessing procedures, are kept identical across all models. Under this experimental framework, the influence of training schedule design can be examined more directly while minimizing variations caused by inconsistent training settings. This setup also enables comparisons between residual-network-based models and representative DL approaches under a unified training protocol.

To maintain consistency across different baseline models, unified training settings are adopted wherever possible. The CNN baseline uses a one-dimensional convolutional (Conv1D) architecture with 32 filters and a kernel size of 3. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function and He normal initialization are employed, while all remaining settings follow the default configuration. The TCN baseline is constructed using stacked Conv1D layers with dilated causal convolutions. Dilation rates are set to 1, 2, 4, and 8 to capture temporal dependencies at different time scales. The activation function, kernel size, and number of filters are kept consistent with those used in the CNN model, while other parameters remain unchanged. For the recurrent baseline, the LSTM model is configured with 64 hidden units, with all other architectural and training settings retained as default. The Transformer baseline adopts a standard encoder-only architecture with an embedding dimension of 64. The model contains one encoder layer with eight attention heads and a feedforward network size of 2048. Positional encoding and dropout with a rate of 0.1 are implemented using standard settings. For the hybrid baselines, the Transformer–LSTM and CNN–LSTM–MMA models are implemented according to the architectural structures and parameter settings reported in their original studies.

During training, all models are optimized under the same training configuration. A mini-batch size of 32 is adopted, and the adaptive moment estimation (Adam) optimizer48 is employed, consistent with previous DRN-based STLF studies25. Model optimization is performed using the loss formulation defined in Eqs. (1)–(3), which has been adopted in both the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks to consider forecasting accuracy together with prediction range consistency. A snapshot ensemble learning strategy is further incorporated to improve generalization performance and reduce overfitting. Under the predefined epoch allocation settings, model states are saved at different training stages, and the final forecasting results are obtained by averaging the outputs of multiple snapshot models42. This strategy enables ensemble diversity to be introduced within a single training process without independently training multiple models49.

To evaluate the statistical significance of performance differences, a nonparametric bootstrap resampling procedure with 10,000 iterations is employed. Compared with the paired Student’s t-test, which assumes normally distributed paired differences, the bootstrap approach does not rely on distributional assumptions and is therefore more suitable for model comparison under uncertain data distributions50. Statistical significance is evaluated using two criteria. First, a performance difference is regarded as statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the mean difference lies entirely above zero. If the interval includes zero, the difference is considered statistically insignificant. Second, statistical significance is additionally supported when the bootstrap-derived p-value is smaller than 0.05. A reported value of p ≈ 0 indicates an extremely small probability, typically below 0.0001, rather than an exact zero.

All experiments are implemented using TensorFlow 2.10.0 and Keras 2.10.0 under a Python 3.8 environment. The experiments are conducted on a Lenovo laptop equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 6800 H CPU, 16 GB DDR5 4800 MHz RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Ti Laptop GPU with 4 GB memory.

Evaluation protocol

Consistent with previous DRN-based STLF studies5, the evaluation metrics used in this work are organized into three groups. Among these metrics, MAPE is selected as the primary evaluation criterion because of its widespread use and straightforward interpretation in STLF research. In addition, MAPE is used as the reference metric for model comparison and statistical analysis.

To provide a more comprehensive evaluation of forecasting performance, the selected metrics are classified according to their evaluation objectives. The first group includes point-forecast error metrics, namely MAPE and mean absolute error (MAE), which measure relative and absolute prediction errors. The second group contains squared-error metrics, including mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and normalized mean squared error (NMSE), which place greater emphasis on large forecasting deviations. The third group consists of correlation-based metrics, specifically the correlation coefficient (R) and the coefficient of determination (R²), which evaluate the consistency between predicted and actual load values.

In accordance with the previously defined evaluation framework, the performance metrics employed in this study are systematically divided into three categories. Included in the first category are point-forecast error metrics, specifically MAPE and MAE (see Eqs. (4) and (5)), where and denote the actual and predicted values of the -th sample, respectively, and represents the total number of samples. Through these metrics, the average relative and absolute deviations between predicted and observed values can be effectively quantified.

Forming the second category are squared-error-based metrics, including MSE, RMSE, and NMSE (as defined in Eqs. (6)-(8)), which place greater emphasis on error dispersion by assigning larger penalties to significant deviations. As a result, these metrics provide deeper insight into the variability of prediction errors. In particular, in Eq. (8) denotes the variance of the observed values.

Comprising the third category are correlation-based metrics, namely the R and the R², as defined in Eqs. (9) and (10). Here, and represent the mean values of the actual and predicted series, respectively. These measures evaluate the overall agreement and goodness-of-fit between predicted and actual load profiles.

Generally, higher values of R and indicate stronger correlation and better model fitting performance, whereas lower values of MAPE, MAE, MSE, RMSE, and NMSE correspond to higher prediction accuracy.

Analysis of PCA components and feature representation

To reduce redundancy among meteorological variables in the MyPJ dataset, PCA was applied before model training. A cumulative explained variance threshold of 90% was adopted to determine the number of retained components, resulting in the selection of five principal components. These components explain 94.12% of the total variance, indicating that most of the original meteorological information is preserved after dimensionality reduction. The first principal component explains 37.88% of the variance, followed by the second, third, fourth, and fifth components with contributions of 19.62%, 14.23%, 12.08%, and 8.54%, respectively. These results indicate that the major variability in the meteorological data can be represented within a lower-dimensional feature space.

The influence of the original meteorological variables on each retained component can be interpreted through their loading values. PCA component 1 is mainly associated with temperature-related variables, with loadings of 0.326146 for rainfall, − 0.579888 for mean temperature, − 0.503639 for minimum temperature, − 0.442409 for maximum temperature, − 0.293040 for mean wind speed, 0.105115 for maximum wind speed, and 0.105159 for maximum wind direction. In contrast, PCA component 2 is more strongly affected by precipitation and wind-related factors, with corresponding loadings of 0.442154, 0.082323, − 0.203754, 0.365551, 0.318846, 0.701837, and − 0.168348, respectively. PCA component 3 is dominated by maximum wind direction, whose loading reaches − 0.946270, substantially larger than those of the other variables. PCA component 4 reflects combined effects of wind speed, temperature, and rainfall, as indicated by loadings of 0.381735, 0.141974, 0.147226, 0.413782, − 0.792360, − 0.087986, and − 0.075194. Meanwhile, PCA component 5 captures additional rainfall–wind interactions under varying wind conditions, with corresponding loadings of 0.709699, 0.086094, 0.123478, − 0.051551, 0.387480, − 0.512842, and 0.240546.

Overall, the retained principal components preserve most of the important information contained in the original meteorological variables while reducing dimensionality and feature redundancy. At the same time, the transformed features still maintain a reasonable degree of physical interpretability. These PCA features are subsequently used as meteorological inputs in the PCA-DRN framework for the analysis of training schedules and forecasting performance.

In addition, PCA-DRN consistently outperforms DRN under identical training schedules, producing mean improvements of 0.001586 (p = 0.010400) and 0.002520 (p ≈ 0), respectively. In all comparisons, the corresponding 95% CIs remain fully above zero, further supporting the statistical significance of the observed improvements.

Overall, the statistical analysis indicates that the forecasting improvements observed in this study are unlikely to be caused by random variation alone. The results also show that both training schedule optimization and PCA-based feature representation contribute to statistically significant improvements in forecasting performance across different datasets.

Discussion

The results obtained in this study provide further insight into the role of training schedule design in DRN-based STLF models. While previous studies have mainly concentrated on architectural modifications, the present analysis shows that epoch allocation itself has a substantial influence on forecasting accuracy, convergence behavior, and generalization performance.

A noticeable observation is that the optimal training schedule differs across datasets with different temporal characteristics. For the ISO-NE dataset, which contains stronger seasonal regularity and smoother long-term trends, longer training durations together with later snapshot extraction stages produce better forecasting performance. This suggests that extended optimization allows the model to capture structured temporal dependencies more effectively. In contrast, the MyPJ dataset reaches its best performance at earlier training stages. Since this dataset exhibits stronger short-term fluctuations and weaker seasonal patterns, prolonged training increases sensitivity to noise and short-term variations, leading to performance degradation and overfitting. These results indicate that epoch allocation should be considered together with the statistical characteristics of the dataset rather than treated as a fixed training configuration.

The experimental results also reveal an important relationship between training schedule design and snapshot ensemble learning. Both insufficient and excessive training negatively affect forecasting performance, although for different reasons. Snapshots extracted during early training stages correspond to undertrained model states that have not fully captured the underlying load patterns. In contrast, snapshots extracted during very late stages become increasingly similar because of excessive convergence, which reduces ensemble diversity and weakens the benefit of snapshot-based learning. The non-monotonic performance fluctuations observed in several later-stage configurations further suggest that certain combinations of snapshots may still provide complementary forecasting information. However, this effect gradually diminishes as the training process continues.

The incorporation of PCA further improves forecasting performance and training stability. By reducing redundancy among meteorological variables, PCA provides a more compact feature representation, allowing the model to learn more efficiently during earlier training stages. At the same time, the reduced feature redundancy alleviates sensitivity to noise during prolonged training, which helps mitigate overfitting. Consequently, the PCA-DRN framework exhibits smoother performance variation across different epoch allocation settings. These observations suggest that feature representation and training dynamics are closely related, since the quality of the input representation directly influences how effectively the model utilizes the allocated training epochs.

From a practical perspective, the findings of this study suggest that training schedules should be selected according to dataset characteristics rather than determined empirically using fixed settings. For datasets with strong short-term variability, earlier convergence stages may provide better generalization performance, whereas datasets with stronger long-term regularity may benefit from extended training and later snapshot extraction. Such an adaptive strategy can improve forecasting performance without introducing additional architectural complexity.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the present analysis is conducted under a fixed snapshot ensemble framework with predefined snapshot intervals, and the interaction between epoch allocation and alternative snapshot extraction strategies is not investigated. Second, although PCA effectively reduces feature redundancy, it is still based on linear transformation and may not fully capture nonlinear relationships among meteorological variables. Future work may therefore explore nonlinear feature extraction approaches, such as autoencoder-based methods, together with adaptive training schedules that dynamically adjust epoch allocation during training.

Overall, the present study shows that training schedule design is an important factor in DRN-based STLF models and should not be treated as a secondary training setting. By systematically examining the influence of epoch allocation across datasets with different temporal characteristics, this work provides additional insight into the relationship between training dynamics, feature representation, and forecasting performance in residual-network-based STLF frameworks.

Conclusion

This study investigated the influence of training schedule design on STLF performance within the DRN framework. Unlike previous studies that mainly focused on architectural modifications or feature engineering, the present work examined the effect of training dynamics, particularly epoch allocation, under a unified multi-stage snapshot ensemble framework.

Experimental results obtained from the ISO-NE and MyPJ datasets show that training schedule design has a clear influence on forecasting accuracy, convergence behavior, and generalization performance. The results also indicate that the optimal epoch allocation depends strongly on dataset characteristics. For the ISO-NE dataset, which contains stronger seasonal regularity and smoother temporal variations, extended training together with later snapshot extraction stages improves forecasting performance. In contrast, the MyPJ dataset reaches its best performance at earlier training stages because prolonged training increases sensitivity to short-term fluctuations and overfitting.

The analysis further shows that the effectiveness of snapshot ensemble learning depends on maintaining a balance between convergence quality and ensemble diversity. Snapshots extracted during early training stages correspond to undertrained model states, whereas snapshots extracted during later stages gradually converge toward similar solutions, reducing ensemble diversity. These observations indicate that suitable epoch allocation is important for achieving stable forecasting performance in snapshot-based DRN training.

The incorporation of PCA-based feature representation further improves forecasting performance and training stability on the MyPJ dataset. By reducing redundancy among meteorological variables, PCA improves feature compactness during early training stages and alleviates overfitting during extended training. The results therefore suggest that feature representation and training dynamics jointly influence the learning behavior of DRN-based STLF models.

Overall, the findings of this study indicate that training schedule design should be considered an important component of DRN-based STLF frameworks rather than treated as a fixed training setting. Dataset-adaptive epoch allocation strategies can improve forecasting performance without increasing model complexity.

Several limitations should also be noted. The present study considers fixed snapshot intervals and does not investigate adaptive or dynamic training schedules. In addition, the PCA-based feature transformation remains a linear method and may not fully capture nonlinear relationships among meteorological variables. Future work may therefore explore adaptive snapshot strategies, dynamic epoch allocation mechanisms, and nonlinear feature extraction methods. Extending the analysis to other important hyperparameters, including model architecture settings and optimization configurations, may also provide more comprehensive guidance for model selection and reduce the need for extensive empirical tuning.
