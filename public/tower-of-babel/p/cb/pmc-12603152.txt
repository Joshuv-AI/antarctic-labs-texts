Submicroscopic infections and two malaria vectors are major contributors to residual malaria in an Amazonian village in Peru
Authors: Mitchel Guzmán-Guzmán, Marlon P Saavedra, Pamela Rodriguez, Carlos Acosta, Joaquin Gomez, Hugo O Valdivia, Juan F Sanchez, Macarena Vittet, Edgar Manrique, Manuela Herrera-Varela, Juliana Morales, Sara A Bickersmith, Joseph M Vinetz, Dionicia Gamboa, Jan E Conn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the Loreto region of Amazonian Peru, the primary malaria vector is Nyssorhynchus (or Anopheles) darlingi. The present year-long study sought to identify specific landscapes with the greatest risk of Plasmodium transmission, incorporating epidemiological data, landscape stratification models, satellite imagery, and vector biology. A monthly longitudinal cohort study and population screening of all residents of the focal village Santa Rita detected, among 353 inhabitants enrolled, a ratio of 17:1 submicroscopic/ microscopic malaria infections. Microscopy-detected human infections were seasonal, generally peaking with rising river levels and increasing rainfall, but submicroscopic infections, detected by qPCR, were perennial. Despite high mosquito net usage, inhabitants with outdoor occupations had significantly higher P. vivax infection rates compared with those with indoor occupations or those not working (35% vs. 21%, p = 0.012). A spatial grid subdivided the study area into anopheline mosquito sampling units where mosquitoes were collected monthly. Plasmodium-infected specimens of Ny. darlingi were detected in each of the 5 landscapes that were determined by a cluster analysis. A putative secondary vector, Anopheles near costai G1, was infected with Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium malariae, reported herein for the first time in Peru. Introduction

Despite the deployment of standard vector interventions [indoor residual spray (IRS); long-lasting insecticide-treated bednets (LLINs)]1 and an overall reduction in malaria burden, this arthropod-borne disease remains a significant global public health threat. In Peru, the National Malaria Control Program (PNMCP), strengthened by the success of an 85% reduction in malaria cases between 2017 and 2020 in the Loreto region, where 90% of the country’s cases are registered, has advocated a new proposal, the “Plan Towards the Elimination of Malaria in Peru” (PTEMP)2. This new integrated effort builds on the growing interest in the elimination phase to combat the remaining malaria transmission pockets (residual malaria) in Loreto and other regions in Peru reporting cases from 2011 to 2020, with an emphasis on districts that reported malaria cases from 2018 to 2020.

PTEMP prioritizes interventions in districts and communities based on the Annual Parasite Index (API; number of cases per year divided by the population at risk per 1,000 inhabitants), and the average number of cases based on microscopic surveillance data2. However, several low-prevalence Amazonian communities have a high proportion of asymptomatic (73%) and submicroscopic (68–74%) infections, with up to two-thirds of Plasmodium vivax infections carrying gametocytes3–5. Seasonal human migration within and between regional river systems is common; some travelers, albeit generally asymptomatic, are hypothesized to contribute to the regional maintenance of elevated malaria transmission levels6,7. Revealingly, connectivity among geographically distant transmission zones of P. vivax in the Peruvian Amazon was detected8,9 that will further complicate efforts to eliminate malaria. Spatial and temporal transmission heterogeneity stems from sociodemographic characteristics that include housing, occupation, mosquito net use, among others; entomological dynamics e.g., mosquito species, behavior, abundance, and vector competence; and environmental factors: local land-cover, river levels, and rainfall10–12.

Dependent on host availability and the environment13,14, behavior of the predominant malaria vector, Nyssorhynchus darlingi15 is plastic and anthropophilic-opportunistic, generally exophagic and exophilic, with variable biting times16. These characteristics limit the expected efficacy of LLINs and IRS12,17. Of several relatively abundant anopheline species in Amazonian Peru12,18, only Nyssorhynchus benarrochi B and Nyssorhynchus dunhami have been detected infected with human Plasmodium in Amazonian Peru; both are considered secondary/seasonal malaria vectors19–21.

Nyssorhynchus darlingi, highly adapted to anthropogenic landscapes, is often the most abundant mosquito in malaria endemic villages in the Peruvian Amazon12,15,22. Many collections of Ny. darlingi have focused inside houses and in peridomestic landscapes (within ~ 10 m of main house entrances), but this species can be locally abundant in occupation-based landscapes (logging camps) along rivers23, and in forest fringe habitat24,25. As the influence of micro-environmental effects on the spatial and temporal distribution of vector mosquitoes has become more apparent26,27, and fine-scale satellite imagery more available28, we took advantage of a planned epidemiological study of a malaria endemic riverine village in the Peruvian Amazon, Santa Rita, to investigate the mosquito species in the surrounding landscapes. Based on available evidence, we expected Ny. darlingi to be the most abundant mosquito in peridomestic and degraded forest patches, and the major mosquito involved in Plasmodium transmission. Key questions were: (1) Are submicroscopic infections seasonal? (2) Where (time and space) are the gaps in transmission protection when a village experiences high net coverage? (3) Is Ny. darlingi ubiquitous in every landscape type surrounding a village? (4) Are additional potential vectors involved in transmission? and (5) Can we pinpoint the most pathogenic landscapes by combining epidemiological and entomological data?

Materials and methods

Study area

In the Peruvian Amazon, we selected the village of Santa Rita (STR), situated along the banks of the Nanay River, a tributary of the Amazon River, located 500 m from the river during the low-water season (between August and October). STR is 10 km from Iquitos and is only accessible by boat along the Nanay River (Fig. 1). STR shares sociodemographic and epidemiological characteristics within the network of villages of the San Jose de Lupuna community (comprising Santa Rita, Lupuna, and San Pedro) in the lower Nanay river basin3. The villages are separated by approximately one km of partially degraded forest. The main occupation of adult villagers is that of farmer, followed by housewife. Villagers live in households with wooden walls, palm leaf or calamine roofs, and dirt floors; they do not have access to electricity, water for consumption is obtained from springs, and excreta disposal is in open field and latrines10,12. Microscopy-detected malaria is seasonal, demonstrated by an annual rise in cases between April and June10, concurrent with the highest levels of the Nanay river (March to May); Ny. darlingi is the main regional vector12.

Study design: sociodemographic and epidemiological data

All households in the community were georeferenced prior to initiating the study and then monthly surveys were conducted from February 2021 to January 2022. During the first survey, a trained team member obtained informed consent/assent from each participant aged 3 months and older prior to initiating the procedures described below. Each household member above 12 years old was asked to answer a structured questionnaire about sociodemographic, clinical, epidemiological, mosquito exposure behavior, and mobility information, that was administered by trained study personnel during household visits. For children under 12 years, an adult household member responded on their behalf. Next, a technician measured the temperature and took a blood sample by fingerpick for a thin and thick blood smear for immediate microscopy assessment in the field, then collected 500 µL in an EDTA microtube for subsequent qPCR assay. Each household head completed a questionnaire of household characteristics, possession of bed nets and provided information about children under 12 years of age. In the case of absence of any member or the whole family during the first and the following eleven surveys, households were revisited up to two more times on a different day and time within a range of 3 days, before being declared as absentee inhabitant and/or empty house.

Mosquito sampling approach

We employed landscape stratification models utilizing satellite imagery to acquire detailed information about the multispectral profile of STR. This process involved the generation of sampling units, wherein sampling points were strategically located and distributed based on disparities in their spectral profiles and vegetation indices. The satellite images came from Sentinel-2 MSI: MultiSpectral Instrument, Level-2 A, accessed through Google Earth Engine. The vegetation indices derived from these images encompass the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), and Soil Adjusted Vegetation Index (SAVI).

We divided our study area using a Hexagonal Spatial Grid, consisting of a total of 123 regular hexagons, each covering 16,000 m2. Each hexagon, excluding those located at village boundaries, had six neighbors, and the centroids were equidistant, at approximately 135 m. Using clustering analysis, we determined that the optimal number of clusters (k), or sampling units, for our study area was five (Fig. 2), and a visual inspection of satellite imagery was conducted to identify the specific land cover associated with each cluster. Clusters 1 and 5 depict landscapes of rural households, with 23 and 9 hexagons, respectively. Forested areas are identified as Clusters 2 and 4, located in proximity, consisting of 42 and 26 hexagons, respectively. Cluster 3 (crops) comprises 23 hexagons. Clusters were labeled K1 to K5 and represent distinct land cover categories: K1: household landscapes, K2: forested areas, K3: crops, K4: degraded forest patches, and K5, seasonally flood-prone areas.

A total of 20 sampling points were randomly selected each month and distributed proportionally among the five clusters as follows: K1: 3, K2: 7, K3: 4, K4: 4, and K5: 2 points, respectively. This methodology for selecting sampling points was designed to systematically identify mosquito collection sites based on landscape characteristics (forested and flood-prone areas) and land use patterns (cultivated plots and households) in STR and its environs.

Mosquito sampling

Twelve monthly mosquito collections were conducted between February 2021 and January 2022. Each collection was conducted for four nights/month at five points each night of the 20 previously selected sampling points. No indoor collections were performed during this study. The Human Landing Catch (HLC) method was employed for a period of 12 h (18:00 to 06:00 h) at each sampling point. Each sampling point (= one hexagon) was sampled by one person from 18:00–12:00 and then a second person from 12:00–06:00, to reduce collector bias. Consequently, 2,880 observations were obtained during this period. Mosquitoes were identified morphologically in the field by trained personnel using existing taxonomic keys29,30. Each mosquito was then preserved individually in a microcentrifuge tube containing silica gel at -20 °C in the Iquitos laboratory.

Laboratory analysis: microscopy

Thick and thin blood smears for microscopic diagnosis were stained with 10% Giemsa solution and diagnosis was done immediately in situ. A blood slide was declared to be negative for the presence of parasites if parasites were not identified after observing 100 microscopic fields. Parasite density in positive blood smears was calculated assuming a concentration of 8,000 white blood cells (WBC) per microliter31.

DNA extraction and qPCR assays in human blood

Genomic DNA was extracted from red blood cells with EDTA using the QIAmp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). We used 200 µL of the sample and followed the protocol provided by the manufacturer; 70 µL of the extracted DNA was kept at -20 °C for subsequent analyses.

Real-time PCR was conducted targeting the cytochrome oxidase III (COX-III) mitochondrial gene (216 bp) for the detection of Plasmodium species DNA, using SYBR Green. The reaction consisted of 0.2 µM of each primer (Forward COXIIIF: 5’-CGGTAGATAGGGAACAAACT-3’ and Reverse COXIIIR: 5’-CTTTGCCTGGAGGTTACG-3’), 12.5 µL PerfeCTa® SYBR® Green FastMix®, 5 µL of DNA and 6.5 µL PCR-grade water per reaction. Reactions were performed in a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s and 68 °C for 15 s, and a final extension at 70 °C for 3 min. The final step consisted of a melting curve from 70 °C to 90 °C with an increase of 0.5 °C/s and up for a total of 5 s.

A second real-time PCR was conducted to differentiate the Plasmodium species by targeting the small rRNA subunit (18 S) of P. vivax and P. falciparum. The protocol by Rougemont et al.32 was followed, with slight modifications. Two separate reactions were performed, both utilizing the same primers (Plasmo 1 and Plasmo 2), with different P. vivax and P. falciparum species-specific probes, to detect each species. The DNA sequences of the primers and probes were previously described by Moreno et al.12. The reaction consisted of 5 µL of DNA mixed with 0.1 µM of each primer and 0.08 µM of species-specific probes added in separate reactions (P. vivax and P. falciparum probes respectively), followed by adding 10 µL TaqMan Environmental Master Mix 2.0 (Applied Biosystems, USA), and 4.44 µL PCR-grade water per reaction. Reactions were performed in a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min and amplification for 45 cycles at 95 °C of 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min3.

Circumsporozoite protein ELISA assays

The analysis of the primary regional vector, Ny. darlingi collected in STR (2021–2022) involved the examination of the head-thoraces33 within a pool comprising one to seven individuals of a single species collected on the same date, at the same hour. Following the ELISA protocol outlined by Wirtz et al.34, we detected circumsporozoite protein (CSP) of each of P. falciparum and P. vivax, specifically targeting Pv210 and Pv247. The P. falciparum sporozoite ELISA kit, MRA-890, and the P. vivax sporozoite ELISA kit, MRA-1028 K, were obtained through BEI Resources, NIAID, NIH, contributed by Robert A. Wirtz. Negative control specimens consisted of Ny. darlingi females reared in the Iquitos laboratory. Optical density at 410 nm was measured 60 min after substrate addition using a Bio-Rad ELISA plate reader. The determination of the positivity cutoff relied on the mean optical density (OD) value derived from seven negative controls on each plate.

DNA extraction and PCR assays of mosquito specimens other than Ny. darlingi

Whole mosquito samples morphologically identified as Anopheles spp. or Nyssorhynchus spp. collected monthly from February 2021 – January 2022 in STR were sent to the Wadsworth Center (New York State Department of Health) Vector Biology & Population Genetics laboratory for molecular species identification and rtPCR Plasmodium testing. DNA was extracted with DNeasy Blood & Tissue kits (Qiagen, Germany) individually (whole mosquito).

Individual mosquitoes were identified molecularly for species confirmation using a PCR of the ribosomal internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) region35, followed by a double-digest restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP)36. The cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) barcode region37,38 was amplified for mosquito samples or subsamples that could not be identified by the ITS2-PCR-RFLP patterns and sent for Sanger sequencing in the forward direction only, at the Advanced Genomic Technologies Core (Wadsworth Center, New York State Department of Health). Raw sequences were cleaned, edited, checked for stop codons and pseudogenes, and queried for species match with NCBI BLASTn using Geneious Prime 2020.2.4 (www.geneious.com).

For Plasmodium testing, samples of potential vectors (such as Ny. dunhami, Ny. benarrochi B) and subsequently of mosquitoes initially identified as Anopheles forattinii/near costai (because they were the second most abundant species after Ny. darlingi) were tested with monoplex and triplex real-time PCR assays as in Bickersmith et al.39 in duplicate on a StepOnePlus real-time PCR instrument (ThermoFisher). 18S rRNA PCR products of Plasmodium amplified from samples in which we could not detect the Plasmodium species based on the rtPCR assays were Sanger sequenced in both directions (primers40 UNR and PLF), and queried for a species match as with COI sequences.

For both detection protocols, sporozoite rates were calculated by dividing the number of Plasmodium-positive mosquitoes by the total number of mosquitoes tested. The Human Biting Rate (HBR) was calculated as the mean number of mosquitoes collected by HLC per person per night. The Monthly Entomological Inoculation Rate (EIR) was determined by multiplying the HBR by the proportion of Plasmodium-infected specimens for each month.

Data analysis

Epidemiological and clinical data

In STR, all participants were enrolled in multiple surveys aimed at assessing sociodemographic and housing characteristics. For the molecular diagnostic tests, study participants with 10 to 12 follow-ups were selected, resulting in a sample size of 109 individuals. Submicroscopic infections were defined as those qPCR (+) and microscopy (-); in contrast, microscopic infections were defined as those qPCR (+) and microscopy (+), both regardless of the presence of symptoms. The overall submicroscopic/microscopic malaria infection ratio was estimated for the overall number of submicroscopic and microscopic infections in the subgroup of participants who had at least 10 follow-ups.

Malaria cases detected from 2020 to 2022 in the periods when the present study was not in progress were obtained from the epidemiological report of the Ministry of Health (MoH). Notably, malaria surveillance activities performed by MoH in STR consist of passive and reactive case detection, with microscopy diagnosis as the principal diagnostic tool.

Human behavior

To analyze the proportion of individuals outside their homes, indoors, and sleeping on an hourly basis (18:00 to 06:00 h) during the dry and wet seasons, a bar chart was constructed. Each bar was augmented with the corresponding number of Ny. darlingi mosquitoes collected at the same hour as the individual (human) was analyzed. A chi-square test was employed to examine variation in the proportions of individuals (outdoors, awake indoors, and asleep) between seasons. An independent samples Student’s t-test was conducted to compare the mean proportion of time individuals spent outdoors between 18:00 and 22:00 h among those who tested PCR-positive and PCR-negative for Plasmodium vivax. Some fluctuations in the number of individuals assessed by microscopy were due to temporary logistical constraints, including participant unavailability and climate-related access limitations.

Entomological data

Descriptive analysis was conducted, showcasing the number of mosquitoes from the most representative species during the dry and wet seasons, as well as monthly. To compare the total mosquito count per month, the Scott-Knott test was employed. This test offers the advantage of avoiding result overlap in clustering, particularly when dealing with many comparisons. For comparing the total mosquito count per cluster within the dry and wet seasons, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized. Variation in the percent of Ny. darlingi and the second most-abundant species, Anopheles forattinii/near costai between the dry and wet seasons was analyzed by a two-sided chi-square test.

To obtain the Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR) of anopheline species, a Poisson GLM was applied using count data of the number of mosquitoes to compare seasons (rainy, dry) and clusters (K1-K5). For the latter analysis, we combined the number of infected Ny. darlingi (determined by ELISA; n = 21) and the number of infected An. forattinii/near costai (determined by real time PCR; n = 10). Calculations were based on the conservative assumption of n = 1 infected specimen per pool17.

Nyssorhynchus darlingi parity rates

To evaluate the distribution of female age within the population, a subset of females underwent dissection (~ 10%) to determine parity rates per hour41,42. An important consideration in this calculation is the challenge of accurately estimating the length of the gonotrophic cycle. We assumed that the initial oviposition requires two or more blood meals, and that the temporal feeding pattern is irregular. Consequently, we followed the calculation approach proposed by Garret-Jones and Grab43. Several studies have provided estimates for the gonotrophic cycle of Ny. darlingi, ranging from 2 ± 3 days44,45. More recently, it was determined to be 2.19 days during the wet season and 2.43 days during the dry season45. In our study, calculations were based on the 2.19-day estimate, consistent with the timing of our Ny. darlingi collections (most were collected during the wet season). To determine whether there were differences of daily survival and age of mosquitoes by season, we used with the Studebaker test.

Spatial analysis

Two maps were generated based on drone imagery for each of the dry and wet seasons, illustrating the number of individual inhabitants with a positive PCR diagnosis per household. Each map incorporated its respective kernel density estimation, with the advantage of interpolating values in unsampled locations, thereby facilitating the identification of geographical concentrations and the localization of hotspots on a map. Additionally, the total number of mosquitoes collected in each of the sampled landscapes (K1-K5) was documented. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R v.4.4.1 software (R Development Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with a significance level of 0.05.

Ethical approval

The research protocol for the human individuals in the study and the protocol for the entomological study were both approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (Sistema Descentralizado de Información y Seguimiento a la Investigación [SIDISI] codes 101518 and 101516, respectively). The approved protocols were sent to the Regional Health Directorate of Loreto for registration and authorization to conduct (85-2018 and 111–2018, respectively). Written informed consent was obtained individually for each adult participant and written informed assent with parental consent was required for minors. In the case of illiterate adults, informed consent was obtained in the presence of an adult family member or a community health worker. Human participants’ names and other identifiers were not included in any part of this manuscript or supplementary material. Human molecular testing was also approved by the Research Administration Program of US Naval Medical Research Unit (NAMRU) SOUTH (NAMRU6.2019.0008). Mosquito collectors were required to provide written informed consent prior to performing HLC. All methods were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations as specified by the appropriate participating institutions.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

We enrolled 353 individuals living in 83 households in the study from February 2021 through January 2022, with an average household size of 4.3 individuals (range (1–16), IQR (3–5)). Descriptive sociodemographic characteristics of the individuals included in the study are found in Supplementary Table S1 that presents in column 1, all survey participants, and in column 2, only those who had 10 or more follow-up visits. These data demonstrate a female-to-male ratio of 0.98, and that most participants were under 30 years old (median age: 28.7). Regarding education, 36.5% of the STR inhabitants had completed secondary school or were currently pursuing it and 5.1% had completed or were currently pursuing a degree. Occupationally, 28.9% of the inhabitants worked in indoor activities and 24.4% in outdoor related activities.

Using the STR census data from 2021 (383 inhabitants) and the number of reported malaria cases (https://datosabiertos.gob.pe/dataset/vigilancia-epidemiol%C3%B3gica-de-malaria) in 2021 (n = 11) and 2022 (n = 35), the APIs were 23 and 91, respectively. For context, the human census and APIs for the other two villages of the San Jose de Lupuna community (San Jose and San Pedro) are included (Supplementary Table S2).

The epidemiological survey conducted in STR (Supplementary Table S1) revealed that the ownership rate of any type of mosquito net (raw cotton, nylon, or insecticide-treated nets) among all participants ranged from 99.7% to 100%. Among the 109 individuals who completed between 10 and 12 follow-up visits, 89% reported always using a mosquito net before sleeping, while the remaining 11% used it on more than half of the occasions. No cases were reported of individuals who never used a mosquito net, and despite the high historical prevalence of malaria (more than 80% of the population has had malaria in their lifetime), only 5.4% of individuals had malaria diagnosed by microscopy in the previous year.

The household characteristics of the study participants who completed the questionnaire (n = 83), are presented in Supplementary Table S3a. Most individuals resided in houses constructed with wooden walls (87%), dirt floors (61%), and tin roofs (99%). Most inhabitants lack access to essential public services, most households lacked access to electricity at home, unless equipped with a power generator that provided a limited supply. The primary sources of drinking and cooking water were springs and river water. Sanitation facilities were inadequate, with many participants relying on pit latrines or other unsanitary practices. The principal fuel source for cooking was firewood (89%).

A bivariate analysis, conducted only on those participants with 10–12 follow ups, for whom PCR P. vivax status was recorded, in Supplementary Table S3b, found that individuals engaged in outdoor-related occupations had significantly higher P. vivax infection rates compared to those with indoor occupations or who were not working (35% vs. 21%, p = 0.012). Adults aged 30–50 and over 50 years were more frequently infected than children under 15 (p = 0.006). Poor housing conditions were also associated with infection: participants living in dwellings with unfinished or wood-based flooring had higher infection rates than those with cement floors (p = 0.004). Likewise, inadequate sanitation facilities were linked to increased prevalence, with 63% of infected individuals residing in homes lacking basic sanitation (p = 0.004).

Rate ratios from a Poisson regression, with variable selection performed using a stepwise procedure based on the Akaike Information, detected several additional factors, independently associated with PCR-detected P. vivax infection (Supplementary Table S3c). Female participants had a lower incidence rate compared to males (IRR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.39–1.00; p = 0.049). The risk of infection increased with age, particularly among individuals aged 30–50 (IRR = 3.19; 95% CI: 1.66–6.19; p < 0.001) and those over 50 (IRR = 2.50; 95% CI: 1.35–4.64; p = 0.004). Outdoor-related occupations (IRR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.33–3.98; p = 0.003), migration (IRR = 0.31; 95% CI: 0.13–0.76; p = 0.003), and inadequate sanitation (IRR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.28–0.80; p = 0.006) were also significant. Living in a fully enclosed house strongly reduced infection risk (IRR = 0.02; 95% CI: 0.00–0.15; p < 0.001). Participants using river or rainwater as their main source had lower infection rates (IRR = 0.03; 95% CI: 0.02–0.20; p < 0.001). A non-significant trend was observed for increased risk in those with wood-pona flooring (p = 0.091).

Prevalence of malaria infection in the study population

Malaria in STR is seasonal, with cases increasing during the months when the river reaches its peak. Additionally, in the months with the lowest river levels (August-October), there is another, often smaller, increase in cases detected by microscopy (Fig. 3), possibly related to the formation of mosquito breeding sites, i.e., pools of water left behind as the river recedes.

Malaria cases by microscopy and PCR in Santa Rita, Peru, from 2020–2022. The bright blue line represents the monthly level of the Amazon River at the mouth of the Nanay River. Dark blue histograms represent microscopy results from the present study; light blue are those detected by the Ministry of Health (see text). Molecular diagnostic tests (green histograms) were performed only for participants with 10 to 12 months of follow-up.

Malaria prevalence diagnosed by microscopy in our surveys was less than 1%, with cases reported in April 2021 and between September 2021 and January 2022. In contrast, the prevalence of qPCR detected cases was 5x higher in the months of microscopy detected malaria infections. Furthermore, malaria infections were detected during every survey, using qPCR (Table 1). The PCR diagnosis was conducted at the conclusion of the 12-month study period only in those individuals with 10 to 12 months of follow-up. A total of 1201 tests in 109 individuals were performed by qPCR, resulting in the detection of 88 Plasmodium vivax infections, two Plasmodium falciparum infections, and no mixed infections. Of these, microscopy detected four P. vivax infections and one P. falciparum infection, yielding a ratio of 17:1 submicroscopic/microscopic malaria infections. In the period from February 2021 to December 2022, 31% (34/109) of the individuals tested had experienced at least one P. vivax infection. Of these, 88% (30/34) were not detectable by microscopy throughout the entire study period. Notably, 32.4% of PCR-positive individuals had more than one positive result over the 12-month follow-up period, indicating potential persistence of low-density infections or repeated exposure to infectious mosquito bites.

The Scott-Knott clustering method was used to evaluate the average number of mosquitoes per cluster across months. Similar to Tukey’s test, this method has the advantage of not producing intermediate or overlapping groups, making it easier to identify distinct groupings. Using a boxplot analysis, results from the Scott-Knott test showed a marked decline in PCR-detected P. vivax infections across all transmission intensity groups starting in June 2021. During the high transmission period (Feb–May 2021), infection counts were notably higher in the high and medium transmission groups, with several extreme values. In contrast, from June to November 2021, the low transmission season, cases were nearly absent across all groups. A moderate resurgence was observed in December 2021 and January 2022, particularly in the medium group (Supplementary Figure S1).

Temporally (by month) Ny. darlingi abundance was compared with PCR-detected P. vivax infections (malaria prevalence) using a Pearson correlation analysis; results were not significant (Pearson’s r = 0.14, p = 0.653) (Supplementary Figure S2).

Mosquito species composition and molecular identification

A total of 14,842 female anopheline mosquitoes were collected. Nyssorhynchus darlingi was the most abundant species (10,994 individuals; 74% of the total). During the rainy months (December-June), 9,142 (61.6%) Ny. darlingi were collected and at the initial increase in the river level (December-February) 1,842 (21.1%) Ny. darlingi were collected. April and May exhibited the highest abundance of anophelines, coinciding with the peaks of the Nanay river. The second most abundant species, An. forattinii/near costai (2,583 individuals; 17% of the total), was collected mainly during the rainy season although unexpectedly, during July (dry season), it was the most abundant mosquito species collected (Fig. 4a, b). The results of the COI sequence analysis of a subsample of 25 individual An. forattinii/near costai together with Anopheles COI sequences from GenBank, identified An. near costai G1 (24 of 25) and An. near costai G2 (1/25). A comparison of the An. near costai G1 consensus sequence with the single An. near costai G2 sequence identified a 4.5% pairwise difference.

The analysis of captured anophelines shows that Ny. darlingi was the most abundant species overall, in all clusters (over 50%; Fig. 5) and in both seasons (76% in wet, 50% in dry; Fig. 6). The results of the chi-square test of percent abundance of Ny. darlingi (76% rainy, 50% dry) and An. near costai G1 (16% rainy, 36% dry) of all mosquitoes collected by season, was highly significant (chi-square = 12.87, p = 0.0003). During the rainy season (December-June), the clusters with the highest abundance were K2 (31%) and K4 (27%), corresponding to forests and degraded forest patches, respectively. Additionally, these clusters recorded a higher species composition, including An. near costai G1, Ny. dunhami, Ny. triannulatus, Ny. oswaldoi, Ny. benarrochi B, Ny. rangeli, and other species of the genus Anopheles, compared with the remaining landscapes (Fig. 5). Clusters K3 (21%) and K2 (14%), representing cropland and household landscapes respectively, recorded lower abundances of anophelines but were higher than flood-prone areas (K5: 7%), which had both low abundance and species composition. During the dry season (June-November), abundances decreased in all clusters.

The abundance of mosquitoes is directly correlated with seasonality; during the wet season (December-June), the highest abundances (71%) and species composition (17 in total) were recorded, whereas in the dry season, both measures decreased (29%, 13 species) (Fig. 6). Nyssorhynchus darlingi was the most abundant species in each season, followed by An. near costai G1. Other species such as Ny. dunhami, Ny. triannulatus, Ny. oswaldoi s.l., and Ny. benarrochi B were also collected in both seasons, but in greater abundance during the rainy season.

The monthly Human Biting Rate (HBR) of Ny. darlingi varies greatly. April and May recorded the highest peaks, with 650 and 674 bites/person/night (bpn), respectively, unlike the months of August and September, which registered the lowest rates, 11 and 9 bpn, respectively (Table 2). In December, a rate of 526 bpn was recorded, decreasing to 130 bpn in January. This may be attributed to the rising river level in December followed by a decrease in January, affecting breeding sites and consequently adult mosquito abundance. The second most abundant species, An. near costai G1, is highest between April and May (HBR of 528 and 571 respectively), and almost disappears in August and September (HBR of 8 and 5, respectively) (Table 2).

Parity rate and longevity of Ny. darlingi

A total of 1,331 Ny. darlingi females were dissected (7% of those collected), with a monthly mean parous rate of 64.3% (range 51.3 ± 81.1). No significant differences were found between months, although the highest parity rates (81%) were in May and August, whereas the lowest rates were in December and January (48.7% and 52.5%, respectively). For nulliparous females, the rate was 32.8% (range 18.9 ± 52.5), with the lowest monthly mean rate in August (19%) and the highest in January (52.5%). No significant differences were found between wet and dry months. The age of Ny. darlingi populations ranged from 2.94 to 11.61 days. Daily survival rates ranged between 71 and 92% (mean of 81.9%), indicating a high probability of daily survival for this species. Ovary dissections were not performed in October and November due to the low quantity of collected mosquitoes (Table 4).

Sleeping behavior and mosquito abundance

In STR, 40% of Ny. darlingi mosquitoes were captured outdoors between 18:00 and 22:00 h, when people were still outdoors or were indoors but awake. The majority of infected Ny. darlingi mosquitoes (81%) were captured between 18:00 and 00:00 h, when many people were unprotected by mosquito nets (Fig. 7). The chi-square test was used to compare the distribution of individuals across three activity states (outdoors, awake indoors, and asleep) between the rainy and dry seasons at specific time intervals. Results indicate that, at the onset of the night (18:00–21:00 h), the proportion of individuals who were awake indoors and/or outdoors was significantly higher during the rainy season compared to the dry season. In contrast, a significantly higher proportion of individuals were asleep during this same time period in the dry season (p < 0.05). Additionally, during the early morning hours (04:00–06:00 h), a significantly greater proportion of individuals were observed outdoors in the rainy season relative to the dry season (Table 5).

The analysis of the comparison of the mean proportion of time individuals spent outdoors between 18:00 and 22:00 h among those who tested PCR-positive and PCR-negative for Plasmodium vivax showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05), suggesting that outdoor exposure during this time window does not differ substantially based on P. vivax infection status.

Distribution of malaria infections, Ny. darlingi abundance, and infected mosquitoes

A Poisson GLM found that Ny. darlingi counts were significantly associated with both geographic cluster and season. Taking household landscapes as the reference category, mosquito abundance was markedly higher in degraded patches (IRR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.63–1.83), forested areas (IRR = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.42–1.60), and crop zones (IRR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.22–1.38). In contrast, flood-prone areas were associated with significantly lower mosquito counts (IRR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.48–0.57). Seasonal variation was also pronounced, with mosquito counts nearly five times higher during the wet season compared to the dry season (IRR = 4.94; 95% CI: 4.70–5.19; p < 0.001); Supplementary Table S7.

In the southeastern sector of STR there was a higher abundance of Ny. darlingi collected by HLC and human Plasmodium infections detected mainly by qPCR (Fig. 8). In this area houses are in close proximity to the river when it reaches its highest level; also, when the river recedes at the end of the wet season there is a greater number of residual water bodies (compared with other sectors of the village) (Fig. 8). In contrast, the highest abundance of An. near costai G1 is in the southwestern sector, which is more forested (Supplementary Figure S3).

Discussion

Findings from a publication on human behavior conducted in four villages in peri-Iquitos, Loreto, suggested that multiple entry and exit of mosquito nets/night may contribute to residual malaria, and recommended that the human behavior results be complemented with concurrent entomological data46. A recent study in the Napo River basin, north of Iquitos, combined human behavior and entomological data to pinpoint time and place of greatest malaria risk, comparing Ny. darlingi abundance and infectivity indoors and in the peridomestic area (within 10 m of the main house entry). Results indicated that the EIR was 56 times higher in the peridomestic area vs. indoors and researchers suggested that some portion of infective bites was acquired where people were working, playing soccer, watching TV outdoors or bathing in springs. In the present study, despite nearly 100% bednet ownership and coverage inside dwellings of greater than ~ 60%, protection of individuals relative to transmission risk outdoors (beyond household landscapes) is evidently inadequate, as demonstrated by the monthly submicroscopic and seasonal microscopic infections detected. Likely contributing factors include: (1) timing—during the peak biting time of Ny. darlingi (18:00–21:00 h), some STR residents engage in bathing, going out for meals, visiting, and/or watching television; (2) mosquito infectivity—most infected mosquitoes (Ny. darlingi and An. near costai G1) were collected during this time interval; (3) place—there were infected mosquitoes in every landscape type; and (4) season—the rainy season resulted in more microscopic Plasmodium infections, although the submicroscopic infections were perennial and did not exhibit a seasonal pattern. Additional contributing factors outside the scope of the present study are the roles of previous malaria infections and recurrences, outdoor occupation, and occupation-related travel9,23,47.

Observations and findings of submicroscopic asymptomatic malaria infections in low transmission regions (mainly Asia and Latin America) have been well-documented and reviewed48,49, with similar findings as in the current study. All asymptomatic submicroscopic inhabitants constitute a potential reservoir of infectivity, raising concerns for malaria vector control, treatment, and elimination50. The epidemiological findings in the present study are congruent with the entomological results, which indicate that Ny. darlingi—the most abundant and infective species—was highly active during early evening hours (18:00–21:00), a period when many individuals are outdoors and not protected by long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs). Moreover, infected mosquitoes were detected across all landscape types, including peridomestic areas, with a concentration in degraded forests. This spatial and temporal overlap between human behavior and vector activity suggests a sustained risk of malaria transmission, despite high LLIN coverage inside dwellings. These results underscore the need for complementary control strategies beyond LLINs, such as spatially targeted interventions, enhanced entomological surveillance, and molecular screening to identify and prioritize high-risk zones. Implementing these strategies could help reduce residual transmission and move the country closer to the goal of malaria elimination. In a confirmation of the important role of these complementary control strategies, especially regarding malaria elimination goals, a study in Candeis do Jamari, southwestern Amazonian Brazil, found that blood from 50% (16/32) asymptomatic people infected 2.5% (43/1,719) colony-raised Ny. darlingi, and although oocyst numbers were low (1–8 per midgut), a few asymptomatic human individuals remained parasitemic for several weeks51. The overall ratio of 17:1 submicroscopic: microscopic human Plasmodium infections detected for the present monthly yearlong study is congruent with an earlier study in this region that included the village network of San Jose de Lupuna, in that the submicoscopic P. vivax infection rate accounted for 67% of infections tested by qPCR/RTqPCR5.

From an entomological perspective, the greatest risk of mosquito transmission in STR is in degraded forest (K4); (14/31 infected mosquitoes), mainly concentrated in the northeast and southeast sectors (Fig. 2b; orange-brown hexagons). Peridomestic landscapes/households (K1) and K2 (forested) each had 5/31 infected mosquitoes. The lowest number (3/31) of infected mosquitoes was found in K5 (flood-prone areas), even though many of the human cases were concentrated near such areas, especially during the rainy season (Fig. 8). The vector biology results differ somewhat from the expectation of such areas as important sites of outdoor transmission, suggested by Saavedra and colleagues17, possibly due to low overall numbers of infected mosquitoes; different locations, river systems and local circumstances; and/or the analysis of multiple outdoor landscapes, in the present study, which allowed for more discrimination by site type.

Several investigations have been conducted on deforestation, forest coverage, and their impacts on biting rates and the distribution of Ny. darlingi. Some of these studies have identified a significant association between highly deforested sites within communities and an increase in the HBR of Ny. darlingi52 whereas others have found the strongest association of Plasmodium-infected Ny. darlingi to be at an intermediate level of deforestation, i.e., 50%53. Furthermore, peak biting times of Ny. darlingi (and perhaps other vectors) are affected by forest coverage, an added complexity that was discovered recently14. The results of our study reveal that Ny. darlingi is ubiquitous across all habitats within the community and in proximity to it. However, Vittor et al.54 posited that forest coverage may not be the primary driver of variation in biting rates among clusters; rather, it would be habitats with larval breeding sites or high host biomass (including humans). This assertion agrees with findings from Navia-Gine et al., whose general hypothesis is that vector mosquito biting is linked to available host biomass, whether human, domestic, or wild animal, in a specific area55.

In STR where collections were conducted, people use forests and fields for economic activities (hunting and agriculture) and flood-prone areas for leisure activities (during the day and crepuscular hours). Additionally, inhabitants are often seated outside their dwellings, sometimes located near larval breeding sites, especially during wet months when it is hot and humid indoors. Furthermore, the PCR diagnosis for Plasmodium in STR inhabitants indicates that most cases were identified during the months of elevated entomological indices, as individuals have more frequent contact with mosquito vectors, increasing the probability of transmission.

The water level of the Nanay River begins to rise in October, with one peak in December and then the largest peak in April and May, and begins to fall in June56. During the wet season, ecological and hydrological conditions conducive to mating, egg oviposition and adult proliferation are created, resulting in increased mosquito abundance. Data from the present study agree with this scenario in that the highest HBRs of Ny. darlingi and An. near costai G1 occur during the rainy season, especially at the beginning (December) and again from March-June/July, correlating with both the annual increase in the level of the Nanay river, influenced by Andean snowmelt22, and seasonal rainfall (Supplementary Figure S4). The highest EIR values for Ny. darlingi are during the peak rainy season (April and May), and for An. near costai G1, early in the rainy season (December). Additionally, only An. near costai G1 was infected with Plasmodium in the months of August, September, and November, and it is possible that these mosquitoes became infected by biting asymptomatic individuals12.

In addition to Ny. darlingi, we collected Ny. dunhami, one of the most prevalent species during the dry season and in the month of February. This species emerged recently as a local vector in nearby Lupuna, where it naturally harbors Plasmodium-infected mosquitoes20. No infected Ny. dunhami were found in STR. This species could be considered a secondary or occasional vector if conditions for Plasmodium infection occur, during the dry months with low Ny. darlingi abundance, when Ny. dunhami increases. Nyssorhynchus benarrochi B is another species collected in STR during some months, although it does not follow a seasonal pattern here. Several studies have shown that this species in the Amazon can be abundant, anthropophilic, and susceptible to infection19,21,57,58.

Models have demonstrated that P. falciparum is likely more sensitive than P. vivax to the survival rates of malaria vectors due to the longer duration of its sporogonic cycle45. Our analysis of Ny. darlingi mosquito age reveals that young populations live ~ 3–12 days. In some months, the age of mosquitoes is not sufficient to complete the sporogonic cycle of either P. vivax or P. falciparum. This could be one reason for why only two mosquitoes infected with P. falciparum were detected, while the rest were P. vivax or P. malariae. However, most human cases were diagnosed as P. vivax, congruent with proportions of P. vivax reported as 71.5% of malaria cases in Peru in 202159. Parity is also associated with seasonality, as mosquitoes survive longer during the wet season60,61 and our data underscore the continuing importance of the rainy season in malaria transmission in this region of Amazonian Peru.

Anopheles near costai is a species complex consisting of at least 5 species, G1-5, according to Bourke et al.62,63. Previously in Peru, An. near costai (morphologically identified as An. forattinii) has been collected by HLC22 and in the larval stage (identified as An. forattinii / near costai / mediopunctatus) by Prussing et al.18. The species identified in STR, An. near costai G1, is widely distributed in Brazil and Colombia63. We also identified An. near costai G2, but little is known about its natural history, distribution, or potential susceptibility to Plasmodium infection. Morphologically, adult females are very difficult to distinguish from An. forattini and An. mediopunctatus64. This complex is generally associated with forested areas63, consistent with the present study in that An. near costai G1 was most abundant in clusters K2 and K4. Although our study did not aim to comprehensively examine the biting behavior of An. near costai G1, Ny. dunhami, or Ny. benarrochi B, capturing these species with HLC provides clues about their feeding preference for humans and their ability to become infected.

The first known detection of An. near costai G1 throughout its broad Neotropical distribution62,65 infected with P. vivax and the quartan malaria parasite Plasmodium malariae, is an important outcome of the present study. Plasmodium malariae is found in humans and is genetically nearly identical to P. brasilianum, that infects New World monkeys66,67. Lalremruata and colleagues68 found evidence in support of the hypothesis that in Latin America these quartan malaria parasites are easily exchanged between humans and monkeys, concluding that quartan malaria is a true anthropozoonosis. Nevertheless, whether P. malariae and P. brasilianum comprise a single or two separate species remains inconclusive45. Interestingly, P. malariae has been detected in several Latin American mosquito species, for example, in a longitudinal study in Amapá state, eastern Brazil, species found infected were Ny. darlingi, Nyssorhynchus marajoara, Ny. nuneztovari, Ny. triannulatus, and Anopheles intermedius69. A review of Brazilian malaria transmission found five additional Nyssorhynchus species infected with P. malariae, mainly in the Amazon Basin70.

Plasmodium malariae is rarely tested for or reported in Peru because the incidence in Latin America is less than 1%, it is difficult to distinguish from P. vivax by microscopy, it is characterized by very low levels of parasitemia as well as a slow growth rate, and most people infected with the parasite have few if any symptoms48. This species also persists in its host for years or even decades, but rarely does clinical malaria caused by this species progress to severe71. Nevertheless, countries focused on eliminating malaria will need to consider how best to tackle P. malariae as it can survive combination therapies used to treat P. falciparum, with which it is commonly observed in mixed infections72.

Limitations of this study

A decrease in the total number of monthly participants in the epidemiology part of this study was observed, primarily attributable to their absence from the community during the days when screenings were conducted due to occupation-related mobility or healthcare seeking, and for extended periods of absence for occupations (such as logging) that required their presence outside the STR community. Additionally, refusal to participate in the interrupted screenings was observed related to the perception of well-being between one screening and another. The cause behind non-participation was not documented, and an estimated of the proportion of individuals who did not partake in each survey is not attainable. Consequently, a subgroup of participants with a minimum of 10 months of follow-up was selected.

The village boundary delimited with a 500 m buffer around the households may be considered relatively small; certainly Ny. darlingi can fly beyond this limit. The strength of the approach was that many (one hundred and twenty-three) contiguous same-area (16,000 m2) patches were tested. In this study, the trade-off of shorter distance was complete coverage. Although the number of An. near costai s.s. sequenced was small relative to the number collected, the focus of this study was primarily Plasmodium-infected mosquitoes. A more robust number (~ 600 individuals) is presently being sequenced to determine whether any additional species complex members might be found in Amazonian Peru.

Conclusions

We determined that in STR submicroscopic infections are perennial and in very high proportion to microscopically-detected infections. Gaps in transmission protection despite high net coverage were mainly due to occupation outdoors and/or unprotectedness during peak malaria vector biting times (crepuscular hours). In this village, Ny. darlingi was ubiquitous in every landscape type, but degraded forest in STR was the most dangerous single landscape. Anopheles near costai G1 was discovered to be a potential secondary malaria vector in STR, infected with both P. vivax and P. malariae. Monthly year-long collections are recommended in malaria endemic areas, to provide data relevant to malaria eradication efforts.
