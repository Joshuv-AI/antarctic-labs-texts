Use of Honey/Gotu Kola/κ-Carrageenan Base Hydrogel Film Dressing on Diabetic Foot Ulcer Patient in Home Care Setting: a case report
Authors: Sari Meisyayati, Amirah Adlia, Aluicia Anita Artarini, I Ketut Adnyana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective management of early diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) is essential for reducing the risks of amputation and mortality. Affordable wound dressings are particularly important for low-income patients because wound healing often requires prolonged treatment. This case report describes the effects of a combined intervention on wound status and quality of life in a 54-year-old woman with a Grade 2 DFU located on the right heel. Following six weeks of standard wound care administered twice weekly at a local healthcare facility, together with glycemic self-management, the patient’s blood glucose level improved from 380 mg/dL to 130 mg/dL. Despite improved glycemic control, wound healing remained limited, and the patient continued to experience substantial impairment in quality of life due to ulcer-related pain. The intervention consisted of a honey/gotu kola/κ-carrageenan base (HGCB) hydrogel film dressing applied daily in a home-care setting, combined with mechanical debridement and structured glycemic self-management. Quality of life was assessed using the Diabetic Foot Ulcer Scale. A cost analysis comparing HGCB hydrogel dressing with standard wound care was performed over an equivalent treatment duration. By week 12, blood glucose remained controlled, while malodor, firmly adherent slough, and peri-wound maceration had resolved, accompanied by marked improvement in quality of life. Furthermore, the cost of care associated with the HGCB hydrogel dressing was approximately half that of standard wound care. Although derived from a single case, these findings provide preliminary data for future larger-scale investigations. No adverse reactions or treatment-related complications were observed during intervention therapy.Keywords: κ-carrageenan, cost-effectiveness, diabetic foot ulcer, gotu kola, honey, hydrogel film dressing

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a common complication of diabetes and represent a major cause of lower-extremity amputation and increased mortality risk [1]. Appropriate management of early-stage DFUs is essential for preventing disease progression and improving clinical outcomes. In low- and middle-income countries, DFU management remains particularly challenging because of limited healthcare resources, inadequate medical infrastructure, and financial constraints affecting access to appropriate wound care [2]. Consequently, the development of effective and affordable treatment strategies remains an important clinical priority.

The management of non-healing wounds extends beyond wound closure alone and includes optimization of patient comfort, pain reduction, odor control, minimization of bacterial burden, careful removal of slough, and maintenance of an appropriate moist wound environment with effective exudate management [3]. In this case report, a cost-conscious therapeutic strategy for early DFU treatment was evaluated using a honey/gotu kola/κ-carrageenan base (HGCB) hydrogel film dressing in combination with mechanical debridement for wound bed preparation and glycemic self-management to support blood glucose control. The HGCB hydrogel film consisted of 10% honey, 1% gotu kola extract, and 2% κ-carrageenan as the hydrogel matrix. In a previous preclinical study, the HGCB hydrogel film demonstrated high release capacity for honey and gotu kola extract, effective exudate absorption through swelling properties, and accelerated wound healing in a rat wound model without evidence of allergic reactions [4].

Successful DFU treatment also depends heavily on patient adherence and treatment compliance, both of which are closely associated with quality-of-life outcomes [5]. Therefore, the use of validated disease-specific instruments to evaluate quality of life (QoL) in patients with DFUs is important for identifying patient-centered concerns and assessing treatment efficacy during wound healing [6]. In the present case, QoL was evaluated using the Diabetic Foot Ulcer Scale (DFS), a validated questionnaire developed by Abetz et al. (2002). In addition, the costs associated with standard wound care and the alternative intervention were compared over an equivalent treatment duration to evaluate relative economic feasibility.

CASE REPORT

A 54-year-old female patient presented with a Grade 2 DFU located on the right heel six weeks before initiation of the alternative intervention (Fig. 1A). At presentation, her random blood glucose level was 380 mg/dL, and oral antidiabetic therapy together with dietary self-management was initiated to improve glycemic control. As a result, her fasting blood glucose level decreased to 130 mg/dL after five weeks. During the initial six-week period, standard wound care (SWC) was administered twice weekly at a local primary healthcare facility. Treatment consisted of mechanical debridement, topical silver sulfadiazine cream, cadexomer iodine as the primary dressing, and a carbomer 940- and glycerin-based hydrogel for the tunneling component of the wound. Sterile gauze and non-woven tape were applied as secondary dressings. Although the wound became less inflamed than at initial presentation, wound healing progression remained limited, with the wound surface still almost completely covered by firmly adherent fibrous slough accompanied by malodor and marked peri-wound maceration (Fig. 1B).

Photographs of the wound prior to initiation of the alternative intervention. (A) Ulceration with peri-wound maceration at initial presentation. (B) Reduced inflammation with persistent complete coverage by fibrous slough after six weeks of standard treatment.

In the quality-of-life assessment, the patient reported low scores across all evaluated domains (Table 1). She frequently experienced fatigue, exhaustion, sleep disturbance, and pain while walking or standing. The patient remained dependent on others for daily activities and reported substantial limitations in routine functioning. These conditions also affected her emotional well-being, with persistent feelings of anger, frustration, worry, and helplessness associated with delayed wound healing and dependence on others. She additionally reported considerable distress related to the wound’s appearance, malodor, exudate leakage, and the need for assistance with wound management. However, despite these challenges, the patient expressed no concern regarding the possibility of future ulcer development. The markedly impaired QoL contributed substantially to her dissatisfaction with standard treatment and influenced her decision to pursue an alternative therapeutic approach.

1. The new intervention procedure

Throughout the intervention period, the patient continued oral antidiabetic therapy and glycemic self-management to maintain blood glucose control. The intervention protocol was conducted as follows: in a home-care setting, the wound was initially irrigated with drinking water followed by normal saline solution. A 10 cm × 10 cm honey/gotu kola/κ-carrageenan base (HGCB) hydrogel film dressing (Fig. 2) was then applied to the wound and secured with sterile gauze and non-woven tape (Ultrafix®); the dressing was replaced once daily. Mechanical debridement was performed weekly at the same local primary healthcare facility, followed by serial wound documentation. Wound size was assessed by tracing the wound perimeter onto transparent film and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software.

Honey/gotu kola/κ-carrageenan base hydrogel film dressing.

2. The outcome of intervention on wound condition

Progression of wound healing during the intervention period is presented in Fig. 3A-F and Fig. 4. The patient reported transient pain shortly after HGCB hydrogel film application. After one week of treatment, the slough was effectively debrided; however, wound contraction remained minimal, and wound leakage persisted. Two weeks later, slough was confined to a small residual area, whereas more than 90% of the wound surface was covered by granulation tissue. At this stage, the wound area had decreased by approximately 30%, with early epithelialization observed in a limited region. After five weeks of intervention, the wound area had decreased by more than 50%, accompanied by accelerated epithelialization. By week 12, the entire wound surface was covered with epithelial tissue, indicating complete wound closure. Throughout the intervention period, the patient maintained dietary self-management. Consequently, fasting blood glucose levels reached 121 mg/dL after five weeks and 110 mg/dL after 10 weeks of intervention, which likely contributed to wound healing progression.

Fibrous slough was no longer observed at week 5 (C). Epithelialization became predominant at weeks 7 (D) and 9 (E). Complete epithelial coverage was observed at week 12 (F). Blue arrows indicate granulation tissue, and black arrows indicate epithelial tissue.

Wound area reduction over 12 weeks of treatment with honey/gotu kola/κ-carrageenan hydrogel film dressing analyzed using ImageJ software. Wound size began to decrease gradually at week 2, wound contraction exceeded 50% by week 5, and complete wound closure was achieved by week 12.

3. The outcome of interventions on patient’s quality of life

Following the alternative intervention, the patient’s quality of life improved substantially compared with that observed during SWC. During the early phase of treatment, the patient reported favorable responses to the new wound-care approach, including reduced pain duration. The patient reported the highest possible scores (100) in the emotional well-being, treatment satisfaction, and overall satisfaction domains, followed by physical health (80) and daily activity (73.33). As pain intensity and duration progressively decreased, sleep disturbance improved, allowing the patient to achieve more restful sleep and improved emotional well-being. Improvement in wound condition also alleviated distress associated with wound appearance, malodor, and exudate leakage. The marked improvement in QoL following transition to the alternative wound-care approach resulted in a high degree of patient satisfaction with the intervention (Table 1).

4. Cost of care comparison

In this case report, the cost of care associated with both interventions was compared over an equivalent six-week period. Detailed cost components and wound reduction outcomes are presented in Table 2. Overall, the cost of SWC was approximately twice that of the alternative intervention, with a cost difference of IDR 1,333,300. The largest expenditure was associated with wound-care services provided at the local primary healthcare facility, followed by helper services and transportation costs. Service-related expenses included wound cleansing, mechanical debridement, and secondary dressing materials, including sterile gauze, irrigation solution, and non-woven medical tape. Under the SWC protocol, the patient attended the healthcare facility twice weekly, whereas only one weekly visit was required during the alternative intervention period. In addition, each healthcare visit required assistance from a helper, further increasing the total cost burden. The primary dressing used during SWC was also more expensive than the estimated production cost of the HGCB hydrogel film dressing. However, because the HGCB hydrogel film was manually fabricated in the laboratory, the cost estimate included only raw material expenses, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

The SWC protocol resulted in a wound reduction of 29.31%, whereas the alternative intervention achieved a substantially greater reduction of 62.32% over the same treatment duration. Furthermore, the cost associated with HGCB hydrogel film treatment progressively decreased after six weeks because of marked wound-size reduction. Consequently, a smaller hydrogel film was required for subsequent applications, and beginning in week 10, the patient was able to attend healthcare visits independently.

DISCUSSION

In the present case, the patient’s heel DFU initially developed from cracked skin that subsequently became infected. Dryness of the foot skin is recognized as an independent predictor of diabetic foot ulceration because of impaired sweat gland function in patients with diabetes [7]. The progression to a more severe wound condition was likely associated with uncontrolled hyperglycemia, which can prolong the proinflammatory phase of wound healing [8]. Delayed wound healing during standard wound care was also associated with persistent hyperglycemia, which may impair the host’s ability to eliminate bacterial biofilms effectively [9]. In addition, the presence of predominant fibrous slough limited wound contraction. Its dense fibrous structure and string-like texture complicated debridement and contributed to pain during mechanical debridement procedures [10]. During the period of SWC, the patient experienced marked impairment in quality of life, primarily related to persistent ulcer-associated pain and inflammation. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated a negative association between DFU-related pain and multiple dimensions of health-related QoL [11].

The alternative intervention consisting of HGCB hydrogel film application, mechanical debridement, and glycemic self-management demonstrated favorable clinical responses during the early treatment phase. A substantial reduction in slough was observed within the initial weeks of intervention. This finding may be associated with enhanced autolytic activity promoted by the moist wound environment and the presence of honey, which supports autolytic debridement and softens slough, thereby facilitating its removal [12]. Slough and biofilm formation may also have been suppressed through the antibiofilm properties of honey and gotu kola extract [13, 14]. These effects support wound bed preparation, which is considered a critical component of diabetic wound management [15]. Furthermore, reduced slough formation may reflect resolution of the prolonged inflammatory phase and progression toward the proliferative phase of wound healing [16]. Gotu kola extract has also been reported to stimulate type I collagen synthesis, thereby promoting epithelialization during the remodeling phase of wound healing [17].

Pain associated with the DFU gradually decreased during treatment, likely in parallel with improvement in wound condition. Honey and gotu kola extract may also have contributed to pain reduction. Previous studies have reported reduced pain scores following honey application in chronic wound management [14]. In addition, gotu kola extract has demonstrated anti-nociceptive and anti-inflammatory properties and has been associated with pain reduction when used as a wound dressing component [17, 18]. Nevertheless, the patient experienced transient pain shortly after HGCB hydrogel film application. This response may have been related to the osmotic activity of honey, which can induce shrinkage of cellular membranes within the wound bed and stimulate sensory neurons [12]. However, the transient discomfort did not substantially affect the patient’s overall sense of comfort because of its short duration. The HGCB hydrogel film also reduced wound exudate and malodor during treatment. Its intrinsic swelling properties facilitated absorption of excess wound fluid [4]. Although wound leakage remained present during the initial treatment period, this issue was controlled by increasing dressing replacement frequency. Furthermore, the reducing sugars present in honey may function as odorless substrates while simultaneously exerting antibacterial effects [14]. In combination with gotu kola extract, these properties may have contributed to reduced bacterial burden and subsequent reduction in wound malodor [17].

By week 12 of the intervention period, complete resolution of the DFU had been achieved. A major contributing factor was likely the maintenance of glycemic control, as the patient’s fasting blood glucose level remained below the hyperglycemic threshold of 126 mg/dL throughout the intervention period. A previous cohort study demonstrated that early intensive glycemic control improves DFU prognosis [19]. Therefore, patient adherence to glycemic self-management likely played an important role in supporting the effectiveness of HGCB hydrogel film treatment.

In this case report, the cost-effectiveness of both interventions was evaluated over an equivalent treatment interval using a Markov model, a widely recognized method for assessing the clinical and economic consequences of healthcare interventions [20]. Although the findings suggested that the alternative intervention may be cost-effective, the results cannot be generalized because they were derived from a single patient. Nevertheless, the preliminary economic findings provide useful information for the design of future larger-scale investigations.

In the cost-of-care analysis, the estimated production cost of the HGCB hydrogel film was approximately one-fifth that of the primary dressing used during SWC because the calculation included only raw material expenses. Accordingly, the actual production cost would likely be higher, as hydrogel fabrication requires additional resources, including equipment, electricity, and labor associated with homogenization, casting, and dehydration processes. However, production of the HGCB hydrogel film may be scalable for larger manufacturing settings. Increased machine capacity and process automation could potentially reduce the production cost per unit.

The alternative intervention may represent a safe, clinically beneficial, cost-conscious, and accessible approach for early DFU management, particularly among low-income patient populations. In addition, the intervention was associated with improvement in patient-reported QoL. However, because of the absence of randomization and the potential influence of uncontrolled confounding factors, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further controlled studies are necessary to determine whether the observed outcomes are directly attributable to the intervention itself.
