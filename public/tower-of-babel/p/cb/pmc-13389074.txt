Spacing effects of discrete bed roughness on sequent depth, jump length, and energy loss in hydraulic jumps
Authors: Hossein Sohrabzadeh Anzani, Sameh Ahmed Kantoush, Sohei Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract This study examines the effect of roughness spacing on the characteristics of hydraulic jumps in open-channel flows, based on experimental results in a rectangular flume with a smooth bottom and three different sets of discrete roughness (spacingsS = 12 cm, 24 cm, and 46 cm; fixed heightK = 2 cm). Tests were conducted with supercritical incoming flows at discharges of 15, 20, and 25 L/s. Flow visualization revealed regime transitions from skimming-like flow at close spacing (S/K = 6) to wake-interference at intermediate spacing, and wake-type hydraulic jumps at the largest spacing (S/K = 23), with increasing discharge promoting unsteady wake–jump interactions, surface rolling, and enhanced turbulence. Results show that increasing roughness spacing significantly reduces relative jump length (Lj/y₁) from 20 to 35 (S = 12 cm) to 10–17 (S = 46 cm), while relative energy dissipation (ΔE/E1) decreases with larger spacing due to reduced flow–roughness interaction density; optimal dissipation occurs at smaller spacings. Velocity profiles, pressure distributions, and roller lengths were also affected, with closer spacings yielding more compact jumps and higher turbulence near the bed. Empirical predictive relationships were developed for relative jump length and a modified conjugate depth ratio incorporating a roughness correction factor, showing good agreement with measurements. These findings highlight the critical role of roughness spacing in optimizing energy dissipation and jump control in stilling basins and hydraulic structures.Keywords: Hydraulic jump, Bed roughness, Relative jump length, Conjugate depth ratio, Open-Channel flowSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Open-channel flow is a fundamental topic in hydraulic engineering, as it governs the behavior of water in natural and engineered systems such as rivers, canals, spillways, and various water conveyance structures. A thorough understanding of flow regime transitions, particularly hydraulic jumps-is essential for the effective design of energy dissipation facilities, stilling basins, and flood control measures1–3. The hydraulic jump occurs when the flow undergoes a sudden transition from a supercritical to a subcritical state, accompanied by intense turbulence, air entrainment, and substantial energy dissipation4–8. Owing to its critical role in mitigating downstream erosion and regulating sediment transport, this phenomenon has been the subject of extensive experimental and theoretical investigations9,10.

The theoretical foundation of hydraulic jumps was initially established by Bélanger11 and later developed by subsequent researchers, leading to classical formulations that relate upstream and downstream flow characteristics through momentum conservation principles12. Despite these advancements, the effects of boundary roughness on hydraulic jump behavior continue to attract significant research attention. Surface roughness along channel beds and sidewalls plays a key role in controlling flow resistance, velocity distribution, turbulence structure, and patterns of energy dissipation13,14,59. As a result, hydraulic jumps formed over rough boundaries often exhibit noticeable deviations in sequent depth ratio, jump length, energy loss, and flow profile compared to those occurring on smooth beds15,16.

Numerous studies have shown that bed roughness modifies hydraulic jump characteristics through several interacting mechanisms. Roughness enhance boundary shear stress, distort the velocity profile, intensify turbulent mixing, and introduce additional zones of energy dissipation within the jump region17,18. Among the governing parameters, the spacing of roughness is particularly influential, as it controls the nature of flow interaction with the roughness. Depending on this spacing, the flow may fall within isolated roughness, wake interference, or skimming flow regimes, each of which exerts a distinct influence on hydraulic jump structure and overall hydraulic performance19,20.

A considerable body of research has focused on hydraulic jumps occurring over rough beds with various geometric configurations. Among the earliest and most influential studies, Rajaratnam16 demonstrated that the presence of bed roughness leads to a reduction in both the sequent depth ratio and the hydraulic jump length. Building on this work, Hughes and Flack15 examined hydraulic jumps over gravel beds and reported substantial changes in velocity distribution as well as a shortening of the roller region. Further investigations by Ead and Rajaratnam21 on corrugated beds revealed the development of complex three-dimensional flow structures accompanied by increased energy dissipation. Similar attention was given by Tokyay22, who analyzed the influence of bed corrugations on jump behavior, and by Abbaspour et al.17, who explored the effects of varying roughness heights on hydraulic jump characteristics. More recently, Nikmehr and Aminpour23 investigated hydraulic jumps over triangular corrugated beds, while Macián-Pérez et al.24 provided a detailed characterization of structural properties in turbulent hydraulic jumps at high Reynolds numbers.

Beyond roughness geometry, the spatial arrangement of roughness-particularly their spacing-plays a decisive role in shaping hydraulic jump behavior. When roughness are closely spaced, the flow tends to skim over the roughness layer with limited interaction with individual roughness. In contrast, wider spacing allows the flow to interact separately with each roughness, generating pronounced wake zones, vortex shedding, and complex flow structures downstream of the roughness19,25. Although the influence of roughness spacing has been extensively examined under uniform flow conditions26,27, its role in highly transitional and rapidly varied flows such as hydraulic jumps remains insufficiently understood.

A clear understanding of the interaction between roughness spacing and hydraulic jump characteristics is therefore essential for the effective design and optimization of energy dissipation systems. Variations in roughness spacing can significantly affect key hydraulic parameters, including roller length, sequent depth ratio, energy dissipation efficiency, and pressure fluctuations along the channel bed10,28. These factors are of critical importance in the design of stilling basins and energy dissipators for hydraulic structures such as dams29–33, weirs34–43, and spillways44–49, where reliable control of flow energy is required to ensure structural safety and downstream stability50,51.

While previous studies investigated roughness height, geometry, and corrugation patterns, the specific influence of spacing between discrete roughness on hydraulic jump characteristics has received limited systematic attention. In most prior works, spacing was fixed or not explicitly considered, despite its potential to significantly alter flow structure and energy dissipation mechanisms.

This study addresses this gap by isolating roughness spacing as the primary variable across three configurations, while maintaining constant channel geometry and discharge conditions. Furthermore, detailed experimental data on flow behavior, pressure distribution, and energy dissipation for different spacing arrangements are essential for developing reliable design guidelines and predictive models. Accordingly, this study examines: (i) hydraulic characteristics, including sequent depth ratio, jump length, and roller length over smooth and rough beds; (ii) flow mechanisms, including velocity distribution, flow structure, and pressure distribution in the jump region; (iii) predictive modelling, through the development of empirical relationships for hydraulic jump parameters as functions of upstream Froude number and roughness spacing.

The core scientific question addressed in this study is how the spacing of discrete bed roughness governs hydraulic jump structure, energy dissipation, and flow regime transitions in supercritical open-channel flow.

Experimental setup

The experiments were conducted in an 8-m-long rectangular flume with a constant width of 0.50 m and a fixed bed slope of 0.02 (Fig. 1). The bed and sidewalls were constructed from transparent Plexiglas, providing a hydraulically smooth boundary. This configuration minimized boundary effects and enabled clear visual observation of flow behavior under sediment-free conditions.

Experimental flume configuration: (a) plan view, (b) longitudinal section, and (c) three-dimensional schematic.

Water was supplied from a constant-head reservoir and recirculated through the flume using a pump controlled by a Mitsubishi FREQROL-A700 variable-frequency drive. Discharge was regulated by a calibrated gate valve and stabilized upstream by a slit weir37,52,53. Three steady discharges-15, 20, and 25 L/s were examined and measured using an electromagnetic flowmeter with an accuracy of ± 1%54. Each experimental run was initiated only after maintaining the target discharge for at least 10 min to ensure steady-state flow conditions.

Flow behavior was recorded simultaneously from the side and top views of the flume. The hydraulic jump formed upstream of the first roughness was analyzed in detail, and its main characteristics-including velocity distribution, flow depth, and jump length-were measured using a velocity meter (VP3000 equipped with a VPT3-200-13P probe) and a point gauge. At each velocity measurement location, 60 samples were collected at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. Flow depth was measured at the channel centerline using a digital point gauge with an accuracy of ± 0.1 mm and was independently verified using high-resolution video recordings.

Three bed configurations were investigated, consisting of discrete roughness with longitudinal spacings of S = 12, 24, and 46 cm (Fig. 2). The roughness elements are rigid, fixed, and water-resistant, preventing deformation during the experiments. Each roughness has a height of 2 cm (K), a streamwise length of 2 cm, and a spanwise width of 50 cm (covering the full channel width). The roughness are uniformly spaced along the channel centerline in a single-row arrangement perpendicular to the flow direction. The total coverage length of the roughness array is 1.5 m. The roughness elements are attached to the Plexiglas bed using waterproof adhesive and remain rigid throughout the experiments.

Schematic view of strip roughness, showing the spacing between roughness and measurement locations.

All experiments were conducted under closed-loop recirculating flow conditions. To ensure adequate spatial resolution across different hydraulic conditions, the number of velocity measurement points varied among the bed configurations. This variation was adopted to ensure adequate spatial resolution under different hydraulic conditions. Measurement sections were distributed along the streamwise direction with higher density near the hydraulic jump region where flow gradients are significant, and at each section, velocity profiles were obtained at three transverse positions (B/2, B/4, and B/8) from the bed to the free surface. This approach maintains consistency in measurement methodology while improving the accuracy of capturing flow characteristics.

Hydraulic jump forms upstream of the first roughness; therefore, measurements were conducted at streamwise locations both upstream and downstream of the jump to capture flow variations along the channel. Velocity was measured at three transverse positions across the channel width, including the centerline (B/2), mid-distance (B/4), and near-wall region (B/8). At each measurement location, vertical velocity profiles were obtained from near the bed to the free surface. This measurement arrangement enables a comprehensive assessment of both streamwise and transverse variations in flow structure, as well as the influence of roughness on hydraulic jump behavior.

Results

Flow behavior

Reference flow over a smooth bed

In the smooth channel, the free surface remains uniform and stable, and the flow is entirely supercritical along the flume (Fig. 3). The absence of bed roughness minimizes flow resistance and suppresses turbulence generation near the bed. This reference case highlights the significant role of bed roughness in inducing instabilities and altering free-surface behavior in subsequent configurations.

Free-surface profile of supercritical flow in a smooth channel.

Backflow formation

Figure 4 shows that when the supercritical flow impinges on the roughness, a strong backflow region forms immediately downstream as a result of abrupt flow separation at the downstream face of the roughness. The adverse pressure gradient induces flow reversal and the development of a recirculation zone adjacent to the bed.

Backflow development downstream of a roughness and its interaction with the approaching supercritical flow.

This reversed flow interacts directly with the incoming supercritical stream, giving rise to a pronounced shear interface between the upstream-directed backflow and the high-momentum approaching flow. The strong velocity gradient across this interface enhances shear-layer instability and triggers the formation of large-scale vortical structures.

As a consequence, turbulence production is significantly intensified in the vicinity of the roughness. The interaction between the backflow, shear layer, and incoming flow promotes unsteady behavior and intermittent momentum exchange toward the free surface. This process manifests as localized surface oscillations and contributes to enhanced energy dissipation in the near-roughness region.

Flow regimes and free-surface behavior as a function of roughness spacing

Increasing the roughness spacing progressively shifts the flow regime from skimming-like to wake-interference (Fig. 5). At the smallest spacing (S = 12 cm, S/K = 6), the flow skims over the roughness crests while quasi-stationary recirculation cells form in the cavities (Fig. 5c). The continuous shear layer isolates the core flow from the near-bed region, generating periodic free-surface undulations (Fig. 5d).

Free-surface flow patterns over roughness with spacing of 12 cm: (a, b) observed free-surface behavior along the flume, (c) skimming-like flow regime over the roughness crests, and (d) free-surface oscillations induced by roughness–flow interactions.

As spacing increases to S = 24 cm (Fig. 6), the flow penetrates deeper into the cavities, leading to distinct wake formation and wake–wake interaction. At the largest spacing (S = 46 cm), fully developed wakes produce strong vortex shedding and intensified vertical turbulent transport, resulting in large-amplitude surface oscillations (Fig. 6c).

(a) Free-surface response of supercritical flow over bed roughness with S = 24 cm, (b) schematic illustration of the wake-interference flow regime highlighting flow separation, wake interaction, and free-surface fluctuations, and (c) longitudinal free-surface profile along the flume for S = 46 cm.

Quantitative criteria for flow regime classification

To enhance reproducibility, the flow regimes were classified based on the relative roughness spacing (S/K). Specifically, a skimming-like regime was identified for S/K < 8, characterized by stable recirculation cells confined within the cavities and the presence of a continuous shear layer; in the present study, this condition corresponds to S/K = 6. A wake-interference regime was defined for 8 ≤ S/K ≤ 20, where interactions between adjacent wakes and vortex shedding dominate the flow structure; here, S/K = 12 falls within this range. Finally, a wake-type or isolated regime31 occurs for S/K > 20, characterized by fully developed wakes and strong unsteady wake–jump interactions.

Effect of discharge on free-surface stability

For the configuration with S = 12 cm and K = 2 cm, increasing discharge from 15 L/s to 25 L/s markedly reduces free-surface oscillations (Fig. 7). At lower discharge (15 L/s), lower velocity allows strong interaction between the shear layer and cavity recirculation, promoting large coherent vortices and pronounced surface waves. At higher discharge (25 L/s), increased flow inertia stabilizes the shear layer, suppresses recirculation growth, and limits upward turbulent transport, resulting in a smoother free-surface profile and a more stable skimming-like regime.

Free-surface flow behavior over bed roughness with spacing S = 12 cm and height K = 2 cm (S/K = 6) at different discharges: (a) Q = 15 L/s, (b) Q = 20 L/s, and (c) Q = 25 L/s.

Hydraulic jump formation and wake-type transition

The hydraulic jump forms (Fig. 8) upstream of the first roughness due to the direct interaction between the incoming supercritical flow and the first roughness. As the flow impinges on the upstream face of the roughness, flow separation occurs, leading to the development of a backflow region. This reversed flow interacts with the incoming high-momentum stream, creating strong flow instability and rapid momentum exchange, which triggers the formation of a hydraulic jump. Compared to classical hydraulic jumps, the jump observed in this study is more compact and shorter, primarily due to intensified turbulence generated by flow separation and vortex interaction near the roughness.

Hydraulic jump upstream of the first roughness due to the interaction between the incoming supercritical flow and the increased bed resistance.

Further downstream, at the largest spacing (S = 46 cm), fully developed wake structures interact with the jump toe (Fig. 9). At Q = 15 L/s, the jump remains relatively steady (Fig. 9a). As the discharge increases (Q = 20 and 25 L/s), stronger wake structures and enhanced flow unsteadiness lead to the transition toward a wake-type hydraulic jump (Fig. 9b, c).

Free-surface behavior over bed roughness with S = 46 cm: (a) steady hydraulic jump at Q = 15 L/s; (b) wake-type hydraulic jump at Q = 20 L/s; and (c) fully developed wake-type hydraulic jump at Q = 25 L/s, showing surface rolling and enhanced free-surface fluctuations due to wake–jump interaction.

Effect of roughness spacing on relative hydraulic jump length

The hydraulic jump length (Lj) is defined as the horizontal distance from the toe of the jump (where the supercritical flow begins to rise abruptly at the upstream face of the first roughness element) to the end of the surface roller, i.e., the location where the free-surface profile becomes nearly horizontal and the velocity distribution approaches subcritical conditions downstream.

In the present experiments, due to the interaction with discrete roughness and the resulting unsteady wake–jump interactions (especially at larger spacings and higher discharges), the end of the jump was determined visually from side-view video recordings as the point where surface fluctuations and roller turbulence significantly diminish. This practical criterion ensures consistency with classical definitions while accounting for the enhanced unsteadiness and compactness induced by bed roughness.

Figure 10a illustrates the relationship between the relative jump length to initial depth ratio (Lj/y₁) and the upstream Froude number (Fr₁) for three different roughness spacings. The results indicate that increasing the roughness spacing significantly reduces the relative jump length. For a spacing of 12 cm, Lj/y₁ values range between approximately 20 and 35, whereas for a spacing of 46 cm, this ratio decreases to about 10–17. This reduction highlights the direct influence of roughness spacing on hydraulic jump compression. In other words, as the distance between roughness increases, stronger turbulence is generated, leading to more rapid energy dissipation over a shorter distance. It is also observed that, within each data series, an inverse relationship exists between Fr₁ and Lj/y₁, such that the relative jump length decreases with increasing Froude number. This behavior is consistent with the classical empirical relationship for hydraulic jumps, which indicates that jump length is a decreasing function of the Froude number.

Effect of roughness spacing on relative hydraulic jump length: (a) variation of Lj/y₁ with upstream Froude number (Fr₁) for different roughness spacings; (b) variation of Lj/y₁ with relative roughness spacing (S/y₁).

Figure 10b illustrates the relationship between the relative jump length and the ratio of roughness spacing to the initial depth (S/y1). This relationship represents one of the most important findings of the study, as it directly demonstrates the influence of roughness geometry on the dimensions of the hydraulic jump. The results clearly show that as S/y₁ increases, the relative jump length decreases significantly. For small S/y₁ values (approximately 3 to 5, corresponding to the 12 cm spacing), the relative jump length ranges from about 20 to 35. In contrast, for larger S/y₁ values (approximately 10 to 12, corresponding to the 46 cm spacing), this range decreases to about 10 to 17. This indicates that with increasing roughness spacing, the hydraulic jump becomes more compact and is completed over a shorter length. This behavior can be explained by turbulent boundary layer theory and the influence of roughness on flow structure. Roughness with moderate spacing promotes the formation of coherent vortices and stronger momentum exchange, leading to a shortened hydraulic jump.

The experimental results show that the relative energy loss for all roughness spacings falls within a range of approximately 5–15%. A notable observation is that for the 12 cm spacing, the energy loss is relatively lower and the data are more tightly clustered, whereas larger spacings exhibit greater scatter. This suggests that smaller roughness spacing produces more consistent energy dissipation behavior.

These trends arise from distinct turbulent mechanisms governed by roughness spacing. At close spacing (S = 12 cm, S/K = 6), the skimming regime creates a continuous shear layer with strong near-bed recirculation and vortex generation in cavities, producing high turbulent kinetic energy and efficient momentum exchange- resulting in higher ΔE/E1. With increasing spacing (S = 24–46 cm), the flow shifts to wake-interference and wake-type regimes featuring pronounced vortex shedding and wake interactions. Although local turbulence intensifies (evident in surface rolling at higher discharges), partial flow recovery between elements reduces cumulative dissipation, lowering overall ΔE/E1.

Velocity profiles confirm steeper near-bed gradients and higher turbulence intensity at smaller spacings, while intensified shear-layer instability drives stronger pressure fluctuations, enhancing energy loss through mixing and air entrainment. Optimal dissipation thus occurs in the wake-interference regime.

Discussion

Flow regime transitions and mechanisms

Increasing roughness spacing induces a clear transition from skimming-like flow (S/K = 6) to wake-interference (S/K = 12) and finally to wake-type hydraulic jumps (S/K = 23), especially at higher discharges. In the skimming regime, a continuous shear layer confines most turbulence to the inter-element cavities, limiting vertical momentum transfer and resulting in longer jumps (Sect. 3.2). With wider spacing, fully developed wakes and strong vortex shedding enhance vertical turbulent transport and destabilize the jump toe, producing more compact jumps but allowing partial flow recovery between elements.

These transitions align with classical regime classifications in uniform rough-wall flows19, yet they are amplified here by the adverse pressure gradient and high shear inherent to the hydraulic jump. The present results highlight that spacing controls the balance between localized dissipation and cumulative energy loss, with wake-interference (S/K = 6–12) appearing optimal for efficient jump control in stilling basins.

Jump length reduction: comparison with previous studies

A key quantitative finding is the marked reduction in relative jump length (Lj/y₁) with increasing S/y1 (Fig. 10). For small spacings (S/y1 = 3–5), Lj/y1 ranges from 20 to 35, while at larger spacings (S/y1 = 10–12), it drops to 10–17. This compression reflects enhanced turbulence production and rapid energy dissipation over shorter distances, driven by stronger coherent vortices and shear-layer instability in wider inter-roughness elements regions.

The observed inverse trend with upstream Froude number (Fr1) is consistent with classical relations for smooth beds1,2, but the magnitude of reduction is significantly greater on rough beds with moderate-to-large spacing. Table 2 presents a comparative analysis of jump length reductions reported in recent literature for various roughness elements configurations. Rajaratnam16 reported 20–30% reductions using corrugated beds, while Ead and Rajaratnam21 observed 25–40% shortening with strip roughness elements at similar Froude numbers. Carollo et al.18 found 30–45% reductions with gravel roughness, attributing the effect to increased boundary shear and additional dissipation zones. More recently, Ghaderi et al.56 documented 35–50% compression using triangular and trapezoidal macro-roughness, consistent with the present findings.

Energy dissipation efficiency: optimal spacing range

In contrast to jump length, relative energy dissipation (ΔE/E1) decreases with increasing roughness elements spacing (Fig. 11b). At smaller S/y1 (denser roughness), higher dissipation (up to approximately 12–15%) arises from greater flow resistance, denser recirculation cells, and stronger near-bed turbulent mixing. As spacing increases, partial flow recovery between roughness elements reduces the cumulative dissipative effect of individual, leading to lower overall ΔE/E₁ (approximately 5–9% at the largest spacing).

At S = 46 cm (S/K = 23), the relative jump length further shortens while energy dissipation decreases. This nonlinear behavior arises because wider spacing allows full development of isolated wakes with strong vortex shedding, which intensifies local turbulence and shear-layer instability, resulting in a more compact jump. However, greater distance between elements permits partial flow recovery, reducing the overall density of flow-roughness interactions and thus lowering total energy dissipation.

This indicates an optimal spacing range around S/K = 6–12, where wake-interference maximizes both jump compactness and energy loss. The observed wake-type hydraulic jump at larger spacing and higher discharges involves enhanced vorticity transport and rapid breakdown of coherent structures, promoting surface rolling but less cumulative dissipation.

Although larger roughness spacing (particularly at S = 46 cm, S/K = 23) produces stronger local turbulence through fully developed isolated wakes and intense vortex shedding (leading to the observed wake-type hydraulic jump and more compact jump length), the overall relative energy dissipation (ΔE/E₁) decreases. This is not contradictory. When spacing is large, the flow regime becomes isolated-roughness type; each roughness element generates its own wake, but the flow has sufficient distance to partially recover its velocity and momentum before reaching the next element. Consequently, the density of successive flow–roughness interactions decrease, reducing the cumulative energy dissipation along the jump region. In contrast, at intermediate spacing (S = 24 cm, wake-interference regime), the wakes from adjacent roughness elements interfere with each other before full recovery occurs. This overlapping of wakes maintains continuous and intense turbulent mixing without significant flow recovery, resulting in higher overall energy dissipation. At the smallest spacing (S = 12 cm, skimming-like), the high density of roughness further enhances near-bed recirculation and shear-layer turbulence, maximizing cumulative dissipation. Therefore, maximum energy dissipation occurs at smaller-to-intermediate spacings, while larger (isolated) spacing favors stronger local turbulence and shorter jump length at the cost of lower total energy loss.

This suggests an optimal spacing range for maximum energy loss, likely around S/K = 6–12 in the current configuration, where wake interference maximizes vortex generation without excessive recovery. Comparative studies support this conclusion: Abbaspour et al.17 found optimal energy dissipation at S/h = 8–10 for triangular roughness, while Habibzadeh et al.55 reported peak performance at S/K = 5–8 for rectangular blocks. The present data extend these findings to discrete roughness elements arrays, indicating that for excessive spacing (S/K > 15), the net benefit for stilling basin performance diminishes-a consideration critical for practical design where basin length and energy control must be balanced.

Recent comprehensive reviews57 similarly emphasize the importance of roughness elements density and spacing for energy dissipation optimization. However, few studies explicitly vary spacing while holding height constant across a systematic range, making the present contribution particularly valuable for establishing design guidelines.

The modified conjugate depth relation with a small negative correction term (Eq. 4) reflects the observed reduction in y2/y1 due to additional momentum redistribution and dissipation in the jump roller. This compares favorably with modifications proposed by Hughes and Flack15 for gravel beds and Pagliara et al.58 for sinusoidal roughness, though the present formulation explicitly accounts for spacing effects through the S/y₁ term. The incorporation of spacing as an independent variable represents a significant advancement over previous models that treated roughness elements effects through equivalent sand grain roughness (ks) or relative roughness elements height alone.

Practical implications and design considerations

These models provide a practical extension for engineers designing roughness stilling basins downstream of spillways, weirs, or chutes. The identified optimal spacing range (S/K = 6–12) balances compact basin length with efficient energy control, offering cost savings in construction and land use. For typical spillway applications with Fr1 = 4–8, the present correlations suggest roughness spacings of 8–15 times the approach flow depth (y1) would achieve near-optimal performance.

Furthermore, the regime classification (skimming → wake-interference → wake-type) provides a diagnostic framework for predicting jump behavior and potential instabilities. Designers should avoid the wake-type regime in applications requiring stable, predictable jump positions, as excessive surface oscillations may compromise sidewall safety or induce vibration-related structural issues.

Tables 2 and 3 shows direct comparability across studies is influenced by differences in experimental conditions. The present study operates at lower Fr₁ (1.5–2.2) compared to many previous works (typically Fr₁ = 3–9), uses discrete strip roughness with controlled variable spacing (while others often fix spacing or use continuous corrugations), and employs a small-scale flume. These factors affect the relative importance of roughness-induced turbulence versus inertial effects, explaining slight variations in reported reduction percentages. Nevertheless, the consistent trend of jump shortening with increasing roughness density or effective resistance validates the dominant role of spacing observed here.

Conclusion

This experimental study demonstrates that discrete bed roughness elements spacing profoundly influences hydraulic jump behavior, flow regimes, and energy dissipation efficiency in supercritical open-channel flows. The key findings are summarized as follows:

i.Increasing roughness elements spacing shifts the dominant flow regime from skimming-like (at S = 12 cm) to wake-interference (at S = 24 cm) and ultimately to wake-type hydraulic jumps (at S = 46 cm), accompanied by stronger vortex shedding, wake–wake interactions, and pronounced free-surface oscillations, particularly at higher discharges.

ii.Relative hydraulic jump length (Lj/y₁) decreases markedly with increasing spacing (S/y1), ranging from 20 to 35 at small spacings to 10–17 at larger ones, reflecting enhanced turbulence generation and more rapid energy dissipation over shorter distances; an inverse relationship with upstream Froude number (Fr₁) is also observed.

Relative hydraulic jump length (Lj/y₁) decreases markedly with increasing spacing (S/y1), ranging from 20 to 35 at small spacings to 10–17 at larger ones, reflecting enhanced turbulence generation and more rapid energy dissipation over shorter distances; an inverse relationship with upstream Froude number (Fr₁) is also observed.

iii.Energy dissipation efficiency (ΔE/E₁) is higher at smaller roughness elements spacings due to denser roughness elements promoting greater flow resistance and turbulent mixing; as spacing increases, dissipation decreases because of partial flow recovery between roughness, indicating an optimal spacing for maximum energy loss.

iv.Flow behavior near roughness elements includes backflow formation, shear-layer instability, and recirculation zones that intensify turbulence production and propagate upward, altering velocity distributions and inducing surface undulations, with higher discharges suppressing oscillations in denser configurations.

Future studies should explore a wider range of roughness elements heights, shapes, channel slopes, and Froud number to generalize these findings. Incorporating advanced numerical simulations alongside PIV measurements could provide deeper insights into three-dimensional flow structures and pressure fluctuations. Additionally, investigating sediment-laden flows and movable beds would enhance applicability to natural rivers and prototype stilling basins, ultimately contributing to more efficient and sustainable hydraulic structure designs.
