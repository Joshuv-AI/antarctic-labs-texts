Integrating multiple hematologic inflammatory indices into a principal component analysis–derived inflammatory burden score for coronary artery disease
Authors: Mert Deniz Savcilioglu, İrfan Veysel Düzen, Ertan Vuruşkan, Murat Sucu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplete blood count–derived inflammatory indices have been associated with coronary artery disease (CAD), but their substantial overlap may limit interpretability when assessed individually. We used principal component analysis (PCA) to integrate six commonly used inflammatory indices into a composite Inflammatory Burden Score (IBS) and evaluated its association with angiographically confirmed CAD alongside the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI).MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 1,261 individuals (477 with CAD and 784 without CAD) who underwent invasive coronary angiography at a tertiary referral center. PCA was applied to six standardized inflammatory indices, and the first principal component was retained as the IBS. Hierarchical logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the incremental contribution of IBS and PNI beyond conventional cardiovascular risk factors. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration metrics, and likelihood-ratio testing. XGBoost and SHAP analyses were performed as complementary explainable machine-learning approaches.ResultsThe first principal component explained 68.1% of the total variance across inflammatory indices, with all variables contributing positively to the composite score. IBS was significantly higher among participants with CAD (p < 0.001). In univariable analyses, both IBS (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.05–1.17, p < 0.001) and PNI (OR 0.05, 95% CI 0.02–0.14, p < 0.001) were associated with CAD. After adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, IBS (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.19) and PNI (OR 0.06, 95% CI 0.02–0.20) remained independently associated with CAD. Addition of either IBS or PNI significantly improved model fit (both likelihood-ratio test p < 0.001). The AUC increased from 0.727 in the conventional risk-factor model to 0.734 after inclusion of IBS and to 0.737 after inclusion of PNI. The XGBoost model achieved an AUC of 0.802, while SHAP analysis identified age and sex as the dominant predictors, followed by IBS and PNI.ConclusionsIn this single-center cross-sectional cohort, a PCA-derived inflammatory burden score and the Prognostic Nutritional Index were independently associated with angiographically confirmed CAD beyond conventional cardiovascular risk factors. Both measures contributed similarly in explainable machine-learning analyses. These findings are exploratory and require prospective external validation before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06315-5.

Background

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 9.14 million deaths in 2019 and representing the single largest contributor to the global cardiovascular disease burden [1, 2]. Although established risk factors including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, and tobacco use account for a large proportion of myocardial infarction risk across diverse populations, a substantial residual risk persists among individuals in whom conventional risk factors are adequately controlled [3]. Over recent decades, converging lines of evidence have established that atherosclerosis is fundamentally a chronic inflammatory disease, driven by immune cell activation, endothelial dysfunction, and plaque instability rather than lipid accumulation alone [4, 5]. This evolving understanding has given rise to the concept of residual inflammatory risk, defined as the excess cardiovascular event rate that remains after lipid-lowering therapy and is attributable to persistent systemic inflammation. Clinical validation was provided by the CANTOS trial, in which IL-1β inhibition with canakinumab reduced recurrent cardiovascular events independent of lipid change [6]. These observations have reinforced interest in inflammatory biomarkers as tools for characterizing cardiovascular risk beyond conventional metabolic parameters.

Numerous complete blood count-derived inflammatory indices have been proposed as accessible surrogates of systemic inflammatory burden in the setting of CAD, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV). Each of these indices has been associated with CAD presence, severity, or adverse outcomes in observational studies and meta-analyses [7–12]. However, because all six indices are mathematically derived from overlapping neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts, they are intrinsically intercorrelated, and evaluating each independently introduces redundancy and multicollinearity that complicate interpretation of their individual contributions. A data-driven strategy to integrate these correlated indices into a unified, parsimonious representation of inflammatory burden has not been systematically applied in the context of CAD assessment.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a well-established dimensionality reduction technique that transforms a set of correlated variables into a smaller number of orthogonal latent constructs, each capturing the maximum possible residual variance [13]. Applied to correlated inflammatory indices, PCA offers a methodologically principled approach to deriving a composite score that reflects the shared underlying inflammatory signal without the collinearity inherent in analysing each index individually. A PCA-derived cytokine composite has previously demonstrated prognostic value in acute myocardial infarction [14]. To our knowledge, however, no previous study has applied PCA to integrate multiple contemporary CBC-derived inflammatory indices (NLR, MLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, and PIV) into a single unified Inflammatory Burden Score (IBS) for the assessment of angiographically confirmed CAD.

In parallel, nutritional and immune status has emerged as a relevant dimension of cardiovascular risk. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin and peripheral lymphocyte count, was originally developed to assess perioperative nutritional status and has since been associated with mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with CAD across multiple independent cohorts [15–17]. Accordingly, PNI was included as a complementary nutritional–immune marker to examine whether it provides information beyond the PCA-derived inflammatory burden score in patients undergoing invasive coronary angiography.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional observational study conducted at the Cardiology Department of Gaziantep University Sahinbey Research and Practice Hospital, Gaziantep, Turkey. Ethical approval was obtained from the Gaziantep University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee prior to data access (Protocol No. 2026/295; decision date: 06.05.2026). Following ethical approval, electronic medical records were reviewed retrospectively to identify consecutive adult patients who underwent invasive coronary angiography between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2025 and met the predefined eligibility criteria. All data were fully anonymized before analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective design and the use of anonymized routine clinical data, the requirement for individual written informed consent was waived by the institutional ethics committee.

Study population

Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged between 18 and 85 years and had been referred to the cardiology outpatient clinic for clinical evaluation. Inclusion additionally required the availability of complete hematological and biochemical laboratory measurements obtained in the course of routine clinical assessment, and a documented coronary evaluation sufficient to classify CAD status per the criteria described below.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded in the presence of conditions known to confound hematological or inflammatory biomarkers, including: active infection or acute inflammatory illness at the time of blood sampling; active malignancy or ongoing oncological treatment; pregnancy; established chronic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases (including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and inflammatory bowel disease); hematological disorders; current use of corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or cytotoxic agents; acute kidney injury or end-stage renal disease; major surgery, acute cardiovascular event, or trauma within the preceding three months; and missing values for any key laboratory or demographic parameter required for index calculation. Definition of coronary artery disease

Obstructive CAD was defined as ≥ 50% diameter stenosis in at least one major epicardial coronary artery on invasive coronary angiography. Because of the retrospective study design, CAD classification was based exclusively on angiographic findings; physiological lesion assessment using FFR or iFR and angiographic severity scoring systems were not consistently available for all participants. Patients with < 50% stenosis in all major epicardial coronary arteries were classified as the non-CAD group. CAD status was determined retrospectively from invasive coronary angiography reports available in the hospital information management system, and no additional investigations were performed for study purposes.

Laboratory measurements

Venous blood samples had been obtained after a minimum overnight fast of eight hours as part of routine clinical practice. Hematological parameters including complete blood count with leucocyte differential (neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte absolute counts), hemoglobin, and platelet count were measured using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XN-1000, Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). Biochemical parameters, including fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), albumin, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), were analyzed in the hospital’s central biochemistry laboratory using standardized automated colorimetric and enzymatic methods on an automated analyzer. Red cell distribution width (RDW) was obtained from the hematology analyzer as part of the complete blood count report.

Derivation of inflammatory indices

Six inflammatory indices were calculated from routine hematological parameters. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was computed as the ratio of absolute neutrophil count to absolute lymphocyte count. The monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) was calculated as the ratio of absolute monocyte count to absolute lymphocyte count. The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) was calculated as the ratio of platelet count to absolute lymphocyte count. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was computed as: SII = (platelet count × neutrophil count) / lymphocyte count. The systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) was calculated as: SIRI = (neutrophil count × monocyte count) / lymphocyte count. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) was computed as: PIV = (platelet count × neutrophil count × monocyte count) / lymphocyte count. PLR, SII, and PIV exhibited right-skewed distributions and were log-transformed prior to analysis; transformed values are denoted LogPLR, LogSII, and LogPIV, respectively.

Derivation of nutritional and hematological indices

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was calculated as: PNI = (10 × serum albumin [g/dL]) + (0.005 × absolute lymphocyte count [/mm³]). Because the PNI distribution was right-skewed, PNI values were log-transformed prior to statistical analyses. The hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) score was computed as: HALP = (hemoglobin [g/dL] × albumin [g/dL] × lymphocyte count [×10³/µL]) / platelet count [×10³/µL]. The HALP score exhibited a right-skewed distribution and was log-transformed prior to analysis (LogHALP). Red cell distribution width (RDW), expressed as a percentage, was included as a hematological marker of erythrocyte morphological heterogeneity.

Principal component analysis and inflammatory burden score

To address the high collinearity among the six inflammatory indices (NLR, MLR, LogPLR, LogSII, SIRI, LogPIV) and to derive a parsimonious composite measure of inflammatory burden, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the standardized (z-score) values of these six variables. The number of components to retain was determined by inspection of the scree plot and by the criterion that the retained component(s) explain a meaningful and interpretable proportion of total variance. The first principal component (PC1), termed the Inflammatory Burden Score (IBS), was retained for all subsequent analyses. The direction of PC1 was oriented so that higher IBS values corresponded to higher inflammatory burden (i.e., all six inflammatory indices exhibited positive loadings on PC1). PCA was performed using the scikit-learn library (version 1.3; Python 3.12). To facilitate reproducibility, the cohort-specific parameters and step-by-step manual calculation procedure for the PCA-derived IBS are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Statistical analysis

General approach

All analyses were performed in Python (version 3.12) using standard scientific computing libraries (pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn, XGBoost 2.0, SHAP 0.45, matplotlib, seaborn). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout.

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median (interquartile range [IQR]) according to distributional characteristics. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for samples of fewer than 50 observations and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test otherwise. For group comparisons of continuous variables, the independent-samples t-test was used when normality assumptions were satisfied; the Mann–Whitney U test was used otherwise. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression

Associations between individual variables and CAD were first examined in univariable logistic regression analyses. Variables of a priori clinical relevance (age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, current smoking) and variables demonstrating a statistically significant univariable association were considered as candidates for inclusion in multivariable models. To evaluate the incremental contribution of inflammatory and nutritional status beyond conventional cardiovascular risk factors, hierarchical multivariable logistic regression models were constructed sequentially. The primary analysis consisted of five nested models: M1 (age and sex), M2 (M1 + diabetes mellitus and hypertension), M3 (M2 + current smoking), M4A (M3 + IBS), and M4B (M3 + PNI). Prespecified sensitivity analyses extended the conventional model with lipid profile (LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides) and renal function (eGFR), followed by separate inclusion of IBS and PNI (Supplementary Tables S2, S3). This sequential modeling strategy was designed to determine whether the observed associations remained independent after adjustment for routinely available clinical and biochemical variables. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and no evidence of problematic multicollinearity was identified.

Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility

Discriminative performance was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (2,000 resamples) and pairwise DeLong comparisons between nested models. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (10 deciles of predicted probability); p > 0.05 was taken to indicate adequate calibration. The Brier score was computed as a measure of overall probabilistic accuracy. Calibration slope and intercept were estimated by regressing observed outcomes on the logit of predicted probabilities from cross-validated predictions. Decision curve analysis was performed to assess the clinical utility of M4A and M4B across a range of threshold probabilities.

Explainable machine learning analysis

A gradient-boosted tree ensemble was fitted using XGBoost (n_estimators = 300, max_depth = 4, learning_rate = 0.05, subsample = 0.80, colsample_bytree = 0.80, random_state = 42) on the full variable set corresponding to M4B (age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking, IBS, and PNI). Model discrimination was evaluated by five-fold stratified cross-validation (AUC with 2,000 bootstrap iterations for confidence intervals). To interpret the trained model, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed using a TreeExplainer. Global feature importance was summarized as the mean absolute SHAP value per feature. Beeswarm plots were used to characterise the direction and distribution of individual feature contributions. SHAP dependence plots were generated for IBS and PNI to illustrate the relationship between feature value and model output contribution.

Participants in the CAD group were older and had a higher proportion of male sex compared with those without CAD (both p < 0.001). The frequencies of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and current smoking did not differ significantly between groups. Principal component analysis of inflammatory indices

Six inflammatory indices (NLR, MLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, and PIV) were submitted to principal component analysis (PCA) after standardisation. The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 68.1% of total variance (eigenvalue 4.09), with all six indices loading positively (Fig. 2).

Model performance

Discriminative performance and calibration metrics for all five models are presented in Table 4. ROC curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, and calibration curves for M4A and M4B are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.

The AUC of M1 was 0.729 (95% CI 0.701–0.756). M2 and M3 yielded comparable discrimination (AUC 0.728 and 0.727, respectively). M4A achieved an AUC of 0.734 (95% CI 0.706–0.764) and M4B an AUC of 0.737 (95% CI 0.708–0.764). Brier scores ranged from 0.197 (M4B) to 0.201 (M3). Calibration slopes were close to 1.0 across all models (range 0.973–0.997). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated adequate calibration for M4B (χ² = 12.03, p = 0.150); statistically significant departures were observed for M1 through M4A (all p < 0.001, Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

The present study presents a methodological framework for integrating multiple correlated CBC-derived inflammatory indices into a single composite inflammatory burden score using principal component analysis. Rather than introducing a novel biomarker, this approach aims to reduce redundancy among established inflammatory indices by summarizing their shared inflammatory signal into a single composite measure. In this cohort, the resulting IBS was independently associated with angiographically confirmed CAD after adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, while PNI demonstrated a comparable independent association. Explainable machine-learning analyses further identified both IBS and PNI among the most influential variables after age and sex, supporting the complementary contribution of inflammatory and nutritional status to CAD risk.

Rationale for a PCA-derived composite inflammatory score

The six inflammatory indices included in the present analysis are all computed from overlapping peripheral blood cell compartments (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and platelets). As a consequence, they are substantially intercorrelated, and their individual inclusion in regression models risks redundancy, multicollinearity, and inconsistent effect estimates across studies [7–12]. This methodological challenge has been increasingly recognised in the cardiovascular biomarker literature [19]. The present study addressed this by applying PCA to reduce six correlated indices to a single orthogonal component that captured their dominant shared variance. The substantially balanced positive loadings observed for all six indices suggest that IBS does not predominantly reflect one specific leukocyte axis but rather integrates multiple overlapping inflammatory signals.

IBS and its association with coronary artery disease

The association between IBS and CAD is consistent with previous studies evaluating individual CBC-derived inflammatory indices in coronary populations [7–12]. Although IBS significantly improved model fit beyond conventional cardiovascular risk factors, the increase in discrimination was modest (AUC 0.727 to 0.734), consistent with the concept of residual inflammatory risk [6], and should therefore be interpreted as incremental statistical information rather than immediate clinical utility. The relatively low prevalence of obstructive CAD (37.8%) reflects the inclusion of consecutive patients undergoing coronary angiography for diverse clinically appropriate indications, thereby reducing selection bias and improving applicability to routine clinical practice. Because of the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

PNI and nutritional–immune status in the context of CAD

Lower PNI values were independently associated with CAD after adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, extending previous evidence linking reduced PNI with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CAD [16, 17]. Since PNI incorporates both serum albumin and lymphocyte count, it likely reflects the interaction between nutritional status and immune function rather than nutrition alone [20, 21]. The comparable SHAP contributions of IBS and PNI suggest that inflammatory burden and nutritional–immune status may represent complementary biological dimensions of cardiovascular vulnerability that warrant further investigation.

Explainable machine learning analysis

SHAP analysis identified age and sex as the dominant contributors to model output, followed by IBS and PNI, whereas diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and smoking showed smaller contributions. These rankings reflect feature importance within the fitted model rather than biological or causal importance. Accordingly, the machine-learning analyses should be interpreted as complementary data-characterization tools rather than evidence supporting clinical risk stratification or causal inference [22]. The absence of independent associations for diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and smoking should be interpreted in the context of the selected angiography cohort, in which these risk factors were highly prevalent across both groups.

Positioning in the literature and potential utility

To our knowledge, no previous study has integrated six contemporary CBC-derived inflammatory indices into a PCA-derived composite for the assessment of angiographically confirmed CAD. Previous PCA-based approaches have primarily focused on cytokine panels or serum inflammatory markers [14, 23], whereas the proposed IBS is derived exclusively from routinely available CBC parameters. Its principal value, therefore, lies in providing a standardized research framework for summarizing correlated inflammatory information rather than replacing existing diagnostic or prognostic tools. Accordingly, IBS should currently be regarded as a research-oriented composite measure that requires external validation across independent cohorts and direct comparison with established inflammatory biomarkers, including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), before its potential clinical utility can be established.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed associations reflect covariation at a single time point and do not establish the temporal sequence between inflammatory or nutritional status and coronary disease.

This was a single-center study at a tertiary cardiology referral center. The patient case-mix, referral patterns, and local laboratory practices may differ from those of community-based or multicenter populations, and the generalizability of the findings to other settings requires evaluation in independent cohorts.

All laboratory values were measured at a single time point concurrent with clinical assessment. Inflammatory indices and nutritional markers are susceptible to transient perturbation by intercurrent illness, medication changes, and physiological variation, and the present cross-sectional measurements may not represent the chronic inflammatory or nutritional state of individual participants.

Although broad exclusion criteria were applied to minimise acute confounders of inflammatory markers, residual confounding by unmeasured variables including dietary intake, physical activity, adiposity indices beyond BMI, psychosocial factors, sleep characteristics, and genetic determinants of inflammatory activity cannot be excluded.

Several potentially relevant clinical characteristics were not available for systematic inclusion in the analyses, including family history of premature CAD, duration and severity of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, glycaemic control (HbA1c), waist circumference, body composition, and menopausal status.

Information regarding contemporary cardiovascular pharmacotherapy, including statins, antiplatelet agents, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, was not consistently available for all participants and therefore could not be incorporated into the multivariable models. Although additional adjustment for lipid profile and renal function demonstrated robust associations, residual confounding related to pharmacological treatment cannot be excluded.

Coronary artery disease was defined according to the presence of ≥ 50% diameter stenosis on invasive coronary angiography, an anatomical definition that does not necessarily correspond to functionally significant myocardial ischemia. Physiological lesion assessment using fractional flow reserve (FFR) or instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR) was not routinely available because of the retrospective design, and angiographic severity scores such as the SYNTAX or Gensini score were not systematically recorded. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting associations with angiographically defined obstructive CAD rather than ischemia-producing coronary disease or overall atherosclerotic burden. Future studies incorporating physiological lesion assessment and validated angiographic severity scores are needed to determine whether IBS is associated with functional lesion significance and CAD complexity.

The inflammatory composite in the present study was derived exclusively from CBC-derived inflammatory indices. Established circulating inflammatory biomarkers, particularly high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), were not routinely available because of the retrospective design and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses. Other protein-based inflammatory biomarkers, including interleukin-6, fibrinogen, and lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A₂, were also unavailable. Consequently, the incremental value of IBS over established circulating inflammatory biomarkers could not be evaluated. Whether incorporation of these biomarkers would alter the structure or performance of the PCA-derived composite remains unknown and should be investigated in future prospective studies.

The IBS is a data-driven PCA-derived composite, and the component loadings may vary across populations with different inflammatory profiles. External validation of both the PCA structure and the observed associations is essential before considering broader research or clinical application.

The machine-learning analyses were conducted as a complementary characterization tool and were validated using stratified cross-validation within the present dataset only. External validation was not performed, and the discriminative performance reported for the XGBoost model may not replicate in independent samples.

Finally, the present study examined associations with prevalent angiographically confirmed CAD. Whether IBS or PNI are associated with incident coronary events, progression of coronary atherosclerosis, or cardiovascular mortality over longitudinal follow-up remains unknown and represents a priority for future investigation.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cross-sectional study, a principal component analysis-derived IBS integrating six correlated CBC-derived inflammatory indices was independently associated with angiographically defined coronary artery disease. IBS remained robust after adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, lipid profile, and renal function, indicating that it captures inflammatory information not fully reflected by routinely available clinical variables. Nevertheless, the incremental improvement in discrimination beyond conventional risk factors was modest and should be interpreted as providing additional statistical information rather than immediate clinical utility.

Compared with individual inflammatory indices, the PCA-derived approach offers a standardized framework for summarizing correlated hematologic inflammatory markers while reducing redundancy among overlapping indices. However, IBS should currently be regarded as a research-oriented composite measure rather than a standalone diagnostic or risk-stratification tool.

Given the retrospective, single-center design and the use of an anatomically defined angiographic CAD endpoint, these findings require cautious interpretation. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating established inflammatory biomarkers, physiological lesion assessment, and external validation across diverse populations are warranted to determine the reproducibility, generalizability, and potential clinical utility of the proposed IBS.
