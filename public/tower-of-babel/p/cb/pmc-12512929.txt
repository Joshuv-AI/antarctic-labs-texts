Gender differences in parent-child alienation and its relationship to depressive symptoms in left-behind children: a comparative analysis of undirected and directed networks
Authors: Ling Li, Lei Ren, Chang Liu, Kuiliang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe elevated prevalence of depression among left-behind children is influenced by parent-child alienation. Gender differences may exist in both depression symptoms and parent-child alienation. This study employs undirected and directed network comparative analysis to explore gender disparities in parent-child alienation and its association with depression symptoms among left-behind children, offering nuanced data support for clinical decision-making.MethodsA self-reported questionnaire assessing parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms was completed by 1,833 left-behind children (55.9% male, 44.1% female; mean age = 14.60 ± 2.13, range = 9–18 years). Descriptive statistics, Gaussian graphical models (GGM) and Bayesian directed acyclic graphs (DAG) were used to examine gender differences in network structures and directional relationships.ResultsThe prevalence of depression among male left-behind children was 19.73%, while among females it was 28.06%. Females reported higher total scores of parent-child alienation compared to males (34.55 vs. 31.79). “Reluctance to ask parents for money” may be a primary factor contributing to parent-child alienation among left-behind children. Maternal alienation exhibits a stronger association with depressive symptoms. Network comparison revealed no significant differences in network structure and global invariance, but significant disparities were found in some edges and nodes. Bayesian network similarity was 22%, with a directional consistency of 45%. Depression symptoms among left-behind children may be a factor leading to parent-child alienation.ConclusionAlthough no significant overall gender differences were found in network structures, subtle disparities existed in edge strengths and directional patterns. The findings suggest potential associations from depressive symptoms to parent-child alienation, informing prevention strategies and targeted research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24796-5.

Introduction

Left-behind children, as a vulnerable group, are associated with a higher risk of serious mental health problems [1]. Left-behind children are those under the age of 18 whose parents have been forced to leave their homes to seek livelihoods in economically developed regions under the adverse effects of poverty, war, disease, etc., leaving their children behind to be cared for by grandparents or other relatives in their hometowns [2]. The economic disparities resulting from rapid urbanization and limited employment opportunities in rural areas compel parents to migrate to urban centers, while housing and household registration policies force them to leave their children behind in their hometowns [3, 4]. This phenomenon is particularly prominent in countries with uneven economic development, and it is estimated that there are approximately 100 million left-behind children worldwide, with about 61 million left behind in rural China alone [5]. Studies have shown that left-behind children are more likely to suffer from depression than non-left-behind children [6], which poses a serious disease burden on social development [7]. Reducing the prevalence of depression among left-behind children in China has therefore become an essential prerequisite for achieving the “Healthy China Initiative”.

The problem of depression among left-behind children is closely related to parent-child alienation [8]. Parent-child alienation refers to the persistent estrangement, hostility, or refusal to interact between parents and children, arising from diverse causes such as neglect, lack of communication, prolonged separation, or, in some cases, one parent’s manipulation of the child against the other (i.e., parental alienation [9, 10]). From this perspective, parent-child alienation can be regarded as a broader extension of parental alienation, encompassing multiple causes of disrupted parent–child relationships. Compared to non-left-behind children, left-behind children may be more susceptible to parent-child alienation due to the chronic lack of time spent with their parents [11]. Studies have shown that children who experience parent-child alienation exhibit higher levels of depression [12, 13]. Left-behind children also tend to exhibit higher levels of parent-child alienation and more severe depressive symptoms than non-left-behind children [14]. This implies a correlation between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms among left-behind children, but the causal direction remains unclear.

A recent longitudinal study of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms in left-behind children showed that parent-child alienation was identified as a predictor of depressive symptoms [15]. In other words, parent-child alienation may be a cause of depression in left-behind children. However, depression manifests itself in left-behind children as a lack of energy [16] and a reduced willingness to communicate [17], which may lead to reduced communication with parents. Therefore, there is emerging evidence suggesting that depressive symptoms in left-behind children may also be a predictor of parent-child alienation. It remains unclear whether parent-child alienation causes depressive symptoms (due to limited parental support), depressive symptoms increase alienation (driven by pubertal developmental changes), or both factors interact reciprocally, requiring further empirical study.

Children often behave differently in their interactions with their parents because of gender differences [18, 19]. In the case of the mother, she is often the most important provider in the child’s life, assuming the main responsibility for the care of the children [20]. However, a mother’s care for her child can sometimes lead to excessive control, especially when the child reaches adolescence [21]. As a result, children generally have a special emotional connection to their mothers. In contrast, fathers typically play a more supportive role in parenting [22]. They are involved in taking care of children for relatively little time [23], which may lead to an increased sense of estrangement between children and their fathers. Research suggests that greater father involvement can reduce children’s behavioral problems and positively affect their development [24], while fatherhood traits can also affect relationships with daughters [22]. This indicates that fathers also play an important role in children’s development. However, attachment to the mother tends to be the main factor influencing depressive symptoms in adolescents [24].

Parental roles are often influenced by social and cultural norms, particularly in Chinese culture [25]. Influenced by traditional gender expectations, mothers are often positioned as primary caregivers, with fathers relegated to a secondary role [26, 27]. While fulfilling these traditional gender roles (e.g., mothers providing nurturing care) is generally viewed as positive, chronic parental absence or emotional deprivation can lead to negative outcomes such as alienation, particularly when societal expectations are misaligned with adolescents’ emotional needs [28]. Research has shown gender differences in parent-child alienation, with male adolescents demonstrating significantly higher alienation scores toward both parents than their female counterparts [29]. This implies that there may be gender differences in parent-child alienation. Firstly, parents may use different parenting styles, which may affect children’s alienation from their parents [30–32]. Secondly, since mothers typically serve as the primary caregivers, children may feel more alienated from their fathers than from their mothers. However, gender differences exist in children’s alienation from their fathers, with males and females showing different patterns [33]. Given the correlation between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms, there may also be gender differences in the relationship between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms in children left behind, but this has not been fully confirmed.

The network analysis approach provides a novel perspective for exploring the relationship between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms. In psychiatry, network analysis, grounded in network theory [34], conceptualizes mental disorders as dynamic networks of interacting symptoms rather than as the manifestation of a single latent variable [35]. Gaussian graphical models (GGM) reveal conditional dependencies among variables through partial correlations, where variables/symptoms are represented as nodes and their associations as edges in the network [36]. This approach has been widely applied in depression research. For instance, previous studies have identified differential connections between emotion regulation strategies (cognitive reappraisal, expressive suppression) and specific depressive symptoms such as suicidal ideation [37]. Moreover, personality traits such as neuroticism may activate the depression network through emotion-related symptoms (e.g., “worthlessness”) [38]. Comparative analyses of depression symptom networks have further indicated no significant differences in network structure or global connectivity strength between male and female groups [39, 40]. This may be due to the fact that the network comparison test emphasizes cumulative differences across all edges while overlooking edge-specific variations [41]. In addition, GGM based on cross-sectional data cannot explore the directional relationship between variables.

The directed acyclic graphs (DAG) can be employed to infer potential statistical causal pathways based on cross-sectional data, thereby addressing certain limitations of traditional GGM [42]. For example, prior research on left-behind children has identified potential causal pathways between depressive symptoms and social anxiety symptoms, showing that the social anxiety node “Social fright” showed precedence in the development of symptoms [6]. Compared with traditional statistical methods (e.g., correlation analysis, regression analysis, and structural equation modeling), Gaussian graphical models and Bayesian networks offer distinct advantages. By adopting a dynamic systems perspective, enabling data-driven causal discovery, and applying probabilistic causal reasoning, they transcend static associations to uncover feedback loops and non-equilibrium interactions among symptoms. They also move beyond pre-specified models, allowing novel mechanisms to emerge from the data [43]. In addition, they provide a bridge between basic research and clinical decision-making. Through the Bayesian network “hypothesis-testing” framework (e.g., estimating the probability of improvement in symptom Y when intervening on node X), this method can directly inform precision interventions [44].

However, no study to date has applied network analysis to examine the relationship between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms in left-behind children, underscoring the novelty of the present research. By integrating network analysis, this study systematically investigates the mechanisms linking parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms in left-behind children, thereby providing theoretical foundations for precision interventions (e.g., enhancing parental emotional support or strengthening emotion regulation strategies). We propose the following hypotheses: (1) Female left-behind children have significantly higher scores in parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms than male left-behind children; (2) In the parent-child alienation network, alienation from fathers is the most important node among females, while alienation from mothers is the most important node among males; (3) There are no gender differences in the network structures and global connectivity strength of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms, but differences exist in certain edges and node centrality; (4) The conditional dependencies and directional relationships in Bayesian networks vary with gender, and the interdependence between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms is stronger among females.

Methods

Participants

In June 2023, five primary and secondary schools were conveniently selected in Chongqing, Sichuan, and Guizhou, China, for questionnaire surveys. Surveys were conducted both online and using paper forms. Online surveys were administered to schools with students who could access smartphones, facilitated by the Wenjuanxing system (https://www.wjx.cn). Paper surveys were distributed by class teachers to students. The questionnaire included introductory instructions and informed consent information. The survey was anonymous, and participants read the questionnaire instructions and signed an informed consent form before the survey. The survey was voluntary and participants could withdraw at any stage without any negative consequences. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Water Resources and Electric Engineering College (Project No. KJQN202203812). The survey protocol and information were presented to school officials and parents through class teachers’ communication channels. A total of 4658 primary and secondary school students participated, of whom 1872 met the criteria for left-behind children (not Living with parents for the past 6 months and aged 9 to 18 years). Subsequently, 39 questionnaires with missing values exceeding 50% were excluded, resulting in a sample exclusion rate of 2%. The remaining 1833 questionnaire data were included in the final analysis.

Measurement tools

Sociodemographic variables assessment

A self-developed questionnaire was administered to collect data on sociodemographic characteristics reported, including age, place of residence, single-child status, and gender, to capture essential demographic information relevant to the study focus on Chinese adolescents and their family dynamics. Participants reported their age in years as a continuous variable (e.g., 15 years), indicated their place of residence by selecting “City” or “Countryside” based on their administrative division as defined by official Chinese regional classifications, and specified their single-child status by choosing “Yes” or “No” to account for family structure in the context of China’s historical one-child policy. Gender was determined based on the resident identity card of participants and recorded as a binary variable (male or female).

Depression symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is one of the most widely used screening and measurement tools for depressive symptoms [45]. It comprises nine items, rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Total scores range from 0 to 27, with ranges indicating absence of depressive symptoms (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (≥ 20) depressive symptoms [46, 47]. The questionnaire demonstrated good reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Parent-child alienation

The Inventory of Alienation Towards Parents (IAP) was used to measure the degree of parent-child alienation among students [14]. The questionnaire consists of 18 items divided into two dimensions: maternal and paternal alienation, each comprising nine items. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree), with higher scores indicating greater alienation. The questionnaire demonstrated good reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.93).

Data analysis

Raw data were preprocessed using Microsoft Excel 2019 [48], including the removal of data entry errors and questionnaires with missing values exceeding 50%. Descriptive statistics and reliability and validity analyses of measurement tools were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 [49]. Data interpolation, descriptive statistics, and network analysis were performed using R version 4.3.3 [50].

Three types of network structure comparison analyses were conducted to validate our three research hypotheses. First, separate networks for parent-child alienation among left-behind children were constructed to examine gender differences in parent-child alienation. This network comprised nine behaviors of alienation from mothers and fathers, respectively. Second, gender differences in parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms were further explored, with networks consisting of alienation from mothers and fathers and nine depressive symptoms. Lastly, Bayesian network analysis was employed to investigate whether gender differences exist in the priority of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms among left-behind children, and to analyze the similarity and consistency of edge direction in male and female Bayesian networks.

Gaussian graphical models

The network analysis utilized GGM to estimate connections between nodes, Spearman rho correlation matrices were employed, and regularization was conducted via the graphical LASSO algorithm to enhance interpretability and stability [51]. A hyperparameter of 0.5 for the Extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) balanced sensitivity and specificity in edge extraction [36]. Visualization utilized the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm through the R-package qgraph [52, 53].

Node expected influence [53], indicating importance in the network, was computed using qgraph, while bridge expected influence [54], highlighting potential contagion risks to other communities, was calculated with networktools [55]. Robustness was assessed using bootnet, with edge weight accuracy evaluated through non-parametric bootstrap and node (bridge) expected influence stability measured by correlation stability coefficient [56].

GGM network comparison

We utilized the NetworkComparisonTest software package to analyze gender variances within the parent-child alienation network and the parent-child alienation coupled with depressive symptoms network, with a permutation of 2,000 trials [41]. The evaluation of network structure invariance allowed us to assess overall network structure across genders, identifying discrepancies in network edges and their quantities. Additionally, we assessed global strength invariance to determine whether the total impact of network edges differed between the two gender groups. Furthermore, we compared expected influence metrics within parent-child alienation networks and examined differences in expected influence as well as bridge expected influence within networks associating parent-child alienation with depressive symptoms.

Directed acyclic graph (DAG)

We utilized directed acyclic networks to analyze cross-sectional data, offering insight into the directionality and interdependence of symptoms. Employing the R-package bnlearn and the hill-climbing algorithm, we constructed a DAG to model symptoms of depression and parent-child alienation [57]. The algorithm iteratively adjusts edges, adding, deleting, or reversing their direction, to optimize fit based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [58]. This iterative process involves multiple random restarts, perturbing the system with 50 different random restarts to avoid local maxima. Following established protocols [58–60], we conducted 100 perturbations per restart. To ensure network stability, we employed a two-step process: first, identifying edges appearing frequently in bootstrapped networks and then utilizing Scutari and Nagarajan’s [61] optimal cut-point method to retain edges with high sensitivity and specificity. Second, when estimating edge direction, we retained directional edges present in 51% or more of the bootstrapped networks. The thickness of edges in the DAG represents the probability of directionality, with thicker edges indicating stronger directional relationships. Nodes closer to the upstream exert greater influence on the overall network structure.

DAG network comparison

We employed the Jaccard similarity coefficient [62] to assess gender variations in Bayesian networks related to parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms. This coefficient calculates the similarity between two sets of networks by dividing the number of common connections by the total number of unique connections in both networks. Additionally, we utilized the directional consistency of connections metric to analyze differences in connection directionality within the networks. This metric measures the proportion of connections in the same direction across both networks relative to the total number of connections in one network [43]. Both the Jaccard similarity coefficient and directional consistency of connections range from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater similarity between the networks. Further details on this coefficient can be found at the following link: https://rdrr.io/github/idroz/gasub/man/jaccardSimilarity.html.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 1,833 left-behind children were included in the final analysis, including 1,024 males (55.9%) and 809 females (44.1%). The mean age was 14.60 (SD = 2.13) in the whole sample, with no significant difference in gender (t = 0.18, p = 0.86), and no significant difference in place of residence (χ²=0.13, p = 0.71), but a significant difference in the single child (χ²=9.57, p = 0.002). On the depression scale, the prevalence of depression among the left-behind children was 19.73% (based on a cut-off score of 10) for males, and 28.06% for females. The Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test revealed significant differences in total depression scores between males and females (W = 355111, p < 0.01). Males reported significantly lower alienation scores with their mothers (16.32) compared to females (17.54; W = 380548, p < 0.01). Males also reported an alienation score of 15.47 with their fathers, while females reported a total alienation score of 17.00 with their fathers, with significant differences observed (W = 370060, p < 0.01). Significant differences were found between males and females in alienation scores with both mothers and fathers (W = 370118, p < 0.01). DAG network comparison

The Jaccard similarity coefficient of networks was 0.22, indicating a Low degree of similarity between the edges of the two networks. A coefficient of 0.22 Suggests that approximately 22% of the edges are shared between the male and female networks of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms. The edge direction consistency was 0.45, indicating that 45% of the common edges between the male and female networks of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms have the same direction. This value suggests that when the same relationships (edges) exist in both networks, the direction of influence differs in over half of the instances.

Discussion

This study bridges a critical gap in understanding the relationship between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms among left-behind children by extending prior research from a unidirectional perspective—where alienation predicts depression—to a bidirectional framework that explores potential interaction influences. Using GGM and DAG, we investigated gender-specific differences in these relationships, providing novel insights for both theoretical development and practical interventions. The results revealed that females had significantly higher prevalence of depressive symptoms, alienation from their mothers, alienation from their fathers, and alienation from both parents than males. Furthermore, the nodes (M4, F4, and F8) for the parent-child alienation network and the nodes (D5, D8, and D9) for the parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms network showed significant differences between genders. DAG analysis further revealed gender-differentiated probabilistic dependencies: all depressive symptoms preceded alienation in males, while females showed interdependencies between these constructs, particularly alienation from their fathers. These results highlight the importance of gender-specific, personalized interventions in addressing the mental health issues of left-behind children and lay a solid foundation for future research and targeted support strategies.

The findings of this study indicate that the overall parent-child alienation scores among left-behind children are consistent with the average levels reported in previous studies [14], with a more pronounced degree of alienation from mothers. Notably, among left-behind children, females generally exhibit higher total alienation scores than males [15], suggesting that gender differences play a significant role in parent-child relationships within this group. In contrast, studies on non-left-behind children indicate that males may experience greater parent-child alienation due to less effective discipline and higher rates of physical punishment [30], resulting in significantly higher alienation from parents for males compared to females [29]. This contrasting pattern underscores the specificity of gender differences in parent-child alienation among left-behind children, as males in this group, due to separation from parents, may experience less parental physical punishment. Additionally, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among left-behind children is notably higher than that of the general adolescent population [63], and slightly elevated compared to left-behind children during the pandemic [6]. Furthermore, females exhibit higher prevalence rates of depressive symptoms at both the 5-point and 10-point cutoff thresholds compared to males, indicating gender differences in depressive symptoms among left-behind children [64]. These results Support hypothesis 1 and highlight the gender-specific nature of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms among left-behind children, underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions to optimize their mental health support systems.

Parent-child Alienation GGM Network

The GGM network analysis of parent-child alienation reveals that, in the male parent-child alienation network, alienation from the mother is identified as the node with the greatest expected influence, whereas in the female parent-child alienation network, alienation from the father has the greatest expected influence, Supporting hypothesis 2. This finding may be related to the “Oedipus” complex, whereby males are more likely to be attached to their mothers and females to their fathers [65]. When alienation occurs with the primary attachment figure (the opposite-sex parent), secondary attachment cannot fully compensate; for instance, when males are alienated from their mothers, even a good relationship with their fathers cannot fully compensate for the loss of the primary emotional attachment [66]. Interestingly, the nodes M3 (“I do not ask my mother for money”) and F3 (“I do not ask my father for money”) exhibited the highest bridge expected influence in the parent–child alienation networks of both males and females. This finding suggest that they play a crucial role in bridging mother- and father-related alienation. In other words, economic avoidance behaviors serve as key hubs connecting the communities of maternal and paternal alienation, highlighting their importance in the potential spread of alienation from the mother to the father and vice versa. A possible explanation lies in the fact that, in many left-behind families, financial support is regularly provided either directly to the child or through caregivers. In this context, requesting money from parents can create opportunities for communication and emotional exchange, whereas parental rejection of such requests may exacerbate alienation. Therefore, teaching left-behind children to express their economic needs in a healthy way, and encouraging parents to proactively provide support, may help reduce parent–child alienation across both maternal and paternal domains.

Additionally, network comparisons show that the nodes M4 (I cannot feel love from my mother) and F4 (I cannot feel love from my father) have higher expected influence values in the female network compared to the male network. This suggests that, compared to males, females are more likely to experience other alienation behaviors due to a perceived lack of parental love, possibly because females rely more on parental emotional support [67]. In the prevailing sociocultural context, females are encouraged to express emotions and seek emotional support [68], whereas males are often encouraged to prioritize independence and avoid emotional expression [69]. Consequently, when parental emotional support is absent, females are more likely to experience a collapse of their emotional support network [70]. Comparisons of bridge nodes indicate that, in the male network, the node F8 (I feel unfamiliar with father) related to father alienation exhibits more negative edge connections with mother alienation nodes, suggesting that males who feel alienated from their father or mother may increase their dependence on the other parent [21].

Parent-child Alienation and Depression Network

The GGM network analysis of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms revealed that the depressive symptom node D4 (Fatigue) had the highest expected influence value in both the male and female networks. This finding is consistent with previous research on depression symptom networks among Chinese adolescents [71]. One possible explanation is the heavy academic burden faced by primary and secondary school students [72], which often results in insufficient sleep and feelings of fatigue [73]. Network comparative analyses showed no significant differences in overall network structure or global connectivity. However, significant differences emerged in the expected influence of specific depressive nodes (D5: Appetite, D8: Motor, D9: Suicidal ideation) and in the bridge expected influence values (D5 and D9), Supporting hypothesis 3. The lack of global structural differences may reflect that local variations were masked by broader similarities, leading to nonsignificant findings at the global level. Overall, when node directionality is not considered, the network structures of males and females appear largely similar, though subtle differences exist. Specifically, compared with males, the female network showed greater influence of D5 and D9 on other nodes, suggesting that females are more likely to be linked to other symptoms through suicidal ideation [74] and disordered eating behaviors [75]. In contrast, D8 was substantially more influential in the male network, indicating that males are more likely to connect to other symptoms through changes in motor activity. These findings support the notion that depressive symptoms exhibit sex differences [76]. Moreover, in both male and female networks, the bridge node was alienation from the mother, underscoring its central role in the development of depressive symptoms and parent-child alienation among left-behind children [77]. From the perspective of attachment theory, early maternal attachment is critical for healthy child development. On the one hand, alienation from the mother may disrupt secure attachment in left-behind children, contributing to depression and externalizing problems [78]. On the other hand, since mothers typically serve as the primary agents of daily parent-child communication, emotional disconnection caused by maternal alienation may further promote generalized parent-child alienation, especially given that fathers often demonstrate poorer communication [79].

DAG network

Bayesian network analysis reveals that depressive symptoms are positioned at the top layer of the DAG for both males and females, suggesting that depressive symptoms may predict parent-child alienation [80, 81]. This finding contrasts with prior studies that predominantly viewed parent-child alienation as a factor predicting depression [15]. Notably, in the male DAG, all parent-child alienation nodes are Located at the Lowest layer of the network and appear dependent on depressive symptoms, indicating a potential unidirectional predictive association between depressive symptoms and parent-child alienation with over 80% statistical probability. In contrast, in the female DAG, father-related alienation nodes are positioned at the intermediate layer, suggesting a potential bidirectional predictive association between depressive symptoms and parent-child alienation. This pattern implies that, for males, depressive symptoms may be more strongly predictive of subsequent parent-child alienation, whereas for females, depressive symptoms and parent-child alienation may exhibit mutual predictive associations. This difference could be related to females’ greater involvement in family interactions or emotional engagement with parents, which might be linked to higher depressive symptoms in the presence of alienation [82]. This study extends the understanding of the predictive associations between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms, particularly among female left-behind children, Supporting hypothesis 4 (i.e., parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms exhibit bidirectional predictive associations). However, these predictive associations require further validation through longitudinal follow-up studies to address the limitations of the current cross-sectional design.

Similarity analysis of the Bayesian network revealed that the DAG network similarity between males and females was only 0.22, and more than half of the nodes were not in the same direction. This suggests that there are differences in the interrelationships between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms between males and females, and thus interventions for both should be targeted. Of interest, although there were no significant differences in the total network structure between males and females in the networks of parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms, significant differences were found in the expected influence of some nodes and the strength of some edge links. In addition, network similarity and edge orientation consistency showed significant differences. This may lead clinicians to confuse the clinical presentation of male and female patients, thus ignoring the small differences between genders.

The findings of this study provide several practical implications for reducing parent–child alienation and depressive symptoms among left-behind children. First, given the bridging role of economic avoidance behaviors (M3 and F3) in parent–child alienation, interventions should promote healthy economic communication. This can be achieved by teaching children to confidently express their economic needs and encouraging parents to proactively offer support to foster emotional bonding. Second, the impact of perceived lack of parental love (M4 and F4) underscores the necessity of strengthening emotional connections. Parent training programs, combined with technologies such as video calls or digital storytelling, may help bridge parent–child alienation and enhance emotional closeness, particularly for females who are more reliant on parental emotional support. Third, addressing father–child alienation is particularly critical for males, as indicated by node F8. Even for long-distance father–child relationships, structured interactions and father-focused parenting workshops can help reduce alienation and enhance paternal involvement. Fourth, interventions should directly target core depressive symptoms, particularly fatigue (D4). In addition, appetite disturbances (D5), motor changes (D8), and suicidal ideation (D9) require gender-sensitive personalized interventions, including school-based psychoeducation, appropriate academic time management, healthy eating programs, physical exercise initiatives, and suicide prevention strategies. Finally, gender-sensitive personalized interventions are crucial: for males, interventions should prioritize reducing depressive symptoms to prevent subsequent alienation; for females, interventions should simultaneously address father-related alienation and depressive symptoms, particularly suicidal ideation. These strategies can be integrated into school, community, and caregiving systems, with counselors, teachers, and caregivers collaboratively monitoring risks and facilitating communication with absent parents. At the policy level, promoting affordable digital communication channels and providing training for caregivers can further support the maintenance of healthy parent–child relationships. Overall, these targeted, systemic interventions may help alleviate alienation and depression in left-behind children while addressing gender-specific vulnerabilities.

Limitations

Despite the contributions of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms. While cross-sectional surveys remain a dominant approach in the field due to practical constraints such as time, cost, and participant cooperation, longitudinal research is necessary to capture the temporal dynamics of these processes. Moreover, the reliance on self-report measures (e.g., the PHQ-9 and parent-child alienation scales) raises concerns regarding potential biases, including social desirability and recall bias. Second, the operationalization of gender relied exclusively on biological sex. Such a binary classification restricts the interpretive scope by neglecting gender identity, non-binary experiences, and intersectional factors, all of which may influence the manifestation of parent–child alienation and depressive symptoms. Future studies should adopt more inclusive and multidimensional measures of gender to capture these complexities. Third, the sample was drawn predominantly from western China, an economically less developed region characterized by a high prevalence of left-behind children. While this focus provides insights into a particularly vulnerable population, the findings may not generalize to other regions of China or international contexts, given variations in economic conditions, cultural traditions, and educational resources. Expanding future research to geographically and socioeconomically diverse samples would help assess the universality versus context-specificity of the observed associations. Finally, the study did not account for the potential protective role of grandparents, who frequently act as primary caregivers for left-behind children in rural China. Grandparental caregiving may mitigate some of the adverse consequences of parental absence and thus constitutes an important resilience factor. Future research should examine the quality and intensity of grandparental involvement to provide a more comprehensive understanding of children’s psychological adjustment. Taken together, these limitations underscore the importance of employing longitudinal designs, improving measurement approaches, incorporating diverse samples, and considering broader family dynamics to advance knowledge in this area.

Conclusion

This study explored the gender differences in parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms among left-behind children using network analysis. The key findings indicate that depressive symptoms are more prevalent among females, who also experience higher levels of parental alienation. Our analysis revealed gender-specific patterns in the relationship between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms, suggesting that the interactions between these factors may be more complex than previously thought. Furthermore, the study suggests a possible bidirectional relationship between parent-child alienation and depressive symptoms, with each potentially influencing the other. These findings have important implications for future interventions, which should take into account the gendered nature of parent-child alienation and depression. Specifically, interventions for females may need to address emotional support and attachment, while interventions for males may need to focus more on emotional regulation and coping strategies. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and explore the causal pathways between these variables through longitudinal studies. By broadening the understanding of the interactions between gender, parent-child alienation, and depressive symptoms, this study contributes to the development of more personalized, gender-sensitive interventions for left-behind children, ultimately improving their psychological well-being.
