Kidney and endothelial injury biomarkers as predictors of complications and mortality in patients with malaria: a systematic review
Authors: Jarinne Camilo Landim Nasserala, Francisco Naildo Cardoso Leitão, Amanda Vitória Rodrigues dos Santos, Alice Maria Costa Martins, Gdayllon Cavalcante Meneses, Rayane Nascimento de Souza, Geraldo Bezerra da Silva Junior, Elizabeth De Francesco Daher, Mauro José de Deus Morais

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) is a severe complication strongly associated with increased mortality. Introduction

Malaria remains a major global health problem, with an estimated 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths worldwide in 2023 [1, 2]. The burden is highest in endemic regions, particularly in Africa, while the Amazon region accounts for most malaria cases in Brazil [3, 4]. Five species of Plasmodium are known to cause malaria in humans: Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale, and P. knowlesi [4].

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent and severe complication of malaria, especially in Plasmodium falciparum infections, and is strongly associated with increased morbidity and mortality [5, 6]. Clinical manifestations include oliguria, metabolic acidosis, and electrolyte disturbances, reflecting significant renal dysfunction [5, 6].

The pathogenesis of malaria-associated AKI is complex and involves microvascular obstruction, hemolysis, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and hemodynamic alterations [2, 5] In addition to renal impairment, patients may develop anemia, jaundice, neurological dysfunction, and intravascular hemolysis, contributing to disease severity [1, 5].

Despite its clinical relevance, early detection of AKI remains challenging. Conventional biomarkers, such as serum creatinine and urea, are widely used but have limited sensitivity, as they typically reflect late-stage renal injury [6–8].

In recent years, novel renal biomarkers, including NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C, have shown potential for early detection of kidney injury [9] Endothelial biomarkers, such as angiopoietin-2, syndecan-1, and adhesion molecules, provide additional insight into microvascular dysfunction and disease progression [8–10, 12].

Although previous studies have evaluated biomarkers in tropical infections, evidence integrating renal and endothelial biomarkers as predictors of AKI, complications, and mortality in malaria remains limited [11, 13, 14].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the role of renal and endothelial biomarkers in malaria and to assess their association with acute kidney injury, disease severity, and clinical outcomes, including ICU admission and mortality.

Results

Literature selection process

The systematic search identified 253 records across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. After removal of duplicates and screening of titles and abstracts, potentially eligible studies were assessed in full text. A total of 13 studies published between 2015 and 2025 were included in the qualitative synthesis. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) and MINORS. Overall, studies demonstrated moderate to high quality. Common limitations included small sample sizes, heterogeneity in biomarker assessment, and limited adjustment for confounders. Detailed quality scores are presented in Supplementary Table 2 and 3.

Baseline characteristics of included studies

The included studies comprised 10,332 patients, including both adult and pediatric populations. Most studies involved Plasmodium falciparum, although other species such as P. vivax and P. knowlesi were also represented.

Geographically, studies were conducted in Africa (n = 4), Asia (n = 4), Europe (n = 2), Brazil (n = 1), and multinational settings (n = 1). The incidence of AKI was high, particularly in severe malaria.

AKI definitions varied across studies. Most studies used KDIGO criteria, while others applied RIFLE or AKIN classifications, and a few used study-specific definitions based on serum creatinine thresholds or urine output. This variability may influence the reported performance of biomarkers.

Biomarkers of tubular injury

NGAL levels were significantly elevated in patients with AKI and increased with disease severity, demonstrating strong associations with tubular injury and clinical outcomes [6, 23] Elevated NGAL was also linked to hypoxic brain injury (PRR 5.5, 95% CI 2.3–13.2), suggesting a role in multi-organ dysfunction. Although NGAL is a sensitive early marker of tubular injury, it is not kidney-specific. NGAL is also released by activated neutrophils as part of the acute-phase response. Therefore, in the context of severe malaria, where systemic inflammation is prominent, elevated NGAL levels may reflect both renal injury and inflammatory activation, potentially limiting its specificity for AKI [6, 23]

[25], showed that Cystatin C and kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) were identified as key biomarkers of tubular injury in patients with malaria-associated acute kidney injury (AKI). Cystatin C consistently detected a higher proportion of AKI cases compared to creatinine-based definitions, suggesting greater sensitivity for early renal dysfunction. In a comparative study, AKI incidence was significantly higher when assessed using cystatin C (51.8%) compared to KDIGO criteria based on creatinine (32.4%), with a significantly increased likelihood of AKI detection (OR = 2.2,95% CI 1.45–3.49) [25].

Similarly, KIM-1 expression was markedly elevated in patients with AKI and demonstrated strong correlations with renal function parameters, including serum creatinine (r = 0.83, p < 0.001) and urea levels (r = 0.73, p < 0.001), as well as histopathological evidence of tubular necrosis (r = 0.96, p < 0.001), supporting its role as a marker of structural tubular injury [16]. MMP-3 acts as a sheddase that cleaves the ectodomain of membrane-bound KIM-1, generating its soluble form (sKIM-1), which can be detected in urine. Thus, increased urinary KIM-1 reflects both tubular injury and active shedding processes, rather than simple elimination [16].

Together, these findings highlight that cystatin C, NGAL, and KIM-1 capture distinct but complementary dimensions of renal injury, with cystatin C detecting early functional decline, NGAL identifying acute tubular stress and injury, and KIM-1 reflecting established structural damage, supporting a multimarker approach for improved detection and risk stratification of malaria-associated AKI [6, 16, 25]. Endothelial injury biomarkers

Biomarkers of endothelial activation and glycocalyx degradation were consistently associated with the development of acute kidney injury (AKI), angiopoietin-2 was identified as a robust marker of endothelial dysfunction and was significantly associated with the occurrence of AKI. In a meta-analysis including 7453 patients, elevated Ang-2 levels were associated with an increased risk of AKI (OR = 1.58; 95% CI 1.39–1.80), with consistently higher levels observed in patients with AKI compared to those without [24]. Similarly, in a prospective cohort study, Ang-2 was an independent predictor of AKI, showing a strong association even after adjustment for parasitemia (OR = 4.41; 95% CI 2.02–9.63) [26].

Syndecan-1, a marker of glycocalyx degradation, was also strongly associated with disease severity and renal dysfunction. Elevated syndecan-1 levels were independently associated with severe malaria (OR = 3.72; 95% CI 2.01–6.89) and correlated with markers of endothelial activation, including angiopoietin-2 and ICAM-1, as well as with serum creatinine levels. These findings support the role of glycocalyx breakdown as a central mechanism linking endothelial injury to organ dysfunction [21].

Hemolysis and inflammatory biomarkers

CFHb, a marker of intravascular hemolysis, was significantly associated with the development of AKI. In a prospective cohort study, higher CFHb levels were independently associated with AKI (OR = 1.52; 95% CI 1.12–2.07), even after adjustment for parasitemia [18]. CFHb levels also correlated with serum creatinine (r = 0.31, p < 0.0001) and markers of microvascular dysfunction, suggesting that hemolysis contributes to renal injury through oxidative stress and endothelial damage. Furthermore, CFHb was linked to endothelial activation, reinforcing its role as a mediator between hemolysis and vascular dysfunction [19].

In contrast, sTREM-1, a marker of innate immune activation, demonstrated strong prognostic value for mortality. In a large prospective cohort study including 592 children with severe malaria, sTREM-1 showed good predictive performance for in-hospital mortality (AUC = 0.78; 95% CI 0.70–0.86), which improved when combined with other biomarkers (AUC = 0.80; 95% CI 0.72–0.87) [2]. Elevated sTREM-1 levels were also associated with higher disease severity and a markedly increased prevalence of AKI among non-survivors (86.1% vs 42.5%, p < 0.001), highlighting its role as a marker of systemic inflammatory burden and poor outcomes [19]

Taken together, these findings suggest that CFHb and sTREM-1 capture complementary mechanisms of malaria-associated AKI, with CFHb reflecting hemolysis-driven endothelial and oxidative injury, and sTREM-1 indicating systemic inflammatory activation and prognostic risk. Their integration supports a multidimensional model linking hemolysis, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and renal injury.

Oxidative stress biomarkers

Markers of oxidative stress, particularly F2-isoprostanes (F2-IsoPs) and isofurans (IsoFs), were strongly associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) and disease severity in malaria. Both biomarkers, which are products of lipid peroxidation, were significantly elevated in patients with AKI and showed robust correlations with renal dysfunction. In a prospective cohort study, levels of F2-IsoPs and IsoFs were strongly correlated with serum creatinine (r = 0.71 for both, p < 0.001), indicating a close relationship with kidney injury [18].

Importantly, these oxidative stress markers were also predictive of severe renal outcomes. Elevated F2-IsoPs and IsoFs were independently associated with the need for renal replacement therapy, with odds ratios of 7.37 (95% CI 1.86–29.23) and 5.50 (95% CI 1.76–17.24), respectively [18]. These findings suggest that oxidative damage plays a central role in the pathogenesis of malaria-associated AKI.

Additional biomarkers of renal dysfunction and endothelial activation

Additional biomarkers reflecting renal dysfunction and endothelial activation included salivary urea nitrogen (SUN), osteoprotegerin (OPG), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and E-selectin. SUN demonstrated good diagnostic performance as a non-invasive marker of kidney dysfunction, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 (95% CI 0.79–0.96), showing high specificity (98%) but moderate sensitivity (67%) for the detection of acute kidney disease [10]. Inflammatory biomarkers

Pro-inflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and the IFN-γ/IL-10 ratio, were significantly associated with acute kidney injury (AKI), disease severity, and adverse outcomes in malaria. Elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were observed in patients with severe disease and renal dysfunction, reflecting an amplified systemic inflammatory response. In addition, an increased IFN-γ/IL-10 ratio, indicative of a shift toward a pro-inflammatory state, was significantly associated with AKI and worse clinical outcomes, suggesting an imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory pathways [15]. TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) is a member of the tumor necrosis factor superfamily that induces apoptosis through activation of death receptors. In the kidney, TRAIL has been implicated in tubular epithelial cell apoptosis and may contribute to renal injury in inflammatory conditions. Its role in malaria-associated AKI likely reflects the interaction between immune activation and renal cell death pathways. Discussion

Clinical implications and feasibility

The findings of this review suggest that renal and endothelial biomarkers may improve early detection and risk stratification of malaria-associated AKI. However, their translation into clinical practice requires careful consideration of feasibility, particularly in resource-limited settings where malaria is most prevalent.

While biomarkers such as NGAL, KIM-1, and angiopoietin-2 have demonstrated moderate to good diagnostic performance in some studies (e.g., AUC values ranging from approximately 0.70 to 0.80), their routine use may be limited by the need for specialized assays, laboratory infrastructure, and trained personnel. In contrast, simpler and low-cost tools, such as salivary urea nitrogen (SUN) dipstick testing, may offer a more feasible alternative for early screening, particularly at the primary care level [12, 24].

Therefore, the implementation of biomarkers should consider the balance between diagnostic accuracy and real-world applicability. In higher-level facilities, multi-biomarker panels may support clinical decision-making, including ICU admission and renal replacement therapy, whereas in peripheral settings, point-of-care approaches may provide greater impact.

Malaria-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) is a complex and multifactorial condition resulting from the interplay between hemolysis, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and direct tubular injury [2, 6, 18, 19, 24].

Tubular injury biomarkers, including NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C, consistently reflected different stages of renal damage. NGAL emerged as an early and sensitive marker of tubular stress and was associated with AKI severity and extra-renal complications such as hypoxic brain injury [6, 20, 22, 23] Cystatin C demonstrated greater sensitivity than creatinine for early detection of renal dysfunction, identifying a higher proportion of AKI cases [25]. In contrast, KIM-1 was strongly associated with structural tubular injury and correlated with histopathological damage and renal function markers [16] Together, these findings support a multimarker strategy that captures both early functional impairment and established tubular injury.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies on kidney biomarkers in tropical infections, which have demonstrated the limitations of serum creatinine and the potential of novel biomarkers for early detection of renal injury. Similar to prior reports, tubular biomarkers such as NGAL and KIM-1 showed consistent associations with AKI, while endothelial markers, including angiopoietin-2, have been linked to disease severity and microvascular dysfunction. However, unlike previous reviews that focused on isolated biomarkers, this study integrates renal and endothelial pathways, providing a more comprehensive understanding of malaria-associated AKI [2, 23, 25].

Endothelial dysfunction was a central component of malaria-associated AKI. Angiopoietin-2 was consistently associated with AKI and disease severity, reflecting endothelial activation and vascular instability [24, 26]. Similarly, syndecan-1, a marker of glycocalyx degradation, was strongly correlated with AKI, microvascular dysfunction, and disease severity, supporting the role of endothelial barrier disruption in organ injury [21]. Additional markers such as ICAM-1, E-selectin, and osteoprotegerin further reinforced the contribution of endothelial activation and vascular inflammation to renal impairment [24, 26].

Hemolysis and oxidative stress emerged as key upstream drivers of endothelial and renal injury. Elevated levels of cell-free hemoglobin (CFHb) were independently associated with AKI and correlated with markers of microvascular dysfunction [18]. CFHb-mediated oxidative stress leads to lipid peroxidation, generating F2-isoprostanes and isofurans, which were strongly associated with renal dysfunction and the need for renal replacement therapy [18]. These findings support a mechanistic pathway linking hemolysis to oxidative damage, vasoconstriction, and kidney injury.

Inflammatory pathways further amplify this process. Elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, along with an increased IFN-γ/IL-10 ratio, indicate a pro-inflammatory imbalance associated with AKI and disease severity [15, 17]. In addition, sTREM-1, a marker of innate immune activation, demonstrated strong prognostic value for mortality and was associated with increased AKI prevalence among non-survivors [19]. These results highlight the role of immune dysregulation as a critical mediator linking systemic inflammation to endothelial and renal injury.

Other biomarkers, such as SUN, provide additional clinical utility. Salivary urea nitrogen demonstrated good diagnostic performance for kidney dysfunction, particularly in resource-limited settings, offering a simple and non-invasive alternative for AKI detection [10]. This is particularly relevant in endemic regions, where delayed diagnosis of AKI contributes to increased morbidity and mortality. SUN was measured at the time of patient presentation and demonstrated good diagnostic performance for kidney dysfunction, supporting its use as an early, non-invasive point-of-care screening tool.

Taken together, these findings support a multidimensional pathophysiological model of malaria-associated AKI, in which hemolysis, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and tubular injury are tightly interconnected and mutually reinforcing processes. This integrated framework highlights that no single biomarker is sufficient to fully characterize disease complexity. Instead, combined biomarker panels may provide superior diagnostic and prognostic performance.

From a clinical perspective, the incorporation of renal and endothelial biomarkers into routine assessment could enable earlier identification of high-risk patients, guide monitoring strategies, and improve clinical decision-making.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies regarding patient populations, malaria species, AKI definitions, and biomarker measurement techniques. Most studies were observational, limiting causal inference. Additionally, the lack of standardized cutoff values for many biomarkers restricts their immediate clinical applicability.

Future research should focus on large prospective studies to validate combined biomarker panels, establish clinically meaningful thresholds, and determine their incremental value over traditional markers such as serum creatinine. Further mechanistic studies are also needed to better elucidate the interactions between hemolysis, endothelial dysfunction, and immune activation in malaria-associated AKI.

Strengths

This systematic review presents several strengths. First, it follows the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO, ensuring methodological transparency and reproducibility. Second, it integrates both renal and endothelial biomarkers within a unified pathophysiological framework, providing a comprehensive understanding of malaria-associated acute kidney injury (AKI).

Unlike previous reviews that focused predominantly on isolated renal biomarkers, this study incorporates markers of endothelial activation, glycocalyx degradation, oxidative stress, immune imbalance, and hemolysis. This integrative approach allows for a broader interpretation of the mechanisms linking malaria severity, microvascular dysfunction, and renal injury.

Additionally, the inclusion of both human and experimental studies contributes to translational relevance by bridging clinical findings with mechanistic evidence. The review also consolidates biomarker evidence across different Plasmodium species, increasing its external applicability in endemic and imported malaria settings.

Limitations

Although meta-analysis can strengthen quantitative inference, it was not feasible in this review due to marked heterogeneity across studies. Differences in biomarker assays, biological matrices, timing of measurement, and AKI definitions limit comparability, even when using standardized approaches such as standardized mean differences. Future studies with standardized methodologies and reporting are needed to enable robust meta-analytic synthesis.

Second, most studies were observational, limiting the ability to establish causal relationships between biomarker elevation and clinical outcomes. Third, several biomarkers lack standardized cutoff values, which may limit direct clinical applicability.

Fourth, differences in healthcare infrastructure between endemic and non-endemic regions may affect the generalizability of findings. Finally, publication bias cannot be completely excluded, as studies with negative biomarker results may be underreported.

Future large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate the prognostic performance of combined renal and endothelial biomarker panels and to define clinically actionable thresholds.

Conclusion

Malaria-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) remains a frequent and severe complication, particularly in severe Plasmodium infections, and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.

This systematic review suggests that renal and endothelial biomarkers may provide useful insights into the early detection and risk stratification of malaria-associated AKI. Tubular injury biomarkers, including NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C, were consistently associated with AKI, while endothelial biomarkers such as angiopoietin-2 and syndecan-1 were linked to disease severity and microvascular dysfunction.

In addition, biomarkers of hemolysis, inflammation, and oxidative stress were associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including renal replacement therapy and mortality. These findings support the potential role of combined biomarker approaches in improving clinical assessment.

However, the interpretation of these results should be cautious due to the observational nature of the included studies, heterogeneity in study design, and lack of standardized biomarker thresholds.

Further well-designed prospective studies are needed to validate these biomarkers, establish clinically relevant cut-off values, and determine their role in routine clinical practice.
