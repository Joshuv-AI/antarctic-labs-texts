Novel tetrameric bispecific KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed memory-like NK cells enhance antitumor efficacy in gastric cancer
Authors: Mengzhu Li, Tianran Chen, Siwen Wu, Yuxiang Li, Qin Liu, Ying Wang, Jingyi Guo, Lanqi Cen, Lu Zou, Manman Tian, Wenxiu Chen, Rutian Li, Jie Shen, Baorui Liu, Jie Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytokine-induced memory-like natural killer (CIML NK) cells demonstrate potent antitumor efficacy against hematological malignancies. Nevertheless, the therapeutic potential of adoptive NK cell transfer remains constrained in solid tumors due to insufficient tumor infiltration efficiency. Developing a novel tetravalent bispecific killer engager (BiKE) to modify CIML NK cells would be a promising strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy.MethodsWe employed SpyTag-SpyCatcher technology to engineer tetravalent BiKEs, specifically KK-LC-1×CD16A, and to equip CIML NK cells with this protein. Flow cytometry and a luciferase reporter gene system were used to evaluate the activation and cytotoxicity of CIML NK cells. Histological analysis and in vivo real-time fluorescence imaging were employed in the xenograft tumor model to confirm the tumor-infiltrating effectiveness of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells. The in vivo antitumor efficacy of armed CIML NK cells was evaluated in subcutaneous gastric cancer xenograft models.ResultsWe successfully developed a tetravalent NK cell engager, KK-LC-1×CD16A, which exhibits excellent binding affinity to its targets. CIML NK cells armed with this protein exhibit enhanced activation and augmented cytotoxicity against tumor cells. In xenograft tumor models, the accumulation of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells at the tumor site was significantly increased, resulting in marked suppression of tumor growth and prolonged survival in mice.ConclusionKK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells demonstrate significant clinical potential as a multifunctional therapeutic platform, effectively overcoming both tumor infiltration limitations and functional suppression in conventional NK cell therapies. This approach provides valuable insights for optimizing the therapeutic efficacy of CIML NK cell therapy.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Gastric Cancer, Adoptive cell therapy - ACT

Background

Gastric cancer remains the fifth most prevalent malignancy worldwide and ranks third in global cancer-related mortality, posing a substantial public health challenge.1 2 Clinical evidence shows that natural killer (NK) cell infiltration density and functional competence in the tumor microenvironment (TME) are independent prognostic markers in gastric cancer.3 4

Cytokine-induced memory-like (CIML) NK cells, generated via sequential interleukin (IL)-12, IL-15, and IL-18 activation, demonstrate superior proliferation, persistence, and antitumor activity compared with conventional NK cells.5 Phase I trials have validated their efficacy in controlling disease and inducing remission in relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML).6 Ongoing investigations are exploring therapeutic applications in epithelial cancers, particularly ovarian and hepatocellular carcinomas.7

Kita-Kyushu lung cancer antigen-1 (KK-LC-1), a cancer-testis antigen, is expressed in 82% of gastric cancers and 79% of early-stage cases.8 Moreover, its high expression in gastric cancer is strongly associated with longer overall survival.6 9 Preclinical studies show KK-LC-1-specific peptide-drug conjugates exert potent antitumor effects in KK-LC-1-positive gastric cancer models with favorable safety.8 These findings indicate that KK-LC-1 is a promising therapeutic target for gastric cancer. CD16A (FcγRIIIa), a low-affinity Fc receptor expressed on NK cell surfaces, serves as the primary mediator of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and emerges as a strategic focus in CIML NK cell-targeted immune engager development.10 11

Adoptive CIML NK cell therapy shows limited efficacy in solid tumors due to poor infiltration. Several innovative approaches have emerged to enhance NK cell function, including antibody conjugation and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-NK engineering.12,17 The CAR-NK platform has shown particular promise in recent studies. CAR-NK cells provide targeted specificity, with anti-CD19 CAR-NK cells showing safety and efficacy in B-cell malignancies.18 Additionally, numerous preclinical studies have highlighted the potential of CAR-NK cells in solid tumor models such as pancreatic cancer,19 breast cancer,20 and glioblastoma.21 While CAR-NK cells can target multiple antigens, their complex manufacturing process limits flexibility, as each target requires a new product. Therefore, we propose the use of immune cell engagers to modify NK cells, which may potentially address these limitations. Currently, several NK cell engagers targeting CD16A have been developed and have demonstrated promising therapeutic efficacy in both preclinical and early clinical studies. A notable example is AFM13, a tetravalent bispecific antibody that simultaneously engages CD30 on tumor cells and CD16A on NK cells. This construct has shown significant antitumor activity in patients with Hodgkin’s lymphoma and other CD30-positive lymphomas, underscoring the clinical potential of CD16A-redirected NK cell therapy.22 23

Traditional immune cell engagers are primarily constructed using full-length antibodies and single-chain variable fragments (scFvs). However, such scFv-based engagers are prone to misfolding and aggregation under certain conditions, which may compromise their bioactivity and functional stability.24 Additionally, during production, these multivalent molecules often face challenges such as inefficient heavy and light chain pairing and difficulties in assembly, limiting their broader application.25 With continuous advances in protein engineering technology, designed ankyrin repeat proteins (DARPins) have emerged as an important platform for constructing multispecific biologics. DARPin molecules do not rely on heavy and light chain pairing, thereby overcoming some inherent limitations of scFvs and enabling more efficient assembly into trispecific or multivalent complexes. At the same time, DARPins maintain high affinity and complete antigen-binding capability, demonstrating significant potential for clinical application. The benefits of these molecules, including their low immunogenicity, high stability, ease of large-scale production, and cost-effectiveness in prokaryotic expression systems, serve to advance their development and utilization in the realm of therapeutics.26 27 Currently, DARPins are widely used across multiple domains, including protein-drug conjugates,28 radionuclide imaging,29 and cell engagers.30 The technology has shown significant progress in clinical application, exemplified by MP0250, a tetravalent DARPins molecule that targets vascular endothelial growth factor and hepatocyte growth factor. This compound is currently undergoing clinical trials for the treatment of multiple myeloma, a hematologic malignancy.31 Capitalizing on the advantageous properties of DARPins, our team established a dedicated DARPin library and a phage display screening platform. Using this foundation, we employed phage display technology to identify two unique DARPins demonstrating specific binding affinity for CD16A and KK-LC-1, respectively.

In this research, we designed a tetravalent bispecific killer engagers (BiKEs), KK-LC-1×CD16A. Given the complex structure of tetravalent proteins and the challenges associated with their production and purification, we introduced a pair of natural molecular glues, SpyCatcher/SpyTag (SpyC/SpyT). These were fused with the effector end (anti-CD16A DARPin) and the targeting end (anti-KK-LC-1 DARPin) of the fusion protein, respectively, allowing for separate production and purification. Leveraging their ability to spontaneously form irreversible covalent bonds, we successfully obtained the desired tetravalent bispecific fusion protein in vitro. Using this agent, KK-LC-1 DARPin could be armed on the CIML NK cell surface via the CD16A DARPin, as illustrated in figure 1A. The functional characterization of these armed NK cells validated their capacity for targeting tumors and enhancing cytotoxicity. Subsequent in vivo investigations underscored their significant therapeutic effectiveness against established solid tumors.

Methods

Cell lines

Human gastric cancer cell lines MKN45 and HGC27 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology. The MKN45 cell line naturally expresses the KK-LC-1 antigen, while the HGC27 cell line was engineered to be KK-LC-1 antigen-positive through adenoviral transfection. The cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Lonsera, Uruguay), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cell line authentication was performed through phenotypic and genotypic characterization. All cells used in experiments were tested for mycoplasma contamination to ensure mycoplasma-free status.

Induction of NK cell differentiation and expansion

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were pre-activated with IL-12 (10 ng/mL; Xiamen Amoytop Biotech, China), IL-15 (50 ng/mL; Xiamen Amoytop Biotech, China), and IL-18 (50 ng/mL; Xiamen Amoytop Biotech, China) for 16 hours to induce differentiation into memory-like NK cells. After two washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), irradiated K562 cells expressing IL-21 on their membrane were co-cultured with PBMCs at a 1:6 ratio to facilitate NK cell expansion. During the initial expansion phase (day 2 to day 7), IL-2 (10 IU/mL; Xiamen Amoytop Biotech, China) and IL-15 (5 ng/mL) were added to increase NK cell proportion. Starting from day 8, the IL-2 concentration was raised to 100 IU/mL to sustain significant NK cell amplification. By day 14 to day 21, the expanded NK cells reached >90% purity, meeting the quality criteria for subsequent applications.

Production and synthesis of KK-LC-1×CD16A

Our team used phage display to screen and identify DARPins that specifically target the extracellular domains of KK-LC-1 (residues 22–113) and CD16A. The selection of DARPins binding to KK-LC-1 and CD16A was conducted following the methodologies outlined in the respective patent applications (CN202411728537X and CN202311760338.2). The following fusion protein constructs were designed: for the experimental group, the bivalent proteins KK-LC-1-SpyC (with the structure: His-tag–KK-LC-1 DARPin–SpyCatcher–KK-LC-1 DARPin) and CD16A-SpyT (with the structure: CD16A DARPin–SpyTag–CD16A DARPin–His-tag); as a control, the monovalent counterparts included KK-LC-1-SpyC (His-tag–KK-LC-1 DARPin–SpyCatcher) and CD16A-SpyT (SpyTag–CD16A DARPin–His-tag); additionally, enhanced green fluorescence protein (eGFP)-labeled bivalent imaging variants, eGFP-KK-LC-1-SpyC (His-tag–eGFP–KK-LC-1 DARPin–SpyCatcher–KK-LC-1 DARPin) and eGFP-CD16A-SpyT (His-tag–eGFP– CD16A DARPin–SpyTag–CD16A DARPin) was constructed.

These DNA sequences were synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) and cloned into the bacterial expression vector pET-21b. After transformation into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) for self-induction-based protein expression, bacterial cells were lysed via sonication. Protein purification was performed using an ÄKTA system equipped with a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva, Connecticut, USA). Purified KK-LC-1-SpyC and CD16A-SpyT proteins were analyzed by 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), dialyzed against PBS, and filter-sterilized (0.22 µm). Protein quantification used a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Equimolar reactions (1:1) of KK-LC-1-SpyC and CD16A-SpyT generated the tetravalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16A. A bivalent control (KK-LC-1×CD16 A1+1) was synthesized analogously for functional comparison.

Validation of protein binding function by flow cytometry

For the combination experiments, we synthesized the following fluorescently tagged proteins: eGFP-KK-LC-1-SpyC, eGFP-CD16A-SpyT, and eGFP-KK-LC-1×CD16A. MKN45 cells, HGC27 cells, KK-LC-1-overexpressing HGC27 cells, or NK cells (2×10⁵ cells per sample) were incubated with 6.7 µg of eGFP-KK-LC-1×CD16A or an isotype control at 4°C for 30 min. Cells were then washed with PBS and subjected to flow cytometric analysis. KK-LC-1 expression on both MKN45 and KK-LC-1-overexpressing HGC27 cells was confirmed via a Leica TCS SP5 microscope (Leica, Germany). Flow cytometry was performed using a CytoFLEX instrument (Beckman Coulter, California, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (V.10.8).

Conjugate stability assessment

The stability assay for the conjugates was carried out in a complete medium with a low IL-15 dosage (1 ng/mL) to maintain cell viability without inducing significant proliferation. The eGFP-KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed NK cells were cultivated under these conditions (37°C, 5% CO₂) and evaluated using flow cytometry at designated time points. Prior to the analysis, cells underwent a gentle centrifugation step (180× g, 5 min, room temperature) to minimize aggregate formation.

NK cell activation and cytotoxicity assays

NK cells were armed with KK-LC-1×CD16A or the corresponding control reagent at a concentration of 1.25 µg per 1×105 NK cells and co-cultured with target cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 10:1. After incubation at 37°C for 24 hours, the activation status of NK cells was analyzed by flow cytometry using the following antibodies: anti-CD3-FITC (AB_395739, BD Biosciences), anti-CD56-BB700 (AB_2744430, BD Biosciences), anti-CD137-PE (AB_396252, BD Biosciences), anti-CD69-BV421 (AB_2737863, BD Biosciences), and anti-CD25-APC (V C012, BD Biosciences). MKN45, HGC27, HGC27(oe-KK-LC-1), MDA-MB-231 and HepG2 cell lines were stably transduced to express luciferase for quantitative cytotoxicity assessment. Target cells were co-cultured with NK cells at indicated E:T ratios in the presence of graded concentrations of KK-LC-1×CD16A or control molecules. Following a 6-hour incubation at 37°C with 5% CO₂, cytotoxicity was assessed using a luciferase-based assay as described previously in a study by Bei et al.32 D-luciferin was added to a final concentration of 1 mM, and luminescence was measured using a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Specific cytotoxicity was calculated as: specific lysis (%) = 100 × (1 − (experimental signal − background) / (maximum signal − background)), where untreated target cells defined maximum survival and cell-free wells provided background values.

Intracellular cytokine detection

NK cells and MKN45 target cells were co-cultured at an E:T ratio of 5:1. GolgiStop reagent (BD Biosciences) was added following the manufacturer’s protocol. After co-incubation for 8 hours, NK cells were stained with a live/dead dye (FVS780; BD Biosciences) for 15 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS containing 3% FBS (Lonsera, Uruguay) and then incubated with anti-CD3-FITC and anti-CD56-BB700 antibody for 30 min at room temperature, followed by two additional PBS washes. The cells were subsequently fixed and permeabilized using a Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences). Intracellular cytokines were detected by staining the cells with anti-IFN-γ-PE antibody (AB_397224, BD Biosciences) and anti-TNF-α-APC antibody (AB_398566, BD Biosciences) for 1 hour at 4°C. Following staining, cells were washed with permeabilization buffer and analyzed using a CytoFLEx flow cytometer.

CD107a assay

NK cells and MKN45 target cells were co-cultured at an E:T ratio of 5:1, with the addition of anti-CD107a-PE antibody. GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences) was introduced after 1 hour of co-incubation, followed by a continued co-culture period of 8 hours. Subsequent steps involved washing the cells with PBS containing 3% FBS, staining with a live/dead cell dye (FVS780) for 15 min at room temperature, and subsequently labeling with corresponding surface marker antibodies, including anti-CD3-FITC and anti-CD56-BB700. The stained cells were ultimately subjected to analysis using a CytoFLEx flow cytometer.

Xenograft mouse model

The experimental design aimed to minimize animal use and reduce suffering. Female BALB/c nude mice, 5-week-old, were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Separately, female NSG mice, 5-week-old, were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Mice were inoculated with 3×106 MKN45 cells or 4×106 KK-LC-1-overexpressing HGC27 cells in subcutaneous tumor models.

In vivo near-infrared imaging of NK cell trafficking

This study employed near-infrared imaging technology to assess the in vivo tumor-targeting efficacy of KK-LC-1×CD16A on CIML NK cells. CIML NK cells were labeled with the near-infrared fluorescent probe DiR (Cambridge Biotechnology, China). In a subcutaneous MKN45 tumor model, mice were intravenously injected with 1×107 CIML NK cells subjected to four treatments: untreated CIML NK cells, KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells, CIML NK cells armed with CD16A-SpyT, and CIML NK cells co-delivered with free KK-LC-1-SpyC. In vivo fluorescence scanning using the CRi Maestro in vivo auto-imaging system was conducted at specified intervals to track CIML NK cell distribution and migration. Subsequently, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia for tissue-level fluorescence imaging analysis of excised tumor tissues, liver, and kidneys.

Immunofluorescence staining of human gastric cancer tissues

Human gastric cancer tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Pathology, Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. Specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), tissue sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated with KK-LC-1×CD16A (1 mg/mL, 100 µL) at 4°C overnight. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (GenScript, #A01802) for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with 4'-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, #C1002) for 5 minutes prior to mounting. Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope with channels configured for Cy5 and DAPI fluorescence.

NK cell labeling and in vivo targeting validation

NK cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) to confirm their targeting ability. Subsequently, the 1×10⁷ NK cells were treated according to their respective experimental group protocols prior to in vivo administration: Cells in the NK+KK-LC-1-SpyC group were co-incubated with 55 µg of soluble KK-LC-1-SpyC without washing; NK, NK+CD16A-SpyT, and NK+KK-LC-1×CD16A groups: Incubated with 45 µg (CD16A-SpyT), 100 µg (KK-LC-1×CD16A), or PBS (NK group only) of the corresponding reagent, followed by thorough washing with PBS to remove unbound material. All treated NK cells were subsequently administered via tail vein injection into tumor-bearing mice. Tumors were excised 24 hours post-injection and subjected to frozen sectioning. The sections were then stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and imaged at 200× magnification.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy and safety evaluation in tumor-bearing mice

Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to five groups. On reaching a tumor volume of approximately 100 mm³ post-implantation, each mouse was intravenously administered with 1×107 CIML NK cells. The 1×10⁷ NK cells were treated according to their respective experimental group protocols prior to in vivo administration: Cells in the NK+KK-LC-1-SpyC group were co-incubated with 55 µg of soluble KK-LC-1-SpyC without washing; NK, NK+CD16A-SpyT, and NK+KK-LC-1×CD16A groups: Incubated with 45 µg (CD16A-SpyT), 100 µg (KK-LC-1×CD16A), or PBS (NK group only) of the corresponding reagent, followed by thorough washing with PBS to remove unbound material. All treated NK cells were subsequently administered via tail vein injection into tumor-bearing mice. A week later, mice in each group received a second intravenous injection of the same treated cells. Recombinant human IL-2 (50,000 IU per mouse) was intraperitoneally injected three times weekly to sustain CIML NK cell activity. Tumor size was monitored every 3 days using the formula length × width² × 0.5 to assess the antitumor efficacy of KK-LC-1×CD16A and the survival duration of the mice. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 1000 mm³, and tumor nodules were weighed and imaged. Blood samples were collected from the orbital sinus for safety assessment, and major organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining. Serum biochemical analysis of blood samples included measurements of blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase.

Statistical analysis

Results

Protein engineering, expression, and characterization of KK-LC-1×CD16A

To synthesize KK-LC-1×CD16A, two fusion proteins were created: KK-LC-1-SpyC and CD16A-SpyT. Both fusion proteins contained a His tag for affinity purification (figure 2A). Expression in E. coli BL21(DE3) was successful, with high solubility. SDS-PAGE analysis showed bands at approximately 45 kDa for KK-LC-1-SpyC and 37 kDa for CD16A-SpyT. Mixing the two proteins in a 1:1 molar ratio resulted in the formation of the tetravalent KK-LC-1×CD16A, which was verified through SDS-PAGE to exhibit a molecular weight of approximately 82 kDa (online supplemental figure 1).

Initially, KK-LC-1 expression in different cell lines was assessed with an anti-KK-LC-1 antibody (online supplemental figure 2A,B), and the binding capability of the tetravalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16A to these cells was then evaluated. Flow cytometry validated the specific binding of KK-LC-1×CD16A to KK-LC-1-positive cells, such as MKN45 and KK-LC-1-overexpressing HGC27, while showing no binding to KK-LC-1-negative wild-type HGC27 cells. Specific binding to NK cells was also effectively demonstrated (figure 1B,C and online supplemental figure 3). These results indicate specific targeting of KK-LC-1 by the protein. Consistent with these findings, confocal microscopy revealed circular binding patterns of the tetravalent complex on KK-LC-1-positive cell membranes, with no binding observed on negative controls, supporting its membrane-associated targeting behavior and high binding specificity (online supplemental figure 4).

Immunofluorescence analysis of KK-LC-1-positive gastric cancer tissues demonstrated that the tetravalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16A specifically binds to tumor regions, exhibiting characteristic membrane-localized ring-like fluorescence staining. These results confirm the specific binding of KK-LC-1×CD16A to KK-LC-1-expressing human gastric tumor tissues and support its potential clinical applicability for targeted recognition of KK-LC-1-positive malignancies (figure 2D). The dose-response relationship of KK-LC-1×CD16A in NK cell binding was investigated, showing saturation at 2.5 µg of KK-LC-1×CD16A for 2×105 NK cells (figure 2E).

The binding stability of KK-LC-1×CD16A on NK cells was monitored over a 48-hour period at multiple time points (2, 12, 24, 36 and 48 hours). Fluorescence analysis showed that 80.1% of NK cells remained protein-bound at the 2-hour time point. This percentage gradually declined over time, reaching 58.6% by 48 hours. A statistically significant decrease was observed between the 36-hour and 48-hour time points (figure 1F,G). Overall, the fluorescence intensity decreased to 20% of the initial value after 48 hours, indicating moderate retention of the complex on the cell surface. With a low concentration of IL-15 (1 ng/mL) in the NK cell culture medium, minimal proliferation of NK cells occurred within 48 hours, ruling out a reduction in fluorescence signal attributed to cell proliferation.

KK-LC-1×CD16A elicits tumor-specific CIML NK cell activation and cytotoxicity

In this investigation, CIML NK cells were derived from PBMCs sourced from seven healthy individuals. The cells underwent activation with IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18, expansion using irradiated K562 cell line, and were collected around 14 days post-activation. Flow cytometry assessment of the cultured CIML NK cells exhibited purity levels surpassing 90%, with CD16 expression exceeding 90% in five of the seven donors (online supplemental figure 5). CD69 upregulation enhances NK cell cytotoxicity, improving their ability to target infected or transformed cells.33 34 CD25 upregulation in NK cells forms a high-affinity IL-2 receptor complex (CD25/CD122/CD132), boosting IL-2 signaling.35 36 Additionally, CD137 is transiently expressed on activated immune cells and regulates cytokine-driven NK cell proliferation and survival.37,39 After 24 hours of co-culture with KK-LC-1-positive MKN45 cells, CIML NK cells armed with KK-LC-1×CD16A showed significantly increased surface expression of CD137, CD69, and CD25, indicating effective activation (figure 2B,C). The extent of CD25 upregulation exhibited considerable donor-dependent variation, with some donors displaying only modest increase (online supplemental figure 6). In control experiments using the KK-LC-1-negative HGC27 cell line, none of these activation markers showed significant elevation compared with baseline, confirming that NK cells remained unactivated (online supplemental figure 7A). This further supports that the activation effect is target-dependent and highly selective.

Furthermore, NK cell secretion of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interferon (IFN)-γ on activation correlates with their functional maturation stage.40,42 Following 8 hours of co-incubation with KK-LC-1-positive MKN45 cells, CIML NK cells armed with KK-LC-1×CD16A exhibited significantly elevated intracellular expression levels of T-helper 1 (Th1)-type cytokines, including IFN-γ and TNF-α (figure 1D). These armed NK cells also demonstrated increased expression of CD107a, a degranulation marker associated with cytotoxicity (figure 1E), indicating enhanced functional activity.43 44 Furthermore, KK-LC-1×CD16A enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity in a dose-dependent manner against various KK-LC-1-positive target cells, including MKN45, KK-LC-1-overexpressing HGC27, HepG2, and MDA-MB-231 (figure 2F,G; online supplemental figure 8A,D). This effect was not observed with KK-LC-1-negative cells (online supplemental figure 7B), demonstrating that the killing activity is target-specific and dose-dependent. We further evaluated the killing efficacy of armed CIML NK cells against multiple KK-LC-1-positive cell lines (such as HePG2 and MDA-MB-231) at various E:T ratios (online supplemental figure 8B,C,E,F). These results indicated that KK-LC-1×CD16A mediated potent NK-cell cytotoxicity in an E:T ratio-dependent manner.

To assess the direct impact of the protein on tumor cell viability, KK-LC-1×CD16A was co-cultured with tumor cells alone for 24 hours. Results showed no significant impact on tumor cell viability (online supplemental figure 9A,B). Additionally, to assess the direct impact of the protein on NK cell viability, KK-LC-1×CD16A was co-cultured with NK cells alone for 24 hours. Results showed no significant impact on NK cell viability (online supplemental figure 10A,B). Notably, even after overnight incubation for 24 hours, evaluation of NK cell activation markers and cytokine secretion demonstrated no induction of NK cell activation in the absence of tumor cells, further confirming the favorable safety profile and low immunogenicity of the protein (online supplemental figure 10C,D). In summary, KK-LC-1×CD16A effectively activates tumor-specific CIML NK cells and significantly enhances their cytotoxic function in a target-specific manner, supporting its potential as a promising immune-engaging therapeutic strategy.

Superiority of the tetravalent KK-LC-1×CD16A protein over bivalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16A1+1 variant

To evaluate the functional benefits of the tetravalent protein form, we generated the bivalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16 A1+1 concurrently (figure 3A). On exposure to MKN45 cells, CIML NK cells equipped with KK-LC-1×CD16A demonstrated elevated levels of CD137, CD69, and CD107a in comparison to those armed with the bivalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16 A1+1, while showing no significant variance in CD25 late activation marker expression (figure 3B,C and online supplemental figure 11). KK-LC-1×CD16A outperformed bivalent KK-LC-1×CD16 A1+1 in stimulating IFN-γ and TNF-γ production by CIML NK cells (figure 3D). A similar enhancing effect was also observed using KK-LC-1-overexpressing HGC27 cells (figure 3E,F). The NK killing enhancement by KK-LC-1×CD16A surpassed that of the bivalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16 A1+1 across all doses (figure 3G,H). Additionally, KK-LC-1×CD16A armed CIML NK cell demonstrated more potent cytotoxicity in an E:T ratio-dependent manner, with tumor lysis still evident even at a reduced E:T ratio of 1:1 (figure 3I). In summary, the tetravalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16A armed CIML NK cells more effectively enhanced the activation and cytotoxicity compared with the bivalent protein KK-LC-1×CD16 A1+1 armed.

KK-LC-1×CD16A modification enhances the infiltration of CIML NK cells in xenograft mouse tumors

In vivo near-infrared imaging revealed that KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells effectively homed to subcutaneous tumors, exhibiting markedly higher fluorescence signals at the tumor site compared with the other control groups (figure 4A and online supplemental figure 12). To confirm these observations, tumors were excised 24 hours post-treatment for ex vivo fluorescence quantification (figure 4B), which aligned with the in vivo imaging results, demonstrating the highest CIML NK cell accumulation in the KK-LC-1×CD16A group. Conversely, no significant enhancement in tumor targeting was observed in the control group. The fluorescence signal intensity of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells peaked at the tumor site 24 hours post-treatment, remained elevated for up to 48 hours, and gradually declined by 72 hours post-treatment (figure 4A,C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed a significantly greater distribution of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells within tumor tissues compared with the control group (figure 4D,E), underscoring the substantial enhancement of CIML NK cell tumor-targeting capability conferred by KK-LC-1×CD16A modification.

KK-LC-1×CD16A modification of CIML NK cells effectively inhibits tumor growth in xenograft mouse models

To further confirm the inhibitory impact of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells on KK-LC-1-positive tumors, we developed two subcutaneous tumor models (figure 5A,D). Tumors exhibited rapid growth in the initial four groups of mice, whereas significant tumor suppression and prolonged survival of mice were achieved by KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells (figure 5B,C,E,G). In the MKN45 xenograft model, the KK-LC-1×CD16A group demonstrated significant suppression of tumor growth and a prolonged survival benefit compared with the control group (figure 5B,C). To further confirm the generality of this effect, a second model was established using HGC27 cells overexpressing KK-LC-1. Consistently, the KK-LC-1×CD16A group also markedly inhibited tumor growth in this model, corroborating the findings observed in the MKN45 model (figure 5E–G). Clinical evaluations have demonstrated that adoptive memory NK cell therapy is safe for treating hematologic malignancies.45 46 During the treatment period, there was no significant difference in body weight observed among the mice (figure 5H,I). Histopathological assessment through H&E staining and serum biochemical analysis indicated the lack of evident systemic toxicity following treatment with KK-LC-1×CD16A, underscoring the safety profile of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells (online supplemental figure 13A–E).

To further explore the content and activation status of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells within the TME, flow cytometry analysis was conducted on tumor tissues obtained from treated mice (figure 5J–L). It is noteworthy that KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells demonstrated elevated levels of the inhibitory receptor TIGIT, as shown in figure 5M. Our results demonstrate that both cell lines express moderate to high levels of PVR (online supplemental figure 14A,B), providing a mechanistic rationale for TIGIT-mediated suppression in our experimental model.

Two animal models were used in our research. To evaluate potential confounding effects from host NK cells in Balb/c nude mice, we performed validation experiments. In vitro assays confirmed that the KK-LC-1×CD16A construct, as well as its individual monomeric components (KK-LC-1-spyC and CD16A-spyT), exhibited no detectable activation of murine NK cells (online supplemental figure 15A,B). To exclude potential confounding effects from residual NK cell activity in nude mice, we repeated the key therapeutic experiments in NSG mice, which are completely devoid of functional NK cells.

Consistent with the results obtained in Balb/c nude mice, the group receiving armed NK cells (NK+KK-LC-1×CD16A) exhibited robust antitumor efficacy. In the NSG mouse model, MKN45 cells were engineered with a luciferase reporter gene prior to implantation to allow longitudinal tracking of tumor growth via bioluminescence imaging (figure 6A). The results showed that tumor growth was significantly reduced in the KK-LC-1×CD16A group compared with groups treated with PBS or NK cells alone (figure 6A–C). Furthermore, this group demonstrated a pronounced prolongation of survival (figure 6D). The treatment also proved to be safe, with no significant changes observed in mouse body weight (figure 6E). These data, together with previous reports supporting the use of Balb/c nude mice in human NK cell therapy studies when appropriate controls are applied,47 48 indicate that host NK cells did not significantly affect the observed outcomes.

Discussion

Low NK cell infiltration at the tumor site limits their antitumor efficacy in patients with gastric cancer.3 49 BiKEs, as a highly promising strategy, can significantly enhance the accumulation and activation of NK cells at tumor sites.23 50 These molecules serve as a bridge between NK cells and tumor cells, recruiting NK cells to tumor cells expressing relevant antigens to mediate tumor cell killing.10 11 Previous studies have demonstrated that NK cells acquire memory-like properties in response to cytokine stimulation.51 Multiple preclinical studies have demonstrated that these memory-like NK cells possess superior anticancer properties.5,751 CIML NK cell therapy has been shown to safely induce complete remission in evaluable patients with relapsed/refractory AML.

Clinical studies of CIML NK cell therapy for solid tumors remain limited, as the immunosuppressive TME impairs NK cell function, trafficking, and infiltration. Although NK cells have been observed to infiltrate primary solid tumors, metastases, and tumor-infiltrated lymph nodes, most solid tumors exhibit low levels of NK cell infiltration.54 Moreover, numerous studies have reported decreased NK cell infiltration in malignant tissues compared with corresponding non-malignant tissues.55 56 To overcome these limitations, several strategies have been developed to enhance the specific targeting ability of NK cells to tumors, including BiKEs and CAR-NK cells.57,59 CAR-NK cell therapy has garnered significant attention due to its favorable safety profile; however, the resistance of NK cells to viral transduction hampers the efficiency of CAR-NK cell preparation. Additionally, the complex and costly manufacturing process of CAR-NK cells limits their widespread clinical application.60 61 In contrast, approaches based on bispecific antibodies, such as AFM13, which targets CD30 on tumor cells and CD16A on NK cells, have exhibited noteworthy clinical effectiveness in CD30-positive lymphomas. This approach underscores the therapeutic potential of exploiting CD16A to redirect endogenous NK cells, presenting a more immediately translatable alternative to cellular engineering.22

Our results demonstrate that KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells exhibit significantly enhanced activation, as evidenced by upregulation of CD69, CD137, and CD25, and increased secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α. Furthermore, these armed NK cells showed potent, antigen-specific cytotoxicity against multiple KK-LC-1–positive tumor cell lines, including gastric, breast, and liver cancer models. The tetravalent format consistently outperformed its bivalent counterpart across functional assays, underscoring the importance of multivalency in promoting sustained NK cell activation and killing efficacy. This study represents the first investigation into the application of KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells in solid tumor therapy.

Notably, in vivo studies revealed that arming CIML NK cells with KK-LC-1×CD16A markedly enhanced their tumor-homing capability, as visualized by near-infrared imaging and immunofluorescence. This improved infiltration translated into significant tumor growth suppression and prolonged survival in two independent xenograft models. Importantly, the treatment was well-tolerated with no observable systemic toxicity, supporting its potential clinical safety.

Unlike previous tetravalent protein designs, our approach employs the SpyCatcher-SpyTag conjugation system, dividing the construct into two components: KK-LC-1-SpyC and CD16A-SpyT. These components are individually produced in E. coli and subsequently assembled into the final tetravalent protein form. This production strategy enables soluble expression of the protein, simplifies the manufacturing process, and facilitates scalability. Furthermore, this modular design allows for the replacement of the targeting domain in the KK-LC-1-SpyC component, enabling multi-antigen targeting. KK-LC-1×CD16A enhances CIML NK cell activation and cytotoxicity versus bivalent formats. Additionally, KK-LC-1×CD16A-armed CIML NK cells exhibit significantly stronger killing activity compared with conventional NK cells (online supplemental figure 16).

Multiple studies have demonstrated that NK cells from patients with cancer exhibit impaired cytotoxic function.62,64 Notably, our research revealed that the modification with KK-LC-1×CD16A not only enhances the tumor-killing efficacy of CIML NK cells but also induces upregulation of the inhibitory receptor TIGIT on CIML NK cells. Studies have shown that TIGIT is associated with NK cell exhaustion in tumor-bearing mice and patients with colon cancer, and blocking TIGIT can prevent NK cell exhaustion and promote NK cell-dependent antitumor immunity in various tumor-bearing mouse models.65 66 Combined TIGIT blockade may optimize therapeutic efficacy by countering this immunosuppressive effect. This represents a promising direction for future investigation.

The KK-LC-1×CD16A bispecific construct demonstrates compelling antitumor efficacy in preclinical models. Initial in vitro studies showed no detectable NK cell activation in the absence of tumor targets (online supplemental figure 10C,D), suggesting a favorable preliminary safety profile. Animal studies further supported this, revealing no significant changes in body weight, histopathology, or hepatic and renal function. KK-LC-1 expression is also highly restricted to testicular and tumor tissues, supporting its selectivity and low risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity. However, these findings are derived from simplified model systems. Subcutaneous xenografts incompletely replicate key features of human gastric cancer, such as the immunosuppressive TME, stromal interactions, and vascular architecture, and do not capture metastatic dissemination. Moreover, current models offer limited ability to evaluate immune-related adverse events such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS). To address these limitations and improve translational relevance, future work will include systematic immunogenicity and CRS assessments in non-human primate models, incorporation of orthotopic and metastasis models that better mimic tissue-specific and systemic disease contexts. We will apply mechanistic studies, such as RNA sequencing, to investigate the functional enhancement of NK cell infiltration and function by KK-LC-1×CD16A. Together, these approaches will provide deeper insights into the therapeutic potential and safety of this strategy under clinically representative conditions.

Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The xenograft models used here do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human gastric cancer, including the immunosuppressive TME and metastatic behavior. Additionally, while in vivo degranulation (CD107a) assays were not feasible, alternative metrics—such as tumor growth inhibition, survival benefit, and immunohistochemical analysis—robustly demonstrated NK cell-mediated antitumor activity.

In conclusion, we present a novel and versatile strategy to enhance the antitumor efficacy of CIML NK cells in solid tumors using a tetravalent bispecific engager. This approach effectively addresses key limitations of current NK cell therapies, including poor tumor infiltration and target engagement, while offering a clinically feasible and scalable production platform. Our findings support further development of KK-LC-1×CD16A as a promising immunotherapeutic agent for KK-LC-1-positive gastric cancers and potentially other malignancies.
