Barriers and facilitators of adherence to cervical cancer screening follow-up among women living with HIV in South Ethiopia: a qualitative study
Authors: Bikila Lencha, Biftu Geda, Kemal Ahmed, Mark Spigt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen living with human immune deficiency (WLWH) had higher risk of developing cervical cancer. Recurrence of the lesions is higher among WLWH than HIV negative women. Effective follow-up is very crucial in order to reduce the persistence of the lesions and avoid recurrence. However, less than half of the women attend yearly follow up in Ethiopia. This study investigated the barriers and facilitators of screening follow-up from the perspectives of WLWH and healthcare providers.ObjectivesTo explore the barriers and facilitators of cervical cancer screening follow-up among WLWH who were treated for pre-cancer lesions in the West Arsi Zone, South Ethiopia.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted among 18 participants (12 WLWH and 6 healthcare providers) in two hospitals and two health centers in West Arsi Zone from January 1 to February 28, 2025. Participants were selected via purposive sampling technique. Data were collected using a semi-structured in-depth interview guide. ATLAS.ti version 25.0.1 was used for data analysis. Data were transcribed, translated verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis, guided by the Social Ecological Model (SEM). Findings were reported using texts and quotations in line with the SEM framework.ResultsThe major barriers were lack of awareness, fear, lack of money, poor counseling, gender preference, lack of privacy, stigma, traditional medicine and religious practices. On the other hand, having awareness, phone reminders, male partner support, good provider approach, free services and availability of alternative treatment options were identified as facilitators of follow-up.ConclusionThere are unique challenges facing WLWH cervical cancer screening adherence in Ethiopia. Ensuring compassionate and respectful care with availability of more female providers is crucial. Continuous awareness creation campaigns, empowering women, involving male partners, and promoting human papilloma virus (HPV) self-test could avert most of the identified barriers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03303-8.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is a cancer that develops in the cells of the cervix, the lower part of the uterus that connects to the vagina [1]. It remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women globally [2]. In 2022, 8,168 new cases and 5,975 deaths were reported, accounting for 10.2% of total cancer diagnoses and 10.9% of cancer-related deaths in Ethiopia [3]. Six out of ten cervical cancer cases in Ethiopia are diagnosed at an advanced stage [4, 5]. CC represents nearly three fourth (73.8%) of all gynecological cancers, with a significant increase reported in Southern Ethiopia [6].

Cervical cancer is totally preventable disease through vaccination, screening, and treatment of the lesions [7, 8]. Screening programs are designed to detect the disease early before the manifestation of symptoms and support women to return regularly for follow-up [9]. Follow-up post treatment for pre-cancer lesions plays a critical role in managing potential recurrences of the lesion and reducing unnecessary morbidity and mortality [10, 11] However, post treatment follow-up remained a major challenge in developing countries. To improve screening and treatment outcomes, it is important to understand why patients miss follow-up appointments [12].

Some studies have identified lack of awareness, transportation cost, anxiety related to painful procedures, lack of adequate information, and poor social support as barriers to follow-up. Structural challenges such as difficulties scheduling appointments, unavailability of providers, and poor integration of cervical cancer services in primary healthcare settings further hinder follow-up [9, 10, 12–14]. In the Ethiopian context, a recent mixed-methods study reported that women missed follow-ups due to lack of information, forgetting appointments, or believing they were no longer necessary [15].

According to the recent WHO guideline, WLWH had six times higher risk of developing CC and should begin screening at age 25 [16]. Studies show that WLWH are more likely to experience recurrence after treatment of pre-invasive cervical lesions than HIV-negative women, highlighting the critical need for ongoing follow-up [17, 18].

Nearly half (43.0%) of the women developed persistent lesions within 1-year post-treatment in Addis Ababa and HIV positive status was independently associated with persistent lesions [19]. However, less than half of the WLWH who were treated for cervical pre-cancer lesions came back for rescreening [20]. Applying the SEM framework to cervical cancer screening and follow-up research helps identify barriers and facilitators at each level, which is crucial to design tailored intervention that address specific barrier at each level [21]. Additionally, it is also important to design strategies that help improve follow-up post treatment for cervical lesions and reduce recurrence of the lesions. We aimed to conduct a qualitative study among WLWH and health care providers in selected health facilities in West Arsi Zone, South Ethiopia.

Methods

Study area and period

This qualitative, exploratory descriptive study was conducted in public health facilities within the West Arsi Zone, South Ethiopia. The study involved two hospitals - Melka Oda General Hospital and Shashemene Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital (SHCSH) as well as two health centers - Abosto and Nagelle health centers. Data collection took place from January 1 to February 28, 2025. All selected facilities provide cervical cancer screening and antiretroviral treatment (ART) services. Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) is available at all sites. HPV DNA testing is performed at the regional laboratory located within SHCSH; samples are collected from nearby health facilities and sent to the regional laboratory. Cryotherapy and thermal ablation are the most common treatment modalities, while Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure (LEEP) is exclusively available at SHCSH [22]. During data collection, seven healthcare providers delivered cervical cancer screening and treatment services across the selected facilities.

Design

A descriptive exploratory study was conducted, utilizing the SEM to organize barriers and facilitators in a comprehensible manner. The qualitative descriptive study design provides a comprehensive, clear understanding of participants experiences, perceptions, and feelings. This approach is frequently utilized within healthcare environments to explore perceptions and experiences of both service users and providers concerning specific phenomenon under study. It is very useful for capturing rich, detailed descriptions that inform clinical practice, policy development and effective service delivery [23, 24].

Population

The study included those women who were in active care and who had missed follow-up appointments. All healthcare providers working in the cervical cancer screening and treatment unit, coordinating the program and leading primary health care units were also included.

Sample size and sampling procedure

Twelve WLWH - six actively in follow-up and six lost to follow-up and six healthcare providers participated in in-depth interviews. Twenty-five WLWH were approached for interview and 13 of them declined the invitation. Four of the health care providers also decline to participate. Data saturation was determined during data collection, when new information from the interviews didn’t add new insight to the study as per the suggestion of the previous study [25].

Purposive sampling was used, a common approach in qualitative research to select individuals who had rich information about the research question [25, 26]. Thus, purposive sampling technique was used to include the WLWH and health care providers who had experience of our research perspective. Women were recruited through collaboration with healthcare providers working at the ART clinic. Patients who are active in their care were recruited in person during their follow-up clinic visits. Patients not active in care were contacted by phone based on information in the registry and invited to the clinic for an interview. If the women don’t respond to the phone, we have contacted them via case manager home visits.

Data collection methods and procedures

A semi-structured In-depth interview guide was developed from pertinent literatures related to our study objectives [9, 10, 12]. The interview guide included questions on participant backgrounds, screening results, knowledge of cervical cancer and post treatment follow-up care, experiences with follow-up services, perceived barriers and facilitators, and suggestions for improvement of follow up. For healthcare providers, questions focused on resource availability, adherence to guidelines, incentives, perceived barriers and facilitators of follow-up, and strategies to enhance follow-up (supplementary file 1). Interview was facilitated by the principal investigator (BL) and field note was taken by a research assistant who is fluent in both local languages (Afaan Oromoo and Amharic). The participant background information was summarized in table form.

Data quality control

Pretest of the interview guide was done with two women and two health care providers and minor amendments were made to the interview guide prior to actual data collection. All interviews were conducted in the local languages (Afaan Oromo and Amharic) based on the preference of the participants. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim in the local language and then translated into English. All data collected in the field was accessed by the Principal Investigator and securely stored using password protection. We have explained the purpose of the study in detail and asked the participants to give honest response in order to minimize social desirability bias. A quiet room was used for the interview. The interview took an average of 30 to 50 min.

Trustworthiness

Our study was guided by the quality assurance mechanisms of qualitative study, as suggested by Shenton [25, 27]. The socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants were described in detail. The interview was recorded using mobile app. Transcription was done daily after each interview. Correction was made for the next interview based on the result of transcription of the former interview. The research team had worked with health care providers on the other related research topics previously and built a good relationship. All the necessary details and decisions were recorded to ensure audit trial. The data was triangulated using rich information from women and health care providers. A colleague who has experience in qualitative research was consulted to reflect on the data collection procedure, analysis and write up of the findings of the study. The research team held regular debriefings to discuss the progress of the research, challenges encountered, and possible solutions to overcome these challenges.

The primary author is a male PhD student working on cervical cancer screening, and this research is a part of that project. He is an epidemiologist with previous experience in mixed methods on HIV status disclosure and malaria prevention. The third author (KA) is a PhD holder with extensive experience in qualitative research. His expertise contributed to ensuring methodological rigor and minimizing biases that might have affected the research findings.

Data analysis

ATLAS.ti version 25.0.1 software for qualitative analysis was used. Social ecological model with thematic analysis method [28] was used for the analysis. SEM has been used in cervical cancer screening and follow-up adherence studies to summarize the findings across multiple levels- individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy [12, 29].

Two interviews were back translated in to English to check consistency of the translation. First, the files were created and organized in a Microsoft word document. Then, the transcripts were uploaded to the software for analysis. The data was read several times to be familiar with the ideas and understand the meaning of each transcript clearly. Then, the initial codes were generated deductively and inductively after repeated reading and rereading [29]. Primary author (B.L) and the third author (KA) did the coding independently and disagreement was solved by a third person. After reaching agreement about codes, similar codes were categorized together using SEM. All transcripts were reviewed to ensure that all the information was coded correctly. The findings were narrated under each theme of the SEM. Quotations and texts were used to report the findings of the study under each theme. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ-32) checklist guided the reporting of this study [30].

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 18 individuals (12 WLWH and 6 HCPs) participated in the study. The mean age of the women and healthcare providers was 38.9 (± 6.18) and 33.2 (± 3.6) years, respectively. Most women, 7 (58.3%), were followers of Orthodox Christianity. Half of the women were illiterate. Two-thirds of the HCPs were working as CCS and treatment providers (Table 1).

Theme I: Personal level barriers

It encompasses the lack of awareness about the importance follow up post-treatment, fear in many perspectives, poor living condition including transportation cost and perceived lack of pain that hinder follow up.

Lack of awareness

Most of them women stated that they do not know about the importance of screening and simply undergo screening because they are told to do so. A 35-year-old woman said that: “I’m not sure about the cause. Once upon a time, I was advised to get screened, and I just completed the screening. Then, they informed me that it showed some signs, and I received treatment here (P-3). Some of the women had misconception. A 29-year-old woman said that: “I have no knowledge of the disease. I was completely unaware until I heard the mothers talking about it and mentioning, ‘It can appear when you sit in the sun.‘” (P-4). Additionally, the lack of awareness is also leading to missed appointments. A 30-year-old HCP said that: “Some women attend their appointments annually as scheduled, while others miss their appointments, possibly due to a lack of awareness” (H-2).

Fear of the disease

Those women who were screened positive and treated for cervical pre-cancer lesions missed the appointment because of fear and anxiety. Most of the women were worried about the double burden of HIV and cervical cancer. A 35-year-old woman said that: “The appointment was scheduled for me to return in six months, but I didn’t go back. I was overwhelmed by fear. It felt like a deep worry, especially because it added to my existing disease (HIV). I remember saying, ‘Just kill me or keep me alive to God’ (P-3).”

Fear of screening procedure

Most of the women felt shame about the way screening procedure is conducted. However, most of them were convinced after advice from health care provider. A 35 years old woman said that: I was still thinking, “I’m afraid. How can I show my private parts? How can I open my legs in front of someone else?” However, the healthcare professionals kept reassuring me, saying, “It’s for your own health.” then, I went through with the screening (P-5).

Health care providers said most women were delaying rescreening because of the screening procedure. A 30-year-old MCH coordinator said that: “They express things like, ‘I refuse to be screened this way again; I will participate only if there’s an alternative method.‘” (H-1).

Fear of pain during procedure

Fear of the machine used for screening (speculum) is also another barrier to rescreening, as raised by few women. A 41-year-old woman said that: “The screening is extremely painful. They insert a device inside, and although I have given birth twice naturally, I did not experience this kind of pain. I do not need the device they use for the screening. Initially, I was open to the idea, but now I have decided that I will not undergo the screening again” (P-7). A 46 years old woman also raised similar issue and delayed rescreening: “I experienced pain following my treatment and was advised to return for a check-up in six months. However, I didn’t go back because I was afraid after the intense pain I felt during the initial procedure.” (P-2).

Perceived lack of pain

Most women believe that follow-up is not needed if someone feels healthy. Some of the women who feel pain also convince themselves that it is not severe pain that needs intervention. A 30-year-old woman said that: “Since I’m not experiencing any pain, I might overlook my scheduled appointments. If they don’t align with my medication (ART), I could miss the follow-up because I’m feeling well.” (P-6).

Poor living condition

Since most of the women living with HIV/AIDS come from low socioeconomic status, they are unable to feed their children and pay for transportation. A 45-year-old woman said, " Since our country has an economic problem, this disease is killing women who are unable to pay for their transportation because of poverty.” (P-1).

Theme II: Interpersonal barriers

It refers to the barriers that could occur due to kind of interaction between patients and health care providers, friends or family members.

Poor patient-provider communication

Most of the women felt that they received inadequate counseling about post-treatment side effect. A 35-year-old woman said: “The doctor didn’t advise me about it (vaginal discharge). They informed me when I returned. They didn’t tell me during the treatment. I wouldn’t have come back if they had told me. I was very frustrated. They prescribed me medication, and now there is no problem.” (P-3). However, some patients witness that they received good counseling about post-treatment side effects. A 49-year-old woman said: “He told me that vaginal discharge would be there for a week. Since he told me first, I wasn’t afraid. I would have been afraid if he had not told me about the vaginal discharge.” (P-11).

Gender of service provider

Most women prefer female HCPs for cervical cancer screening and follow-up adherence. Especially, young women tend to prefer female providers. A 29-year-old woman said: “I was not happy during the first screening. It is better if the healthcare provider is female. The first time I was examined by a male doctor. In the second round, I was seen by a female doctor. She told me, ‘Now you are cured and you can be free for 5 years.’ The way she treated me was good.” (P-4).

This idea of gender preference was supported by healthcare providers. A 30-year-old female HCP said: “Let me tell you, for example, most of the time they prefer me over Musa (male provider, name changed) for screening. They say, ‘We will come during your shift,’ because he is male and they don’t want to be screened by him. There is a woman who refuses and says, ‘I will not be screened by a man.’ There is also a woman who came back and was screened after I finished my night duty and returned for daytime activities.” (H-1).

Older women do not mind about the sex of the provider. They raised the issue of delivery and compared it with CCS. They argued that the sex of the provider is not a reason to avoid screening. A 42-year-old woman said: “This can’t be a reason. Do we ask for options during labor? The healthcare professional present will help us.” (P-9).

Lack of male partner support

The HCPs recommend abstinence for four to six weeks post-treatment for cervical pre-cancer lesions. The women face challenges from their husbands concerning post-treatment abstinence. A 30-year-old HCP said that: “Let me tell you what I encountered, when you treat for VIA positive, you tell them abstinence for 4 weeks. There is a woman who suffered because of pressure from her husband and came to tell me. She came to me after two weeks of treatment and asked me, “What should I do? That was also a big challenge to me as a provider.” (H-2).

Theme III: Health system level barriers

It is a barrier related to lack of resources at the health facility level that could impair the services. Additionally, the way providers handles their patients, the rotation system and lack of incentive for health care providers are also challenging follow up services.

Lack of privacy

Most of the patients delayed rescreening because of the privacy issues. A 29-year-old woman said that: “To be honest, I came back for rescreening several months after my scheduled appointment. I felt very disappointed by the way he handled my case in front of numerous students. It’s particularly important for those of us living with HIV to receive specialized care, in a private room.” (P-4).

Lack of incentives for providers

The healthcare providers (HCPs) are using their personal phones to follow-up with women. One HCP, who serves as a leader in a primary healthcare unit said that: “We have no incentive for the provider working in the clinic. He is working hard even though there is no incentive.” (HM-2).

Staff rotation

Until the newly appointed provider becomes familiar with the work environment, it takes some time that could lead to the interruptions to the service. An HCP who works as maternal and child health coordinator said that: “The challenge arises during staff rotation; when a provider moves to another department, the new provider requires time to adapt to the work. This transition can further delay operations.” (HM-1).

Occasional shortage of resource

Sometimes, HCPs face a shortage of resources that leads to disruption of services for a short time. A 36-year-old provider said that: “Sometimes we run out of cryotherapy gas (CO2) and at other times the acetic acid will be finished. There are also instances when we stop due to a lack of alcohol, but that is quickly solved.” (H-3).

Theme IV: Community level barriers

Stigma, traditional medicine and religious practices were the barriers of follow up at community level in our setting.

Stigma

Cervical cancer is just kept secret like that of HIV in the society. A 36-year-old woman said that: “You know what; we don’t want other people to know how we have this disease (HIV). This one (cervical cancer) is also considered as a shame.” (P-8). A 30-year-old HCP also said that: “The other thing is, especially among HIV positive women, they don’t want their secret to be known by others. As people raise the issue of why she is always going to the hospital, there is a possibility of absenteeism due to fear.” (HM-1).

Use of traditional medicine and spiritual practices

Many women are spending a lot of time with traditional medicine and come to the hospital with the severe form of the disease. A 45-year-old woman said that: “I know many who have died using traditional medicine. Among these traditional medicine practitioners are those who burn women to cure it. I know many who have died. I can’t count them. It is not free like the hospital service.” (P-1).

Another woman said that she was using holy water and perceived that she is feeling healthy. However, it has been two years since she was treated for the lesion and didn’t come back for the appointment. She said that: “After that, I am going to Tsebal (holy water) for my religious observance. Until then, I am healthy.” (P-3).

Facilitators of adherence to follow-up

Theme I: Personal level facilitators

Keeping my own health

Most of the women agreed that the benefit of attending follow-up is for their own healthy. A 49-year- old women said that: “Why do I wait until I am sick and have to be carried by others? Why do I stay in bed and bother people? It is better to be treated while I am healthy.” (P-11).

They are also concerned about taking care of their children and other family members if they fall sick. A 30-year-old woman said that: “The main concern is my own health, and it’s my responsibility to seek regular check-ups. That’s why I’ve returned; I need to focus on my health first.” (P-6).

Fear of the disease

The anxiety and fear that surround positive screening make them come back for rescreening. A 36-year-old HCP said that: They often wonder, “What could happen next year?” and wait for it with much emotional turmoil. When we inform them that a positive result is a sign of precancer, they fear it might develop into cancer. I believe this is why they are eager to get screened again (H-3).

Having awareness

Those women who had awareness came back for follow-up to the appointment given by HCPs. A 42 years old woman said that: “Prevention involves receiving treatment. It’s important to seek timely treatment, have regular check-ups, and follow the doctor’s advice before it progresses to a more advanced stage” (P-9).

Theme II: Interpersonal level facilitators

Health care providers approach

Women considered health care professionals as their main source of information and comfort when they feel emotional. A 35-year-old woman said that:

“My husband has died. The healthcare professionals have paid a great price for me. When they call me on the phone, I always lie about where I am. I provide different excuses, such as saying I am menstruating. Eventually, I decide to go since they always call regarding my health issues. They know what’s best for me, don’t they?” (P-5). A 38-year-old provider also said that: “If you take care of the clients, provide excellent service, and solve their problems, they will even refer other women for screening and follow-up. This is what people are witnessing firsthand.” (H-4).

Phone call reminder

Health care providers use their own phone number to remind the women about the appointment date. A 30-year-old male provider said that: “We have a phone number for that. We follow-up them via phone call. If they have missed their appointment, we will check their registration. After that, we will call them again.” (H-1).

Male involvement

Some of the women had got support from their husbands to go for screening and follow the appointment attentively. A 39-year-old woman said that: “My husband accompanied me and reassured me not to be afraid.” (P-10).

Most of the women also recommended husbands to accompany their wife to clinic to create awareness about screening and post treatment issues. A 42-year-old woman said that: “In my view, having the husband accompany us during this screening is beneficial. It’s crucial to raise awareness about important issues.” (P-9).

Theme III: Health system level facilitators

Free service

Cervical cancer screening and treatment is provided for free in public health facility. A 36-year-old woman said that: “Do you think many people would seek treatment if they had to pay for it? Offering this service for free is a valuable opportunity.” (P-8).

Awareness creation

The facilities are working to improve awareness of the community about CCS and follow-up. A 35-year-old health center manager said that: “There is also improvement on the awareness of the community. The health education is provided repeatedly in our health facility. We do have TV and loud speaker. I am not hearing the sound now. But education is given repeatedly in our health facility via loud speaker. For the outside community, health extension workers are creating awareness.” (HM-2).

Having alternative treatment modalities

The availability of both cryotherapy and thermocoagulation treatment options helped HCPs to provide the treatment service without interruption. A30-year-old female HCP said that: “Having both cryotherapy and thermal ablation is an advantage. Now the CO2 used for cryotherapy is finished. But since we have thermal ablation, our service is not interrupted. So having both treatment options is an advantage.” (H2).

Strong linkage between ART and cervical cancer screening clinic

Almost all health care providers stressed about the strong linkage between both clinics. A 36-year-old HCP said that: “For those who come for ART, VIA screening is done mandatory. Awareness will be given and they will be linked to CCS clinic” (H-3).

Theme IV: Community level facilitator

Awareness creation

Awareness creation is the major strategy proposed by all interviewees to improve CCS and follow-up. They raised the issue of mass media use, teaching in the community gatherings and religious institutions and giving adequate counseling during the clinical consultations. A 42-year-old woman said that; “It would be beneficial for Sisters (Nurses) to be sent to teach at women’s meetings.” (P-9). One of the HCPs said that: “It is good if people are well aware of this service, just like child vaccination and antenatal care. Therefore, work should be done on this issue.” (H-2).

Searching for lost cases

Going home to home and searching for the lost cases were proposed by patients and HCPs as they did for HIV positive patients who were lost from the follow-up. A 36-year-old HCP suggested that: “For example, if ART patients miss their medication for two weeks, there is a mechanism in place to go home and search for the patient. It is encouraging that this mechanism also applies to cervical cancer.” (H-3).

Community based screening

Health care providers emphasized on the importance of incorporating community-based screening alongside the existing efforts. One health care manager said that: “The most recent guidelines (2021) focus on health facility-level support and do not address community-level screening. However, comprehensive health services in rural areas can either refer individuals to health facilities or perform the screenings themselves, provided the necessary infrastructure is in place.” (HM-2).

Home to home screening is also suggested taking HIV AIDS as an example. One woman said that: “As HIV screening is conducted from home to home; it would be beneficial to do the same for cervical cancer screening. They were performing HIV tests door-to-door, and I also tested my children and my mother. It is advantageous to have testing done in this manner.” (P-6).

Integration in to primary health care

Strong linkage from the community and within the facility linkage is also recommended to increase cervical cancer screening and treatment. A 36-years old male provider said that: “It is important to integrate CCS with units like family planning. For instance, last time, while I insert IUCD, I suspect and finally identified suspicious cancer and referred her.” (H-3).

Discussion

The study explored the barriers and facilitators of cervical cancer follow-up rescreening after treatment for cervical lesions. SEM framework was used to describe the barriers and facilitators at personal, interpersonal, health system and community levels.

Barriers of adherence to follow-up

Lack of awareness was the main barrier to follow-up screening. Similar findings were reported by studies conducted in Malawi [31], Ethiopia [32], Cameroon [9], Uganda [33], and Peru [10]. Most of the women don’t know the importance for follow-up [34]. This emphasizes the importance of creating awareness at the individual level during clinical consultations and within the community using health extension workers. Awareness creation is recommended to improve cervical cancer screening [35] and follow-up [36]. In our study, participants recommended awareness creation to improve adherence to CCS.

Fear of the disease was identified as both a barrier and a facilitator in our study, similar to findings from other parts of Ethiopia [37]. The fear is related to the double burden of cervical cancer and HIV among WLWH [38]. Similarly, another study found that fear of pain and fear of premature death could also serve as facilitators for attending scheduled follow-up visits [12]. Some women fear that a diagnosis of cervical pre-cancer is a death sentence, while others seek to avoid death and suffering. Therefore, fear of the disease may discourage some women due to anxiety, while for others, it serves as motivation to seek reassurance and take preventive action.

Fear about the screening procedure is a common barrier among our study participants. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Malawi [31], Cameroon [9], and Uganda [12], where embarrassment and shyness hindered screening. Similarly, embarrassment and pain associated with the procedure were barriers to follow-up in Southern India [36]. Feelings of shame related to exposing the bodies for pelvic examination is reported as a barrier irrespective of nationality and socio-economic status in Europe [39]. Fear of pain during the procedure also deters many women from follow-up in our setting, consistent with findings from Ethiopia [32], Uganda [12], and Cameroon [9, 40] and high income settings [41]. A study from Peru identified fear or anxiety about treatment as a main barrier to follow-up [10].

Perceived lack of pain is also a barrier of follow-up, similar to findings from Uganda and Cameroon, where women believed that if there was no pain, follow-up was unnecessary [9, 12]. In Ethiopia, more than two-thirds of cervical cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [4, 5], increasing the risk of lesion recurrence or progression to invasive cancer without early detection [17].

Poor living conditions hinder follow-up due to lack of transportation funds. Similar barriers have been reported in Cameroon [40], Mexico [42], Malawi [43], and Peru [10], where patients lack money for transportation. Transportation difficulties also hinder timely diagnosis and follow-up in Tanzania [38]. Poverty remains a significant barrier to screening and follow-up globally [44], underscoring the need to address socioeconomic factors to improve adherence and outcomes [34].

Poor counseling has contributed to loss to follow-up in our study. Nurses in Cameroon reported inadequate post-treatment counseling [40]. Similarly, a study from Peru identified poor communication from health professionals as a barrier to follow-up [10]. In Ethiopia, most women did not receive counseling about follow-up from healthcare providers [32]. As a result, women felt frustrated. A study in Cameroon also identified disrespectful treatment by healthcare providers as a barrier to cervical cancer screening [45]. Counseling before screening is crucial to reduce fear and help women feel comfortable during and after the procedure [44].

Gender of the service provider is another barrier identified in previous studies [44, 46, 47]. A study in Malawi among HIV-positive women reported that male providers were a barrier to screening and follow-up [31]. Most women preferred female doctors and left clinics without screening due to this reason. Similarly, in Debre Berhan, female providers contributed to client satisfaction [35]. Our study also found that many women preferred female care providers, which contributed to delays in rescreening.

Lack of male partner support is a barrier related to the issue of post-treatment abstinence. Women from Cameroon and Zimbabwe faced similar problem: their husbands were unwilling to admit abstaining for 4 weeks [40, 48]. In a study conducted in Malawi, male partners were perceived as both a barrier and an important source of support [43]. This aligns with our findings, where some women complained about the challenge of post-treatment abstinence, while others supported male involvement to improve follow-up. An interventional study from Uganda found that male partner involvement significantly reduced loss to follow-up [49]. Males often hold societal dominance, and decisions made in their absence are less likely to succeed. HCPs should encourage women to bring their husbands to the clinic and counsel them together about cervical cancer screening, treatment, and post-treatment abstinence [50–52]. To improve follow-up, male involvement in cervical cancer screening is desired by both men [53] and women [43]. Additionally, spouse or family support was identified as a key facilitator in Uganda [50, 54].

Young women in our study expressed concern about the lack of privacy during cervical cancer examinations, which delayed rescreening. This is similar to findings from Uganda, Nepal and Eastern Mediterranean Region where lack of privacy was reported as a barrier [12, 33, 44, 55]. Fear of undressing in the absence of privacy was common among HIV-positive women in Uganda [33]. Therefore, ensuring client privacy is crucial for maintaining the cervical cancer care continuum and achieving the WHO 2030 goal of cervical cancer elimination.

A shortage of skilled providers was identified as a major facility-related barrier in Debre Berhan [35]. However, in our study, we found that there are enough trained HCPs at each facility. The main barrier was staff rotation within the facility, leading to service interruptions. Similar findings were reported from South Africa where staff rotation delayed operation [56]. Similarly, high staff turnover was reported in Ethiopia [57], and in Peru [58].

Occasional resource shortages also hinder follow-up in our setting, consistent with findings from Debre Berhan [35], Peru [10], and other parts of Ethiopia [57]. A study from Ethiopia showed that higher scores on facility-related barriers were associated with an increased likelihood of women being lost to follow-up [59]. Similar issues were reported in Tanzania, where resource shortages affected screening and follow-up [38]. Lack of incentives for airtime for providers also hinders follow-up. Studies in Ethiopia have shown that phone call reminders are effective strategies to improve follow-up [59]. In Uganda, the absence of reminder systems, such as phone calls, posed significant challenges [33].

Stigma related to HIV and cervical cancer hinders women’s follow-up. Misconceptions, such as associating cervical cancer with sexual immorality or promiscuity, contribute to this stigma. A study from Tanzania identified stigma as a major barrier to screening and follow-up [38]. Fear of social rejection was also common at community and family levels in Nepal [44] and couple of studies from Uganda [50, 54]. The study from Peru reported that women with HPV positive results were blamed and labeled as promiscuous [60]. This stigma stems from a lack of awareness at both individual and community levels.

Use of traditional medicine and spiritual practices is another barrier, similar to findings from Uganda [12]. In Uganda, traditional healers are often accessed before formal healthcare providers [50]. In Peru, women prefer traditional medicine over modern treatments, some of which are promoted by nurse-midwives as helpful [58]. Unfortunately, women who seek traditional healers often present at advanced stages of disease, leading to poorer outcomes. Therefore, counseling women during initial screening about the consequences of untreated lesions and the importance of follow-up is vital.

Facilitators of adherence to follow-up

Most women wanted to prioritize their own health, which was reported as a key facilitator. This aligns with findings from districts in Uganda, where women desired to know their health status and maintain their health [50]. A positive perception of self-worth was also identified as a facilitator in South Western Uganda [12]. Free services and the presence of supportive partners were identified as facilitators in a study conducted in Debre Berhan [35]. Similar findings were observed in our study. The likely reason is that most women come from low socioeconomic backgrounds and prefer free screening and follow-up services.

Phone reminder systems were among the facilitators identified in our study. Similarly, another study from Ethiopia found that reminder systems, such as cards and phone calls, were effective in improving follow-up adherence [32]. An evidence-based patient reminder system can enhance follow-up, especially with the increasing availability of phones. However, consistent registration of patients needing follow-up and their contact details is essential [32]. Phone call reminders are suggested to address common barriers like forgetfulness or lack of awareness about scheduled appointments. Similarly, a study from South India proposed similar solutions to improve cervical cancer follow-up screening [36]. Findings from Cameroon also reported similar results [40].

The availability of alternative treatment was identified as a facilitator in our study. Specially, the presence of thermal ablation was identified as a major facilitator by health care providers. This is because it provides effective treatment with in less than a minute and with a minimal discomfort to the client.

Cross-cutting issues

Poor implementation of the integration of cervical cancer screening into primary health care and the lack of outreach activities remain barriers to follow-up screening. A similar finding was reported from Uganda [12]. Therefore, better integration of cervical cancer services into existing HIV care programs is needed to strengthen processes and reach more women This is important because health extensions workers can visit homes to identify women who missed screening, follow up, and link them back to clinics. Similar strategies were suggested in a few studies conducted in Uganda [12, 38].

Community outreach screening was recommended to increase screening uptake and follow-up in our study. Additionally, a study from Uganda found that women who attended community outreach services for cervical cancer screening were more likely to engage with screening services [50].

Strengths and limitations of the study

There is a possibility of researcher bias. It is difficult to exclude social desirability bias entirely, as those who responded to the phone call attempt might differ from those who did not respond. To mitigate these limitations, we continued conducting interviews until no new ideas emerged from the participants. We also triangulated healthcare providers’ perspectives to validate the findings. The participants were well informed about the purpose of the study prior to the interviews. Additionally, we included both women who are actively engaged in follow-up and those who are not. Case managers were used to contact those who didn’t respond to the phone call. The barriers and facilitators at policy level were not identified.

Conclusion

The study identified multiple barriers and facilitators affecting adherence to post-treatment follow-up among WLWH treated for cervical pre-cancer lesions. The major barriers included lack of awareness, fear of disease and screening procedures, poor counseling, gender preferences for providers, lack of partner support, privacy concerns, stigma, traditional medicine practices, and weak health system integration. Societal stigma further limit women’s follow up, while health system issues such as staff rotation, and lack of incentives contribute to loss to follow-up. On the other hand, facilitators such as increased awareness, supportive healthcare providers, partner support, free services, phone reminders and positive perceptions of own health enhance follow-up adherence. Addressing these complex, multi-faceted factors is essential to improve follow-up, early detection, and ultimately reduce cervical cancer morbidity and mortality among women living with HIV.
