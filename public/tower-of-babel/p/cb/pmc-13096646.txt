Art therapy-oriented space design for pediatric dental clinics: a Kano-AHP-FCE framework
Authors: Hengwei Zhu, Yubin Li, Yingping Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study takes the needs of pediatric dental patients as the starting point and integrates art therapy concepts into the space design of pediatric dental clinics, with the goal of enhancing both the treatment experience and clinical efficiency. By applying the Kano model and Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), a comprehensive evaluation and design framework tailored to pediatric dental care requirements was established to assess art-therapy-oriented spatial experiences. Subsequently, practical design interventions were implemented and validated through Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation (FCE). The optimized scheme, which emphasized key elements such as parent accompaniment area, rich spatial color schemes, non-slip antibacterial flooring, independent treatment zone, warm ambient tones, child-safe corner protection, and overhead interactive projection, achieved a 26.58% higher comprehensive satisfaction score than the original clinic environment. The integrated Kano-AHP-FCE framework effectively links children’s psychological and functional needs with spatial strategies for art therapy-oriented pediatric dental clinics. This study provides a replicable methodology and practical design guidelines for child-centered healing environments in contemporary healthcare settings.Keywords: Kano model, Analytic hierarchy process, Fuzzy comprehensive evaluation, Art therapy, Pediatric dental clinicsSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Dental caries remain one of the most prevalent chronic diseases among children, affecting an estimated 60–90% of the pediatric population1. Beyond pain and discomfort, untreated dental problems may lead to long-term health risks and behavioral resistance to future care2. Pediatric dental care encompasses multiple areas, including caries treatment, orthodontics, preventive care, trauma management, and oral hygiene instruction3. However, successful treatment often depends on children’s behavioral cooperation and tolerance of prolonged procedures—factors that are easily undermined by anxiety and fear.

Dental anxiety in children arises from various sensory and environmental triggers, such as the smell of disinfectants4, the metallic gleam of instruments, or the intimidating unfamiliarity of clinical surroundings. These stressors can elicit physiological arousal and emotional avoidance, resulting in immediate resistance and even long-term neglect of oral health5. Addressing these emotional responses requires not only medical care but also a therapeutic environment that supports psychological comfort and behavioral compliance.

As an essential part of modern healthcare architecture, the spatial design of pediatric dental clinics directly affects children’s perception, emotional state, and treatment cooperation. Florence Nightingale’s early advocacy in Notes on Hospitals highlighted that a well-designed environment accelerates recovery and reduces psychological stress6. Subsequent research has confirmed that poorly designed spaces—characterized by windowless corridors, narrow layouts, and strong odors—exacerbate tension and anxiety7. Yet, for decades, pediatric dental clinics have often neglected children’s distinct psychological and perceptual needs.

In the twenty-first century, the integration of color, lighting, and digital technologies has significantly advanced pediatric healthcare design. Studies show that warm color palettes, soft lighting, and immersive audiovisual media can redirect attention and alleviate treatment-related anxiety8,9. Despite these improvements, many clinical spaces still lack evidence-based and user-centered design frameworks capable of systematically linking spatial attributes to children’s psychological responses (Fig. 1).

Existing problems in pediatric dental clinic space design.

In Fig. 1, the advantages and limitations of three typical spatial configurations in pediatric dental clinics are summarized as follows:

Functional layout

As shown in Fig. 1a, although the arrangement of dental equipment satisfies operational requirements for dentists, it insufficiently considers the need for parental accompaniment. The absence of a designated waiting or seating area neglects children’s psychological reliance on their parents, which may hinder cooperation between families and medical staff and disrupt the smooth progress of dental procedures.

Color application

As shown in Fig. 1b, current designs often use bright, saturated colors to attract children’s attention and alleviate anxiety. However, the lack of evidence-based coordination in color and pattern selection can lead to either overstimulation or visual monotony, limiting genuine emotional comfort and, in some cases, intensifying sensory stress.

Ceiling design

As shown in Fig. 1c, existing ceiling configurations generally meet basic lighting requirements but overlook their potential impact on children’s visual comfort and emotional engagement. The monotonous surfaces lack visual stimuli to capture children’s attention, which may increase discomfort and anxiety during longer treatment sessions.

To address these limitations, recent research has turned to art therapy, which promotes emotional regulation and anxiety reduction through multisensory artistic expression. Art therapy-oriented environments employ visual, auditory, and tactile stimuli to facilitate self-expression and cognitive distraction, helping children achieve emotional stability during medical procedures. However, few studies have quantitatively analyzed how art therapy principles can be systematically embedded in pediatric dental environments in a way that simultaneously meets functional requirements and supports children’s psychological needs.

Therefore, this study adopts a service design perspective10 to develop a systematic and data-driven approach that bridges user needs and spatial experience. By integrating the Kano model, Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), and Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation (FCE), the research constructs an evidence-based framework for art-therapy-oriented design in pediatric dental clinics. This framework aims to identify, prioritize, and evaluate children’s psychological and functional requirements, providing theoretical and practical guidance for creating a child-friendly dental environment that supports emotional comfort, cooperation, and healing. The aim of this study is to develop and empirically validate an integrated Kano-AHP-FCE model for art-therapy-oriented pediatric dental space design.

Related work

Dental anxiety and pediatric dental environments

The spatial design of pediatric dental clinics plays a critical role in shaping children’s emotional responses during treatment. Recent research has proposed several approaches to reducing dental anxiety in children. First, optimizing spatial layout and overall atmosphere can ease tension. Kim11 reported that warm color palettes, soft lighting, and interactive decorations effectively reduce stress. Second, technological interventions such as virtual reality (VR) have proven beneficial. Trusculescu et al.12 demonstrated that pre-exposure to a virtual dental environment familiarizes children with procedures, lowering their fear of treatment. Third, sensory factors such as scent and sound influence emotional comfort. Yan et al.13 found that soothing background music and the reduction of unpleasant odors significantly decrease anxiety and feelings of isolation.

Collectively, these studies show that environmental design can alleviate anxiety by enhancing emotional regulation, perceived safety, and attention distraction. Nevertheless, most existing approaches focus on isolated elements—such as colors, media installations, or VR experiences—rather than on the systematic integration of these interventions within a coherent, user-centered design and evaluation framework. In particular, few studies model children’s needs and emotional responses in a way that directly informs multi-criteria spatial design decisions.

Art therapy in pediatric healthcare and spatial design

Art therapy originated in the late eighteenth century as part of the “Moral Treatment” movement for psychiatric rehabilitation14. Influenced by Freud’s psychoanalytic theory and Jung’s symbolism, it evolved into a therapeutic method for children and was widely adopted in healthcare settings15. Margaret Naumburg’s school-based art therapy established its psychological foundation16.

Within pediatric healthcare environments, art therapy principles have been translated into spatial features such as soft colors, nature-inspired imagery, and organic forms to soothe emotions17; playful and interactive elements such as coloring walls and projection games to increase engagement18; multisensory interventions—dynamic projections, ambient music, tactile furnishings—to create calming, immersive atmospheres19; and narrative or personalized spatial storytelling to strengthen emotional connection and self-expression20,21. These studies indicate that art-therapy-oriented environments can alleviate children’s anxiety by promoting emotional regulation, sensory comfort, and narrative engagement.

However, most applications remain qualitative or case-based, relying on designers’ intuition rather than systematic user research. Empirical evaluations are often limited to general satisfaction ratings, with little analysis of how specific spatial attributes linked to art therapy affect children’s emotional states and behavioral cooperation. A more structured, data-driven approach is therefore needed to integrate art therapy principles into spatial design and to quantitatively assess their effectiveness in pediatric dental settings.

User-centred and multi-criteria evaluation methods

UCD is a methodology that prioritizes users’ needs and experiences throughout the design process22. Applying UCD to pediatric dental environments enables designers to systematically capture the interaction between children and their surroundings, providing insights for functional optimization and emotional support.

A range of analytical tools has been developed within the UCD tradition. The Kano model classifies user requirements into satisfaction categories and reveals nonlinear relationships between performance and satisfaction23. It is often used to distinguish basic and attractive attributes in service and product-service systems. The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) converts qualitative judgements into quantitative weights by structuring decisions into hierarchies and performing pairwise comparisons24, and is widely applied to prioritize environmental factors or rank alternative design options. The Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation (FCE) method handles uncertainty in user judgements by mapping linguistic ratings to membership functions and aggregating them into overall scores25. It has been used for multi-criteria evaluations such as service quality and environmental performance.

Despite their advantages, each of these methods has limitations. Kano classifications can be sensitive to questionnaire wording and response tendencies, and the discrete category labels may oversimplify nuanced emotional reactions, especially in pediatric settings. AHP relies on subjective expert judgements and requires consistency checks; the resulting weights can change with the expert group or comparison matrices. FCE depends on the definition of membership functions and aggregation operators, introducing additional modelling assumptions that may influence final scores.

Considering the complexity of pediatric dental care, this study integrates the Kano, AHP, and FCE methods into a unified framework that links need identification, priority weighting, and comprehensive evaluation. In this framework, the Kano model is used primarily as a screening tool to structure environmental requirements according to user perceptions and to distinguish core attributes from less influential ones26,27. AHP is then employed to assign quantitative priorities to the Kano-filtered requirements, translating expert consensus into a stable importance vector while explicitly checking the consistency of subjective judgements28,29. Finally, FCE integrates user ratings of different spatial schemes with the AHP-derived weights to obtain overall satisfaction scores under uncertainty30,31. By combining these complementary roles, the integrated Kano-AHP-FCE framework provides a coherent chain from requirement classification to priority setting and scheme evaluation, and offers an empirical basis for linking children’s psychological and functional needs to spatial design decisions in pediatric dental clinics.

Research hypotheses

This study applies the Kano-AHP-FCE methodology to systematically analyze the needs of pediatric dental patients, with the goal of optimizing spatial design, enhancing user satisfaction, and fostering a psychologically comfortable clinical environment. By integrating user-centered analytical models with design strategies, the research develops a structured framework for creating child-friendly dental spaces. Based on this methodological framework, three hypotheses are proposed:

H1:

The Kano model can systematically identify user demand characteristics in pediatric dental clinics and reveal the differential effects of various requirement types on user satisfaction and emotional responses.

H2:

The AHP can quantitatively evaluate pediatric treatment needs and determine the relative weight and priority of each requirement in spatial design practice.

H3:

The FCE method can objectively validate the rationality of conceptual design proposals and assess the effectiveness of design optimization strategies in improving children’s emotional comfort and overall user experience in practical applications.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling strategy

This study adopted a mixed-method, cross-sectional design combining questionnaires, semi-structured interviews, and focus groups. To ensure that the findings reflected different service levels and usage scenarios, a three-stage stratified convenience sampling strategy was used.

In the first stage, four types of pediatric dental institutions were selected as sampling strata according to service level and patient flow (Table 1). This institutional stratification was intended to capture both high-volume, standardized care and everyday, community-based visits.

In the second stage, pediatric dental patients and their caregivers were consecutively recruited in the waiting areas of each institution during the data collection period, using proportional allocation so that the sample size from each stratum roughly reflected its annual patient volume.

In the third stage, participants covered three age bands (4–6, 7–9, and 10–12 years) to reflect developmental diversity, rather than to support age-stratified inference. Only children who were attending a dental visit and whose guardians provided informed consent were invited to participate. Children with severe cognitive impairment or acute medical conditions that could interfere with participation were excluded.

Basic demographic information (child age, gender, visit type, and previous dental experience) and response rates for each stratum are reported in Sect. 5.1.

Data collection procedures

Questionnaire survey

A structured questionnaire was developed to collect quantitative data on environmental needs and satisfaction. The instrument comprised three parts: (1) demographic information; (2) items on spatial, sensory, and service attributes of pediatric dental clinics; and (3) paired functional/dysfunctional items for Kano classification. For each attribute, respondents (children, assisted by caregivers when necessary, and caregivers themselves) indicated their attitudes on a 5-point scale reflecting liking or tolerance of the presence and absence of the attribute.

Questionnaires were administered in the waiting area after the visit. Trained researchers read items aloud to younger children and clarified any questions to minimize misunderstanding. For children aged approximately 4–7 years, a pictorial faces rating scale aligned with the 5-point response options was used to support comprehension, and researchers briefly checked understanding before recording each response. Incomplete questionnaires were checked on site, and obviously inconsistent responses were excluded according to pre-defined criteria.

Semi-structured interviews

To explore implicit needs and contextual factors not easily captured by questionnaires, semi-structured interviews were conducted with a subset of caregivers and children, as well as with pediatric dental staff. The caregiver–child interviews focused on: (1) children’s emotional states before, during, and after treatment; (2) perceived environmental stressors (e.g., noise, smell, visual elements); and (3) spatial features they found comforting or disturbing. Staff interviews examined their observations of children’s behavior, perceived environmental bottlenecks, and practical constraints on spatial modification.

All interviews followed a semi-structured guide, were audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts using thematic analysis, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Inter-coder agreement was checked on a subset of transcripts to ensure reliability. Themes from the interviews were used both to refine the questionnaire items and to support the later translation of user requirements into art-therapy-oriented design strategies.

Focus groups

Focus groups were organized to integrate perspectives from different stakeholders and to synthesize candidate environmental indicators. Separate sessions were held with (1) pediatric dentists and nurses, (2) healthcare architects and interior designers, and (3) caregivers. Each session lasted approximately 30–45 min and followed a discussion guide covering: key environmental problems in current clinics, prioritization of children’s psychological needs, and possible spatial interventions.

Discussions were audio-recorded and summarized into indicator lists. Overlapping or redundant indicators identified from questionnaires, interviews, and focus groups were merged, and items with low relevance or feasibility were removed. The resulting list of candidate requirements formed the basis for the Kano questionnaire and subsequent modelling.

Kano-AHP-FCE method

Based on the integrated framework outlined in Sect. 2.3, this study operationalized the Kano-AHP-FCE pipeline in four steps: (1) Kano-based demand attribute analysis; (2) AHP-based weighting of key requirements; (3) translation of high-priority requirements into art-therapy-informed spatial strategies; and (4) FCE-based evaluation of demand satisfaction for alternative design schemes (Fig. 2).

Design framework.

Demand attribute analysis based on the Kano model

The Kano model was used to classify environmental requirements according to their effect on user satisfaction and emotional responses32. Requirements identified from the questionnaires, interviews, and focus groups (Sect. 4.2) were converted into paired functional and dysfunctional questions in the Kano questionnaire.

Responses from children, caregivers, and staff were coded using the standard Kano evaluation table (Appendix A), yielding a five-category classification: Must-be (M), One-dimensional (O), Attractive (A), Indifferent (I), and Reverse (R). For each requirement, the distribution of functional/dysfunctional response combinations was used to determine the dominant Kano category and to compute satisfaction and dissatisfaction indices.

Attributes classified as Indifferent or Reverse (I/R), which have little or negative influence on satisfaction, were excluded from further analysis33. Must-be, One-dimensional, and Attractive attributes (M/O/A) were retained as core environmental indicators and formed the reduced indicator set passed to the AHP weighting stage.

Demand weight calculation based on AHP

To quantify the relative importance of the Kano-filtered requirements, the AHP was applied34. A three-level hierarchy was constructed with "optimal pediatric dental environment" as the goal level, Kano categories (M, O, A) as the criteria level, and specific environmental requirements (M1–Mn, O1–On, A1–An) as the sub-criteria level (Fig. 3).

Hierarchical model.

An expert panel comprising pediatric dentists, healthcare architects, interior designers, and child psychology/art-therapy specialists performed pairwise comparisons of elements within and across the Kano categories using the standard 1–9 scale. Individual judgement matrices were aggregated, and eigenvalue-based methods were used to derive local and global weights. Consistency indices and ratios (CI, CR) were calculated; matrices with CR > 0.1 were revised until acceptable consistency was achieved35.

The resulting weight vector indicates the priority of each requirement in spatial design and was subsequently used as the coefficient set in the Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation of different design schemes. Detailed computation steps and formulas are provided in Appendix B39.

Translation of key user requirements integrating art-therapy concepts

Following Kano classification and AHP weighting, high-priority requirements with strong emotional relevance were selected and translated into concrete spatial strategies informed by art-therapy principles.

First, drawing on interview and focus-group results, the research team mapped each key requirement to potential art-therapy functions such as emotional soothing, anxiety reduction, attention diversion, narrative engagement, and sense of safety. Second, candidate spatial responses were proposed for each function, including elements related to light, color, texture, spatial rhythm, parental accompaniment, spatial openness, visual distraction, and tactile engagement36.

A multidisciplinary expert panel (pediatric dentists, designers, and art-therapy specialists) reviewed these mappings in several iterative workshops, evaluating feasibility, clinical practicality, and innovation37. Through this process, a set of core spatial modules—such as sensory-guided waiting areas, emotionally adaptive color schemes, and immersive ceiling projections—was defined and incorporated into the conceptual design of the optimized pediatric dental clinic. Each module was explicitly linked to one or more psychological goals within the art-therapy framework, ensuring that the resulting design strategies addressed functional, emotional, and therapeutic needs.

Demand satisfaction calculation based on FCE

To evaluate how effectively the conceptual design satisfied the weighted user requirements, a FCE was conducted38. An indicator system aligned with the AHP hierarchy was established, covering all retained M/O/A attributes. Both experts (with at least three years of relevant clinical or design experience) and caregivers (parents or guardians accompanying children to dental visits) participated in the evaluation. Participants rated two schemes—the optimized art-therapy-oriented design (Sample A) and the existing clinic environment (Sample B)—on a 0–100 scale for each indicator, where higher scores indicate better fulfilment of user needs.

Scores were grouped into four rating intervals (0–60, 60–75, 75–90, 90–100), corresponding to a fuzzy evaluation set T = {T1, T2, T3, T4} (dissatisfied, neutral, satisfied, very satisfied). Linguistic ratings were converted into membership degrees, forming fuzzy relation matrices for each scheme. The AHP-derived weight vector was then applied to the fuzzy matrices to obtain comprehensive evaluation vectors and defuzzified satisfaction scores.

By comparing the overall indices and indicator-level scores between Sample A and Sample B, the study assessed whether the integrated Kano-AHP-FCE framework and art-therapy-oriented design strategies led to meaningful improvements in children’s emotional comfort, perceived environmental quality, and overall experience in pediatric dental clinics39.

Ethical considerations

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Dongguan City University. The committee provided a written approval letter but did not issue a unique approval number for this study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines, local regulations, and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

For adult participants (caregivers, dental staff, and experts), written informed consent was obtained prior to questionnaire completion, interviews, focus groups, and evaluation of design schemes. For pediatric participants, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and age-appropriate oral explanations of the study were given to children before data collection. Children were asked for their verbal assent and were free to decline or withdraw at any time without any impact on their treatment. All questionnaire and interview data were anonymized at the point of entry, and no personally identifiable information was reported in any publications or presentations.

Based on the valid questionnaire samples (N = 213), a clear pattern of user needs emerged across different participant groups. Among dental staff (N = 76), 68.2% reported problems such as unreasonable spatial layout, interference with clinical workflow, and insufficient environmental comfort. Among caregivers (N = 54), 58.6% indicated that the clinic environment did not sufficiently alleviate children’s anxiety. Among pediatric patients (N = 83), 60.0% expressed that the clinics lacked engaging features and interactive facilities. Overall, these results reflect consistent deficits in spatial layout, environmental comfort, and psychological support mechanisms across user groups.

Using systematic coding and inductive analysis, four core themes were extracted from 30 interviews. All interviews followed a semi-structured guide covering treatment experience, emotional reactions, and perceived environmental facilitators/barriers; recordings were transcribed verbatim and coded line-by-line by two trained researchers. A shared codebook was developed iteratively, intercoder agreement was checked on a random subset of transcripts, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. In brief:

A. Layout-process coordination: Insufficient space and suboptimal equipment placement disrupt clinical workflows.

B. Safety and human-centered needs: Emphasis on safety protection, accessibility, and caregiver-staff communication.

C. Child psychological care: Low environmental engagement hampers anxiety relief; caregivers expect stronger soothing functions.

D. Design-function balance: Lack of child-friendly elements and rational equipment layout leads to an aesthetics-function mismatch.

According to the Kano model results, the Waiting Area Entertainment Corner was classified as an Indifferent requirement, indicating that it has little influence on overall satisfaction and therefore does not require design prioritization.

By contrast, elements such as the Parent Accompaniment Area, Independent Treatment Zone, Appropriate Treatment Orientation, and Soft Ambient Lighting were categorized as Must-be attributes. These represent essential design requirements—if absent, user satisfaction would drop sharply.

Requirements that directly correlate with children’s perceptual and emotional comfort—such as Rich Spatial Color Scheme, Warm Ambient Tones, Healing Symbolic Decorations, and Consistent Environmental Materials—were classified as One-dimensional attributes, where satisfaction increases proportionally with fulfillment. These represent key features for optimizing both functionality and psychological experience.

Meanwhile, features such as Overhead Interactive Projection, Playful Floor Guidance, Soothing Background Music, Audio-Visual Interactive Experience, and Child-Safe Corner Protection were identified as Attractive attributes. Although their absence does not affect basic satisfaction, their inclusion greatly enriches user experience and enhances emotional engagement.

In summary, the Kano analysis clarified that the Must-be category addresses functional and emotional safety needs; the One-dimensional category represents quality-enhancing environmental attributes; and the Attractive category contributes to delight, immersion, and engagement. These findings provide a hierarchical foundation for subsequent quantitative weighting using the AHP method, ensuring that design decisions align with both functional necessity and emotional well-being.

Weight calculation of design requirements for pediatric dental clinics

After classifying pediatric user needs through the Kano model, the AHP was employed to quantify the relative importance of each requirement and identify priority design attributes. The Must-be, One-dimensional, and Attractive attributes were defined as the criteria layer, while the specific needs within each category formed the sub-criteria layer, thus constructing the hierarchical structure model shown in Fig. 5.

Hierarchical structure model of pediatric dental clinics design.

To ensure both scientific rigor and practical reliability, expert evaluation was conducted by professionals from two complementary perspectives—academic research in healthcare architecture and practical experience in pediatric dental design. All theoretical experts possessed AHP application experience, while the participating designers had over five years of clinical project practice.

Following Saaty’s 1–9 pairwise comparison scale40, experts rated the relative importance of each requirement within and across the three attribute categories. Individual judgement matrices were first constructed for each expert; an aggregate group matrix was then obtained by taking the element-wise geometric mean across experts. Eigenvalue-based methods were used to derive local and global weights from the group matrices41. For each matrix, the maximum eigenvalue (λmax), consistency index (CI), and consistency ratio (CR) were calculated using the standard random index table; when CR exceeded 0.1, the corresponding experts were asked to review and adjust their comparisons until acceptable consistency was achieved. The results of the criterion-layer and sub-criterion-layer weight analyses are presented in Appendices C and D.

The CR for all final matrices was ≤ 0.1, confirming the logical reliability of the expert judgments. Among the criterion-level attributes, Must-be requirements carried the highest overall weight, indicating that functional and emotional safety needs should be prioritized in pediatric dental clinic design.

Since weights derived from different attribute categories existed on separate scales, a unified comparison was achieved by multiplying the criterion-level weights by the sub-criterion-level weights, producing normalized relative weights. This enabled prioritization of pediatric user requirements under a single dimension (Table 6).

Conceptual design of pediatric dental clinic scheme

Building on the results of the AHP weight analysis, this section presents a conceptual design scheme for pediatric dental clinics that integrates art-therapy principles with user-centered spatial optimization.

Design background and site selection

Field investigations were conducted in several children’s hospitals to analyze the spatial structures, functional layouts, and environmental characteristics of existing pediatric dental clinics. The findings revealed that while current facilities meet basic functional needs, they fall short in psychological comfort, human-centered experience, and emotional healing.

To ensure empirical validity, a tertiary grade-A children’s hospital dental center was selected as the design site. Its large patient volume and established management system provided a suitable basis for experimental validation (Fig. 6).

Prototype of the improved pediatric dental clinic design.

Design strategy and art-therapy integration

The conceptual design adopts a multi-level integration model, translating psychological and physiological needs into concrete spatial strategies through an art-therapy framework. On the basis of the Kano-AHP results, user requirements with higher normalized weights were selected as key design drivers for spatial optimization.

To ensure that this transformation from abstract requirements to design strategies was both theoretically grounded and practically feasible, a multidisciplinary expert panel was convened, consisting of the same 13 experts described in Sect. 5.3. The translation process followed a two-round Delphi-style focus group procedure. In the first round, experts participated in two 30-min semi-structured focus group sessions. Using a structured discussion guide, they were asked to: (1) review each high-weight user requirement (M/O/A attributes); (2) identify its primary psychological function; and (3) propose one or more candidate spatial responses. The research team recorded and transcribed the discussions, then constructed an initial "requirement–function–strategy" mapping matrix. A directed content analysis approach was used to code experts’ suggestions into thematic categories, and overlapping strategies were merged.

In the second round, the same 13 experts independently rated each provisional strategy in the matrix on three 5-point Likert scales: therapeutic relevance, clinical feasibility, and design innovation. For each strategy, an agreement index was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning a score ≥ 4 on therapeutic relevance and feasibility. Strategies with agreement ≥ 0.80 on both dimensions were retained; those below the threshold were either revised according to experts’ qualitative comments or removed from the final scheme.

Key art-therapy spatial features

The proposed conceptual scheme clusters the high-weight user requirements into five art-therapy-oriented spatial feature groups, each explicitly linked to the corresponding Kano-AHP codes:

Emotional Safety and Caregiver Anchoring: Parent Accompaniment Area (M1), Independent Treatment Zone (M2), and Appropriate Treatment Orientation (M3) are jointly used to construct a stable sense of emotional safety. The accompaniment seat is placed within the child’s direct visual field but outside the core operating zone, while the treatment chair orientation and visual corridors are adjusted to maintain eye contact between child, caregiver, and dentist. This configuration operationalizes children’s need for visible support without disrupting clinical workflow, translating high-weight "safety and reassurance" demands into spatial proximity and controlled separation.

Visual and Chromatic Comfort: Rich Spatial Color Scheme (O1) and Warm Ambient Tones (O2) respond to children’s preference for low-arousal visual environments. In the scheme, low-saturation blues and pinks are combined with warm white lighting to reduce glare and visual tension. These features translate one-dimensional attributes related to “comfort” into specific hue, saturation, and color-zone allocations, using chromatic modulation as a non-verbal medium for emotional soothing and anxiety reduction.

Material and Tactile Safety: Non-slip Antibacterial Flooring (M5) and Child-Safe Corner Protection (A7) address both functional protection and perceived bodily safety. R10-grade flooring with soft geometric patterns reduces the risk of falls, while rounded corners and curved transitions reduce collision anxiety and harsh visual impressions. Together, these elements embody high-weight requirements related to hygiene and safety, and at the same time provide gentle tactile and morphological cues that support children’s basic trust in the environment.

Attention Diversion and Multimodal Engagement: Overhead Interactive Projection (A1), Soothing Background Music (A3), and Audio-Visual Interactive Experience (A4) operationalize Attractive (A) attributes focused on distraction and engagement. Dynamic ceiling imagery, soft slow-tempo music, and local interactive media nodes create parallel channels of sensory input, enabling children to shift attention away from treatment procedures. These features translate “playfulness” and “interest” requirements into concrete multimodal stimuli, supporting cognitive distraction and emotional regulation during potentially stressful procedures.

Ecological and Symbolic Healing Elements: Healing Symbolic Decorations (O3) and Green Plant Accents (O5) provide nature-related and symbolically coherent visual anchors. Low-saturation illustrative motifs are coordinated with embedded low-allergen greenery to avoid overstimulation while maintaining a continuous theme. This combination responds to user demands for "natural, relaxing atmosphere" and "coherent style," transforming them into symbolic and ecological cues that reduce environmental stress and enhance overall spatial coherence.

Design outcomes

Based on these strategies, the conceptual design establishes a pediatric dental environment that harmonizes safety, comfort, playfulness, and healing. The space operates not only as a medical facility but as an emotionally adaptive environment, transforming the treatment process into a psychologically supportive experience (Fig. 7).

This integration of the Kano-AHP demand analysis with art-therapy-based spatial translation demonstrates how data-driven frameworks can be converted into tangible, human-centered design interventions. The resulting conceptual model provides both a theoretical framework and a practical reference for future pediatric healthcare space design.

Satisfaction analysis of the conceptual design for pediatric dental clinics

To examine the validity and practical effectiveness of the proposed design framework, this study used FCE to compare user satisfaction between the optimized conceptual design (Sample A) and the existing pediatric dental clinic (Sample B). In addition, paired inferential statistics were conducted on participants’ overall raw satisfaction scores to test whether the observed difference was statistically reliable.

Experimental setup

A tertiary children’s hospital dental center was selected as the study context. Participants included pediatric patients from different treatment categories (no treatment needed, non-extraction treatment, and extraction treatment), their caregivers, and pediatric dentists. All participants completed one questionnaire containing two visual stimuli (Sample A and Sample B). To control for order effects, the presentation order was randomly counterbalanced (A → B vs. B → A). Ratings were collected based on AHP-derived user requirement indicators.

Altogether, 62 questionnaires were distributed and 57 were returned (response rate: 91.9%); all 57 were valid after completeness checks. Among them, 43 questionnaires were completed by pediatric patients (aged 5–12) and caregivers (aged 26–52), and 14 by pediatric dentists and professional designers with ≥ 5 years of pediatric healthcare design experience (aged 30–48).

Although the sample included both pediatric patients and caregivers and covered multiple treatment categories, subgroup effect comparisons (e.g., children vs. caregivers; extraction vs. non-extraction vs. no-treatment) were not conducted in the present analysis. This was primarily due to practical constraints in the clinical setting, including uneven and potentially small subgroup sizes across treatment categories, which would reduce statistical power and may yield unstable estimates. In addition, participants’ treatment pathways and appointment availability were determined by routine clinical scheduling, limiting the feasibility of balanced recruitment and controlled stratification within each subgroup. Future work could address this limitation through a larger multi-site sample and stratified recruitment to enable subgroup-specific and interaction analyses.

Key findings

The FCE results suggest that integrating art therapy principles into spatial design can improve children’s reported emotional experience and treatment cooperation. Within the scope of this study, these improvements can be interpreted through several mechanisms:

(1) Spatial Construction of Safety and Emotional Support

High-weight demands such as Parent Accompaniment Area and Independent Treatment Zone indicate that emotional visibility and controlled proximity are important in unfamiliar medical environments. Spaces are required to balance procedural efficiency with the provision of psychological anchors that help reduce anxiety through the presence or perceived availability of caregivers. However, the present data do not disentangle environmental effects from possible expectancy or placebo effects; caregivers and children may have rated redesigned spaces more positively simply because they were presented as “optimized” or "child-friendly."

(2) Psychophysiological Impact of Color and Lighting

Design strategies involving Rich Spatial Color Schemes and Warm Ambient Tones were associated with higher satisfaction and lower reported tension. By carefully regulating hue, brightness, and saturation, the environment appears to function as a non-verbal regulatory medium, broadly consistent with environmental psychology findings on color and stress recovery. At the same time, these results were obtained in a specific cultural and institutional context. Color preferences, symbolic meanings, and tolerance for visual intensity may vary across regions and cultural groups, which limits the straightforward generalization of the present palette and composition to other settings.

(3) Attractive Attributes and Attention-Shift Mechanisms

Features such as Overhead Interactive Projection and Audio-Visual Interactive Experiences, classified mainly as Attractive (A) attributes, played a supporting role in cognitive distraction and situational engagement. These elements seemed to help children shift attention away from treatment stimuli, which aligns with distraction-based interventions in clinical psychology. Nevertheless, it remains unclear to what extent the observed benefits arise from the novelty of the installations, the presence of researchers, or sustained therapeutic value over repeated visits. Longitudinal data would be needed to verify whether these effects persist once the novelty has faded.

(4) Integration of Safety Design and Healing Semantics

Functional components like Non-slip Antibacterial Flooring and Child-Safe Corner Protection illustrate how material tactility and morphological softness can convey psychological safety. Rounded edges, gentle textures, and warm materials appeared to integrate physical protection with therapeutic symbolism, contributing to both practical safety and perceived comfort. However, some of these specifications (e.g., higher-grade materials, customized corner details) may be more feasible in well-resourced hospitals than in small community clinics, raising questions about economic scalability and maintenance costs.

The comparison between Samples A and B indicates that satisfaction improvement is not only quantitative but also qualitative, with more participants describing the optimized clinic as “comfortable”, “interesting”, or “less scary” rather than merely “acceptable”. At the same time, all outcome measures in this study were based on self- or proxy-reported evaluations, which are susceptible to social desirability, placebo effects, and short-term mood fluctuations. Without behavioral or physiological indicators, the results should be interpreted as evidence of perceived improvement rather than definitive proof of deeper or long-term therapeutic change.

Within these limitations, the findings support the idea that pediatric dental design, when guided by structured user-centered methodologies, can move beyond purely functional optimization and begin to address emotional and behavioral needs in a more systematic way. The integrated Kano-AHP-FCE framework helped link children’s and caregivers’ needs to design priorities and provided a transparent procedure for comparing spatial schemes. Future work should build on this foundation by incorporating cross-cultural comparisons, explicit cost–benefit analysis for resource-limited clinics, and multimodal outcome measures to more rigorously evaluate the psychological and practical impact of healing-oriented spatial interventions.

Conclusion

This study developed and empirically tested an integrated Kano-AHP-FCE framework for art-therapy-oriented and user-centered space design in pediatric dental clinics. Through a multi-stage investigation combining questionnaires, interviews, and focus groups, children’s psychological and spatial needs were identified, classified into Kano requirement types, and weighted using AHP. FCE then compared the existing environments with an optimized conceptual scheme. The results showed that the proposed design achieved a 26.58% increase in the overall satisfaction index relative to current clinics, indicating that the framework can effectively improve children’s perceived comfort and cooperation experience.

The empirical findings demonstrate that art-therapy-informed environmental features exert measurable effects on children’s emotional comfort. Requirements related to emotional safety, parental companionship, visual comfort, and interactive sensory engagement were predominantly classified as Must-be or high-weight One-dimensional/Attractive attributes, and the optimized design that strengthened these elements consistently obtained higher satisfaction scores in the FCE analysis. These results suggest that translating art therapy principles into specific spatial configurations can reduce anxiety and support more stable emotional states during dental treatment, beyond merely providing aesthetic decoration.

This research provides methodological and practical implications for pediatric healthcare design. Methodologically, it shows that the combined use of Kano, AHP, and FCE offers a structured and replicable way to transform user perceptions into weighted design parameters and to verify the performance of alternative spatial schemes. Practically, it supplies evidence-based guidance for the layout and environmental features of child-friendly dental clinics, especially regarding the integration of art-therapy-oriented elements.

The study has several limitations. The sample size and number of participating institutions were modest, and the evaluation relied primarily on self-reported and proxy-reported measures of satisfaction and emotional comfort. Future studies should replicate the framework in larger and more diverse settings and consider incorporating physiological indicators to triangulate emotional states and examine longer-term psychological outcomes of healing-oriented spatial design.
