MicroRNA-mediated regulation of proliferation, lineage differentiation, and apoptosis in neural stem cells
Authors: Yukyeong Lee, Camilla Boschian, Kinarm Ko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeural stem cells (NSCs) are multipotent stem cells with self-renewal capacity, able to differentiate into all neural lineages of the central nervous system, including neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes; thus, their proliferation and differentiation are essential for embryonic neurodevelopment and adult brain homoeostasis. Dysregulation in these processes is implicated in neurological disorders, highlighting the need to elucidate how NSCs proliferate and differentiate to clarify the mechanisms of neurogenesis and uncover potential therapeutic targets. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression involved in many aspects of nervous system development and function. Multiple studies have shown that miRNAs control the balance between self-renewal and differentiation during development through transcriptional networks and fine-tuned signalling pathways. They also regulate key biological processes, including cell fate determination, developmental timing, neurogenesis, gliogenesis, and apoptosis. Transcriptomic analyses and high-resolution profiling have revealed temporally and spatially restricted miRNA expression patterns in NSCs and their progeny, suggesting highly context-dependent regulatory functions. Here, we provide an integrated overview of recent advances in miRNA biology relevant to NSC maintenance and lineage specification, with a focus on the mechanistic understanding of miRNA roles in neuronal differentiation, glial development, and programmed cell death across neural development.KEYWORDS: Neural stem cell, microRNA, lineage differentiation, neurogenesis, gliogenesis, apoptosis, neurodegenerative disease, neuropsychiatric disease, epigenetic regulation, transcriptomic networks

Introduction

Neural stem cells (NSCs) are multipotent stem cells that play a central role in neurogenesis in the central nervous system (CNS) [1,2]. They are defined by their ability to self-renew (generating identical daughter cells) and to differentiate into the major neural lineages, which include neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes (Figure 1(A)) [1,2]. In the adult brain, NSCs reside mainly within two major neurogenic niches: the subventricular zone (SVZ) and the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus. Both of these niches actively support neurogenesis throughout adulthood (Figure 1a). NSCs can also be experimentally derived from pluripotent or somatic cells, approaches that provide useful models but are not the main focus of this review (Figure 1(B)) [3–6]. Following their derivation, the proliferation and differentiation of NSCs are regulated by an interaction of extrinsic signals, endogenous transcriptional programmes, and epigenetic mechanisms, including the activity of microRNAs (miRNAs) [7–12].

Among these regulatory factors, miRNAs have emerged as important bidirectional regulators of gene expression. These small non-coding RNAs, approximately 22 nucleotides in length, bind to complementary sequences in the 3′ UTR of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), thereby mediating mRNA degradation or translational repression [13–16]. miRNAs serve as critical modulators in an extensive repertoire of gene regulatory mechanisms, including cell proliferation, fate determination, and neural differentiation [17–19]. In particular, they can control the balance between neurogenesis and gliogenesis, a central aspect of cell fate determination, through their modulation of pathways, lineage-specific transcription factors, and signalling pathways [9,12,18–20].

The brain exhibits a distinctive and extensive miRNA repertoire, many of which show spatiotemporal specificity and contribute to CNS complexity [18,21], beyond its developmental roles, miRNAs also regulate programmed cell death, targeting both pro- and anti-apoptotic genes, highlighting their dual function in preserving neural homoeostasis and guiding developmental trajectories [17,21–24].

Recent advances in high-throughput miRNA profiling and functional screening have identified novel regulators of NSC fate decisions and neuronal – glial differentiation [25–27]. However, while these studies have substantially expanded the repertoire of candidate miRNAs, they often investigate a single miRNA at a time, which limits the understanding of network-level interactions [18]. A critical gap remains in explaining how diverse miRNAs converge on a limited number of regulatory hubs – such as NR2E1/TLX, STAT3, EZH2, and Wnt – that ultimately govern NSC self-renewal, lineage specification, and survival. Furthermore, the phenotypic effects of the same miRNA can vary with developmental timing, lineage competence, and epigenetic state, indicating that miRNA function should be studied within dynamic regulatory networks rather than as discrete single-gene regulators. Collectively, these insights support a systems-level model in which miRNAs converge on key regulatory hubs to form multilayered feedback loops that integrate developmental timing, lineage competence, and stress signals. This systems-level perspective move beyond descriptive catalogues, offering a predictive framework for how miRNA perturbations reprogram NSC fate decisions in health and disease [18].

miRNA in the regulation of neural stem cell proliferation and differentiation

Extensive studies have demonstrated the essential role of miRNAs in regulating NSC maintenance and cellular mechanisms of neuronal commitment [10,28–30]. miRNAs can simultaneously target multiple genes to modulate diverse signalling pathways and fine-tune gene expression networks that modulate NSC proliferation and systematic lineage commitment (Figure 2). Expression patterns, target predictions, and functional analyses emphasize miRNAs as core regulators in NSC biology.

Neural stem cell proliferation

A hallmark of NSCs is their ability to regenerate themselves, maintain their population, and differentiate into neuronal and glial cells. During brain development, miRNAs govern progenitor/stem cell proliferation, neuronal maturation, and functional integration [31–34]. For instance, during embryonic development, miR-9 inhibits the proliferation of neural progenitors via modulation of transcription factors such as HES1, HES5, HES6, and HER9 (Figure 2) [35–37] and it promotes neuronal differentiation by facilitating cell cycle exit by inhibiting ELAVL3 and FOXG1 (Figure 2) [38–40].

In addition to miR-9, other miRNAs contribute to the regulation of neuronal fate in embryonic development. A key example is miR-124, which promotes neural differentiation by directly repressing multiple target genes, including SCP1, PTBP1, LAMA1, ITGB1, and ACTL6A (BAF53a) (Figure 2) [41–44]. Together with miR-124, miR-9* regulates chromatin-remodelling mechanisms involved in the switch of neural progenitor proliferation [44,45].

Overexpression of miR-137 promotes NSC proliferation, while its repression via negative feedback by NR2E1 maintains their proliferation [46]. This mechanism operates through embryonic development and into adulthood (Figure 2) [47,48]. Notably, miR-9 targets NR2E1 (nuclear receptor subfamily 2, group E, member 1, formerly referred to as TLX), a key factor for self-renewal in neural progenitors (Figure 2). By repressing NR2E1, miR-9 establishes a negative feedback loop that helps balance NSC proliferation and differentiation, thus preserving the stemness of NSCs during adulthood [49,50]. Similarly, let-7b suppresses NR2E1 and CCND1 to promote neuronal differentiation [12], miR-124 targets SOX9 and JAG1 to stimulate neuron differentiation also in vivo in the subventricular zone (SVZ) stem cell niche [51]. miR-137 also balances proliferation and differentiation in the brain by directly targeting key regulators such as CDK6 and EZH2 [48,52]. Indeed, CDK6 is involved in cell cycle progression, so the repression mediated by miR-137 prevents the excessive proliferation of NSCs [48]. EZH2 is a histone methyltransferase important to maintain stemness, so when miR-137 represses EZH2 post-transcriptionally, it promotes the differentiation of NSCs by lifting the repression on differentiation-related genes [52]. In mice, the loss of miR-137 leads to increased EZH2 expression, resulting in excessive proliferation, impaired synaptic plasticity, dendritic overgrowth, and behavioural abnormalities and cognitive impairments [53].

Interestingly, increased levels of miR-184 also promote NSC proliferation and inhibit neural lineage progression by targeting NUMBL [54]. Taken together, these findings indicate a network-level regulation in which multiple miRNAs converge on shared hubs such as a limited set of regulatory hubs NR2E1, CDK6, and EZH2 (Figure 2).

Cell fate determination and differentiation

miRNAs are emerging as critical regulators of NSC heterogeneity and developmental potential by mediating transitions between distinct transcriptional programmes that control lineage-specific differentiation. During neurogenesis and gliogenesis, NSCs exhibit sequential and tightly regulated expression of distinct miRNA families in a temporally and spatially restricted manner. For example, miR-124 and miR-128 are highly expressed in neurons, miR-26 and miR-29 are predominantly enriched in glial cells, and miR-23 expression is largely restricted to astrocytes [42,55–57]. Global profiling has discovered more than 100 cell-type-specific miRNAs that exhibited a specific temporal and spatial expression in neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes during neural development [31,58–65]. These findings underline how specific sets of miRNAs contribute to neuronal versus glial lineage differentiation (Figure 3).

Several miRNAs exhibit context-dependent and sometimes divergent effects that cannot be explained solely by their primary targets. For example, miR-124 promotes neuronal differentiation in cortical progenitors while simultaneously suppressing astrocytic fate through STAT3 inhibition [45], and let-7 family regulates the neurogenic-to-gliogenic switch [12,66]). demonstrating how a single miRNA family can direct lineage choice at different developmental stages. Similarly, miR-137 suppresses proliferation to accelerate embryonic NSC differentiation [48], yet in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived NSCs, it paradoxically enhances pluripotency markers such as OCT4 and SOX2, reflecting differences in lineage competence and reprogramming context [67]. These differences demonstrate that a single miRNA family can direct lineage choice at different development stages, and emphasize how the same miRNAs can strengthen stemness in reprogrammed human cells while promoting differentiation in embryonic NSCs, demonstrating the critical role of cellular context. Recognizing this variability is essential for interpreting seemingly contradictory results across models and for identifying mechanisms that are most relevant to human NSC biology.

Gliogenic regulation presents comparable complexity, with miRNAs orchestrating the specification towards astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and Schwann cells through stage-specific and context-dependent mechanisms. Recent studies suggest that miRNAs determine the fate of neurons and glia, both originating from neuronal precursor cells in the CNS and the PNS [18]. The processes of neurogenesis and gliogenesis occur at different times during development and involve many intermediate cell types. The miRNA expression is cell-type-specific, spatially limited, and time-specific [31,58–64,68] during in vivo neurogenesis.

Multiple independent profiling studies have demonstrated that distinct sets of miRNAs are consistently associated with either neuronal or glial differentiation, with over 100 miRNAs showing distinct expression patterns across neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes [59]. Importantly, the effects of specific miRNAs experimentally produce divergent outcomes contingent on cellular competence, developmental timing, and spatial context, underlining that miRNAs act not as static determinations of fate but as context-dependent regulators integrated into broader transcriptional and epigenetic networks (Figure 3).

One of the most studied miRNAs in neurogenesis is miR-9, enriched in the neuronal lineage [69]. In mice, NR2E1 inhibits miR-9 expression, whereas the overexpression of miR-9 induces neuronal differentiation, directly inhibiting the nuclear NR2E1 receptor. These findings suggest a sophisticated feedback mechanism that precisely controls the balance between NSC renewal and differentiation [50]. In combination with miRNA-124, miR-9 can reprogram human fibroblasts into functional neurons, demonstrating extensive epigenetic reconfiguration [70]. By suppressing the expression of pro-glial factors, miR-9 also controls the number of neurons in the mouse cortex [71].

Notably, several neurogenic miRNAs target NR2E1 and the Wnt signalling pathway, a representative modulator regulated by multiple miRNAs during neurogenesis, investigating evidence for network-level regulation [18,72]. For instance, miR-137 interferes with NR2E1 function in the embryonic mouse brain by inhibiting NR2E1 transcription [73]. Thus, similar to the miR-9/NR2E1 interaction, NR2E1 and miR-137 constitute a negative feedback mechanism. These emerging roles for miRNAs have implications for understanding the evolution of the nervous system and how several miRNAs and target genes can function as either activators or repressors during development.

During late neurogenesis and early postnatal development, neural precursor cells undergo lineage specification to generate different types of glial cells such as astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the PNS [74]. miRNAs can regulate gene expression programmes essential for gliogenesis and oligodendrocyte differentiation [75,76]. Conditional ablation of DICER1, an endoribonuclease essential for miRNA biogenesis and specifically expressed in oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), inhibits OPC maturation into oligodendrocytes, indicating that miRNAs are crucial for this developmental process [75,76].

Among the miRNAs enriched in OPCs, miR-138, miR-219, and miR-338 are upregulated around the time of birth and play a central role in promoting the differentiation into oligodendrocytes [75,76]. These miRNAs suppress the translation of negative regulators of differentiation, such as precursor genes ZBTB18 (Zfp238) and transcriptional inhibitors of oligodendrocyte maturation (i.e. SOX6, HES5) [77–79]. Interestingly, the reduction of miR-219 and miR-338 in DICER1-deficient OPCs restores their differentiation capacity into mature oligodendrocytes, further highlighting their role in lineage progression [19].

In contrast, miR-153 targets NFIA and NFIB to inhibit neuronal differentiation and its downregulation is a prerequisite for the acquisition of gliogenic competence in neural precursor cells [80]. Similarly, the downregulation of miR-17/miR-106 family during development facilitates the expression of gliogenic factors, enabling the neuro-to-gliogenic switch [20]. The let-7 family of miRNAs also contributes to neuroglial lineage specification by fine-tuning gene networks involved in glial cell lineage determination [66,81,82].

Importantly, miRNAs traditionally known for regulating neuronal fate have also been shown to promote the gliogenic process. For example, miR-23a induces maturation during oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelin synthesis, while miR-92a, belonging to a miRNA cluster implicated in neurogenesis, plays a crucial role in astrocyte lineage commitment and differentiation [83]. Viewed collectively, these findings support a systems-level model in which gliogenesis is governed through dynamic, stage-specific regulatory circuits of miRNAs that reinforce or antagonize transcriptional and epigenetic programmes, rather than a single set of regulators [52,66].

miRNAs in the regulation of apoptosis in neural cells

In NSCs and their progeny, miRNAs affect the balance between survival and programmed apoptosis, functioning as key modulators in cell fate decisions (Figure 4 and Table 1). Several lines of evidence suggest that miRNAs do not simply control apoptotic responses, but target core components of the apoptotic signalling cascades and act as molecular switches to determine cell survival outcomes [24]. miRNAs exert their regulatory function through post-transcriptional inhibition of mRNA targets, including both pro- and anti-death genes [24]. This bidirectional control enables miRNAs to integrate intracellular and extracellular signals and fine-tune the threshold for apoptosis initiation in a context-dependent manner. Instead of acting randomly across pathways, apoptosis-regulating miRNAs converge on a limited set of decision nodes, which can be classified into three functional tiers: (i) mitochondrial threshold control (BCL-2 family), (ii) apoptosome formation (Apaf-1, caspase-9), and (iii) death receptor signalling (Fas/FADD, caspase-8/10). Mapping miRNA convergence to these tiers clarifies how apoptotic sensitivity is dynamically tuned into NSCs.

In NSCs and differentiating neuronal populations, specific miRNAs have been shown to directly interact with apoptosis-related effectors, such as the BCL-2 family, caspases, and p53 pathway components, to regulate survival dynamics during development and in stress responses [24,84]. Despite increasing evidence, miRNA-mediated apoptosis control has yet to be fully identified in its mechanistic aspects, especially within the nervous system. Future studies should aim to define the role of individual miRNA-target interactions and their integration into the higher-order regulatory networks. Omics-based approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and integrative networks analysis, will be critical to systematically map these miRNA circuits and thereby elucidate how apoptosis is selectively activated or suppressed during neurogenesis and neural maturation.

Several miRNAs converge on the Bcl-2 family, targeting both pro- and anti-apoptotic members such as BAX, BAK, BCL-2, and thereby modulating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway in neural cells. miR-365 and miR-125b inhibit pro-death members (Bax and Bak), whereas miR-15a/16–1, miR-24, and miR-148a suppress the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, establishing a conserved axis of mitochondrial apoptosis regulation [84–89]. Similarly, miR-195 directly targets ARL2, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death in human embryonic stem cell-derived nerve precursors, further supporting the hypothesis that certain miRNAs may interfere with mitochondrial homoeostasis in the early nervous system and induce intrinsic cell death [84]. In contrast, the miR-17–92 cluster inhibits tumour suppressor PTEN and activates survival accelerator PI3K/Akt signals to promote progenitor cell survival and proliferation after ischaemic stroke [90], further converging on Sonic Hedgehog-c-Myc axis that facilitates neural regeneration under stress [90]. Together, these findings highlight that multiple miRNAs are associated with the BCL-2 axis to regulate apoptotic sensitivity in NSCs, targeting both pro- and anti-apoptotic members to balance between elimination and differentiation of precursors. Beyond the BCL-2, miR-195b has been shown to target mitochondrial effectors such as ARL2, broadening the involvement of miRNAs in intrinsic apoptotic regulation.

MiRNAs are also involved in regulating the assembly and activation of the core effector complex of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. For example, miR-23a/b and miR-27a/b act as post-transcriptional regulators of Apaf-1, a core scaffold protein essential for apoptosome formation. By repressing Apaf-1 expression, these miRNAs reduce the recruitment and oligomerization of caspase-9, attenuating neuronal susceptibility to apoptotic stimuli [91]. Downstream of apoptosome activation, miR-24 and miR-133 have been shown to directly inhibit the translation of caspase-9 mRNA, suppressing caspase activation and modulating intrinsic apoptotic thresholds that influence neural progenitor cell fate decisions [92,93].

miRNAs act as critical modulators of death receptor-mediated signal transmission within the extrinsic apoptotic pathway; for instance, miR-K10a suppresses TWEAK-induced apoptosis by targeting its cognate receptor, Fn14, thereby interfering with TNF superfamily-mediated cell death signalling [94]. Additionally, miR-34a, miR-181c, and miR-187 have been reported to mediate post-transcriptional downregulation of tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) expression, attenuating cell death and inflammatory cascades downstream of TNF receptor activation [91,95].

Epigenetic regulation of miRNA expression represents an added regulatory mechanism that can substantially impact cellular sensitivity to apoptotic stimuli. The expression of miR-128a is subject to epigenetic silencing, which contributes to apoptosis resistance by derepressing Fas-associated death domain protein (FADD), a critical adaptor in the assembly of the death-inducing signalling complex (DISC) [96].

Beyond apoptosis, miRNAs intersect with alternative forms of regulated cell death such as necroptosis. As an example, miR-874 inhibits caspase-8 and facilitates RIPK3-mediated necroptotic signalling [97], while miR-186 has been shown to repress caspase-10 [98]. These findings indicate that miRNAs integrate multiple regulated cell death pathways, not limited to apoptosis, thereby broadening their functional impact in neural development.

These studies demonstrate that miRNAs constitute a multifaceted regulatory framework whereby they target key molecular effectors of the intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic cascades, orchestrating the balance between neuronal survival and apoptosis. Furthermore, the involvement of miRNAs in regulating necroptosis demonstrates their broader role in integrating various modes of apoptosis during neurodevelopment and in the pathological context.

Dysregulation of miRNAs in neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders: implications for neural stem cell function

Beyond their role in regulating the apoptosis pathway, miRNAs are increasingly recognized as key regulators whose dysregulation contributes to neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders through disruption of NSC homoeostasis. Dysregulation of certain miRNAs in neurological diseases significantly hinders the functional dynamics of NSCs and substantially contributes to disease progression. While miRNAs are indispensable for NSC growth, differentiation, and maintenance under physiological conditions, aberrant miRNA expressions in the pathological context can alter the balance of stem cell fate, impair regenerative capacity, and exacerbate neuropathological processes. Neurological and neuropsychiatric diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), multiple sclerosis (MS), schizophrenia (SCZ), bipolar disorder (BD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) support the hypothesis that disease-related miRNA disturbance converges to NSC dysfunction and is tightly linked to neurogenesis and progressive neurodegeneration (Table 2).

Among various neurodegenerative diseases, AD serves as a representative model demonstrating the pathological consequences of miRNA dysregulation on neuronal functions and NSC homoeostasis. In particular, miR-124, predominantly expressed in the CNS, is known to be widely involved in regulating key neurodevelopmental processes, including nervous system formation, synaptic plasticity, and adult neurogenesis. Theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence suggest that miR-124 downregulation in AD is not merely an epiphenomenon but a mechanism that inhibits NSC proliferation and differentiation capacity, weakens synaptic structures, and ultimately inhibits neuronal viability [99–101]. This downregulation also affects the alternative splicing of amyloid precursor protein (APP), resulting in increased production of amyloid-β peptides, a hallmark of AD pathology [102,103]. These findings support a new paradigm in which miRNA-regulated gene networks function as central modulators of stem cell dynamics and neuroplasticity, with their dysregulation leading to a collapse of finely tuned control circuits, which is recognized as an integrated pathological mechanism in various neurodegenerative diseases.

miR-34 is implicated in AD because of its role in promoting neuronal apoptosis [101,104,105]. In AD brains, elevated levels of miR-34a suppress the anti-apoptotic genes such as BCL2 and SIRT1, increasing neuronal cell death [101,105,106]. In addition to pro-apoptotic activities, miR-34a can directly regulate NSC pools, targeting stemness-associated and survival-promoting factors [107–109]. These combined activities critically exacerbate AD pathology.

miR-125b was upregulated in AD, affecting tau phosphorylation and neuronal apoptosis [110–112]. Its overexpression correlates with increased phosphorylated tau and decreased synaptic protein expression, exacerbating neurodegenerative processes [113–115]. It also suggests that the modulation of miR-125b in inflammatory pathways may be involved in AD-related neuroinflammation [112,115,116].

miR-29a protects neuronal function by regulating BACE1 expression, an enzyme involved in amyloid-β production. In AD, decreased levels of miR-29a lead to upregulation of BACE1, resulting in increased amyloidogenic processing of APP [102,117–120]. The downregulation of miR-29a has been implicated not only in amyloid-β plaque formation but also in the disruption of neuronal survival and functional integrity [118,121].

Overall, the aberrant expression of these miRNAs (i.e. miR-124, miR-34a, miR-125b, and miR-29a) plays a critical role on AD pathophysiology by affecting multiple processes such as neurogenesis, apoptosis, inflammation, and amyloid processing. Indeed, their dysregulation disrupts the equilibrium between apoptosis signalling (miR-34a, miR-125b) and neurogenesis-supporting pathways (miR-124, miR-29a), ultimately linking stem cell dysfunction to progressive neuronal loss. These findings suggest that investigating such mechanisms could provide a platform for miRNA-targeted therapeutics aimed at mitigating AD progression.

In MS, miRNA dysregulation is tightly associated with neuroinflammation and demyelination [122,123]. Notably, miR-155 is significantly upregulated in MS lesions and circulating immune cells. In addition to its pro-inflammatory role, increased miR-155 modulates cytokine signalling and disrupts the neurogenic niche by compromising blood-brain barrier integrity [123,124]. In an autoimmune encephalomyelitis model, miR-155 inhibition has been shown to affect NSC survival and differentiation, suggesting that miR-155 negatively modulates endogenous repair [125]. Similarly, miR-326 promotes Th17 cell expansion, exacerbating inflammation and indirectly inhibiting a supportive NSC microenvironment. miR-146a, upregulated in both MS and AD, enhances chronic inflammation by targeting components of the NF-κB pathway and further compromising NSC function. Overall, these observations indicate that MS-associated miRNA dysregulation operates through dual mechanisms: amplifying inflammatory responses and impairing NSC-mediated repair.

In ALS, miRNA-mediated disruption of neuronal-glial interactions contributes to motor neuron degeneration. Upregulation of miR-155 impedes neuroglial interaction, facilitating neuroinflammation, while its inhibition restores a neuroprotective phenotype of glial cells, supports endogenous stem cell survival, and delays disease progression [126–128]. In parallel with MS, ALS-related miRNAs dysregulation modulates primarily via inflammatory and glial pathways that converge on NSC dysfunction.

Genetic association studies of psychiatric disorders, such as SCZ and BD, reveal aberrant expression of miR-137, suggesting its strong link to increased risk of neuropsychiatric disorders. GWAS consistently identify miR-137 as the most robust SCZ-associated locus, located near the SNP rs1625579, which correlates with poorer memory, reduced prefrontal cortex thickness, and altered brain connectivity in SCZ patients [129,130]. In the presence of miR-137 dysregulation, significant changes in the expression of SCZ risk genes, such as MAD1L1, DPYD, and BDNF, were observed [129]. This miRNA can also control core epigenetic regulators such as EZH2 and LSD1, which are essential for neural lineage differentiation [130]. In patient-derived neuronal models, altered miR-137 expression impaired neural progenitor proliferation and synaptic maturation, potentially leading to cortical thinning and neural circuit dysfunction [131]. Furthermore, downregulation of other miRNAs, such as miR-9 and miR-219, in later SCZ brain tissues indicates that miRNA networks are broadly involved in abnormal neurodevelopment and neuroglial dysfunction [132–134].

These findings indicate that miRNAs play a central role in maintaining NSC homoeostasis, and their dysregulation contributes to disease-specific alterations in stem cell function across neurological disorders. Comparative analyses across diseases delineate two mechanistic patterns: in neurodegenerative disorders (e.g. AD, MS, ALS), dysregulated miRNAs predominantly affect apoptosis regulators, inflammatory mediators, and myelination pathways, leading to impaired NSC-mediated repair and neuronal survival. Conversely, in neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders (e.g. SCZ, BD), miRNAs such as miR-137, miR-9, and miR-219 disrupt developmental trajectories, progenitor proliferation, and synaptic maturation. This dichotomy positions miRNA dysfunction as either a degeneration-driven loss of regenerative capacity or a developmental incorrect specification of neural circuits. Such a framework advances beyond disease-specific descriptions and offers a unifying perspective with informed biomarker discovery and the design of tailored therapeutic strategies. Therefore, interventions targeting miRNA dysregulation, whether through antagonists, mimics, or network-level modulation, offer potential for restoring neurogenesis and improving neurological function.

Conclusion and prospects

miRNAs are core regulatory structures governing NSC maintenance, differentiation, and lineage commitment, interacting with transcriptional, epigenetic, and signalling pathways to ensure precise spatial and temporal regulation of neuro-gliogenesis. Although dysregulation of miRNA under pathological contexts offers potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, important challenges and opportunities remain. The functions of individual miRNAs in different NSC subtypes (e.g. adult vs. developing, different sites, and cell types) are not fully understood. A promising strategy involves integrating single-cell transcriptome profiling with spatial perturbation tools to identify the role of individual miRNAs developing within a defined biological context. Overcoming this will require high-resolution strategies that integrate developmental stage and niche-specific microenvironments.

Furthermore, our understanding of how miRNA networks are rewired in pathological conditions (e.g. neurodegenerative diseases, glioblastoma) or injury remains to be elucidated yet could provide crucial insights for discovering new therapeutic targets. Finally, while miRNA-based approaches to control NSC fate show considerable promise for regenerative medicine, significant challenges – such as optimizing delivery methods, minimizing off-target effects, and ensuring long-term efficacy – must be addressed for these strategies to reach clinical translation.

A more comprehensive understanding of miRNA dynamics in both normal development and disease will provide valuable insights into the advancement of neurobiology and regenerative medicine. In conclusion, miRNAs represent an integral component of the regulatory architecture controlling NSC dynamics and differentiation. A deeper mechanistic understanding of miRNA function in the neural environment will not only enrich insights into neurodevelopmental biology but also enhance the rational design of precision-targeted, miRNA-based therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting brain recovery and controlling disease progression. Several key questions remain. First, the extent to which divergent effects of the same miRNA can be predicted from the transcriptional and epigenetic context remains unresolved; Second, whether multiple miRNAs converge on a small number of regulatory hubs, and whether targeting these hubs is more effective than manipulating individual miRNAs, is insufficiently understood; Third, the validation of disease-specific miRNA signatures as predictive biomarkers for therapeutic response remains an unmet challenge. Addressing these questions will require integrating single-cell multi-omics with functional perturbation studies, enabling a shift from descriptive catalogues to predictive, mechanistic frameworks with translational relevance. In parallel, translational progress will depend on overcoming delivery challenges (e.g. lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors) and validating disease-specific miRNA signatures as biomarkers in early-phase clinical trials. Several miRNA mimics and antagomirs, such as MRX34 (miR-34a mimic) and RG-101 (anti-miR-122), have already reached human testing in oncology and viral diseases [135,136], highlighting that NSC-targeted miRNA therapies are conceptually feasible and may soon advance towards neurological indications.
