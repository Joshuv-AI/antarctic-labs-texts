Incidence and Predictors of Valve Pop-up During Navitor Valve Implantation
Authors: Ryosuke Higuchi, Itaru Takamisawa, Mitsunobu Kitamura, Makoto Ohno, Mamoru Nanasato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Navitor valve shows excellent valve function and high deliverability. However, valve pop-up during valve implantation remains a concern.Methods and ResultsWe defined pop-up as an upward valve dislocation ≥3 mm after implantation. Pop-up occurred in 13 (19%) of 67 patients, with 2 (3.0%) patients requiring a second valve. The depth ratio between non- and left-coronary cusp side at the 80% release and aortic valve calcification score were identified as predictors of valve pop-up.ConclusionsPop-up of the Navitor valve occurred in 19% of patients. Inconsistent valve depth and severe leaflet calcification were predictors of valve pop-up.

In transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the Navitor valve (Abbott, Santa Clara, CA, USA) offers several advantages, including excellent deliverability, a larger effective orifice area, and minimal perivalvular leakage.1 However, procedural atrioventricular block remains as a drawback of the Navitor valve. While deeper implantation may lead to conduction disturbance, shallower implantation increases the risk of valve embolization.2 Understanding valve motion during valve release is essential to ensure optimal deployment.

Methods

Between June 1, 2022 and March 31, 2025, we performed TAVI using the Navitor or Navitor Vision valve in 69 patients with severe aortic stenosis. After excluding 2 patients owing to non-analyzable imaging, 67 patients were included in the analysis. The study was approved by the institutional ethical committee of Sakakibara Heart Institute. TAVI was performed under conscious sedation guided by transthoracic echocardiography. Predilation based on the annulus short diameter was routinely performed. Dual fluoroscopic views, the double cusp view and left anterior oblique view, were used during implantation, targeting a depth of 3–5 mm at the non-coronary cusp (NCC). Control pacing was selectively used. Valve size was determined using the manufacturer’s sizing chart. The study endpoint was valve pop-up, defined as upward valve dislocation ≥3 mm after release. This threshold was based on the 3 mm depth recommended by the manufacturer. Using the INIFINIT PACS system (ver. 7.0.0.6, INFINIT Healthcare, Seoul, South Korea), we measured implantation depth at the NCC and left-coronary cusp (LCC) side, as well as the angles between the Navitor and aortic root (i.e., coaxiality) on aortography (Figure). Results

The median age of the 67 patients was 85 years, and the mean transvalvular pressure gradient was 49 (41–61) mmHg. A bicuspid aortic valve was present in 2 (3.0%) patients. The annulus perimeter measured 69.5 (66.5–74.7) mm, and the aortic angle was 53 (47–57) degrees. The aortic valve calcium (AVC) score was 1,885 (1,452–2,745) Agatston units. Moderate or severe left ventricular outflow tract calcification was found in 14 (21%) patients. The 25-mm valve was most frequently used in 28 (42%) patients. At the 80% valve release, the implantation depth was 4.1 (3.5–5.2) mm at the NCC side, and 4.5 (3.4–6.1) mm at the LCC side. After valve release, the Navitor valve shifted upward by −0.8 (−2.0 to 1.1) mm at the NCC side and downward by 1.4 (0.0–2.9) mm at the LCC side after valve release (Figure). Pop-up occurred in 13 (19%) patients, with 2 (3.0%) cases requiring a second valve due to valve embolization. Implantation depth ratio and AVC score were predictors of valve pop-up in receiver operating curve analysis (Figure).

The incidence of a second valve was 2.5% in the PORTICO NG trial, 1.5% in the OCEAN-TAVI registry,1 and 3.0% in the present study. Given that most major complications occur in fewer than 1–3% of contemporary TAVI cases, these rates are not negligible. Recapture and repositioning functions, along with optimal fluoroscopic angles and implantation methods, have been developed to improve transcatheter heart valve positioning. The spineless catheter and the elasticity of the Navitor valve may exert external forces. Following valve release and the release of this tension, the Navitor tends to rotate clockwise, resulting in upward motion at the NCC side and downward motion at the LCC side in the left anterior oblique view. The depth ratio between the NCC and LCC, along with leaflet calcification, were identified as predictors of valve pop-up. Patients without valve pop-up showed greater coaxiality at 80% valve release. In patients with severely calcified valves, optimal balloon predilation and careful attention to coaxial alignment may help reduce the risk of valve pop-up. Before valve release, ensuring consistent valve depth on both sides is crucial.

Conclusions

Valve pop-up, defined as unintentional upward motion of ≥3 mm after the valve release, was observed in 13 (19%) of 67 patients, with 2 (3.0%) requiring a second valve. Implantation depth and leaflet calcification were predictors of pop-up. Procedural modifications, including optimal predilation and precise valve positioning, may help mitigate this complication.
