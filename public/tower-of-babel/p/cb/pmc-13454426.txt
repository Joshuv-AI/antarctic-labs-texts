In-silico study of the bioactive compounds of Blighia sapida koenig revealed a novel Plasmodium falciparum dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibitor for malaria
Authors: Deborah O Akinyemi, Tope T Odunitan, Fiyinfoluwa D Ojeniyi, Adeyemi O Akinyemi, Noor Rahman, Humaira Zafar, Leonard O Ehigie, Olusegun K Afolabi, Olusola Ojurongbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to the challenge of drug-resistant malaria, this study investigated the antimalarial potential of B. sapida ethanolic leaf extract using an in silico approach. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was performed to identify compounds present in the extract. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations were conducted to assess interactions and stability between selected lead compounds and the target protein PfDHODH using AutoDock Vina and GROMACS, respectively. A total of twenty-one compounds were identified and the three most abundant were D-Fructose 3-O-methyl- (21.02%), phytol (20.77%), and hexadecanoic acid ethyl ester (13.11%). Complementary in silico docking analyses demonstrated interactions between all 21 compounds and PfDHODH. Among them, three compounds demonstrated strong binding affinities (− 8.1 to − 10.0 kcal/mol) comparative to the positive control (N-(2,2-diphenylethyl)-4-hydroxy-1,2,5-thiadiazole-3-carboxamide) with binding energy − 8.9 kcal/mol. Linoleic acid ethyl ester, octadecanoic acid ethyl ester, and 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one displayed favorable drug-likeness and ADMET profiles. Among these, 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one emerged as the most stable inhibitor (RMSD = 0.12, RMSF = 0.10, ROG = 2.02, SASA = 171.90), followed by octadecanoic acid ethyl ester (RMSD = 0.13, RMSF = 0.11, ROG = 2.02, SASA = 172.31). These results highlight the promising antimalarial potential of B. sapida and provide insight into its bioactive constituents.Keywords: Antimalaria, Drug-likeness, Blighia sapida, Molecular docking, ADMET, molecular simulationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemical biology, Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Malaria remains a major challenge to global public health despite sustained eradication efforts. The disease continues to cause substantial mortality in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in Africa, where pregnant women and children are disproportionately affected1. Although the global malaria community has achieved significant progress in combating this burden2, reported cases have risen in the post-pandemic period3. According to the World Malaria Report4, more than 249 million malaria cases were recorded worldwide, representing an increase of 16 million compared with the 233 million cases documented in 2019 before the pandemic. This trend underscores the ongoing need for more proactive and effective control strategies.

A wide range of anti-malarial drugs has been developed over the years; however, their efficacy is increasingly compromised by the emergence of multidrug resistance, particularly in Plasmodium falciparum, one of the most lethal malaria parasites5,6. Evidence of parasite resistance has even been reported against World Health Organization–recommended artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) in several African countries, including Nigeria7,6. Consequently, the development of effective treatments requires intensified investigation of anti-malarial agents with novel mechanisms of action.

Plasmodium falciparum dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (PfDHODH) is a critical enzyme in the pyrimidine biosynthesis pathway, catalyzing the oxidation of dihydroorotate to orotate, an essential step in the de novo synthesis of pyrimidine nucleotides required for DNA and RNA production in P. falciparum8. Because Plasmodium species cannot salvage pyrimidines from the host, they depend almost entirely on this pathway, making PfDHODH a highly attractive therapeutic target9. In comparison to human DHODH, which is found in the mitochondrial, PfDHODH is localized within the apicoplast, an organelle indispensable for parasite survival. This distinct localization, together with structural differences from the human enzyme, enables selective inhibition while minimizing host off-target effects9. Brequinar is among the well-characterized PfDHODH inhibitors10, and others such as tipifarnib and IMU-838 inhibit parasite growth by blocking pyrimidine synthesis thereby disrupting DNA replication, a process essential for parasite division and proliferation11. Recently, several newly identified molecules have emerged as promising lead candidates, with some advancing to clinical evaluation for malaria therapy6,12,13. Importantly, DSM265, discovered through experimental studies, has progressed to Stage 2 clinical trials9.

Blighia sapida, a woody perennial fruit tree commonly known as ackee or akee apple and locally referred to as Isin in southwestern Nigeria, belongs to the Sapindaceae family and is widely distributed in the woodlands of West African countries, including Nigeria14,15. Traditionally, the plant has been recognized for its medicinal value, with various parts used to treat conditions such as cancer, childhood fever, gonorrhea, diarrhea, backache, constipation, psychosis, hernia, stomachache, rheumatism, typhoid, and malaria15. These therapeutic effects have been attributed to its phytochemical constituents16, whose composition varies according to plant part and extraction solvent. Olayinka et al.17, reported that the aqueous leaf extract of B. sapida contains appreciable levels of flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, and tannins, consistent with other findings showing that its secondary metabolites include alkaloids, saponins, flavonoids, tannins, polyphenols, terpenoids, and glycosides16,17. Experimental evidence further demonstrates that B. sapida presents antimicrobial, antidiarrheal, anticancer, antidiabetic, anti-malarial, and antioxidant activities18–23. supporting its candidacy for further pharmacological investigation.

In silico methodologies have become indispensable tools in modern drug discovery, providing rapid and cost-effective approaches for identifying and evaluating potential therapeutic agents before experimental validation. Molecular docking, for instance, predicts interactions between small molecules and biological targets, facilitating the identification of promising drug candidates22. ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) analysis plays a crucial role in the preliminary screening and validation of bioactive compounds22,24. In comparison, molecular dynamics simulations enable detailed exploration of molecular conformations, protein folding, protein–ligand interactions, and macromolecular behavior that are often difficult to observe experimentally25.

Currently, no published study has reported an in silico evaluation of Blighia sapida bioactive compounds as potential anti-malarial agents, despite documented evidence of its anti-malarial activity23,26. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify the bioactive constituents of B. sapida using GC–MS analysis. The compounds detected were subsequently screened and assessed for conformational dynamics through molecular docking, drug-likeness prediction, ADMET profiling, and molecular dynamics simulations. This work contributes to ongoing efforts to address the growing challenge of anti-malarial drug resistance and supports the development of more effective and affordable therapeutic options.

Study methodology

Gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy analysis of Blighia sapida

The ethanol extract of Blighia sapida leaves was analyzed using an Agilent 8860 gas chromatograph coupled to a 5977B mass spectrometry detector (MSD) and equipped with an Elite-5MS capillary column (30 × 0.25 μm ID × 0.25 μm film thickness) composed of 5% diphenyl and 95% dimethylpolysiloxane. GC–MS detection was performed using an electron ionization system operating in electron impact mode at 70 eV. Helium (99.999%) was employed as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min, with an injection volume of 1 µL and a split ratio of 10:1.

The injector temperature was maintained at 300 °C, and the ion source temperature was set to 250 °C. The oven temperature program began at 110 °C with a 1-minute hold, then ramped at 15 °C/min to 310 °C, which was maintained for 2 min. Mass spectra were acquired at 70 eV with a scan interval of 0.5 s, detecting fragment ions within the mass range of 45–450 Da. A solvent delay of 0–3 min was implemented.

Ligand selection and preparation

The phytochemical compounds identified through GC–MS analysis, along with the reference compound N-(2,2-diphenylethyl)-4-hydroxy-1,2,5-thiadiazole-3-carboxamide (DZB), were retrieved from the NCBI PubChem database in SDF format. Ligand files were converted from SDF to PDB format using PyMOL tools. Ligand preparation was performed with AutoDock Vina version 1.5.7, during which Gasteiger charges and non-polar hydrogens were added, followed by the detection of rotatable bonds and the assignment of torsional degrees of freedom (torsdof). Both protein and ligand structures were subsequently saved in PDBQT format. Drug-likeness of the compounds was assessed using Lipinski’s filter27.

Protein target selection and preparation

The crystal structure of the DHODH protein complex (PDB ID: 6I55) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org) (Fig. 1). Protein preparation, including removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens, energy minimization, and identification of the binding site, was performed using Discovery Studio Visualizer v24.1.0.2329828. The grid center coordinates were defined as X = 16.589087, Y = 22.366391, and Z = 35.210261. Grid box configuration, addition of Kollman and Gasteiger charges, and assignment of AD4 atom types were carried out using AutoDock MGL Tools version 1.5.7.

Structure of the Plasmodium falciparum dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (pfDHODH) target Protein (Protein DataBank, www.rcsb.org).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.5.729 following a standard protocol30. Protein and ligand structures were converted into PDBQT format with AutoDock Tools. A grid box was defined around the active site corresponding to the co-crystallized ligand of the complex, and docking simulations were executed with an exhaustiveness parameter set to 8. AutoDock Vina generated multiple binding conformations ranked according to binding affinity (kcal/mol), and the highest-scoring pose was selected for subsequent analysis. Visualization and interpretation of ligand–protein interactions were conducted using Discovery Studio Visualizer.

Validation of the docking method

The AutoDock Vina protocol was validated by redocking the co-crystallized ligand from the PDB structure (PDB ID: 6I55). The ligand was reintroduced into the active site using the same grid box parameters and exhaustiveness settings applied to the test compounds. The heavy-atom root mean square deviation (RMSD), which quantifies the average atomic distance between two superimposed molecular structures (i.e., the top-ranked docked conformation and the crystallographic pose), was calculated using DockRMSD31.

Interaction and visualization

Docked complexes were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio Visualizer (v24.1.0.23298) to examine critical interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts. Binding energy values and interaction profiles were analyzed comparatively to assess the affinity and stability of each ligand–protein complex.

Drug-likeness and ADMET profiling of the isolated compounds

The SMILES (Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System) strings for each isolated compound were retrieved from the PubChem database for computational analysis. Drug-likeness, evaluated according to Lipinski’s Rule of Five, was predicted using the SwissADME web tool, which integrates multiple algorithms for in silico characterization of small molecules32. In addition, both the SwissADME platform and the pkCSM web server were employed to assess the ADMET properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) of the selected lead compounds.

Drug-likeness assessment

The drug-likeness of the three lead compounds was assessed using key physicochemical parameters, including molecular weight (MW), partition coefficient (LogP), number of hydrogen bond donors (HBD), number of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), and topological polar surface area (TPSA). These attributes were evaluated in accordance with Lipinski’s Rule of Five, a widely recognized standard for predicting oral bioavailability33. Additional metrics, such as bioavailability score, were also calculated to supplement the primary Lipinski criteria. This score, which ranges from 0 to 1, estimates the likelihood that a compound will demonstrate oral bioavailability in humans34.

ADMET profiling

All ADMET parameters (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) were predicted using the pkCSM platform, which applies graph-based signature models to generate reliable pharmacokinetic and toxicity estimates35. Absorption was evaluated through human intestinal absorption (HIA) and Caco-2 cell permeability, while distribution was assessed using predicted blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability. Metabolic profiling focused on potential interactions with cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoenzymes. Excretion and toxicity were examined using in silico models that predict hepatotoxicity and AMES mutagenicity. Collectively, these computational approaches enable rapid and cost-efficient screening of candidate molecules, supporting early-stage evaluation of pharmacokinetic characteristics and safety profiles in line with contemporary drug discovery practices36.

Molecular dynamics simulation (MDS)

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is a powerful computational approach for investigating the mechanistic behavior and temporal stability of biological macromolecules37. In this study, the dynamic behavior of the 6I55 receptor in complex with the lead compounds and the reference inhibitor was analyzed using the CUDA-accelerated version of GROMACS38. Protein topology was generated with the AMBER-ILDN force field39, while ligand parameters were derived using the ACPYPE tool40. The resulting protein–ligand complexes were constructed and placed within a cubic simulation box, with a 10 Å buffer between the protein surface and the box boundaries. The system was solvated with the TIP3P water model and neutralized by adding Cl⁻ counterions41.

Energy minimization was first performed to eliminate steric clashes, followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT ensembles to stabilize the system at 300 K and 1 atm42. Subsequently, each system underwent a 100 ns production MD simulation. Trajectories were visualized using VMD, while stability parameters were analyzed and plotted with XMGrace. Structural and dynamic descriptors, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), radius of gyration (ROG), and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), were evaluated throughout the simulation. Results

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometer of ethanolic leaf extract of B. sapida

GC–MS analysis of the ethanolic extract of B. sapida identified a total of twenty-one compounds (Table 1). The earliest-eluting compound detected was benzoic acid methyl ester, with a retention time of 4.724 min. Among the identified constituents, the most abundant phytochemicals were D-fructose (21.02%), 3-O-methyl (20.77%), and hexadecanoic acid, ethyl ester (13.11%). Compound identification was established based on peak area, retention time, molecular formula, structural configuration, molecular mass, and predicted fragment patterns (Fig. 3).

Molecular docking

The binding affinities of the GC–MS–identified compounds toward the DHODH protein were evaluated using AutoDock 4.2.7 (Table 1). Overall, the screened compounds demonstrated favorable binding interactions with DHODH. Bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate was excluded from further analysis because it is recognized as a plastic contaminant rather than a plant-derived metabolite43. Three lead compounds were ultimately selected based on their superior docking scores (Table 2), compliance with Lipinski’s Rule of Five parameters, and favorable ADMET profiles27. Their chemical structures are presented in Table 3. Linoleic acid ethyl ester displayed the strongest binding affinity (− 10.0 kcal/mol), followed by octadecanoic acid ethyl ester (− 8.3 kcal/mol) and 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one (− 8.1 kcal/mol) (Table 3).

Protein–ligand interaction analysis revealed that the lead compounds target the PfDHODH active site through a combination of polar and non-polar interactions. Specifically, 2-Methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one and Linoleic acid ethyl ester were anchored by a key conventional hydrogen bond with ARG 265 mirroring the binding profile of the reference inhibitor, DZB. Whereas GLY507, SER529, SER505, and SER477 formed hydrogen bonds with octadecanoic acid ethyl ester. Beyond these polar contacts, the stability of the complexes was significantly enhanced by Van der Waals interactions and hydrophobic contacts (Pi-Alkyl and Pi-Sigma) with a cluster of residues including LEU 172, ILE 263, and TYR 528 (Table 4). Furthermore, spatial superimposition (Fig. 5) of the lead compounds with the positive control (DZB) demonstrated a high degree of conformational alignment, with 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one exhibiting near-perfect conformational mimicry.

Physicochemical and drug-likeness assessment

All selected lead compounds displayed favorable physicochemical characteristics and are predicted to possess good oral bioavailability (Table 5), as indicated by their overall compliance with Lipinski’s Rule of Five (Ro5)33. Their molecular weights (≤ 325.38 Da), hydrogen bond donor counts (≤ 2), and hydrogen bond acceptor counts (≤ 3) all fell within the acceptable Ro5 limits. Although octadecanoic acid ethyl ester had a slightly elevated log P value of 6.71, it still met the Ro5 criteria because it satisfied the remaining parameters. In addition, the compounds showed relatively low topological polar surface areas (TPSA) of 17.07-103.09 Å², indicating favorable passive absorption and membrane permeability. Collectively, these attributes yielded a uniform bioavailability score of 0.55 for all four ligands, supporting their promise as orally active drug candidates.

ADMET analysis

The predicted ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) pharmacokinetic profiles of these compounds, summarized in Table 6, provide important insight into their therapeutic potential. The fatty acid ethyl esters, linoleic acid ethyl ester and octadecanoic acid ethyl ester, together with the ketone 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one, demonstrated favorable absorption characteristics, including high gastrointestinal and Caco-2 permeability, as well as predicted blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration, suggesting strong systemic bioavailability. However, the two fatty acid esters were associated with several risk indicators, such as predicted P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate status, inhibition of CYP1A2 and CYP2C19 isoenzymes, and potential hERG-II inhibition.

Molecular dynamics simulation of the complexes

Comparison of compound-bound states: stability and flexibility profiles of receptor

The static nature of molecular docking limits its ability to reflect dynamic physiological environments. To overcome this limitation, a cumulative 400 ns MD simulation was performed for three selected compounds and one reference compound bound within the receptor’s active site. System stability and time-dependent behavior were subsequently evaluated using standard analytical metrics. The RMSD of backbone atoms (N, Cα, C, and O) from their initial conformations was used to quantify overall structural deviation. Similarly, RMSF analysis was conducted to assess residue-level flexibility and identify regions of the binding pocket undergoing conformational changes.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, all ligand–receptor complexes showed varying deviation profiles, with RMSD values consistently remaining below 0.14 nm throughout the simulation. Compound C1 (2-Methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2- en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one) demonstrated stronger stabilizing effects on the receptor than the reference compound, with an average RMSD of 0.12 ± 0.01 nm compared with 0.13 ± 0.01 nm for the reference complex. Compound C3 (Octadecanoic acid, ethyl ester) showed a stabilizing profile comparable to the reference, maintaining an average RMSD of 0.13 ± 0.01 nm. In comparison, C2 (Linoleic acid ethyl ester) showed slightly greater structural deviation, with an average RMSD of 0.14 ± 0.01 nm.

To assess residue-level fluctuations and pinpoint sources of side-chain perturbations, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) profiles were calculated, as presented in Fig. 7. Receptor stability upon ligand binding was reflected in the RMSF values: C1 demonstrated lower fluctuations than the reference compound, whereas C2 and C3 showed patterns comparable to the reference. The mean RMSF values for the receptor complexes with C1, C2, C3, and the reference compound were 0.10 ± 0.05, 0.11 ± 0.05, 0.11 ± 0.05, and 0.11 ± 0.05 nm, respectively. Detailed examination indicated similar RMSF distributions across all systems, particularly within the P-loop and activation loop regions.

Analysis of folding dynamics and molecular compactness via radius of gyration

Protein folding characteristics and variations in structural compactness were evaluated by calculating the radius of gyration (Rg), which tracks the distribution of constituent atoms relative to the molecular center over the course of the simulation. Lower Rg values indicate tighter packing, commonly associated with α-helical and β-sheet structures, whereas flexible loop regions typically display higher Rg values. As illustrated in Fig. 8, ligand binding influenced receptor compactness. However, the Rg profiles of all simulated systems remained stable and closely paralleled that of the reference complex during the 100 ns simulation. Compounds C1, C2, C3, and the reference ligand displayed mean Rg values of 2.02 ± 0.00, 2.03 ± 0.00, 2.02 ± 0.00, and 2.02 ± 0.00, respectively (Table 7), indicating high structural stability and consistency across the complexes.

SASA analysis

Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) analysis was performed to assess how ligand binding influenced the receptor’s overall structural architecture during the 100 ns MD simulation. Across all systems, SASA profiles remained stable within a range of 165–185 nm2 (Fig. 9), indicating that none of the ligands induced substantial structural expansion or collapse. The SASA values of the C1–protein complex closely matched those of the reference inhibitor, suggesting minimal perturbation of the receptor surface. The C2–protein complex demonstrated slightly greater fluctuations after 60 ns, consistent with localized conformational adjustments that did not affect global stability. In comparison, the C3–protein complex maintained consistent surface exposure throughout the simulation and displayed behavior comparable to that of the reference ligand–protein complex.

Post-MD binding and hydrogen bond persistence analysis

Post-MD interaction evaluation was carried out to assess the persistence of docking-predicted interactions under dynamic conditions, as shown in Fig. 10. In the 2-Methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl) hept-2-en-4-one complex, Arg265 preserved a highly stable hydrogen bond with a bond distance of 1.98 Å and an occupancy of approximately 87%, showing strong and persistent electrostatic stabilization throughout the simulation. Similarly, in the DZB complex, Arg265 and His185 displayed hydrogen-bond occupancies of 85% and 29%, respectively, while an additional interaction with Gly181 was observed and maintained throughout the simulation, with a bond distance of 2.78 Å and an occupancy of 55%, as shown in Fig. 10. Furthermore, in the linoleic acid ethyl ester and octadecanoic acid ethyl ester complexes, no hydrogen bonds were observed in the final frame of the MD simulation; however, both compounds established stable contacts through hydrophobic interactions. The hydrogen-bond occupancy of Arg265 in the linoleic acid ethyl ester complex was observed to be up to 55%. Conversely, the octadecanoic acid ethyl ester complex exhibits hydrogen-bond occupancies of 21%, 33%, 18%, and 43% with Gly507, Ser529, Ser505, and Ser477, respectively, as shown in Fig. 11.

Discussion

The relative area percentages of the 21 compounds detected in the Blighia sapida ethanol extract varied considerably, indicating differences in their abundance within the sample. D-fructose, 3-O-methyl- (21.02%) and phytol (20.77%) were the most abundant constituents, whereas benzoic acid methyl ester was present at the lowest level (0.75%). The identified compounds represented diverse chemical classes, including fatty acids, esters, alcohols, and terpenoids, reflecting a chemically heterogeneous profile. Similar findings were reported by Edewor and Kazeem45 in their analysis of the methanolic leaf extract of B. sapida, where related constituents such as phytol, hexadecenoic acid methyl ester, and n-hexadecanoic acid were detected, with phytol among the predominant components. Phytol is a diterpene alcohol known for antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities46. At the same time, D-fructose derivatives are associated with roles in energy metabolism and possible prebiotic effects47, supporting the pharmacological relevance of these major constituents.

Docking analysis demonstrated significant variation in ligand binding affinities, suggesting differential potential to inhibit parasite replication9. Linoleic acid ethyl ester showed the strongest interaction with the target protein, yielding the lowest docking score (− 10.0 kcal/mol), followed by octadecanoic acid ethyl ester (− 8.3 kcal/mol) and 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one (− 8.1 kcal/mol). These results indicate favorable binding orientations supported by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Hydrogen bonds enhance specificity and stabilize ligand positioning within the active site, whereas hydrophobic contacts anchor non-polar ligand regions within the enzyme pocket. ARG265, located in a flexible region, may facilitate ligand-induced conformational adaptation48, thereby stabilizing binding and improving specificity. Hydrophobic residues such as ILE263, ALA224, and CYS184 contribute to structural integrity within the DHODH binding site49, reinforcing the likelihood of effective ligand engagement. Importantly, residues including ARG265, TYR528, ILE263, VAL532, LEU172, and GLY181 were shared between the tested ligands and the co-crystallized ligand, indicating overlap in critical binding hotspots. Targeting such conserved residues may enhance efficacy while reducing the risk of resistance50. The relatively strong affinities observed for the lead compounds (− 8.1 to − 10.0 kcal/mol) compared with the co-crystallized ligand (− 8.9 kcal/mol) can be attributed to their strategic spatial arrangement within the PfDHODH catalytic pocket. This may imply that these natural products could match or surpass the reference ligand in DHODH inhibition, underscoring their promise as anti-malarial candidates.

Drug-likeness evaluation based on Lipinski’s Rule of Five (Ro5) confirmed the suitability of the selected phytochemicals as orally active drug candidates33. All tested ligands satisfied Ro5 thresholds, MW ≤ 500 Da, HBD ≤ 5, and HBA ≤ 10, except octadecanoic acid ethyl ester, which showed a slightly elevated logP value of 6.71. Such a single deviation is often acceptable for bioactive molecules51. Moreover, all ligands showed low topological polar surface area (TPSA < 140 Å²) and molecular weights well below 500 Da, indicating favorable membrane permeability and bioavailability potential, reflected in uniform bioavailability scores of 0.55. In particular, 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one displayed a low TPSA (17.07 Å²) and moderate logP (3.7), parameters strongly associated with efficient oral absorption and possible transdermal delivery, supporting its classification as a promising drug-like candidate52.

ADMET profiling revealed meaningful distinctions among the lead compounds despite their uniformly favorable gastrointestinal absorption and Caco-2 permeability, consistent with established in silico pharmacokinetic screening models32,35. The terpene-like compound 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one was uniquely predicted to be a non-substrate of P-gp and capable of penetrating the blood–brain barrier, features that are advantageous for systemic exposure and potential CNS distribution53. In comparison, the fatty-acid esters and the reference ligand showed predicted P-gp transport susceptibility and metabolic liabilities, including CYP1A2/CYP2C19 inhibition or CYP3A4 substrate activity. Importantly, the thiadiazole reference compound showed multiple toxicity alerts, including predicted AMES mutagenicity, hepatotoxicity, and elevated hERG inhibition risk, whereas the lead phytochemicals were predicted to be non-mutagenic and non-hepatotoxic54. These findings indicate that the terpene-like and lipid-derived candidates possess comparatively favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles, whereas the reference analogue may require structural optimization or experimental toxicity validation.

The molecular dynamics results highlight the importance of dynamic simulation in complementing static docking predictions, as time-resolved trajectories capture conformational fluctuations and solvent effects that influence binding stability but are not observable through docking alone55,56. RMSD analysis confirmed robust stability across all complexes, with values consistently below 0.14 nm and narrow distributions throughout the 100 ns trajectories (Fig. 1)57. Such minimal deviation indicates that none of the ligands induced destabilizing conformational changes in the receptor backbone, thereby preserving active site integrity54. Importantly, C1 displayed a lower average RMSD (0.12 ± 0.01 nm) than the reference complex (0.13 ± 0.01 nm), suggesting slightly stronger structural stabilization. These findings were supported by radius of gyration analysis, which showed highly consistent mean values (2.02–2.03 nm) with negligible variance (Fig. 3), confirming preservation of a compact global fold and excluding large-scale unfolding or expansion upon ligand binding58.

Residue-level flexibility assessed by RMSF further demonstrated overall rigidity, with low mean values (0.10–0.11 nm) across all systems59. Consistent with RMSD trends, C1 produced the lowest average RMSF (0.10 ± 0.05 nm), indicating superior restriction of side-chain mobility relative to the reference and other analogues. Importantly, regions typically associated with high mobility, such as the P-loop and activation loop, remained comparably stable across complexes, suggesting that ligand binding does not disrupt essential catalytic or regulatory motions.

SASA analysis confirmed that structural stability was maintained without compromising surface integrity. Average SASA values remained tightly constrained (171–176 nm2), indicating that ligand binding did not induce major expansion or exposure of buried hydrophobic residues. Complexes containing C1 and C3 demonstrated SASA profiles most similar to the reference inhibitor, with C1 showing the lowest mean value (171.90 ± 2.74 nm2). This reduced surface exposure, combined with the lowest RMSD and RMSF values, implies that C1 promotes a compact and energetically favorable binding configuration58,60. Although C2 displayed minor fluctuations after 60 ns, its overall SASA remained within the stable range, suggesting only localized surface rearrangements without compromising complex integrity.

The post-MD interaction and hydrogen bond occupancy analyses further confirm the structural stability observed in the global MD simulation. The high persistence of key interactions, in particular the main anchoring role of Arg265 in selected complexes, confirms that the binding mode predicted through docking remains dynamically stable under explicit solvent conditions. Even in systems where hydrogen bonds were not retained in the final simulation frame, acceptable occupancy values and sustained hydrophobic contacts indicate constructive stabilization rather than complete interaction loss.

Collectively, the MD simulation data from RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA analyses consistently demonstrate that all three novel compounds (C1, C2, and C3) form stable, persistent complexes with the receptor, corroborating the initial docking predictions. Among them, C1 emerged as the most promising lead, consistently demonstrating the lowest RMSD and RMSF values and thus the strongest stabilizing influence on receptor backbone and side-chain dynamics. These computational results strongly support the predicted binding modes and structural stability, justifying progression of these compounds to experimental validation to confirm their pharmacological potential61.

Conclusion

In conclusion, GC–MS analysis conducted in this study revealed approximately eighteen previously unreported compounds compared with earlier investigations. Molecular docking results indicated that linoleic acid ethyl ester, octadecanoic acid ethyl ester, and 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one present in the plant may inhibit DHODH activity, an enzyme essential for parasite metabolism and survival. By interfering with this critical pathway, these phytochemicals demonstrate potential as natural anti-malarial agents. Their predicted pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles further support this potential, showing non-hepatotoxicity, efficient clearance, favorable gastrointestinal absorption, and adequate blood–brain barrier permeability, all of which indicate suitability as therapeutic candidates. Among the evaluated compounds, molecular dynamics simulation identified 2-methyl-6-(4-methylenecyclohex-2-en-1-yl)hept-2-en-4-one as the most promising lead, owing to its strong hydrogen-bonding interactions and consistently stable structural parameters. With continued optimization and validation, this compound could represent a cost-effective and sustainable alternative to synthetic anti-malarial drugs, particularly in regions with high disease burden. Overall, these findings provide a foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo investigations to confirm the pharmacological efficacy of these phytochemicals.
