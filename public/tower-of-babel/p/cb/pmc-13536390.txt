Rigorous validation of machine learning models for orthodontic extraction prediction using nested cross-validation and smotenc-integrated pipelines
Authors: Anh Thi-Ngoc Do, Hung Trong Hoang, Trang Thi-Thuy Ho, Hieu Ngoc Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeMachine learning (ML) in orthodontics often suffers from optimistic bias due to data leakage and improper handling of class imbalance. This study implemented and evaluated a production-grade validation framework - nested cross-validation (CV) - for three distinct algorithm classes (logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting) using a consensus-labeled orthodontic dataset.Materials and methodsA retrospective dataset of 500 patients (334 non-extraction/166 extraction) was curated with 41 processed features. Two stratified split protocols (70/30 and 80/20) were evaluated to assess model robustness. To ensure methodological rigor, a 5-fold outer/3-fold inner nested cross-validation was implemented. Crucially, SMOTENC (synthetic minority over-sampling technique for nominal and continuous) was integrated inside the training pipeline to prevent data leakage.ResultsAll models achieved high discrimination (ROC-AUC >0.87). XGBoost achieved the highest discrimination (ROC-AUC: 0.9177) and demonstrated the best overall balance for clinical decision support (balanced accuracy: 0.8566, F1-score: 0.8125). Logistic regression showed the highest internal stability (CV mean balanced accuracy: 0.8521 ± 0.0281) despite lower performance on the hold-out test set.ConclusionThe use of nested CV combined with pipeline-integrated SMOTENC provides a conservative and reliable estimate of model performance. While gradient boosting (XGBoost) offers superior predictive power, the framework highlights the trade-off between stability (linear models) and performance (boosting) in dentistry.Keywords: Orthodontic extraction prediction, Machine learning, Nested crossvalidation, Model validation, Clinical decision support

Introduction

Premolar extraction remains one of the most critical decisions in orthodontic treatment planning, directly affecting occlusal stability, facial esthetics, and long-term outcomes.1 Globally, approximately 20–30% of orthodontic patients undergo extractions, most commonly to relieve crowding or correct skeletal discrepancies.2,3 While appropriate extraction facilitates optimal arch coordination, inappropriate decisions may lead to adverse outcomes, including flattened facial appearance or post-treatment relapse due to occlusal instability.4 Despite its clinical importance, extraction decision-making relies heavily on subjective judgment, representing a complex interplay of theoretical knowledge and personal preference.5,6 Consequently, substantial inter-clinician variability exists, with agreement rates sometimes falling below 60%.7,8 This inconsistency stems largely from differences in how clinicians weigh cephalometric and soft tissue indicators, as well as variations in experience, posing significant challenges for less experienced practitioners.9,10

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have emerged as promising tools for enhancing objectivity by identifying subtle, nonlinear relationships in diagnostic data.11–17 The application of AI represents a paradigm shift, streamlining processes for more personalized patient care.18–20 Several studies employing deep learning architectures have reported prediction accuracies exceeding 90%.21 Furthermore, research continues to validate these algorithms; for instance, a 2024 study by Huang et al. achieved 86% accuracy using a decision tree model, identifying crowding as a critical factor.22,23 These findings reinforce the goal of creating robust clinical decision support systems to assist orthodontists with data-driven insights.24,25

However, a substantial challenge remains in the transition from experimental models to reliable clinical decision support systems (CDSS). Most existing models rely primarily on image-based data, which demand extensive computational resources and often lack interpretability.26–28 More critically, a technical gap exists regarding the methodological rigor of studies utilizing structured clinical data. Current literature often reports high accuracies based on simple train-test splits. Critics have noted that this approach is prone to sampling bias and “optimistic performance estimates” often resulting from what is essentially “lucky” partitioning rather than true model generalization.27–29 Furthermore, orthodontic datasets are inherently imbalanced and contain mixed data types (categorical and continuous). There is a notable lack of rigorous handling of these features during resampling; standard techniques often result in data leakage or corrupt categorical variables, undermining the clinical validity of the model.

Unlike low-stakes domains where a false positive is merely an annoyance, the cost of error in orthodontics is profound. A false-positive for extraction entails the removal of four healthy premolars from a patient who did not require it - a decision that is physically irreversible without complex orthognathic surgery. Such errors can lead to a flattened facial profile (dished-in face) and airway constriction. Consequently, a model that achieves high accuracy simply by defaulting to the non-extraction majority is clinically dangerous.

Furthermore, while general medicine benefits from big-data, orthodontics often operates in a small-data environment. With datasets typically numbering in the hundreds rather than millions, standard split validation is highly prone to overfitting. A single 80/20 partition might accidently segregate “easy” cases into the test set, yielding optimistic performance estimates that collapse when applied to the variability of real-world practice. Therefore, rigorous technical validation is not merely a statistical exercise but an ethical requirement.

To address these gaps, this study comparatively evaluates three supervised ML algorithms - logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) - using a rigorous nested cross-validation framework and a synthetic minority over-sampling technique for nominal and continuous (SMOTENC) - integrated pipeline on a Vietnamese cohort. Specifically, this study aims to evaluate whether a nested cross-validation framework provided a more reliable performance estimation compared to final test set results and whether it effectively mitigated the overfitting typically observed in single-split studies. Furthermore, the research investigated whether a specific algorithm could achieve an optimal trade-off between precision, to avoid unnecessary extractions, and recall, to identify true extraction cases, when applied to an imbalanced dataset (see Table 1).

Materials and methods

Study design and data construction

This retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from 500 consecutive orthodontic patients aged 16–40 years who received comprehensive orthodontic treatment at a dental center between 2018 and 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant institutional review board prior to data collection. The final dataset comprised 500 samples, with a class distribution of 66.8% (n = 334) non-extraction and 33.2% (n = 166) extraction cases. Pre-treatment records included extraoral and intraoral photographs captured with a DSLR camera and ring flash under standardized conditions. Dental models were digitized to STL format using the TRIOS color pod system (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark), and lateral cephalometric radiographs were stored in DICOM format. To establish a reliable ground truth, two board-certified orthodontists (>15 years of experience) independently reviewed all records. In instances of disagreement between the three primary reviewers, representing clinically ambiguous or borderline cases, a formal consultation among three senior dental specialists was conducted. These cases were only assigned a final label once a consensus was reached by the panel based on the Steiner stick visualized treatment objective (VTO).3 This multi-tiered approach ensured that the dataset remained representative of real-world clinical complexity by including difficult cases rather than excluding them. The final feature set consisted of 41 processed variables, including 21 categorical features (e.g., gender, angle classification, chief complaint) and 20 continuous cephalometric variables. Prior to modeling, a multicollinearity screening was conducted using Pearson correlation and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. Nine features demonstrating high inter-correlation (r > 0.90 or VIF >10) were excluded, yielding a final curated set of 41 features used in all subsequent analyses. To quantify measurement consistency, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated for all continuous cephalometric variables using a two-way mixed-effects model (absolute agreement). All retained measurements achieved ICC ≥0.85, indicating excellent inter-rater agreement. Where the two raters differed by more than one standard deviation, the arithmetic mean of their values was adopted as the final data point.

Technical pipeline and data partitioning

To ensure methodological rigor and to assess the model’s sensitivity to training data volume, two separate partitioning protocols were evaluated: a 70/30 split (training n = 350/test n = 150) and an 80/20 split (training n = 400/test n = 100).

Crucially, to address data leakage and class imbalance, the preprocessing steps were encapsulated within a nested cross-validation framework. Model testing and evaluation

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test sets corresponding to the chosen split protocols: n=150 for the 70/30 split and n=100 for the 80/20 split (see Table 4). The primary metric selected was balanced accuracy [(sensitivity + specificity)/2], chosen to penalize bias toward the majority class in this imbalanced dataset. Secondary metrics included the receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC), weighted F1-score, and confusion matrix analysis to assess class-specific errors.30 Robustness was confirmed by reporting the mean and standard deviation of metrics across the outer CV folds.

Results

With the 70/30 rate of train-test, the rigorous nested cross-validation framework revealed distinct stability profiles among the algorithms. Logistic regression (LR) demonstrated the highest stability, achieving a mean balanced accuracy of 0.8368 ± 0.0351 across the outer folds. This variance profile is visualized in Fig. 2, which highlights the lower variance of logistic regression compared to the tree-based models across the validation folds. In contrast, while the boosting architectures showed strong predictive potential, they exhibited higher variance; XGBoost achieved mean balanced accuracy of 0.8217, respectively, but with higher standard deviations (0.0544). This variance indicates that gradient boosting model is more sensitive to data fluctuations within the smaller training folds (~280 samples) compared to the linear stability of LR, highlighting a trade-off between model complexity and stability when data is limited. For the 80/20 rate of train-test, the rigorous nested crossvalidation framework revealed distinct stability profiles. Logistic regression (LR) demonstrated the highest stability and internal performance, achieving a mean balanced accuracy of 0.8521 ± 0.0281 across the outer folds. In contrast, XGBoost achieved a mean balanced accuracy of 0.8273 ± 0.0318. While the boosting model was slightly less stable across folds than LR, it maintained competitive performance.

Generalization performance (test set results)

For the 70/30 rate of train-test, upon evaluation on the independent test set (n = 150), XGBoost emerged as the overall performance leader, achieving the highest balanced accuracy (0.8850) and weighted F1-score (0.8431). A comprehensive summary of the performance metrics, including balanced accuracy, ROC-AUC, and F1-scores for both split protocols, is presented in Table 4. Logistic regression (LR) showed the lowest test results, recording the lowest ROC-AUC of 0.9092 and even the F1-score of 0.7917, indicating excellent separation between extraction and non-extraction classes. Random forest (RF) exhibited the lowest generalization performance with a balanced accuracy of 0.8250. Notably, XGBoost’s performance on the full test set surpassed its CV estimates (0.8850 vs 0.8217). This observation suggests that the model benefits significantly from the complete training sample size (n = 350) compared to the reduced folds in the CV loop, validating the necessity of sufficient data volume for optimizing nonlinear decision boundaries.

For the 80/20 rate of train-test, upon evaluation on the independent test set (n = 100), XGBoost emerged as the distinct performance leader, achieving the highest balanced accuracy (0.8566) and weighted F1-score (0.8125). Interestingly, logistic regression dropped to a balanced accuracy of 0.7890 on the test set, significantly lower than its CV estimate (0.8521). This discrepancy reinforces the importance of nested CV; a single 20% split might contain difficult edge cases that linear models fail to capture, whereas the CV score provides a more realistic expectation of long-term utility (see Fig. 2).

Discussion

The necessity of nested cross-validation in dentistry: a primary contribution of this study is the implementation of a rigorous nested cross-validation framework, addressing a critical methodological gap in dental AI research. In standard machine learning literature, hyperparameters are frequently optimized on the same dataset used for evaluation, or worse, tuned based on test set performance - a methodological flaw known as data leakage that leads to optimistic-bias and inflated accuracy reports.29,30,34 By separating the optimization process (inner loop) from the performance evaluation (outer loop), our framework ensured that the model’s hyperparameters were selected without ever seeing the validation data. This rigorous separation provides a conservative, yet highly reliable estimate of how the models will generalize to unseen clinical patients, establishing a higher standard for technical validation in orthodontics.25 This approach is particularly critical given the dataset size common in dental research (n = 500). Nested cross-validation serves as the only reliable method to simulate model behavior across variations of the patient population, effectively providing clinical error bars. For a clinical decision support system (CDSS) to be viable, high accuracy is insufficient if the variance is high. By reporting the standard deviation (e.g., logistic regression ± 0.0281), we differentiate between a model that is lucky once and a model that is reliable always.

SMOTENC vs. standard SMOTE: preserving clinical integrity. Dealing with class imbalance in orthodontic datasets presents a unique challenge due to the mix of continuous cephalometric variables (e.g., SNA, FMA) and categorical clinical features (e.g., gender, angle classification). Orthodontic decisions represent a complex interplay of geometry (continuous cephalometrics) and categorical diagnosis (malocclusion class). Standard over-sampling techniques utilizing Euclidean distance risk generating mathematical noise - synthetic patients with biologically impossible traits, such as a “class 1.5 malocclusion” or “0.5 gender”. The implementation of SMOTENC was strictly required to respect these biological constraints. By forcing categorical consistency while interpolating continuous metrics, the pipeline ensures the model learns from medically plausible synthetic patients rather than statistical artifacts, thereby preserving the biological validity of the decision boundary. By utilizing SMOTENC, this study avoided such artifacts. SMOTENC preserves the clinical integrity of categorical variables like chief complaint and angle classification by using value-voting, while interpolating continuous variables. This ensured that the synthetic minority samples generated to balance the training data remained biologically and clinically plausible, thereby stabilizing the learning process without introducing noise.

Clinical recommendation: stability vs. accuracy

The present findings underscore a clinically meaningful trade-off between model interpretability and predictive accuracy, which should inform algorithm selection according to specific clinical objectives. Logistic regression (LR) demonstrated the highest stability across cross-validation folds, suggesting consistent performance and reduced variance, thereby enhancing reliability in routine clinical decision-making. Importantly, its linear structure allows direct interpretation of coefficients as odds ratios, enabling clinicians to quantify the magnitude and direction of associations between predictors and treatment outcomes. This level of transparency aligns closely with the principles of evidence-based dentistry and strengthens clinical trust in model deployment, particularly in contexts where explainability and reproducibility are prioritized over marginal gains in predictive precision.11,27

However, for the primary task of prediction, XGBoost is the superior choice. These gradient-boosting models achieved the highest weighted F1-scores and ROC-AUC values, outperforming LR by capturing the complex, non-linear interactions between soft-tissue esthetics and skeletal patterns that linear models miss.11,27 While the “black box” nature of boosting algorithms is often cited as a limitation compared to the transparency of LR,35 the observed 4–5% gain in balanced accuracy (0.8850 vs. 0.84) justifies this trade-off. Consequently, we recommend a hybrid approach: utilizing XGBoost as the core predictive engine for high-accuracy decision support, potentially augmented by explainable AI techniques in future iterations to bridge the interpretability gap.24,36,37

Clinicians bear the ultimate legal and ethical liability for treatment plans, creating a natural resistance to “AI black box” where the logic cannot be verified.38 This study quantifies the trade-off required to bridge this trust gap. Our results from the 80/20 split indicate that logistic regression offers superior internal stability (lowest CV standard deviation: ±0.0281), making it a safer, transparent choice for explaining risk factors. However, the XGBoost model demonstrated a distinct performance advantage on the independent test set (balanced accuracy 0.8566 vs. 0.7890). This suggests that while linear models are safer, they fail to capture the non-linear complexity of borderline cases. Therefore, the 5–6% gain in accuracy justifies the use of the “black box” XGBoost architecture, provided it is deployed within a rigorous validation framework that safeguards against the irreversible cost of false-positive extraction errors and addresses algorithmic fairness.39 Furthermore, while this study utilized the standard classification threshold of 0.5, clinical deployment of such models requires careful calibration. For irreversible procedures like extraction, the decision threshold could be adjusted to prioritize precision (reducing false positives) to ensure that healthy teeth are not removed unnecessarily. Future research should explore the use of precision–recall curves to determine the optimal clinical operating point for these models.

A significant strength of this study, addressing a common pitfall in dental AI research, is the explicit inclusion of clinically ambiguous or borderline cases. While many studies achieve high accuracy by excluding cases where clinicians disagree, such an approach creates an easy dataset that does not reflect true clinical practice. In this study, all cases of initial inter-clinician disagreement were resolved through a specialized three-doctor consultation. By training and testing the models on these hard cases, our results (e.g., XGBoost’s 0.8566 balanced accuracy) provide a more honest and reliable estimation of how the AI will perform in a real-world orthodontic clinic, where decisions are rarely clear-cut.
