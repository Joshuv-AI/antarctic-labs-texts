Prevalence of defensive medicine behaviors, associated risk factors and its mitigation strategies among physicians in Gaza Strip, palestine: a mixed method study
Authors: Emad Radwan, Ahmed Hassan Albelbeisi, Ashraf Al-Jedi, Edris Kakemam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDefensive Medicine (DM) is defined as all medical services that physicians provide without real benefit to patients, and the real reasons are to protect themselves from legal issues. Little is known about this issue in the Gaza Strip, from the perspective of physicians. This study aims to assess the prevalence of DM behaviors, associated risk factors, and the mitigation strategies among physicians in the Gaza Strip, Palestine.MethodsThis study utilized an explanatory sequential mixed methods design including both quantitative and qualitative designs from April 2023 to July 2023 in eight governmental hospitals in the Gaza Strip. In the first phase, quantitative data was collected using a self-reported questionnaire through a cross-sectional survey among 200 physicians who were recruited using a simple random sampling method. In the second stage, qualitative data were gathered using a semi-structured interview with ten key informants who were selected via a purposive sampling method to identify mitigation strategies for DM prevalence. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics via SPSS-22. The content analysis method was used to analyze interview data.ResultsThe prevalence of DM-related behaviors ranged from 30.5% to 75.5%. The highest rate of DM performed behaviors were unnecessary consultations (75.5%), issuing excessive medical instructions (65.5%), referring patients to the emergency room (63.5%), unnecessary imaging (61.5%), and unnecessary laboratory investigations (60.5%). Risk factors in the logistic regression analysis for DM-related behaviors were; gender, work department, qualification, previous lawsuits, type of working shift, and work hours (p < 0.05). The main strategies to prevent or reduce DM are Designed continuous training and teaching programs, the relationship between doctor and patient, reforming the liability and complaints system, Health system reform.ConclusionsThe study displayed the proportion of physicians who frequently performed DM-related behaviors and the socio-demographic variables of the physicians that positively affect the frequently performing of the DM-related behaviors. In addition, the study identified four categories of possible solutions to reduce the practice of DM which include designing continuous training and teaching programs, enhancing the trust relationship between physician and patient, reforming the liability and complaints system, and health system reform.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01313-2.

Background

The term Defensive Medicine (DM) first originated in the United States between 1974 and 1978. Essentially, the concept of defensive medicine is a physician’s deviation from normal behavior or practice intended to reduce or prevent the risk of legal litigation from patients or their families [1, 2]. In other words, they are all medical services that doctors provide to patients without real benefit to the condition of these patients, and the real reasons are to protect themselves from the legal issues that may arise behind these services [2–4].

DM has been behaviorally divided into two parts, the first behavior is positive, and the second behavior is negative or avoidance [2]. The first behavior is carried out by doctors to reduce liability for malpractice, such as dispensing unnecessary medications or ordering unnecessary diagnostic tests, as well as by unnecessarily referring patients to other specialists. The second behavior is negative or avoidance, which doctors only do to protect themselves from any sources of risk associated with the profession, even if they are beneficial to the patient, such as avoiding giving high-risk medications or ordering a high-cost test [2, 5].

The concept of DM receives great attention among various countries of the world and they worked to reduce it because of its harm to the medical service and the public budget. The consequences of DM such as drug overuse and low-quality care are emerging as an important concern in modern health policy and practice [5]. Although the cost of DM is unclear, a previous study showed that most physicians believe that DM increases the cost of health care services [6, 7].

Previous studies found that DM behaviors were highest rated among doctors, especially specialists in critical departments, including general surgery, emergency rooms, and departments where patients are referred for overnight stays and are monitored continuously [8–11]. Also, the studies have shown that most doctors believe that DM a heavy burden on the health sector in terms of the cost of medical care services, as it increases the cost, and this affects the health sector budget significantly. The cost of DM ranges from 8.0 to 20.0% of total health care costs [6, 7].

The occurrence of DM varies from one country to another and from one medical specialty to another, high rates were estimated at 99.8% in Iran [12], 98% in Japan [13], 88% in the United States [14], 71.8% in Sudan [15], 72% in Turkey [16], 60% in Italy [9], and between 52% and 62% in Israel [11, 17]. Many studies on DM have been conducted in high-income countries [6, 9, 13, 18] while little information is known about this topic in low- and middle-income countries. In Palestine, as far as we know through research, none of the researchers looked at this phenomenon, despite its existence and its high cost to the Ministry of Health (MoH) [19]. It is important to know that studying such behaviors directs decision-makers to pay attention to reducing defensive medicine behavior, such as the formula of patient consent to medical procedures, and making laws that reduce the use of this behavior while working on legal reforms in a way that takes into account the interests of the patient and the protection of the doctor as well [5]. This study aims to assess the prevalence of DM behaviors, associated risk factors and the mitigation strategies among physicians in the Gaza Strip, Palestine.

Methods

Study design

The design of this study was an explanatory sequential mixed methods approach including two phases of quantitative and qualitative. In this design, quantitative and qualitative data were collected sequentially. Firstly, quantitative data were collected and analyzed. Then, qualitative data were collected and analyzed. Qualitative data was utilized to complement the quantitative data, assuring complementarity and giving physicians a complete and in-depth understanding of the mitigation strategies to prevent or reduce DM behaviors among physicians [20].

First phase: quantitative data

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among physicians working in eight governmental hospitals at the MoH in the five Gaza Strip Governorates, Palestine from April 2023 to Jun 2023.

Sample and sampling

The target population included 400 physicians who worked in eight governmental hospitals at MoH in the five Gaza Strip Governorates. Inclusion criteria included all physicians who are working in the hospital departments at MoH in the five Gaza Strip Governorates and physicians who had work experience of more than six months. Internship physicians and physicians who were on leave were excluded from the study. We determined the appropriate sample size for the study according to the Cochran formula [21], It was determined that a sample size of 184 physicians would be needed, with a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level. To allow for a non-response rate of 20%, we increased the sample size to 221. We assigned each hospital a quota based on the number of physicians and recruited physicians from each ward using a simple random sampling technique.

Instrument

We used a self-administered survey to collect data, which included demographic questions and 18 DM behaviors.

The first part includes the socio-demographic information of physicians such as gender, marital status, age, qualification, work department, clinical experience, work hours per week, work type, and previous medical lawsuits.

The second part of the tool consists of 18 items (13 positive DM behaviors and 5 negative DM behaviors). A five-point Likert scale items were used to examine the extent of agreement with statements (0 = ‘never,’ 1 = ‘rarely,’ 2 = ‘sometimes,’ 3 = ‘often, 4 = ‘always’). DM-related behaviors were extracted after conducting an extensive literature review and consultation of experts [2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 22, 23].

To ensure the validity, the questionnaire was sent to 10 various experts including professional experts, researchers, academics, and statisticians. All comments about questions, arrangement, face, and content validity were taken into consideration. Face validity was ensured and content validity to examine to which extent the instruments include all major elements relevant to the construct being measured. All questions that reached less than 75% consensus were removed. In addition, before the main study, a pilot study was conducted by gathering 30 questionnaires from 30 eligible physicians. The Cronbach’s Alpha for the study tool was 0.84, indicating high reliability and consistency.

Data collection

The data was collected from April 2023 to Jun 2023. Two researchers conducted hospital visits on various weekdays during morning, evening, and night shifts to gather the necessary data. Questionnaires were distributed, completed, and collected by the researchers.

Data analysis

The quantitative data were analyzed using the SPSS version 22.0. All data were summarized and described using descriptive statistics such as number, and percentage. The DM-related behaviors were dichotomized according to physicians’ responses (Always, Often, and Sometimes) are considered to have been experienced, and the responses (Rarely and Never) are considered never experienced [8]. Factors associated with DM behaviors were identified using logistic regression analysis. 0.05 was considered as the decision level for significance.

Second phase: qualitative data

An exploratory descriptive design was used in the qualitative phase of the study.

Sample and sampling method

A purposive sampling method was utilized to recruit ten key informants (eight senior physicians and two heads of the Legal affairs and complaints department in MoH).

Data collection

In-depth interviews were conducted with key informants including eight senior physicians and heads of legal affairs and complaints department in MoH during July 2023. Each interview session lasted between 30 and 45 min and focused on asking them about management and mitigation strategies to reduce the practice of DM among physicians. An interview guide was developed based on previous studies [24, 25] and expert opinions (Supplementary file). In adherence to research ethics principles, the study’s objectives and methodology were thoroughly explained to the participants. We continued to collect data until saturation was achieved and themes were saturated at the tenth interview.

Data analysis

Qualitative data was analyzed using the content analysis method in conjunction with the interviews. Each interview was transcribed verbatim immediately after it happened. The transcripts were then read repeatedly, and initial codes were extracted. Relevant codes were then grouped into main subcategories and finally to relevant themes. To ensure transferability, the research process was explained to the participants. Two experts with qualitative research experience also reviewed the research results to ensure the reliability of the research process. Regarding credibility, the researchers who collected the data independently read and coded the transcripts to analyze the data.

The logistic regression analysis of predictors for DM behaviors

The probability to referring patients to the emergency room to avoid legal liability was 2.61 times (OR = 2.61) higher among physicians hold Bachelor’s degree than other hold a Board, Master, or PhD certificates. The probability to performing unnecessary patients’ hospitalization was 3.92 times (OR = 3.92) higher among physicians working in pediatric departments than physicians working in other departments.

The likelihood of performing unnecessary biopsy by physicians who faced lawsuits was 2.44 times (OR = 2.44) by those who did not. The likelihood of performing unnecessary endoscopies by female physicians was 2.29 times (OR = 2.29;) than that by male physicians and was 2.15 times (OR = 2.15) by physicians who faced lawsuits than those who did not. In addition, the possibility of avoiding dealing with patients who have complex medical problems by female physicians was 2.00 times (OR = 2.00) higher than that by male physicians and was 1.08 times (OR = 1.01) higher among physicians whose working overtime (> 36 h) than whose working (≤ 36 h). The possibility of avoiding dealing with patients who have excessive anxiety was 2.00 times (OR = 2.00) higher among physicians who are working overtime (> 36 h) than those working less than 36 h.

Exaggerating documentation for patients in detail to avoid blame from colleagues and issuing excessive medical instructions can potentially exhaust the supporting medical staff was higher among physicians working rotating shifts compared to physicians working fixed (morning) shifts. Avoiding the changing from intravenous to oral treatments despite improving the patient’s health condition was 1.90 times (OR = 1.90) higher among physicians who worked overtime (> 36 h) than doctors who worked 36 h and less.

Theme 1: designed continues training and teaching programs

There is a consensus among participants that training and continuing education programs are one of the most critical ways to reduce DM behaviors. They pointed out that the physicians working in Gaza graduated from different local and international universities, which may lead to a different treatment way of dealing with the same patient.

“…Doctors need continuous training and updating their treatment methods to deal with different types of cases according to the local or international protocols used in the institutions of the MoH” (P 4).

“…Doctors need continuous training and updating their treatment methods to deal with different types of cases according to the local or international protocols used in the institutions of the MoH” (P 4).

“…Before beginning to implement a new protocol or clinical practice guideline in the specialty, it is essential to ensure that all doctors are well trained and aware of this” (P 5).

“…Before beginning to implement a new protocol or clinical practice guideline in the specialty, it is essential to ensure that all doctors are well trained and aware of this” (P 5).

In addition, the participants’ stressed the necessity of implementing workshops and enhanced awareness about the DM phenomenon and its cost and consequences, more attention and daily communication between senior physicians and junior physicians about the cases visited and the clinical interventions used, and Internship health curricula should be modified to include courses dealing with litigation issues.

Theme 2: the relationship between doctor and patient

Participants emphasized that in the era of easy access to social media and easy posting by everyone. The apparent lack of trust from ordinary people in the healthcare system has become clear. The most important reason for the lack of trust from the perspectives of the participants is the exaggeration of any complications of cases treated inside MoH in the media and posted as cases of neglect and ignoring the great daily work in the institutions of the MoH. The participants’ stressed on the necessity of using social media and mosques to enhance a trust relationship, creating an alliance between the doctor and civil society (such as the Friends of the Health Care institution).

“…There is a need for a workshop and discussion groups with civil society representatives to enhance their awareness about the good functioning of the Palestinian health system and health indicators compared to neighbouring countries” (P 7).

“…There is a need for a workshop and discussion groups with civil society representatives to enhance their awareness about the good functioning of the Palestinian health system and health indicators compared to neighbouring countries” (P 7).

Furthermore, some participants also announced that, given of the work overload, the shortage of staff, some doctors dealing with patient in tough and inappropriate manner, they stressed the need for more communication and clarifications with the patients’ families about the patient’s health. They urge workers in psychological departments to be part of the treatment process and participate in managing the conflict between doctors and patients.

Theme 3: reforming the liability and complaints system

In this theme, there are two points of view, the first is the opinions of the heads of legal affairs and complaints department and the second is the opinions of physicians. The heads of the legal affairs and complaints department confirmed that the MoH has a clear method for dealing with patients’ complaints and that they are always on the side of healthcare providers. In addition, they have lawyers to defend health care providers in the courts when necessary. They declared that most complaints are resolved within the MoH and do not reach the courts and they announced that they cannot prevent the patient from going to court if he/she feels medical negligence has been practiced.

“…More than 85% of complaints are resolved within the MoH and various administrative measures are taken within the MoH against those proven to be practicing medical negligence.” (P8).

“…More than 85% of complaints are resolved within the MoH and various administrative measures are taken within the MoH against those proven to be practicing medical negligence.” (P8).

In addition, they confirmed that there is a draft law in the Palestinian Legislative Council that needs approval that specifies medical responsibility.

Regarding the opinions of physicians, there is a consensus among the participating physicians that the main reason for practicing DM behaviors is the lack of a sense of security and protection, administratively and legally, in the workplace. Most of them declared that they had known colleagues who had been subjected to lawsuits or complaints in the courts. The Physicians participants stressed on the necessity of differentiate between medical negligence and medical complications.

“…Physicians need to feel safe and protected, both administrative and legal, in the workplace.” (P1, P2, P3, P4).

“…Physicians need to feel safe and protected, both administrative and legal, in the workplace.” (P1, P2, P3, P4).

All viewpoints (Heads of Legal Affairs and Complaints Department and Physicians) agreed on the need to establish health courts and specialized courts with judges trained in healthcare, as these courts would be able to understand the unique culture and context of the health field.

Theme 4: health system reform

Physicians reported the need for health system reforms by redistributing health procedures between primary, secondary, and tertiary care and relying on primary health care as the first choice for the patient which could lead to less workload on secondary care and more time consuming for physicians to deal with the patient. These interventions give a good opportunity to obtain a complete medical history and reduce the practice of DM behaviors. Furthermore, participants announced the need to promote collaboration in multidisciplinary teamwork, which may lead to reduced individual fear of responsibility, better service provision, and reduced DM behaviors. They pointed out the need to establish clinical auditing committees and focus on studying malpractice cases to avoid reappearance, reform the rewards and promotion system, and rely on the MoH to implement compensation procedures for a patient who has suffered medical malpractice.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies in low- and middle-income countries that used mixed methods to assess DM behaviors, associated factors and the possible solutions to reduce practice of these behaviors. In addition, this is the first study that highlighted this issue in the Gaza Strip, from the perspective of physicians.

Before Oslo agreement between the Palestinian Liberation Organization and Israel, the the public health law number 40 for the year 1940 (Egypation law) applied in Gaza districts, and the public health law number 43 for the year 1966 (Joradian Law) applied in West Bank districts. Since Oslo agreement, the Palestinian MoH has been the major provider of health care in the Gaza Strip and West Bank according to the Palestinian Public Health Law which lastly modified and approved on 22nd of December 2004.

Our study results display that the DM-related behaviors ranged from 30.5% to 75.5%. The highest rate of DM performed behaviors were unnecessary consultations with departments and other consultants, issuing excessive medical instructions that can lead to fatigue of supporting medical staff, referring patients to the emergency room to avoid legal liability, unnecessary imaging, and unnecessary laboratory investigations.

A recent study conducted among Iranian physicians demonstrated that the practicing of positive DM behaviors ranged from 14.5% to 94.5%, and the highest reported behavior performed was the description of details and patient participation in treatment selection and the lowest behavior performed by physicians was the unnecessary transfer of a patient to the emergency departments [8].

Another study conducted among Turkish demonstrated that the practicing of DM behaviors ranged from 22.6% to 69.5%, and the highest reported behavior performed was order extra tests and the lowest behavior performed by physicians was placed more emphasis on informed consent [2].

Variations were reported between our results in terms of percentages of practicing DM behaviors and other studies, a possible explanation for these differences may be due to differences in the items and procedures measured by study instruments, inconsistencies in people’s culture, and health organizational structures. Although the results differ, the results of the current study showed that the majority of Palestinian physicians have experience in practicing DM behaviors.

A possible explanation can be clearer through the deep understanding done by the researcher in the qualitative part, physicians’ consensus that the main reason for practicing DM behaviors is the lack of a sense of security and protection, administratively and legally, in the workplace. The most important reasons for practicing DM behaviors are the lack of trust from the perspectives of the participants is the exaggeration of any complications of cases treated inside MoH in the media and posted them as cases of neglect and ignoring the great daily work in the institutions of the MoH.

Our study displays that DM-related behaviors were positively affected by some socio-demographic variables of the physicians. The results demonstrated that the probability to performing unnecessary consultations with other departments and consultants was higher among physicians working in medical departments, and the probability of performing unnecessary patients’ hospitalization was higher among physicians working in pediatric departments than physicians working in other departments. Variations were recorded in the previous studies and can be attributed to that some DM behaviors are higher in some specialties due to the high-risk nature of medical procedures for the client and the greater chance of misuse lawsuits.

The probability to referring patients to the emergency room to avoid legal liability was higher among physicians hold Bachelor’s degree than other hold Board, Master, or PhD certificates. A possible explanation of the current results can be due that the majority of physicians with bachelor’s degree are recently graduated physicians’ and considered as juniors physicians compared to boarded physicians who’s always considered as seniors’ physicians’ and they more practicing of DM behaviors due to lack of experience. In the qualitative part, the physicians pointed out that the doctors working in Gaza graduated from different local and international universities, which may lead to a different treatment way of dealing with the same patient.

The likelihood of performing unnecessary biopsies by physicians who faced lawsuits was higher compared with those who did not. A previous study demonstrated that the rise in lawsuits against physicians exposes them to concerns about medical malpractice. This may force them to practice DM behaviors to prevent the risk of legal claims [26].

The likelihood of performing unnecessary endoscopies by female physicians was higher compared to male physicians, In addition, the possibility of avoiding dealing with patients who have complex medical problems by female physicians was higher compared to male physicians. These results are consistent with previous studies [12, 14]. A previous study showed that females are more evidence-based, patient-cethe ntred practitioners, and provide more advice to their patients than males [27].

The possibility of avoiding dealing with patients who have excessive anxiety was higher among physicians who worked overtime than whose working those who did not, avoiding the changing from intravenous to oral treatments despite improving the patient’s health condition was higher among physicians who worked overtime than who did not and exaggerate documenting for patient in details to avoid blame from colleagues and issuing excessive medical instructions can potentially exhaust the supporting medical staff was higher among physicians working rotating shifts compared to physicians working fixed (morning) shifts. A possible explanation for these results could be that senior and managerial physicians usually work fixed shifts (morning) and junior physicians work overtime and rotating shifts. So, junior physicians could be feeling more safety by referred complex cases, and overly anxious patients to the managerial physicians who always work fixed shifts.

Our study identified four possible solutions and strategies to reduce the practice of DM behaviours, design continues training and teaching programs, built a trust relationship between doctor and patient, reform the liability and complaints system, and health system reform. The previous studies identified many ways to decrease the practice of DM behaviors such as improving doctors’ training, educating related to suitable care methods in clinical environments, establishing, updating and distributing clinical practice guidelines or protocols directing common DM activities, providing the systematic practice of evidence-based health and structured care, investment in the social worker’s roles to contribute in handling the misunderstanding or conflict between doctors and patients, create clinical records management, create clinical auditing system and health debriefing, establishing committees to revision malpractice clients to avoid repetition, methods of complaints and inquiry must be promoted and improved, create alternatives to the current lawsuit system, and the founding of health courts and particular courts with qualified judges in the sector of health systems [2, 9, 12, 16, 28–41].

Study limitations

Potential limitations of the study are the study is the use of the convenience sampling method, which could lead to selection bias. In addition, based on a self-administration questionnaire it can lead to recall and social desirability bias. Furthermore, the small sample size is one of the important limitations of the study. In addition, it was conducted in government hospitals and did not include private institutions, non-governmental organizations and the United Nations Relief and Works Agency.

Conclusions

This study revealed that the prevalence of MD behaviors is high among most physicians in the Gaza Strip. Findings suggest that to reduce MD behaviours, managers should focus on interventions for female physicians, physicians working in pediatric and medical departments, physicians who faced lawsuits and worked rotating shifts, and those who worked overtime. In addition, various solutions and strategies are needed to reduce defensive behaviors such as structured training and education, restoring doctor-patient relationships, health system reform, and liability system reform.
