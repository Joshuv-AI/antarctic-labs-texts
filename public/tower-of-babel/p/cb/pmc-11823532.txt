Applications of Artificial Intelligence in Choroid Visualization for Myopia: A Comprehensive Scoping Review
Authors: Ali M. Alhalafi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Numerous artificial intelligence (AI) models, including deep learning techniques, are being developed to segment choroids in optical coherence tomography (OCT) images. However, there is a need for consensus on which specific models to use, requiring further synthesis of their efficacy and role in choroid visualization in myopic patients. A systematic literature search was conducted on three main databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) using the search terms: “Machine learning” OR “Artificial Intelligence” OR “Deep learning” AND “Myopia” AND “Choroid” OR “Choroidal” from inception to February 2024 removing duplicates. A total of 12 studies were included. The populations included myopic patients with varying degrees of myopia. The AI models applied were primarily deep learning models, including U-Net with a bidirectional Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory module, LASSO regression, Attention-based Dense U-Net network, ResNeSt101 architecture training five models, and Mask Region-Based Convolutional Neural Network. The reviewed AI models demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, including sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve values, in identifying and assessing myopia-related changes. Various biomarkers were assessed, such as choroidal thickness, choroidal vascularity index, choroidal vessel volume, luminal volume, and stromal volume, providing valuable insights into the structural and vascular changes associated with the condition. The integration of AI models in ophthalmological imaging represents a significant advancement in the diagnosis and management of myopia. The high diagnostic accuracy and efficiency of these models underscore their potential to revolutionize myopia care, improving patient outcomes through early detection and precise monitoring of disease progression. Future studies should focus on standardizing AI methodologies and expanding their application to broader clinical settings to fully realize their potential in ophthalmology.

Introduction

The burden of myopia is increasing worldwide due to increasing prevalence.[123] It is forecasted that, in 2050, 938 million people will be living with myopia, accounting for approximately 9.8% of the world’s population.[1] Eyes with high myopia are frequently prone to pathological myopia, which is a leading cause of blindness and visual impairment.[456]

Myopia is associated with changes in choroidal vasculature, with higher degrees of myopia being proportional to increased choroidal thinning.[7] Patients with high myopia consistently exhibit an even thinner choroid highlighting the importance of measuring choroidal perfusion in assessing myopia.[8] However, choroidal thinning can also be observed in aging healthy eyes,[9] and it may complicate conditions such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or Fuchs’ uveitis syndrome.[101112] Additionally, fundus diseases such as central serous chorioretinopathy,[13] AMD,[14] and diabetic retinopathy[15] can exhibit changes in choroidal blood perfusion.

The choroid is a vascular structure located between the retina and the sclera, which is responsible for nourishing the outer retina and performing essential physiological functions, such as regulating intraocular pressure and absorbing light.[1617] A method for quantifying choroidal perfusion change is the swept-source optic coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA), which has the advantages of a longer wavelength, faster scan speed, and deeper penetration.[181920] Among the frequently used biomarkers are choroidal vessel volume (CVV) and choroidal vascularity index (CVI). Otherwise known as the choroidal luminal volume (LV), CVV is defined as the volume of the big and medium choroidal vessels. The CVI is computed by dividing the CVV by the total volume of the choroid.[2122] Nevertheless, the binarization approach, which was extensively employed in earlier studies to compute CVV and CVI, was restricted to B-scan pictures that crossed the fovea and could not depict volumetric features.[23]

The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning has considerably advanced image-based ophthalmology, enhancing the identification and characterization of various ocular diseases.[2425] Reports by Gu et al.,[26] Lin et al.,[27] and Phene et al.[28] demonstrated the remarkable utility of AI in assessing glaucoma, cataracts, and keratopathy. Additionally, Sogawa et al.[29] and Li et al.[30] showed the usefulness of AI models in identifying ocular lesions associated with high myopia, such as retinoschisis and myopic choroidal neovascularization (mCNV), via the analysis of optical coherence tomography (OCT) pictures. However, pathologic myopia-related retinal problems are still difficult to identify from OCT pictures, and AI has not yet realized its full potential in this regard.

Many deep learning techniques are being developed to segment choroids in OCT images.[313233] However, there is no agreement on a specific model to be used, and there are inadequate data about the efficacy and the role of these models in choroid visualization in myopic patients. This scoping review aims to analyze the available AI models used in choroid visualization and their effectiveness in diagnosing and assessing myopia and predicting its eventual complications. These insights are crucial for establishing the clinical utility of AI-based ophthalmological imaging in myopia and identifying areas for further development.

Methods

Data sources and searches

Literature searches were conducted on three main databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) for our scoping review. The search strategy included the terms: “Machine learning” OR “Artificial Intelligence” OR “Deep learning” AND “Myopia” AND “Choroid” OR “Choroidal,” covering the period from inception to February 2024. The resulting articles were imported into EndNote to remove duplicates, and the final set of articles was uploaded to Rayyan for screening.

Eligibility criteria and screening

The eligibility criteria included primary original articles published in peer-reviewed journals that investigated the use of AI models, such as machine learning or deep learning, for the visualization of the choroid in patients with myopia. We excluded articles with different aims, reviews, and case reports. Title and abstract screening were conducted independently by two authors, with any disagreements resolved by consensus or by a senior author if necessary. Full-text screening followed, assessing the entire article to determine its eligibility based on the specified criteria.

Data extraction

We extracted data providing an overview of the study, including summary, baseline characteristics, and results. This encompassed the country of the study, study design, population, sample size, AI model, mechanism of the AI model, study aim, main findings, effect estimates, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) of the AI model. Data synthesis: We presented the objectives of various studies and summarized the main findings related to these objectives. Effect estimates, such as correlations or means of measurements, were included to provide a numerical overview of the findings and the different parameters investigated by AI models in choroid visualization. Additionally, we focused on sensitivity, specificity, and AUC to demonstrate the effectiveness of various AI models in diagnosis and prognosis.

Results

Database searching and screening

A total of 51 articles were obtained after searching the included databases, with a total of 12 articles being included.[233435363738394041424344] The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses diagram for the search and inclusion process is presented in Figure 1.

Summary and baseline characteristics

Seven of the included studies were conducted in China, two in Taiwan, and one each in the USA, Singapore, and Japan. Among these, three were cross-sectional studies, and nine were cohort studies, with a total sample size of 2817 patients. The population in these studies consisted of myopic patients with varying grades: low, mild, moderate, high myopia, pathologic myopia, emmetropia, and nonmyopic patients.

Artificial intelligence models used

The AI models applied in the included studies were primarily deep learning models. Specific models included U-Net with a bidirectional Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory (C-LSTM) module, LASSO regression, Attention-based Dense (AD) U-Net network, ResNeSt101 architecture training five models, and Mask Region-Based Convolutional Neural Network (R-CNN). Six of the studies utilized SS-OCTA to analyze various parameters with the help of AI models for choroidal segmentation, including choroidal vascularity, choriocapillaris (CC) perfusion, choroidal thickness (ChT), CVI, CVV, and other choroidal parameters.

Different mechanisms of AI were identified in the included studies. In order to create OCT pictures, Xu et al.[37] investigated the effectiveness of AI models for the segmentation of the choroidal lumen, while Wang et al.[38] investigated the effectiveness of deep learning model for the segmentation of choroidal borders. A particular deep learning technique, called group-wise context selection network (GCS-Net), was used by Li et al.[34] for choroid segmentation in myopic and nonmyopic patients using SS-OCTA. In addition to these models, Wu et al.[39] employed a Boundary Enhancement Module composed of three parallel branches: (1) a Feature Extraction Branch, which acquires feature maps with various receptive fields; (2) a Channel Enhancement Branch, which gathers channel boundary data; and (3) a Boundary Activation Branch, which enhances boundary information using a unique activation function. Ye et al.[36] implemented a ResNeSt101 architecture, which was used to train five separate models to identify the following five myopic maculopathies: dome-shaped macula (DSM), macular Bruch membrane (BM) defects, subretinal hyper-reflective material (SHRM), macular choroidal thinning, and myopic traction maculopathy (MTM) [Table 1].

Aims of the included studies

Most of the included studies aimed to investigate the relationship between various choroidal parameters and myopia. Other studies focused on the association between these parameters and different degrees of myopia. Some studies had more specific aims. For example, Wang et al.[38] aimed to understand changes in the choroid vasculature of patients with pathologic myopia and its relation to their visual acuity. The studies by Li et al.[34] and Chen et al.[35] sought to evaluate the relationship between myopia and ChT. Ye et al.,[36] on the other hand, aimed to develop deep learning models using OCT images to recognize myopic maculopathy in individuals with extreme myopia.

Main findings

Five of the included studies consistently demonstrated that high-myopia patients had statistically significantly lower ChT compared to those with different degrees of myopia or normal subjects.[2335373839] According to Xu et al.,[37] several measures, including three-dimensional (3D) CVI, LV, stromal volume (SV), and total choroidal volume (TCV), were reduced in the choroidal regions of the high-myopia group compared to the low- and moderate-myopia groups. Additionally, Wang et al.[38] demonstrated that pathologic myopia was associated with decreased CVI, luminal area (LA), and stromal area (SA) values compared to emmetropia, low myopia, and simple high myopia. There was no difference in CVI between the emmetropia/low-myopia and high-myopia groups, but ChT, LA, and SA values were lower in simple high myopia compared to emmetropia and low myopia.

Chen et al.[35] presented estimates from the included patients and found that the high-myopia group had significantly thinner ChT (mean, 134.84 ± 43.83 µm) than the nonhigh-myopia group (mean, 204.83 ± 46.29 µm, P < 0.001), as determined by automatic segmentation using the mask R-CNN model. The ChT estimated by automatic segmentation was related to age (β = −0.151, P = 0.024) and axial length (AL) (β = −0.784, P < 0.001) in the multivariate linear regression. There was a significant inverse association between AL and ChT, as determined by computerized segmentation (r = −0.765, P < 0.001).

According to Luo et al.,[23] the groups with high (1.930 ± 0.865), moderate (2.589 ± 1.083), and mild (3.238 ± 1.033) myopia had all significantly lower global CVVs than the emmetropia (3.486 ± 0.992) group. Additionally, the group with severe myopia had a lower global CVI (0.258 ± 0.061) than the groups with mild myopia (0.320 ± 0.051), moderate myopia (0.286 ± 0.066), and emmetropia (0.320 ± 0.055) (P < 0.001). Compared to eyes with emmetropia (377.532 ± 80.593), mild myopia (348.367 ± 78.191), or moderate myopia (300.197 ± 87.175), eyes with extreme myopia (242.753 ± 65.641) had a thinner ChT (P < 0.001).

Ye et al.[36] demonstrated that different myopic maculopathy lesions – including macular choroidal thinning, macular BM defects, SHRM, MTM, and DSM – could be screened and identified using deep learning models based on OCT images with high sensitivity and specificity. This finding supports the broader usefulness of AI models in diagnosing choroidal lesions. The implemented models varied in terms of sensitivity and specificity. For example, Ye et al.’s model had a sensitivity of 73.9%,[36] while Lu et al.’s[43] binary classification model achieved the highest sensitivity of 100% for classifying AL from ChT in fovea-centered, two-dimensional single OCT images.

Similarly, specificity ranged from 84.8% in Ye et al.’s[36] model to 99.82% in Li et al.’s[34] GCS-Net model, which automatically segmented the choroid and measured its thickness on SS-OCTA images of patients with high myopia. Furthermore, the AUC for all models in the trials varied between 92.7 and 99.5 [Table 2].

Synthesis of the biomarkers and their diagnostic utility

Table 3 summarizes the findings on various biomarkers, their outcomes, and diagnostic efficacy from multiple studies using different AI models. ChT, assessed in 7 out of the 12 included studies, was consistently found to be significantly thinner in high myopia, making it useful for monitoring myopia progression and pathology (sensitivity: 92.42%–100%, specificity: 88.7%–99.82%, AUC: 92.7–99.51). The choroidal vascularity index (CVI), examined in 3 studies, was lower in high and pathologic myopia, strongly correlating with best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA). CVV, studied by Luo 2022,[23] was significantly lower in high myopia, indicating its value in assessing choroidal atrophy and myopia severity. LV and SV, both lower in high myopia as shown by Xu 2021,[37] reflect vascular and structural changes in the choroid. LA and SA, studied by Wang 2022,[38] were reduced in pathologic myopia, highlighting their role in evaluating choroidal integrity. Macular choroidal thickness (MCT), identified by Ye 2021,[36] is crucial for diagnosing myopic maculopathies, with a high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity: 90.5%, specificity: 88.7%, AUC: 92.7). CC perfusion, assessed by Xu 2021,[37] was lower in high myopia, useful for evaluating microvascular changes, although specific diagnostic metrics were not reported. Furthermore, the ResNeSt101 architecture model by Ye 2021 was highly performant in identifying myopic maculopathy signs including damage to BM, SHRM, MTM and DSM, suggesting interest in large-scale, cost-effective screening for high myopia.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This scoping review synthesizes research on AI models used in choroid visualization for patients with varying degrees of myopia. These models are operated with diagnostic imaging such as SS-OCTA and normal OCT. Most models are based on deep learning, including U-Net with a bidirectional C-LSTM module, LASSO regression, AD U-Net network, ResNeSt101, and Mask R-CNN. The study demonstrates the availability of different AI models that can be integrated with OCT to visualize the choroid in myopic patients, allowing for the investigation and measurement of various parameters correlated with myopia severity. Biomarkers such as ChT, TCV, SV, LV, SA, LA, CVI, and CVV were found to decrease as myopia severity increased. High-myopia patients exhibited smaller values compared to those with low or moderate myopia and nonmyopic individuals. The AI models showed high diagnostic accuracy, with impressive sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values, indicating their strong potential for future myopia diagnosis and associated complications. Additionally, some models effectively detected maculopathies in myopic patients, such as macular choroidal thinning, macular BM defects, SHRM, MTM, and DSM, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy.

Diagnostic imaging in myopia

In the assessment of myopia, various diagnostic imaging modalities play crucial roles in providing valuable insights into the condition. OCT has emerged as a significant tool, offering high-resolution imaging that can delineate early myopic macula pathology and aid in the detection of choroidal neovascularization.[45] Additionally, combining OCT with fundus photography has been recognized as the gold standard for examining high myopia, providing detailed information on vessel morphology, retinal characteristics, and AL.[46] Multimodal imaging, which integrates different imaging techniques, is essential for monitoring treatment response and comprehensively evaluating myopia.[45]

The advancements in ophthalmic bioimaging modalities have enabled the identification of fine structural details in myopia, particularly changes in the chorioretinal and scleral layers that are critical in understanding the pathogenesis of pathologic myopia.[47] Moreover, the development of swept-source OCT/OCTA and widefield imaging systems has significantly improved the diagnosis and treatment of myopia and its related complications.[48]

The advent of AI has further enhanced biometry assessments in myopia, being utilized in conjunction with imaging modalities such as fundus photography and OCT to assist in the detection, diagnosis, and prediction of myopia progression,[49] notably in highly myopic individuals.[50] AI applications extend to the automated detection of pathologic myopia using 3D OCT images, showcasing the potential for AI to enhance diagnostic capabilities in myopia.[51] The combination of AI with the different imaging modalities enabled detailed insights into myopic changes, aided in the early detection of complications, and offered valuable support in clinical decision-making for individuals with myopia.

Clinical and pathological value of the different biomarkers

The different imaging modalities enable the assessment of ChT thinning and other biomarkers of progressive degrees of myopia. While accurate quantitative measurement has been achieved by various manual techniques, these approaches are labor-intensive and unsuitable for screening. The present review demonstrates the effectiveness of AI models in detecting changes in these biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity, suggesting the usefulness of these models in large-scale screening and early detection of myopia complications.

A 2-year longitudinal observational study, by Li et al.,[52] showed that myopic individuals with a thinner choroid were generally more likely to develop myopic maculopathy. Diffuse atrophy in highly myopic eyes also develops due to increasing choroidal thinning.[53] The extent of chorioretinal atrophy and increased severity of high myopia are highly correlated with macular diffuse choroidal atrophy. The interest of AI is to enable early detection and measure of these biomarkers, aiding in early treatment and prevention of worse outcomes.[53]

It remains unknown how exactly high myopia causes degenerative and atrophic alterations in the eyes. The most likely causes of myopic maculopathy development are the mechanical stretching of the retina and ischemia caused by prolonged AL, which may reduce the density and diameter of the CC.[6] The clinical validity of AL in indicating myopic maculopathy has been extensively researched. AL elongation is a significant indicator for tracking myopic progression due to its objective nature.[54] Furthermore, longer AL has been linked to a higher prevalence of myopic maculopathy, suggesting its predictive value in identifying individuals at risk for macular complications.[55] In addition to AL, age is also a significant mechanism. The aging process and atherosclerotic changes in the choroidal arteries, along with susceptibility to systemic diseases such as hypertension and hyperlipidemia, could explain the correlation between choroidal thinning and aging. These microvascular alterations may result in a decrease in ChT.[5657]

Regarding CC, eyes with myopic maculopathy, particularly those with patchy atrophy, exhibit complete loss of the CC structure, emphasizing the relevance of CC assessment in understanding the pathology of myopic maculopathy.[58] The use of AI models showed that CC perfusion is significantly lower in high myopia,[37] indicating the interest of this biomarker in assessing microvascular changes in the choroid associated with myopia severity.

Patchy atrophy and mCNV-related macular atrophy are characterized by BM abnormalities. BM deficiencies indicate significantly reduced visual acuity and correspond to an absolute scotoma, psychophysically, due to the absence of photoreceptors.[53] Therefore, early identification and ongoing monitoring of BM deficiency lesions are critical, both being potentially facilitated by AI models. mCNV is a common SHRM-associated condition that typically results in significant visual impairment and requires prompt management using anti-vascular endothelial growth factor.[59] Not only may prompt therapy enhance visual acuity in the short term, but it also lowers the risk of BM abnormalities.[60]

As OCT becomes a standard technique, MTM may be easily identified in day-to-day clinical settings. Numerous investigations have shown that maculoschisis patients do not experience any subjective symptoms, and the condition typically has a steady natural course.[6162] On the other hand, maculoschisis carries a threat of worsening to the point where foveal detachment and the macular hole become fully thick, resulting in significant vision loss.[6263] Timely surgical intervention is essential for improving visual function in these patients.[6162]

Regarding DSM, it is present in 18.5% of high-myopia patients and has a positive correlation with the degree of myopic maculopathy.[6465] While BCVA appears to be stable over time, DSM raises the risk of developing a number of complications, such as retinoschisis, mCNV, and subretinal effusion.[6667] Subfoveal serous detachment is more likely to develop when the DSM bulge height exceeds 400 µm.[67] As a result, identifying DSM is essential for preventing situations that could jeopardize vision.

Performance of deep learning models in choroid visualization of myopic patients: Comparative data on the performance of AI and deep learning models versus physician assessors showed impressive results, indicating the added value of these technologies. Ye et al.[36] employed the ResNeSt101 AI system to diagnose choroidal atrophy, and the system’s sensitivity was comparable to that of human ophthalmologists. For BM deficiency and MTM, the AI system outperformed junior retinal specialists in sensitivity. The AI system’s AUC for detecting SHRM was 0.927. Based on a review of the patient’s clinical records, about half of the patients with SHRM were diagnosed with mCNV, while the remaining patients were classified as having either macular atrophy or hemorrhage. Consequently, this AI system aids in referring patients with SHRM for additional testing and care. In certain circumstances, the AI system outperformed human experts and achieved an AUC of 0.974 for MTM detection, suggesting its usefulness for patients with MTM who require follow-up. The heatmaps demonstrated the AI model’s ability to recognize the characteristics of lesions caused by myopic maculopathy. In multiple instances, the AI system outperformed human specialists.[36]

Li et al.[34] reported that the GCS-Net deep learning algorithm is far more reliable and efficient than conventional techniques. Unlike traditional methods that require preprocessing steps like denoising, augmentation, and retinal layer segmentation, the GCS-Net method generates results from the original B-scans end-to-end. Many conventional approaches, including graph-based ones, require the choroid region to be continuous to function well. However, GCS-Net can reliably identify discontinuities caused by pathological atrophy, the optic disc, or retinal folding abnormalities. Furthermore, GCS-Net has a low number of parameters and is a lightweight network, allowing smooth learning even with medium-sized training datasets.[346869]

Strengths and limitations

Our study offers a comprehensive overview of current AI models developed to visualize the choroid, demonstrating their superior performance in myopic patients. We provided insights into the efficacy, accuracy, and applicability of these models in various contexts. However, several limitations exist, including heterogeneity among models, differences in patient characteristics, and variability in the biomarkers assessed across studies, with some biomarkers not receiving sufficient attention. This variability hinders the analysis of the reproducibility of these performances and the standardization of approaches. This underscores the need for further research to provide robust data on various biomarkers using diverse AI models.

Conclusion

The integration of AI models in ophthalmological imaging represents a significant advancement in the diagnosis and management of myopia. The use of AI in conjunction with OCT and SS-OCTA imaging allows for detailed visualization and quantification of choroidal parameters, aiding in the monitoring and management of myopic progression and related complications.

The reviewed studies highlight the effectiveness of deep learning models, such as U-Net with a bidirectional C-LSTM module, LASSO regression, AD U-Net network, ResNeSt101, and Mask R-CNN, in accurately diagnosing and assessing myopia-related changes in ChT and other biomarkers. The high diagnostic accuracy and efficiency of these models highlight their potential to revolutionize myopia care, improving patient outcomes through early detection and precise monitoring of disease progression.

On the other hand, the review reveals a substantial gap in the literature regarding the reproducibility of the diagnostic accuracy of these biomarkers. Future studies should focus on standardizing AI methodologies and expanding their application to broader clinical settings to fully realize their potential in ophthalmology.
