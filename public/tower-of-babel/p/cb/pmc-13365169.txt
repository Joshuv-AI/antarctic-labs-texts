Systemic Inflammatory Indexes as Predictors of Delirium, Thromboembolic Events, and Mortality After Hip Fracture and Major Orthopedic Surgery: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Suyeon Park, Ki Tae Park, Naye Kang, Eunseo Joung, Nah Yon Kim, Byung-Ho Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHip fractures and other orthopedic surgeries are often associated with poor post-operative outcomes, including thrombus formation, delirium, and mortality. Systemic inflammatory markers, such as the systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), have shown potential for predicting adverse outcomes in cancer and cardiovascular diseases. This meta-analysis evaluates the predictive value of SII and SIRI for complications following orthopedic surgeries, aiming to establish them as practical tools for risk stratification to assist the surgeons’ decision for optimal treatments.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science through January 2025, adhering to PRISMA guidelines. Cohort studies reporting the relationship between pre-operative SII/SIRI and post-operative complications were included. Outcomes included thrombus formation, delirium, and mortality. Random-effects models were used for meta-analyses, and heterogeneity was assessed with I2 statistics. Sensitivity analyses were performed to mitigate the heterogeneity.ResultsNineteen studies with 8,315 patients published up to January 2025 were included. High pre-operative SII was associated with post-operative delirium (standardized mean difference, 1.28; 95% confidence intervals, 0.25–2.32; I2 = 96.5%; P = 0.015). Based on two studies, elevated pre-operative SIRI showed a trend toward higher delirium risk (pooled odds ratios, 3.07; 95% confidence intervals, 2.66–3.48; no heterogeneity; P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis suggested a potential association, but statistical significance was not achieved.ConclusionSII and SIRI show promise as predictors for post-operative delirium but have limited evidence for thrombus formation or mortality. Future research should focus on prospective studies with standardized cut-offs to validate their clinical utility.Keywords: Systemic Inflammatory Markers, SII, SIRI, Orthopedic Surgery, Post-Operative Complications, Meta-Analysis

INTRODUCTION

Hip fractures are a major health concern, particularly among elderly individuals. Hip fractures are dangerous not only in isolation but also due to serious post-operative complications such as thrombus formation and delirium.1,2 These complications contribute to higher social expenditure including prolonged hospital stays. The complexity and difficulty inherent in orthopedic procedures are correlated with increased post-operative mortality rates.3,4,5 Therefore, predicting adverse complications and mitigating negative outcomes of patients undergoing orthopedic surgeries is pressing. Moreover, with the acceleration of global aging, it has become increasingly necessary to improve the prognosis and the management of orthopedic surgeries.6,7

Monocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, which are components of those indexes, are all essential cells involved in immune and inflammatory responses. However, their role is not limited to these functions; they also exert significant influence across various physiological processes in the body. Therefore, utilizing composite indicators, such as systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), may provide a valuable tool for effectively predicting post-operative patient outcomes.

Recent studies have increasingly reported the potential of systemic inflammatory markers, including SII and SIRI to predict post-operative complication in cancer and cardiovascular diseases. Consequently, there is a growing trend in the field of orthopedics to investigate and apply these indices. For example, Guo et al.8 reported that SII ≥ 541.1 was significantly associated with post-operative surgical site infection after hip fracture (odds ratios [ORs], 2.29; 95% CI, 1.18–4.42).

If it is demonstrated that SII and SIRI possess robust predictive power for various clinical outcomes, they offer significant advantages in clinical decision-making and patient consultations. Unlike C-reactive protein or cytokine assays, which require additional reagents and processing, SII and SIRI can be automatically calculated from routine blood counts, providing practical clinical accessibility.

Although numerous studies have demonstrated that these indexes can serve as indicators of clinical outcomes in cancer and cardiovascular contexts, analyses for their role in predicting outcomes in non-cancer, non-cardiovascular surgeries are insufficient. Emerging evidence suggests that SII and SIRI may have predictive value for adverse outcomes following orthopedic surgery, but their utility remains not fully established. Given the intricate relationship between inflammation, immune responses and post-operative complications, it is plausible that SII and SIRI could offer independent tools for predicting such complications.

This study aims to evaluate the predictive value of SII and SIRI for post-operative complications after orthopedic surgeries with a focus on thrombus formation, delirium, and mortality. We hope to establish these systemic inflammatory markers as reliable tools for assessing post-operative outcomes in orthopedic surgery.

METHODS

Search methods for the identification of studies

We conducted a comprehensive search in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science up to January 2025 following the PRISMA guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). The keywords to search studies about relationship between SII and SIRI and post-operative outcomes were “Systemic Immune-Inflammatory index,” “Systemic Inflammation Response index,” “SII,” “SIRI,” “mortality,” “survival,” “thrombus,” “thrombosis,” “thromboembolism,” “delirium.” Detailed search strategy on each database is included in Supplementary Data 1. There was no language limitation (studies not written in English are also included).

Criteria for selecting studies

Studies which meet these inclusion criteria are included: 1) the study design is a cohort study; 2) the surgery type should be an orthopedic surgery; 3) As study outcome, relationship between pre-operative SII/SIRI and post-operative outcomes (including mortality, thrombus formation, and delirium); 4) Effect sizes included OR with 95% CI for thrombus formation and delirium, and HR with 95% CI for mortality. For studies about SII and delirium, each group’s mean of SII should be provided to calculate the standardized mean difference (SMD).

Studies meeting these exclusion criteria are excluded: 1) not providing full-text; 2) review, letters, meta-analysis; 3) animal studies; 4) incomplete data in which the blood test were conducted after surgery or timing is unknown.

In case of studies that offer multiple effect size in the same cohort, the multivariate ORs and HRs were included because they could present more scientific and comprehensive results reflecting and adjusting other factors. A sensitivity analysis comparing pooled results derived from univariate and multivariate estimates yielded similar effect directions, indicating robustness to model selection.

Outcome measures and data extraction

The main post-operative outcomes and complications we focused on were thrombus formation, mortality, and delirium. We regarded studies including deep vein thromboembolism or venous thrombosis as reporting thrombus formation. Delirium should be diagnosed by appropriate assessing tools such as the Confusion Assessment Method questionnaire. SII is calculated by the definition (Platelet Count) × (Neutrophil Count)/(Lymphocyte Count), and SIRI is measured by the definition (Monocyte Count) × (Neutrophil Count)/(Lymphocyte Count).

Data from eligible studies are organized in spreadsheet. We extracted data as follows: the last name of the main author, the publication year, the number, mean age, and gender ratio, enrollment period of included patients, the type of surgery, the follow-up term, the mean of SII and SIRI, the cut-off value, the measuring time and the effect sizes of each study.

Quality assessment and publication bias

We performed quality evaluation of each study with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scales (Supplementary Table 2). It has 3 parameters to assess observational studies: selection, comparability, and outcome. All included studies’ qualities are meant to be moderate or high.

Funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test were applied to explore potential publication bias. It is acknowledged that these statistical approaches have reduced reliability with smaller study samples.9 Therefore, the results were interpreted considering both the possibility of publication bias and natural sampling variation (Supplementary Fig. 1).10 Instead, sensitivity analysis was performed and non-English studies (Chinese, German) were also reviewed.

Statistical analysis

In terms of the thrombus formation and delirium, the OR of each study used as the effect size since most of the included studies conducted analysis grouping the subjects according to their SII/SIRI levels. For mortality, the HR was the focused effect size because the mortality is a time-dependent outcome. During analysis, the standard error was calculated by the 95% CI. For studies which didn’t offer OR but only offered the mean and standard deviation (SD) of SII/SIRI of each group (post-operative delirium [POD] vs. non-POD), we calculated and analyzed SMD. All computations of this study were performed by R-studio (Posit, Boston, MA, USA) with R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (following packages were used: ‘meta,’ ‘metafor,’ ‘dplyr,’ ‘readxl’).

We assessed the heterogeneity among the included studies by the I2 statistic. Fixed-effect models were applied when I2 < 50%, and random-effects models when I2 ≥ 50%, consistent with Cochrane guidelines. And we performed the sensitivity analysis to eliminate an outlier and mitigate the heterogeneity if possible.

Due to heterogeneity in the SII/SIRI cut-offs, subgroup meta-analysis by cut-off values was not feasible. However, to test the impact of major clinical difference, a subgroup analysis was performed by stratifying data into Tibial plateau fracture and Hip fracture categories. The analysis showed no significant difference between groups (χ2 = 0.03, df = 1, P = 0.854), indicating consistent effects across major fracture categories (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Ethics statement

Ethics approval was not needed for this study because it was a meta-analysis of previously published studies and did not involve any individual patients’ data.

SII

Relationship between SII and post-operative mortality

Six studies reported an HR to investigate the relation between high-SII and post-operative mortality over 1 year. The pooled HR was 1.33 (95% CI, 0.82–1.83), indicating no statistically significant association between high SII and post-operative mortality. We conducted random-effect model because there was a heterogeneity (I2 = 94.6%) (Fig. 2A). Because of extremely high heterogeneity, we performed sensitivity analysis. However, the results were still not statistically significant (Fig. 2B).

Relationship between SII and post-operative thrombus formation

Seven studies reported an OR to investigate the relation between high-SII and thrombus formation. The pooled OR was 2.66 (95% CI, 0.78–9.04), indicating no statistically significant association between high SII and post-operative thrombus formation. We conducted random-effect model because there was a heterogeneity (I2 = 99.6%) (Fig. 3A). Considering high heterogeneity, we performed sensitivity analysis. However, the results were still not statistically significant (Fig. 3B).

Relationship between SII and POD

Two studies (3 cohorts) reported mean and SD of each POD group and non-POD group to investigate the relation between high-SII and POD. The calculated SMD was 1.28 (95% CI, 0.25–2.32). We conducted random-effect model because there was a heterogeneity (I2 = 96.5%). These findings from two studies (three cohorts) require cautious interpretation (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

Initially, the SII and SIRI were introduced as prognostic markers for cancer patients, as these blood cell components are known to influence tumor growth and defense mechanisms.30 More recently, an increasing number of studies have explored their predictive value for various clinical outcomes including cardiovascular diseases and surgical prognosis beyond oncology. For example, Wang et al.17 investigated the utility of pre-operative SII in predicting post-operative mortality in hip fracture patients and found that it was a reliable predictor.

SII and SIRI are derived from neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts. Platelets play a crucial role in hemostasis, including thrombosis, by interacting with immune cells and mediating complex coagulation mechanisms.31,32 Moreover, systemic inflammation has been implicated in acute cognitive dysfunction, including delirium, as reported by Cunningham.33 Thus, beyond merely reflecting immune and inflammatory responses, these indexes can provide comprehensive insights into a patient's overall physiological status.

In this study, we aimed to assess the predictive potential of SII and SIRI for post-operative mortality, thrombus formation, and delirium following orthopedic surgeries, thereby evaluating their clinical utility as low-cost and readily available prognostic markers.

Regarding mortality, the pooled HR for high SII was 1.33 (95% CI, 0.82–1.83). Although a numerical trend toward higher mortality was observed in patients with elevated SII, the association did not reach statistical significance. Even after conducting a sensitivity analysis to address heterogeneity, statistical significance was not observed. Due to the limited amount of data available, the association between SIRI and mortality could not be analyzed.

With respect to thrombus formation, the pooled OR for high SII was 5.39 (95% CI, −0.15 to 10.94). The wide CI observed in the overall random-effects model appears to be driven by the substantial heterogeneity, particularly due to the inclusion of an outlier study with an unusually large OR and variance (Melinte et al.23: OR, 18.87; CI, 5.90–60.42). To examine the stability of our results, we performed pre-planned sensitivity analyses excluding each study one at a time. The association consistently remained statistically non-significant, confirming limited evidence for this outcome. Due to the small sample size, caution is needed when interpreting the predictive value of SIRI for thrombus formation. However, some individual studies indicate a possible association. In particular, Ling et al.20 demonstrated that a high SIRI (≥ 3.95) was correlated with a higher occurrence of thrombus formation with adjustment for various clinical factors (OR, 2.090; 95% CI, 1.044–4.182).

The last outcome we focused on was POD. The pooled SMD between the POD and non-POD groups was 1.28 (95% CI, 0.25–2.32), which means that patients with POD had apparently higher SII, but this result should be interpreted carefully because of the limited number of studies. Similarly, a comprehensive analysis of the association between SIRI and POD was not feasible due to data limitations. However, the included studies suggest a certain degree of association between systemic inflammatory indexes and POD.

SII and SIRI are blood cell indexes reflecting the immune balance and estimating the systemic inflammatory status.34 Delirium is recognized as a consequence of the interplay between systemic inflammation and neurodegenerative vulnerability. Exaggerated peripheral inflammation, particularly elevated interleukin-6 levels, can activate microglia and disturb neurotransmission, leading to acute cognitive dysfunction.35,36 Therefore, the observed association between SII, SIRI, and POD may indicate that these indexes reflect a wider immune–inflammatory dysregulation underlying delirium pathophysiology.

The previous study of Pan et al.37 included 2 studies for SII and post-operative mortality. We included more studies focusing systemic inflammatory indexes adding studies about SIRI. There were meta-analyses which assessed the relevance between SII and all-cause mortality.38 We focused on the post-operative outcomes following orthopedic surgeries and researched for more various outcomes for the first time. However, this study also has limitations.

Despite an extensive search, the number of eligible studies was small, limiting assessment of publication bias. Substantial heterogeneity—arising from variations in surgical type, patient characteristics, and inconsistent SII/SIRI cut-off values—prevented the establishment of standardized prognostic thresholds. These factors, along with small sample sizes, likely reduced the precision and reliability of pooled estimates. Because subgroup analyses were underpowered, stratified evaluations were not feasible, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Therefore, subsequent studies should strictly control and report factors by including prospective studies and it would be beneficial to conduct studies presenting a standardized cut-off value for clinical use. Although SII and SIRI are low-cost and easily accessible, this study did not conduct a formal cost-effectiveness analysis. Future research should explore economic evaluations to better assess their financial feasibility in clinical settings. In addition, similar to the study of Zhou et al.,18 combining various indexes with systemic inflammatory indexes can offer more reliable indicator for post-operative outcomes.

If further research clarifies a more obvious and statistically significant relationship between these indexes and various clinical outcomes, SII and SIRI could serve as powerful, cost-effective biomarkers for predicting broad clinical outcomes, helping identify high-risk patients and assisting physicians in making appropriate clinical decisions.
