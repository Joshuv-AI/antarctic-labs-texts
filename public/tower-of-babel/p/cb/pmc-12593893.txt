High-grade uterine endometrial stromal sarcoma harboring GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplifications
Authors: Lili Zhang, Lijuan Luan, Lei Zhang, Yufeng Huang, Wenyi Gu, Shui Yu, Licheng Shen, Jie Huang, Yang Shao, Jieakesu Su, Yingyong Hou, Chen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
GLI1 gene alterations including fusions and amplifications compromise a subset of malignant mesenchymal tumors exhibiting characteristic monomorphic nested morphology and frequent S100 positivity, which mimic glomus tumors or well differentiated neuroendocrine tumors. We report four high-grade uterine endometrial stromal sarcomas (ESS) harboring GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplifications with a median age of 51.5 years (range 43 ~ 72 years). Histologically, tumors showed a heterogenous morphology, including ovoid to spindle cells, showing nested/nodular arrangement (4/4). Myxoid background was observed at least partially in 4 tumors with prominent capillary networks. Mitoses index was 2 to 20/10 HPF (median 9.5/10 HPF). Immunochemically, tumors showed diffuse staining of CD10 (3/4) with frequently positive CyclinD1(2/4 tested) and mostly negative S100 protein (3/4). Next-generationsequencing (NGS) studies revealed GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification in all cases (4/4) and GLI1 fusion in 1 case (1/4), which were validated by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis. BCOR fusions were firstly identified with GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification in 2 cases (2/4). Copy number (CN) segmentation data showed GLI1 co-amplified cases present generally a single peak at the 12q13.3-15 locus. Follow-up (range:3 to 112 months; median 37.5 months) showed recurrence and/or metastasis in all cases (4/4), in which 1 patient developed lungs and liver metastasis. Relapse-free survival (RFS) analysis showed similar median RFS between GLI1 co-amplified HGESS and GLI1 non-amplified HGESS groups, which were shorter than LGESS group. Unusual clinicopathologic features of these HGESS with GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification mimicked other neoplasms, which caused significant diagnostic challenge and pitfalls. However, identification of GLI1 alterations in these tumors is beneficial for diagnosis and potential use of targeted GLI1 inhibitors.

Introduction

The glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (GLI1) gene located on chromosome 12q13 is a transcription factor as a downstream effector of the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway [1, 2]. Dysregulation of the Shh-Ptch-Gli pathway leads to several human diseases, including birth defects and cancers. GLI1 alteration was described as recurrent molecular alterations in multiple mesenchymal neoplasm entities, including several established subtypes such as pericytoma with t (7;12) translocation, plexiform fibromyxoma, gastroblastoma, and recently described GLI1-altered malignant epithelioid mesenchymal neoplasm [3–6]. In addition, co-amplifications of CDK4 and MDM2 genes was identified in a subset of GLI1-amplified soft tissue neoplasms with wide morphologic spectrums and inconsistent immunoprofiles [7]. Recently, GLI1 co-amplification was identified in well-differentiated/dedifferentiated liposarcomas (WDLPS/DDLPS) in a large cohort of 92 Cases [8]. In that study, careful investigation of tumor location, a well-differentiated liposarcoma component, and co-amplification of other spatially discrete genomic segments (1p and 6q) which recurrently present in DDLPS [9–12] were rendered to aid in distinction from tumors with true driver GLI1 alterations. Therefore, mesenchymal tumors with GLI1 aberration represent a heterogenous group with wide spectrum of morphology, immunoreactivity and molecular signatures, leading to additional potential diagnostic pitfalls include confusion with dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS), etc.

GLI1 expression in normal adult tissues is restricted to the fallopian tube, myometrium and testis [13]. However, most of GLI1-altered tumors are located in the head and neck region, the extremities or the stomach, merely a dozen of cases have been reported arisen from the genitourinary tract or gynecological tract [3, 4, 14–19]. Therefore, GLI-1 altered mesenchymal tumors in the gynecological tract are not well described and whether these tumors occur in uterine stromal tumors remain to be elucidated. In the present study, we retrospectively collected 4 high-grade uterine sarcomas with GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification, and described their histopathological and molecular features.

Materials and methods

Cases

This retrospective study was performed with the approval of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University review board. The pathology files were searched for endometrial stromal sarcomas (ESS) of surgical specimens in female patients from January 2016 to August 2022 including inpatient pathology and consultation pathology records. A total of 31 cases of ESS (20 LGESS and 11 HGESS), 1 uterine adenosarcoma (sarcoma component is HGESS) were included. After careful molecular study, 4 uterine GLI1-altered cases were identified, in which hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides of the primary tumor were available for 3 cases (Case1, 3 and 4), and paraffin blocks from reoperation after recurrence were available for all 4 GLI1-altered cases. We reviewed the clinicopathologic and molecular findings of all the cases. Details of treatment and follow-up were obtained from medical records or from contributing pathologists.

Survival outcome

Clinical records of all cases included and the 10 cases reported were reviewed for survival status. Relapse-free survival was defined as the first occurrence of tumor recurrence/metastasis after surgical resection with no evidence of residual disease or if the patient died of disease, whichever came first. If neither recurrence nor death occur during the follow-up period, the patient’s RFS is censored at the last follow-up date. Survival analyses were performed and plotted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2) using Mantel-Cox test. P values<0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC studies were performed on a Dako or VENTANA Autostainer using primary antibodies listed as following: smooth muscle actin (clone 1A4, 1:100 dilution, Thermo), desmin (D33, ready-to-use, Dako), CD10 (56C6, 1:300 dilution, Leica Biosystems), ALK (D5F3, ready-to-use, Roche), ER (6F11, 1:80 dilution, Leica Biosystems), PR (16, 1:100, Leica Biosystems), Cyclin D1(EPR2241, 1:100 dilution, Abcam), ROS1 (D4D6, 1:250 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology), BCOR (ab135801, 1:100, Abcam), STAT6(GPR500, 1:300 dilution, Gene Tech), HMB45(HMB45, 1:200 dilution, Dako), Melan-A(M7196, 1:200 dilution, Dako), S-100(IR504, ready-to-use, Dako), SOX10(GM005, 1:300 dilution, Gene Tech), c-Kit(GR521, 1:100 dilution, Gene Tech), DOG1(SP31, 1:200, Gene Tech), Pan-TRK(EPR17341, ready-to-use, Gene Tech).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FISH on interphase nuclei from paraffin-embedded 4-micron sections was performed applying commercial available break-apart probes (YWHAE (17p13) Probe, lot: F.01183-01; JAZF1 (7p15) Probe, lot: F.01352-01; BCOR (Xp11) Probe, lot: F.01220-01; GLI1(12q13) Probe, lot: F.01311-01) and amplification probes (MDM2 (12q15) Probe, lot: F01017-01; CDK4 (12q14) Probe, lot: F.01273-01; GLI1(12q13) Probe, lot: F01037-01), LBP Medicine Science and Technology Company, LTD, Guangzhou, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions. In short, slides of tumor sections were deparaffinized, pretreated, and hybridized with denatured probes overnight, followed by post-hybridization washes and interphase nucleus counter staining with DAPI. Microscopic analysis was performed using a Leica DM6000B microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) by Leica DFC310 FX imaging system. FISH images were processed by the LAS V4.5 (Leica Microsystems) software. For examination, at least 100 nonoverlapping tumor nuclei were analyzed. Gene rearrangement was considered if ≥ 20% of tumor nuclei demonstrated break-apart signals. Samples were considered positive for amplification as previously described [7, 20] when one of the following signal patterns of amplification was observed: homogeneously staining regions (HSR) are defined as large intrachromosomal regions that lack the longitudinal differentiation ordinarily shown by G-banding; double-minutes (DM) which are acentric, autonomously replicating circular DNA structures; ring chromosomes and MDA-multiple dot-like amplicons in various sizes that appeared as discrete, punctate signals by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH).

Next-generation sequencing

6 cases of HGESS with MDM2 amplification were successfully tested by both DNA and targeted RNA NGS.

Briefly, for the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples, five 10 μm tumor slices were used for DNA extraction using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality was assessed by spectrophotometry with absorbance at 230,260, and 280 nm, and quantified by Qubit 2.0. Libraries were prepared by 1 µg of fragmented genomic DNA underwent end-repairing, A-tailing and ligation with indexed adapters sequentially, followed by size selection using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Hybridization based target enrichment was carried out with GeneseeqOne™ pancancer gene panel (506-cancer-relevant genes, Geneseeq Technology Inc.), and xGen Lockdown Hybridization and Wash Reagents Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies). Captured libraries by Dynabeads M-270 (Life Technologies) were amplified in KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems) and quantified by qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems). Target enriched libraries were sequenced on the HiSeq4000 platform (Illumina) with 2 × 150 bp pair-end reads. Sequencing data were demultiplexed by bcl2fastq (v2.19), analyzed by Trimmomatic to remove low-quality (quality < 15) or N bases, and mapped to the reference hg19 genome (Human Genome version 19) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner. PCR duplicates were removed by Picard (available at: https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) was used to perform local realignments around indels and base quality reassurance. SNPs and indels were called by VarScan2 and HaplotypeCaller/UnifiedGenotyper in GATK, with the mutant allele frequency (MAF) cutoff as 0.5% for tissue samples and a minimum of three unique mutant reads. Gene fusions were identified by FACTERA and copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed with ADTEx.

RNA from FFPE samples was extracted using miRNeasy FFPE kit (QIAGEN). Ribosomal RNA was depleted using KAPA Stranded RNA-seq Kit with RiboErase (HMR) (KAPA Biosystems). RNASeq libraries were prepared using 1 µg RNA with 201 gene panel (sarcoma-relevant genes, Geneseeq Technology Inc.). Then target enriched libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina) with 2 × 150 bp pair-end reads. All reads were analysed by FusionCatcher (verison 0.99.4e) with parameters (--aligners blat, bowtie2, star, otherwise default parameter) for fusion analysis. The final fusion candidates were manually reviewed on IGV for confirmation. And KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using the ClusterProfiler package by R scripts.

Amplification pattern of genes spanning the 12q13.3-15 region in uterine GLI1 co-amplified mesenchymal tumors were examined by CNV analysis of DNA NGS results. Copy number (CN) amplifications were defined as gains of gene-level copy number greater than 4 (CN_high_cutoff = 1), while deletions were lower than 1.2 (CN_low_cutoff=-0.75). CN segmentation data were summarized and visualized by the R package “GenVisR” version 1.35.3.

Results

Clinical history of HGESS cases with GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification

The clinical data of 4 GLI1-coamplified HGESS cases are summarized in Table 1. Briefly, patient age ranged from 43 to 72 (median 51.5, mean 54.5) years. 2/4 (Case1,4) patients presented with abnormal vaginal bleeding for months, and 1/4 (Case 3) patient had a history of “uterine leiomyoma” for 30 years with sudden enlargement of the tumor. All patients underwent surgical resection as the primary treatment modality.

Pathologic findings

Gross examination demonstrated 1 tumor (Case 1) was well-circumscribed, while 2 tumors (Case 2, 3) were poorly marginated. 2 tumors (Case1, 2) appeared to be grossly nodular or lobular. A single tumor was observed in Case1-3 respectively, with 4.5 to 17.5 cm in largest dimension. The cut surface was tan-white and/or tan-red with medium firm and partial viscous texture. Hemorrhage was seen in 3 cases (Case1-3).

Histological features were summarized in Table 2. The tumors showed a heterogenous morphology, and composed of short spindle to ovoid cells arranging in nested/nodular (4/4) (Fig. 1A, B), microcystic (2/4, Case2, 4) (Fig. 1C), angiomyxoma-like (3/4, Case 1,2 and 4) (Fig. 1D), perivascular whorling (2/4, Case 4,1) (Fig. 1F, G) patterns showing “meningo-endothelial-like” appearance. In Case 3, tumor nodules were separated by thick irregular fibrous septa (Fig. 1B). Myxoid background was observed at least partially in all tumors, and was prominent in tumors with angiomyxoma-like patterns (Case 2, Fig. 1D). The tumors were moderate cellular, while in angiomyxoma-like areas less cellular with abundant myxoid stroma was observed (Case 2, Fig. 1D). Prominent capillary networks were characteristically present in all tumors, and 2 tumors (Case 4, 1) showed perivascular whorling architecture (Fig. 1F, G). Neoplastic cells exhibited short spindle to round shape with palely eosinophilic or vacuolated cytoplasm (Case 4, Fig. 1I) with mild to moderate cell atypia with scattered atypical cells observed in most tumors (Case 2, Fig. 1E). Mitoses index was 2 to 20/10 HPF (median 9.5/10 HPF) in GLI1-coamplifed cases. However, most recurrences showed similar or part of the features of the primary tumor. The primary uterine adenosarcoma of Case 1 presented with spindle tumor cells surrounding normal endometrial glands (Case 1, Fig. 1H), besides abdominal recurrences of HGESS with similar histological features as primary tumor showed perivascular whorling architecture (Case 1, Fig. 1G). Moreover, unlike DDLPS (the most common sarcoma with MDM2/CDK4 amplification), there is no evidence of well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDLPS) component in all 4 cases of uterine GLI1 co-amplification cases.

Molecular findings

For all the enrolled 31 ESS (20 LGESS and 11 HGESS) and 1 adenosarcoma (sarcoma component is HGESS), FISH analysis for JAZF-1, YWHAE, BCOR break apart, MDM2, CDK4 amplification was performed. JAZF-1 translocation was detected in 10/20 LGESS, YWHAE translocation in 3/11 HGESS and BCOR translocation in 2/11 HGESS. MDM2 and CDK4 amplifications were identified in 5/11 HGESS (in which 1 with YWHAE translocation and 2 with BCOR translocations) and 1 adenosarcoma. Ultimately, the 5 HGESS and 1 adenosarcoma with MDM2/CDK4 amplification were subjected to both DNA and targeted RNA NGS. As a result, GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification was identified in 3 HGESS and 1 adenosarcoma by NGS, and confirmed by FISH (Fig. 3E, G).

Top 30 genetic findings, including structural rearrangements, mutations, and copy number alterations, are summarized in Fig. 3A. 4 cases were identified with GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification, among them, 1 recurrent tumor (Case 4) showed both MED13L exon1::GLI1 exon4 fusion and GLI1 amplification (Fig. 3C, E). BCOR fusions (ZC3H7B as fusion partner) were identified in 2 cases (Case 2 and 4) by NGS and FISH (Fig. 3D, F). Our findings are the first to identify BCOR fusions together with GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification. Copy number were 9.59 to 17.91, 10.47 to 26.81 and 10.02 to 20.29 corrected to the portion of tumor area for GLI1, MDM2 and CDK4 respectively (Table 2). GLI1/MDM2 ratio were 0.32 to 1.58 (mean 0.84) and GLI1/CDK4 ratio were 0.81 to 0.99 (mean 0.88) in uterine GLI1 co-amplification tumors. To understand the biological influence of the mutations, we performed the KEGG pathway analyses of mutated genes of 4 patients. We found these aberrations mainly involve several enriched pathways including the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MicroRNAs in Cancer, Transcriptional misregulation and MAPK signaling pathway, as shown in Fig. 3B.

Gene amplification pattern at the 12q13.3-15 locus were examined in tumors with and without GLI1 co-amplification (Fig. 4A, B). GLI1 co-amplification cases generally showed a continuous copy number gain peak in the 12q13.3-15 region including STAT6 (12q13.3), GLI1(12q13.3), TSPAN31 (12q13.3), CDK4 (12q14.1), HMGA2 (12q14.3), IFNG (12q15) and MDM2(12q15), indicating probability of a single amplicon pattern of the entire 12q13.3-15. Non-GLI1 amplification cases showed copy number gain starting from CDK4 (12q14.1) to MDM2 (12q15). Moreover, recurrent chromosome arm-level alterations (Fig. 4C) were seen in GLI1 co-amplification cases, including amplifications in 12p (n = 3), 18p (n = 2) and deletions in 11p (n = 4), 13q (n = 3), 9p (n = 2), 10p (n = 2), 1p (n = 2).

Survival analysis

Follow-up was available in all 4 patients with a median follow-up of 37.5 months (3 to 112 months). All patients had recurrence and/or metastasis, in which 4/4 pelvic and abdominal recurrence/metastasis, 1/4 (Case 2) lung and liver metastasis. 3 patients were alive with disease and 1 patient died of disease (Case 3). Relapse-free survival (RFS) analysis showed similar median RFS between GLI1 co-amplified HGESS and GLI1 non-amplified HGESS groups, which were shorter than LGESS group (Fig. 5A).

Discussion

In this study, we identified a small series of 4 cases of uterine high-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma (HGESS) harboring GLI1 genetic abnormalities with co-amplification of MDM2/CDK4 gene. These primary cases were initially diagnosed ESS and 1 adenosarcoma (sarcoma component was HGESS), the recurrent tumors were misdiagnosed as DDLPS in 3 cases (Case 1,2 and 4) with findings of MDM2 amplification. Morphologically, these tumors were nested or lobulated arrangements, composed of ovoid to spindle cells and frequently with perivascular proliferations of tumor cells and rich capillary networks with variable immunoprofiles.

GLI1, located on chromosome 12q13.3, is within an oncogene-rich 12q13–15 region including CDK4, DDIT3, HMGA2, MDM2, STAT6, etc. Alterations of GLI1 have been described in a wide spectrum of benign and malignant tumors, including benign mesenchymal neoplasms, carcinomas, and sarcomas [2, 4, 21–23]. GLI1 gene fusions were first described in pericytoma with recurrent t (7;12) translocations [5], resulting in an ACTB-GLI1 gene fusion. Pericytoma with GLI1 fusions was typically composed of ovoid to spindle cells with distinctive perivascular proliferation of tumor cells around thin-walled arborizing blood vessels [4, 5], and was positive for SMA staining. MALAT1-GLI1 gene fusion was identified in gastric plexiform fibromyxoma [22, 23] and gastroblastoma [24]. Another evolving subset of neoplasms with GLI1 fusions was named “malignant epithelioid neoplasms with GLI1 gene rearrangements”, showing monomorphic epithelioid cells arranged in nests and cords, set in a myxoid and richly vascular stroma, and strongly positive for S100 [13, 25].

Besides, a group of malignant soft tissue tumors harboring GLI1 amplification was described with a distinctive nested growth pattern and variable immunoprofile [7]. In their study, tumors with GLI1 amplification were reported to share a similar morphology to the phenotype described in soft tissue tumors with GLI1 fusions, in addition they found GLI1 with co-amplification of CDK4 (12q14.1) in 9 (90%) and MDM2 (12q15) in 8 (80%) cases. Beside these emerging GLI1 molecular defined entities, one recent study discovered GLI1 co-amplification with MDM2/CDK4 in large-scale cohort of WD/DDLPS, and part of these tumors demonstrated recurrent histological features including meningothelial whorl-like and GLI-altered morphology in dedifferentiated areas [8]. Despite overlaps of clinicopathological features of GLI1-rearranged and GLI1-amplified tumors, a recent study performed a comparative investigation between the two groups [26], in which they found a worse overall survival, and a shorter median progression-free survival in GLI1-amplified patients. Moreover, GLI1-amplified tumors exhibit mostly a single amplicon at the 12q13-15 locus, compared with DDLPS, which showed a 2-peak amplification centered around CDK4 (12q14.1) and MDM2 (12q15).

Most of the previously reported GLI1 gene-altered tumors have arisen in soft tissue, stomach (the site of origin of plexiform fibromyxoma and gastroblastoma) [5, 22–24], or tongue [25]. To date, only 10 cases were reported originated from gynecological tract [3, 4, 15, 16, 18, 19](summarized in Table 4). The age at presentation of the reported cases ranged from 11 to 88 years (mean 46.1, medium 47). 5/10 cases located at ovary, 5/10 uterine and 1/10 cervix involved. Primary tumor size ranged from 7 to 21 cm (mean 11.22, medium 9.5). Necrosis was identified in 4/10 cases. Tumor cells were ovoid to spindled (5/10) or epithelioid (2/10) morphology arranged in a distinctive nested-trabecular (7/10) and macrofollicular pattern (1/10) with rich vascular network (8/10). Immunochemically, tumors were positive for CD10 (7/7 tested), CyclinD1 (4/4 tested), including focally or patchy for S-100 (5/8 tested). 2/10 GLI1 amplifications and 8/10 GLI1 fusions with various fusion partners (3 ACTB, 3 PTCH1, 1 MALAT1, 1 SRGN and 1 PAMR1) were identified. Nine cases demonstrated aggressive to malignant biological behavior with recurrent and/or metastasis, except 1 case with limited follow up data.

Mesenchymal tumors of the uterus comprise common entities including smooth muscle tumors and endometrial stromal tumors, as well as several miscellaneous mesenchymal tumors. Smooth muscle tumors can be identified by the morphology and positivity for smooth muscle markers (desmin, h-caldesmon). With regard to endometrial stromal tumors, besides the morphological and IHC findings, molecular studies can be applied in the diagnosis, for example, JAZF1-SUZ12 fusion identified in the low grade endometrial stromal tumors and YWHAE-NUTM2A/B, BCOR, BCORL1, KDM2B and other fusions in the high-grade endometrial stromal sarcomas [27, 28]. The miscellaneous mesenchymal tumors consist of a spectrum of various subtypes, requiring comprehensive histological and molecular studies in the differential diagnosis. All the present 4 uterine cases with GLI1 co-amplification were initially diagnosed as ESS. Further molecular studies especially in recurrent tumors demonstrated MDM2/CDK4 amplification, which mislead to the diagnosis of DDLPS in 3 cases (Case1, 2 and 4).

In the present study, all 4 tumors were positive for CD10 with frequently CyclinD1 expression, lack of definite smooth muscle markers and S-100 expression. BCOR fusion, a molecular signature of high-grade ESS, was identified in 2 cases. In HGESS, a subgroup of BCOR rearrangements were reported to be associated with recurrent CDK4 Pathway alterations and MDM2 amplifications [16, 29–32]. Particularly, the morphological features of ZC3H7B-BCOR HGESS were previously reported to be included collagen plaques, small arterioles without prominent perivascular tumor cell whirling architecture [29]. However, GLI1 status was not evaluated in these tumors. We are the first to describe GLI1 alteration coexists with BCOR fusions and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification. There are no collagen plaques in the present 2 cases of ZC3H7B-BCOR HGESS with GLI1 co-amplification, in which our 1 case showed “meningo-endothelial-like” appearance and perivascular whirling, which are morphologically different from the previous report. These complex molecular alterations lead to great diagnostic challenges with overlaps of molecular and clinicopathological features. Three possibilities might be considered when categorize these tumors: (1) DDLPS with co-amplifications of GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4; (2) ESS with GLI1 alterations; (3) a distinct pathologic entity. In the present series, the recurrent/metastatic tumors of 3 cases (Case 1, 2 and 4) were initially diagnosed as DDLPS based on FISH results of MDM2/CDK4 amplifications until carefully enquiring of ESS history and compare the morphology and immune phenotype of the primary and metastatic lesions. Liposarcoma of the lower genital tract is rare, and vulva and vagina are the common site of occurrence. Liposarcoma of the uterus may be extremely rare, if it exists. Besides, WDLPS areas were absent in all the cases, while no gene amplifications of 1p and 6q were identified as recurrently present in DDLPS [9, 11, 12]. It is possible in female patients, some GLI1 co-amplified tumors occurs in abdominopelvic cavity may originated from uterine primary diseases, which could be underestimated. In addition, our present GLI1 co-amplified cases generally showed a continuous copy number gain peak in the 12q13.3-15 region indicating probability of a single amplicon pattern of the entire 12q13.3-15 region similar to the reported study [26], which is different from DDLPS. It is possible that not only the amplified genes but also the amplification pattern that have important influence on tumor development and/or progression. Therefore, the current GLI1 co-amplified uterine sarcomas are not DDLPS based on morphology and genetics.

Other differential diagnosis included PEComa (perivascular epithelioid cell tumor), Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST), and GLI1-rearranged myopericytoma-like tumor. PEComa is composed of epithelioid or spindled cells with clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm arranged in nests or sheets around delicate vascular channels with expression of melanocytic (HMB-45, Melan-A) and myogenic (SMA, desmin) markers. MPNST is usually composed of spindle cells that originate from the peripheral nerve or exhibits differentiation of different components of the nerve sheath, which typically displays fascicular, marbled growth of hyperchromatic, wavy or buckled nuclei with frequent mitoses and geographic necrosis. Immunohistochemically, S-100 and SOX-10 are often focally/weakly positive in MPNST. GLI1-rearranged myopericytoma-like tumor usually arise in the head and neck region with indolent biological behavior [5, 14]. Morphologically the tumors are characterized by a richly vascular, nodular proliferation of ovoid-to-spindled cells with prominent haemangiopericytoma-like vessels with frequent SMA positivity. GLI1 FISH and/or NGS tests help to recognize characteristic GLI1 fusions of these tumors. The current neoplasms are distinct from the above-mentioned entities in morphology, IHC profiles and molecular changes.

Furthermore, 2 GLI1 co-amplified cases were morphologically nodular/nested, immunochemically positive for CD10 negative for smooth muscle markers and demonstrated no evidence of BCOR Internal Tandem Duplication, BCORL1, KDM2B or other gene rearrangements associated with HGESS through RNA and DNA NGS testing [27, 28]. Whether these tumors should be categorized as an entity as “high-grade uterine sarcoma with GLI1 alterations” still need to be elucidated. Due to the histological similarities, these 4 cases were tentatively categorized as ESS under the currently established classifications. In the future, whether GLI1 amplification as the driver mutation or secondary event should be further investigated in larger case series.

Hedgehog/GLI inhibitors are presently undergoing clinical trials for treatment of advanced solid tumors and hematologic malignancies [33, 34]. Therefore, the detection of oncogenic GLI1 alterations in these tumors may pave the way for targeted GLI1 inhibitors to be utilized, especially for patients with advanced diseases, offering a potentially effective therapeutic option.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective study from a single institution. Second, the sample size is small. This is mainly because the rarity of the neoplasms with GLI1 amplification. Confounding clinical variables cannot be adequately controlled in this small cohort. Therefore, the survival findings revealed in the study should be regarded as exploratory rather than conclusive.

In summary, we describe 4 new cases of uterine high-grade endometrial stromal sarcomas harboring GLI1 and MDM2/CDK4 co-amplification. These neoplastic cells demonstrated nodular/nested arrangement and frequently “meningo-endothelial-like” appearance, as well as CD10, CyclinD1 immunostaining. These tumors differ from DDLPS in location, lack of WDLPS area and molecular signatures. NGS testing for uterine mesenchymal tumors with nodular/nested and “meningo-endothelial-like” appearance morphology is necessary to provide diagnosis and therapeutic evidences.
