Introduction

The jejunum is the primary site of nutrient digestion and absorption. In the context of animal production, the efficiency of nutrient digestion and absorption heavily influences growth rates providing economic advantages to producers (Campbell et al., 2013; Patience et al., 2015). In addition, the jejunum is a part of the gastrointestinal tract, the largest immune organ in the body, that directly interacts with dietary and environmental antigens and toxins. The intestinal immune system is characterized by complex interactions between the native microbiota, intestinal epithelial cells, intestinal immune cells, and host signaling pathways that protect intestinal tissue and respond to potential threats (Burkey et al., 2009). Considering that the functional capacity of the jejunum related to both digestion and immune functions is a key determinant of production efficiency, maintaining intestinal integrity during the weaning transition is critical in modern pig production systems. However, when weaned at this early age, the intestine and its immune system are still immature (Moeser et al., 2017). As a result, nursery pigs are vulnerable to intestinal damage from weaning stress and pathogenic colonization causing inflammation and oxidative damages (Kim and Duarte, 2021).

To support the function of the jejunum post-weaning, it is critical to understand interactions among the microbiota, jejunal epithelium, and its immune system, and how these relationships are influenced by external factors. Along the gastrointestinal tract the luminal and mucosa-associated microbiota differ in composition based on their location and substrate availability (Kelly et al., 2017; Adhikari et al., 2019). The mucosa-associated microbiota is physically associated with intestinal epithelial and immune cells; therefore, the small intestine is particularly sensitive to alterations in the composition of the mucosa-associated microbiota (Duarte and Kim, 2022). Common weaning-associated stressors, like abrupt dietary changes, toxins, and pathogen exposure, disrupt these microbial populations (Holanda and Kim, 2022; Garavito-Duarte et al., 2025). Pattern recognition receptors of the innate immune system continuously sample the mucosa-associated microbiota and recognize commonly conserved bacterial motifs, modulating immune signaling in response (Kawai and Akira, 2010). Interactions with beneficial bacteria promote tolerance and anti-inflammatory signaling, whereas interactions with pathogenic bacteria trigger pro-inflammatory signaling cascades that activate the immune system (Wells et al., 2011). Excessive activation of these pathways can induce inflammation and oxidative stress, compromising epithelial integrity and increasing energy demands for tissue repair (Gormley et al., 2024; Lopez-Rincon et al., 2026). This diverts energy away from growth and increases susceptibility to opportunistic or pathogenic bacterial populations, ultimately reducing growth. Understanding how weaning-induced intestinal damage affects the functions of the small intestine is essential to improve intestinal health and productivity of nursery pigs (Figure 1).

Impacts of challenges associated with weaning on the jejunum of nursery pigs. Influences on the jejunal health and function have downstream effects on health and performance outcomes.

To address these challenges, this review summarized the research findings using a series of meta-analyses to quantitatively evaluate how immune and oxidative stress biomarkers in the jejunal mucosa relate to growth performance in nursery pigs. Specifically, the first goal of this summary was to characterize the intestinal immune status of nursery pigs to differentiate between normal and challenged states. The second goal was to determine the impact of specific intestinal health biomarkers on growth and other relevant production parameters.

Data Collection

The data from 38 studies involving 1,618 pigs were compiled to evaluate how intestinal health indicators relate to growth performance in nursery pigs. Across studies, pigs were managed under highly comparable conditions: pigs were individually housed, weaned at approximately 21 days-of-age and 6.5 kg body weight, and were sampled between 42 and 64 days of age. Diets were similar in composition and formulated to meet or exceed the requirements outlined in the Nutrient Requirements of Swine (NRC, 2012) and all studies followed consistent internal protocols for sample collection and laboratory analysis. All studies were conducted at North Carolina State University (Raleigh, NC, USA) and have been published in peer-reviewed journals (Table 1).

Data Management and Processing

Growth performance was evaluated using average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and gain-to-feed ratio (G:F) as dependent variables. These outcomes were examined in relation to key biomarkers including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-6 (IL-6), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin A (IgA), malondialdehyde (MDA) and protein carbonyl (PC), and villus height (VH), villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD), and Ki-67 positive cells (Ki-67+), as independent variables. Each analysis included the subset of studies with relevant data (Tables 2–4).

Intestinal Biomarkers Relative to Growth Response

Evaluating intestinal biomarkers independent of relevant production parameters limits their interpretation. These measurements become meaningful to the broader industry when they can be related to outcomes that influence the productivity of pig production, like growth performance. The production metrics of ADG, ADFI, and G:F are often used as practical indicators of overall health (Patience et al., 2015). Many immune and physiological processes will fluctuate within normal, tolerable ranges. However, determining the point at which these fluctuations will result in significant changes in performance would provide relevant threshold data that could be useful to both researchers and producers. A 5% reduction in the growth performance metrics (ADG, ADFI, G:F) was selected as a pragmatic threshold as it reflects a level of change that is sensitive enough to capture early physiological changes yet large enough to be consistently measurable and economically relevant in commercial pig production. Mucosal Pro-Inflammatory Cytokines and Growth Response

Tumor necrosis factor-α

Tumor necrosis factor-α is a pro-inflammatory cytokine produced primarily by macrophages and T cells. As a key signaling molecule, TNF-α promotes the recruitment of additional immune cells to the site of infection or tissue injury (Bradley, 2007). In nursery pigs, the stressors associated with weaning and the introduction of feedstuffs containing anti-nutritional compounds and allergenic proteins can induce marked shifts in the intestinal environment that can damage the jejunal epithelium and negatively impact growth performance (Holanda and Kim, 2022; Gormley et al., 2024). Concentrations of TNF-α concentrations in the jejunal mucosa are commonly used as a biomarker of intestinal inflammation (Kim and Duarte, 2021). However, basal production of TNF-α is a normal outcome of a functional innate immune system and can be tolerated based on the magnitude and duration of TNF-α production. Establishing a range for tolerable TNF-α concentrations, as well as defining the extent to which increases in TNF-α concentrations influence key performance metrics, would improve the interpretation of inflammatory responses in newly weaned pigs.

Data compiled from 31 studies involving 1,490 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa TNF-α concentration and ADG of nursery pigs (Figure 2a). Across the studies evaluated, ADG remained relatively stable at approximately 0.40 kg/d until TNF-α concentrations reached 0.38 pg/mg protein. Beyond this point, increasing TNF-α concentrations were associated with a linear decline in ADG. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG would correspond to an increase of TNF-α concentration from 0.38 to 1.38 pg/mg protein (263% increase).

Reductions in ADG associated with elevated TNF-α concentrations are unlikely to occur independently of other performance metrics. Immune activation and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, have been documented to suppress appetite, reduce voluntary feed intake, and repartitions nutrients away from growth toward the energetic demands of the immune response (Pastorelli et al., 2012). In nursery pigs, where stimulating voluntary feed intake is already challenging, increases in pro-inflammatory signaling may further exacerbate reductions in ADFI and other performance metrics. Data compiled from 29 studies involving 1,406 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa TNF-α concentration and ADFI in nursery pigs (Figure 2b). Increasing TNF-α concentrations exhibited a linear relationship with ADFI, however, a relatively large increase in TNF-α concentrations was required to elicit a 5% reduction in ADFI. Across the evaluated studies, an increase in TNF-α concentration by approximately 1.50 pg/mg protein was required to induce a 5% reduction in ADFI. Across these studies, the baseline ADFI was 0.61 kg/d, whereas an increase in TNF-α concentrations by 1.50 pg/mg would correspond to an ADFI of 0.58 kg/d. The gut–brain axis is significant in the context of examining intestinal inflammation. In the case of TNF-α, TNF-α has been shown to cause a potent anorexigenic effect in the hypothalamus, therefore, increasing concentrations of TNF-α in the intestine could also reduce feed intake as driven by the nervous system (Romanatto et al., 2007). Unsurprisingly, this pattern mirrors the relationship observed between TNF-α and ADG, which is expected given that gain is largely driven by intake.

In contrast to ADG and ADFI, G:F appeared to be comparatively less sensitive to increasing concentrations of TNF-α in the jejunal mucosa. Across the 29 evaluated studies involving 1,406 pigs, an increase in TNF-α concentration by approximately 1.70 pg/mg protein was required to induce a 5% reduction in G:F (Figure 2c). Across these studies, the baseline G:F was 0.68, whereas an increase in TNF-α concentrations by 1.70 pg/mg would correspond to a G:F of 0.65. This may reflect the relative stability of feed efficiency metrics, which is influenced by the strong relationship between ADG and ADFI. At higher levels of TNF-α, however, inflammation can increase overall maintenance requirements and diverts nutrients away from growth and toward immune functions, abatement of inflammation, and cellular repair (Lang et al., 2007), thereby reducing feed efficiency beyond what would be caused by a simple reduction in feed intake.

Interleukin-8

Interleukin-8 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine with strong chemoattractant properties and is produced by a wide range of cell types, including intestinal epithelial cells. Its expression is responsive to multiple inflammatory stimuli such as live bacteria, bacterial cell components, and the presence of other pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α (Hoffmann et al., 2002). In nursery pigs, intestinal development is incomplete and particularly sensitive to the physiological and environmental stressors associated with weaning. Weaning-associated physical damage to the intestinal epithelium can increase intestinal permeability, increasing interactions between the external environment and the host immune system, resulting in elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-8. As with TNF-α, excessive production of IL-8 drives persistent inflammation and immune cell recruitment, which is both damaging to tissue and energetically expensive (Hoffmann et al., 2002). Although basal levels of pro-inflammatory signaling are required to maintain immune surveillance and defend against minor insults, elevations beyond a tolerable threshold may negatively impact performance.

Data compiled from 17 studies involving 832 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa IL-8 concentration and ADG of nursery pigs (Figure 3a). Across the studies evaluated, ADG remained stable at approximately 0.39 kg/d until IL-8 concentrations reached 0.57 ng/mg protein. Beyond 0.57 ng/mg protein, increasing IL-8 concentrations were associated with a linear decline in ADG. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG would correspond to an increase of IL-8 concentration from 0.57 to 1.06 ng/mg protein (86% increase).

Interleukin-6

Interleukin-6 is a multifunctional pro-inflammatory cytokine that is involved in coordinating immune activation and the acute-phase response (Kopf et al., 1994). Unlike cytokines that primarily facilitate immune cell recruitment to the site of injury, IL-6 is a key systemic mediator that links local inflammatory events to broader physiological and metabolic responses. The production of IL-6 can be stimulated by epithelial injury, sensing of microbe-associated molecular patterns, or upstream cytokine signaling, making it a relevant indicator of intestinal inflammation during the post-weaning period (Jeffery et al., 2017). In addition to its role in immune regulation, IL-6 contributes to metabolic adaptations during inflammation, including nutrient partitioning and muscle protein turnover due to its influence on the endocrine system (Navarra et al., 1991). Therefore, measuring IL-6 may provide insight not only into intestinal immune activation, but also to systemic consequences of weaning stress. As with TNF-α and IL-8, increases in IL-6 concentrations are necessary to manage localized epithelial damage, whereas sustained elevation may contribute to impaired growth performance (Johnson, 1997).

Data compiled from 8 studies involving 408 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa IL-6 and ADG in nursery pigs (Figure 4a). Across these studies, ADG remained stable at 0.39 kg/d until IL-6 concentrations reached 4.68 pg/mg protein. Beyond this point, increasing IL-6 was associated with a linear reduction in ADG. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG would correspond to an increase of IL-6 concentration from 4.68 to 5.33 pg/mg protein (14% increase). This indicates that ADG is particularly sensitive to increases in IL-6 concentrations.

Mucosal Immunoglobulins and Growth Response

Immunoglobulin G

Immunoglobulins function to recognize and neutralize specific foreign antigens through a variety of mechanisms. In nursery pigs, the innate immune system provides the majority of early protection, as the adaptive immune system is still developing (Moeser et al., 2017). The first immunoglobulins are acquired from sow colostrum, and although endogenous immunoglobulin production typically begins at around 1 to 4 weeks-of-age, times vary considerably based on factors such as health status and colostrum and milk intake (Levast et al., 2014). Based on the age of the pigs used within the evaluated studies, it would be reasonable to assume that all pigs were within the window of active immunoglobulin production, and therefore, immunoglobulin concentrations could be evaluated in relation to performance metrics as indicators of overall health and immune competence. Specifically, immunoglobulin G is the most abundant antibody found in circulation and provides long-term, specific immunity through B cell memory, allowing for rapid production high-affinity IgG on repeat exposure to antigens (Spiegelberg, 1974).

Data compiled from 14 studies involving 692 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa IgG concentration and ADG of nursery pigs (Figure 5a). Across these studies, ADG remained relatively stable at 0.44 kg/d until IgG concentrations reached 0.13 µg/mg protein. For concentrations greater than 0.13 µg/mg protein, increasing IgG concentrations were associated with a linear decline in ADG. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG would correspond to an increase of IgG concentration from 0.13 to 2.33 µg/mg protein (1,693% increase).

Immunoglobulin a

Immunoglobulin A is the second most abundant class of immunoglobulins, after IgG. Unlike IgG, IgA is primarily localized to the mucosal membranes and bodily secretions, such as the respiratory and intestinal epithelium, as well as in saliva, mucus, and milk (Spiegelberg, 1974). The role of IgA is particularly relevant in the context of intestinal health, where IgA serves as the first line of humoral defense by limiting pathogen adherence and progression beyond the mucosal surface (Mantis et al., 2011). Although IgA shares antigen specificity and memory characteristics with IgG, its strategic location allows it to neutralize antigens before they breach the epithelial barrier.

Data from 13 studies involving 604 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa IgA and ADG of nursery pigs (Figure 6). The ADG declines with increasing IgA concentrations until 2.09 µg/mg protein. Beyond this point, ADG remained stable at 0.38 kg/d. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG prior to the plateau would correspond to an increase of IgA concentration from 1.42 to 2.09 µg/mg protein (30% increase). Beyond 2.09 µg/mg protein, increases in IgA concentrations did not influence ADG. Although increasing jejunal mucosa IgA concentrations were associated with reductions in ADG, no significant relationships were observed for ADFI of G:F. This suggests that the mucosal IgA responses may influence growth more through localized effects on intestinal function rather than through changes in voluntary feed intake or overall nutrient utilization.

Mucosal Oxidative Damage Products and Growth Response

Malondialdehyde

Oxidative stress occurs when there is an imbalance in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidants in the environment. Although ROS are a normal by-product of cellular metabolism, under certain conditions, their production can exceed the body’s capacity to neutralize them. In nursery pigs, weaning can trigger a systemic inflammatory response due to allergenic proteins and anti-nutritional compounds in feeds, disruptions to the native microbiota, potential colonization by opportunistic bacteria, and the immature immune and antioxidant systems, which have a reduced capacity to handle these stressors (Kim and Duarte, 2021). The accumulation of ROS within the intestinal environment can damage the epithelial tissue, reducing the efficiency of nutrient digestion and absorption, and increasing the energy required for tissue repair, which diverts energy away from growth. One method to evaluate the extent of oxidative stress is by measuring resulting oxidative damage products, such as MDA, a primary by-product of cell membrane peroxidation.

Data compiled from 29 studies involving 1,344 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa MDA concentration and ADG of nursery pigs (Figure 7a). Across the studies evaluated, ADG remained stable at approximately 0.40 kg/d until MDA concentrations reached 0.46 nmol/mg protein. Beyond 0.46 nmol/mg protein, increasing concentrations of MDA were associated with a linear reduction in ADG. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG would correspond to an increase of MDA concentration from 0.46 to 1.13 nmol/mg protein (146% increase).

Protein carbonyl

Like MDA, PC is a product of oxidative damage, formed when ROS introduces carbonyl groups into protein side chains. This modification can impair protein function and increase susceptibility to degradation. The accumulation of PC is generally considered as harmful, not only as a marker of oxidative stress, but also because it increases the energy required to compensate for the inefficiencies caused by protein dysfunction (Suzuki et al., 2010).

Data from 29 studies involving 1,322 pigs were compiled to evaluate the relationship between jejunal mucosa PC concentrations and ADG (Figure 8). No effects of the increasing concentrations of PC were observed for ADFI or G:F. It is possible that PC exerts a greater impact on lean tissue accretion than on voluntary feed intake or feed efficiency. The increase in protein turnover and modification in response to PC accumulation may explain the observed reductions in ADG due to reduced lean tissue deposition.

Jejunal Intestinal Morphology and Growth Response

Intestinal morphology refers to the structural characteristics of the intestine. Because the jejunum is the primary site of food digestion and nutrient absorption, its structure and physiological function are directly related with growth performance (Hedemann et al., 2003). Villi are finger-like projections extending into the intestinal lumen that increase surface area and house the epithelial cells responsible for nutrient absorption. As a result, VH is commonly used as an indicator of intestinal health and functional capacity, with significant implications for overall growth performance.

Data compiled from 29 studies involving 1,380 pigs were used to evaluate the relationship between VH and ADG of nursery pigs (Figure 9a). Across the studies evaluated, ADG remained stable at approximately 0.40 kg/d when jejunal VH was greater than or equal to 397 µm, with reductions in ADG being observed at a VH less than 385 µm. For example, a 5% reduction in ADG would correspond to a decrease in VH from 385 to 327 µm (15% decrease).

Crypt depth often increases in response to intestinal injury due to elevated epithelial cell proliferation, whereas the villi are susceptible to shortening or blunting at their tips (Hedemann et al., 2003). Consequently, a reduction in the VH:CD is typically interpreted as evidence of intestinal damage following stressors such as weaning or infection with pathogenic bacteria.

The ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells within the jejunal crypt is commonly used as an indicator of epithelial proliferation and epithelial turnover. The Ki-67 is a nuclear protein expressed during active phases of the cell cycle and is absent during the non-active phases, making it a reliable marker for epithelial cell proliferation (Kim et al., 2019; Holanda et al., 2020). Within the jejunal crypt, controlled epithelial cell proliferation is essential for maintaining villus structure and supporting the efficiency of nutrient absorption via continuous epithelial renewal. At the same time, excessive proliferative activity can sometimes reflect an adaptive response to epithelial injury or inflammation, increasing the energetic cost of tissue maintenance (MacDonald, 1992). Consequently, understanding the relationship between intestinal cell turnover and growth performance metrics would be advantageous for identifying physiological thresholds at which increased epithelial proliferation shifts from supporting intestinal maintenance to imposing energetic costs that compromise growth efficiency in nursery pigs.

The ADFI of pigs is similarly affected by increases in the ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells. Across the data compiled from 26 studies involving 1,183 pigs, ADFI remained stable at 0.56 kg/d until the ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells reached 0.21 (Figure 11b). Beyond 0.21, increases in the ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells were associated with a linear reduction in ADFI. For example, a 5% reduction in ADFI would correspond to an increase in the ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells from 0.21 to 0.27 (29% increase). Although there was no relationship between the ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells and G:F, these results demonstrate that although epithelial proliferation is necessary to support intestinal function, excessive increases in the ratio of Ki-67+ cells to total cells are associated with reductions in growth and voluntary feed intake, highlighting the threshold beyond where proliferative activity imposes a metabolic burden and compromises growth performance metrics in nursery pigs.

Implications and Conclusion

A meta-analysis of 34 studies, involving up to 1,490 pigs, revealed significant correlations between intestinal health biomarkers and growth performance in nursery pigs. This synthesis was made possible by the high degree of similarity in research conditions across the studies. A primary contribution of this summary is the establishment of baseline reference values for jejunal mucosa biomarkers, including pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8), oxidative damage markers (MDA, PC), and intestinal integrity indicators (VH, VH:CD, %Ki-67+), specifically for pigs aged 3 to 7 weeks with an ADG of 0.38 to 0.41 kg/d. These thresholds empower producers and researchers to accurately assess the health status of pig herds. Furthermore, the analysis identified IL-6 and PC as more sensitive indicators of impaired growth than other biomarkers, suggesting that medical or nutritional interventions could prioritize reducing these specific inflammatory and oxidative markers to restore intestinal integrity and thus growth performance.
