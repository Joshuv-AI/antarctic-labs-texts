Unpacking the Risk of Misinformation: A Communication‐Based Critique
Authors: Dominic Balog‐Way, Katherine McComas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA raft of authors argue that society is drowning in a sea of misinformation, disinformation, and fake news. Some claim we are living in a new world disorder, misinformation age, or post‐truth era, fueled in part by social media, influential podcasters, and emerging AI systems. We argue that the now dominant interpretation of the risk of misinformation has been undergirded by an oversimplified understanding of broader communication processes. Thinking of misinformation as a standalone risk object has distorted conceptions of messages, messengers, and audiences, as well as how the underlying problems associated with false and misleading information could and should be addressed. Our article unpacks and then constructively critiques the dominant interpretation of misinformation by examining the tendency to (i) define misinformation in isolation from communication, (ii) neglect messengers’ intentions, (iii) perceive audiences as susceptible misinformation recipients, and (iv) reduce communication to a one‐way process of misinforming. We conclude by arguing that a communication‐based approach, grounded in the agency of messengers and audiences, offers a more nuanced and holistic foundation for interpreting and addressing the complex challenges associated with false and misleading messages. This perspective encourages policymakers and researchers to approach communication in its full complexity, engage in multiway processes, draw on the existing interdisciplinary communication literature, and remain attentive to both the challenges and opportunities of today's evolving communication ecosystem.

Introduction

Misinformation, often defined broadly as any false or misleading information (Aven and Thekdi 2022), is not new (Posetti and Matthews 2018; Novaes and Ridder 2021). Although some trace the phenomenon to the dawn of language (Roozenbeek and van der Linden 2024), Posetti and Matthews (2018) provide extensive historical examples from Octavian's propaganda campaign against Marc Antony in Circa 44 Bce, to the great moon hoax of 1835, and more recent events like the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal. However, around the time of the 2016 US election and Brexit referendum, the term “misinformation” rose to prominence, reflecting at least three key developments.

First, public discourse around misinformation increased markedly. As shown in Figure 1, the terms “misinformation,” “disinformation,” and “fake news” witnessed a sharp rise in usage in 2016. This uptick corresponded with growing concerns that people have become misinformed on issues ranging from climate change and vaccine safety to the origins of COVID‐19. In 2021, US Surgeon General, Vivek Murthy, declared health misinformation a “serious threat to public health” (Murthy 2021). In 2022, the Director of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Rochelle Walensky, labeled vaccine misinformation “among the biggest threats” to public health (Edwards 2022). In 2023, former US President Barack Obama identified disinformation as a “threat to our democracy” (Obama 2023). Even more recently, former US President Joseph Biden Jr. warned shortly before leaving office that “Americans are being buried under an avalanche of misinformation and disinformation, enabling the abuse of power” (Davies 2025).

Second, as these examples also show, greater public discourse was accompanied by serious, widespread concerns over the risk that misinformation poses to public health, the environment, democracy, and the production of scientific knowledge (Wardle 2019; O'Connor and Weatherall 2019), among many other highly valued public goods. A 2019 World Risk poll (N = 154,195) reported that 57% of Internet users across 142 countries, and across all socioeconomic groups and ages, view false information online as a major concern (Lloyd's Register Foundation 2019). Similarly, a 2022 survey by The Pearson Institute and AP‐NORC (2021) found that 95% of US adults believe misinformation is a major (82%) or minor (13%) problem. This has been fueled by concerns over the transformative impacts of the latest technologies, especially the Internet, social media, and AI (Novaes and Ridder 2021).

Third, there has been a surge of research and policy aimed at combating false and misleading information. Interventions have concentrated on (i) moderating online content, for example, deleting messages deemed false, (ii) debunking false claims, for example, highlighting why some claims are misleading, and (iii) prebunking, for example, preemptively explaining how manipulative tactics work (Basol et al. 2020; Roozenbeek et al. 2020; Kozyreva et al. 2023). Notably, William McGuire's (1961a, 1961b) inoculation theory has received renewed attention among researchers seeking to address the spread of misinformation relating to climate change (van der Linden et al. 2017), vaccinations (Wong 2015), and other scientific topics (Compton et al. 2021).

In this article, we argue that the dominant interpretation of the risk of misinformation that emerged since 2016 has been undergirded by an oversimplified understanding of broader communication processes. The intense focus on misinformation as a standalone risk object has contributed to a tendency to downplay, overlook, and ignore the intentions of messengers, responses of audiences, and complexities of multiway communication. Although our goal is not to negate the centuries old problems associated with false and misleading messages, we contend that the dominant way misinformation has been interpreted since 2016 has distorted our understanding of the underlying problem and limited the scope of potential solutions. We begin by unpacking the dominant interpretation using Boholm and Corvellec's (2011) relational theory of risk as an orienting framework (Section 2). We then constructively critique the interpretation by examining the tendency to (i) define misinformation in isolation from communication (Section 3), (ii) neglect the intentions of messengers (Section 4), (iii) perceive audiences as susceptible misinformation recipients (Section 5), and (iv) reduce communication to a one‐way process of misinforming (Section 6). We conclude by arguing why a communication‐based approach, grounded in the agency of messengers and audiences, offers a more nuanced and holistic foundation for interpreting and addressing an important set of issues categorized under the crude misinformation catchall (Section 7) (Cho et al. 2014; Bostrom et al. 2018; Fischhoff and Scheufele 2014, 2019; Balog‐Way et al. 2020).

Unpacking the Risk of Misinformation

Our perspective draws on the relational theory of risk (Boholm and Corvellec 2011, 2014), an interpretive theory that aims to answer how and why any given activity, hazard, or behavior is considered a risk. We chose the relational theory of risk because it provides a conceptually robust, interdisciplinary framework for analyzing how the concept of “misinformation” has been constructed and interpreted as a risk object since 2016, which is the central focus of this article.

Boholm and Corvellec (2011) argue that risk can be understood as a social phenomenon. Risk emerges when an observer—such as an individual, group, or organization—establishes a relationship between a “risk object” and an “object at risk” so that the risk object is considered to threaten the valued object at risk (Hilgartner 1992; Boholm and Corvellec 2011). A risk object can be any kind of artifact deemed to pose a risk, whether physical (e.g., lightning), cultural (e.g., national ideology), or social (e.g., drinking alcohol) (Boholm and Corvellec 2011). An object at risk is something to which value is ascribed and considered at stake, such as life, health, nature, or money (Boholm and Corvellec 2011). A relationship of risk refers to the relationship an observer establishes between a risk object and object at risk whereby the former is understood to threaten the value of the latter (Boholm and Corvellec 2011).

These three components can be applied to the dominant interpretation of misinformation: i. Risk object. Misinformation has increasingly been established as a centrally important and isolated risk object that needs to be addressed (Section 1). Aven and Thekdi (2022, 1272), for instance, comment that “misinformation [risk object] is seen as a threat to evidence‐based communication and decision‐making.” Krause et al. (2020, 1053) argue that “misinformation should be viewed as a risk [object] in its own right” [emphasis added].ii. Objects at risk. A wide array of valued objects at risk have been deemed at stake from misinformation, including elections (Bovet and Makse 2019), public health (Wong 2015), the environment (van der Linden et al. 2017), and evidence‐based communication (Aven and Thekdi 2022). In 2023, for example, organizers of the National Academy of Sciences (2023) Nobel Prize Summit identified trust in science as the valued object at risk: “Misinformation [risk object] is eroding our trust in science [object at risk] and runs the risk of becoming one of the greatest threats to our society today.”iii. Beyond deconstructing risk objects, objects at risk, and relationships of risk, another central tenet of the relational theory of risk is the proposition that risk emerges from situated cognition (see Boholm and Corvellec 2011 for a discussion). Rather than trying to answer the ontological question, “What is the risk of misinformation?” (e.g., Aven and Thekdi 2022; Hansson et al. 2021), the concept of situated cognition draws our attention to the epistemological question, “How do different observers like scientists, the public, and journalists differently understand the risk of misinformation?” (e.g., Osman et al. 2022). The upshot is that the theory provides a framework for understanding and comparing differing perspectives on what is ostensibly the same risk issue, not to automatically value one perspective over another.

The relational theory of risk allows us to trace how misinformation was reinterpreted as a serious risk object that many influential observers increasingly causally linked to different valued objects at risk. Although false and misleading information has existed for centuries (Posetti and Matthews 2018; Novaes and Ridder 2021), in 2016, misinformation was widely (i) established as an influential risk object that needs to be taken more seriously and (ii) progressively linked to an increasing number of valued objects at risk (cf. Hilgartner 1992). An illustration of this reinterpretation can be found in Deborah Lupton's book Risk (2023: 9–10), where “misinformation risks” were added as a major category of contemporary global concerns, listed alongside environmental, health and lifestyle, medical, interpersonal, economic, criminal, and political risks. “Misinformation risks” was notably absent from the previous edition that was published just a decade earlier (Lupton 2013, 22).

This dominant interpretation has not gone unchallenged. In the academic community, debate continues over how to interpret the risk of misinformation (Osman et al. 2022; Aven and Thekdi 2022; Williams 2023a, 2024; Altay et al. 2023). Some argue that misinformation concerns reflect an alarmist narrative or moral panic that persists despite evidence its prevalence and impact are overstated (Sperber et al. 2010; Altay et al. 2023; Williams 2024). These authors have questioned the strength of the relationship between the spread of misinformation online as a risk object and the “real” threat to the public and other objects at risk. Altay et al. (2023), for example, argue that the Internet is not rife with misinformation, people do not believe everything they see, and the influence of misinformation on behavior is “overblown.” In a large representative survey (N = 4407) from four countries (Russia, Turkey, the United Kingdom, the United States), Osman et al. (2022) found that the public often interpret the risk of misinformation differently from scientists. Although scientists tend to distinguish between the unintentional and intentional spread of false and misleading information, members of the public in Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States tend to interpret misinformation as intentional only.

Other observers have questioned whether misinformation should be established as the central risk object. Williams (2024), for instance, argues that misinformation is a symptom of a “deeper public malaise” and that concentrating on false and misleading information in isolation is a distraction. Further still, some observers have been concerned not by misinformation as the risk object but by the surge of new approaches designed to combat misinformation (Kozyreva et al. 2023). A prominent argument is that interventions like content moderation should be considered the “real” risk object, not misinformation, as they pose a serious threat to freedom of speech through censorship (Kozyreva et al. 2023).

Taken together, these perspectives highlight how the way different observers interpret the risk of misinformation fundamentally shapes thinking about the nature of the underlying problem and what could and should be done (Boholm 2009; Boholm and Corvellec 2011, 2014; Nisbet 2015). These debates matter because, as Paul Slovic (1999, 689) argues, “Whoever controls the definition of risk controls the rational solution to the problem at hand. If [the] risk [of misinformation] is defined one way, then one option will rise to the top as the most cost‐effective or the safest or the best. […]. Defining risk is thus an exercise in power.”

Drawing on the relational theory of risk as an orienting framework, the next section examines the challenges of defining misinformation as a standalone risk object detached from broader communication contexts. By focusing on the interplay between three core communication features—message truth/falsity, messenger intentions, and audience responses—we argue that a communication‐based approach, grounded in the agency of messengers and audiences, provides a more nuanced and holistic foundation for interpreting and addressing the complexities of false and misleading information.

Defining Misinformation

One challenge with the dominant interpretation centers on defining misinformation as a standalone risk object. Although some authors use the term interchangeably with “false information,” others define misinformation as “any false or misleading information” (e.g., Aven and Thekdi 2022). A limitation with these definitions is that they treat misinformation as a ubiquitous catchall term that encompasses a vast array of interrelated but distinct concepts (Williams 2023a). Such broad definitions make no distinction between lies, propaganda, brainwashing, hoaxes, fictional novels, inaccuracies, false connections, false contexts, manipulated content, misstatements, misreports, and so forth. Both fictional novels by Charles Dickens and unintentionally false statements about the COVID‐19 vaccine would fall under the same catchall category as outright lies. The problem is that overly broad definitions lack conceptual precision, undermining our ability to assess intent, evaluate harm, and develop proportional responses.

To move beyond these issues, several authors have sought to unpack the underlying dimensions of misinformation and related concepts (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017; Mayorga et al. 2020). By combining the dimensions of accuracy (e.g., degree of falseness) and intentions (e.g., to deceive, confuse, and harm), a variety of types have been created. A popular approach distinguishes between (i) misinformation as “false information being shared without intent to harm (or deceive)”; (ii) mal‐information as “accurate information taken out of its original context and manipulated with an intent to harm (or deceive)”; and (iii) disinformation as “information that is knowingly false and shared with intent to harm (or deceive)” (Mayorga et al. 2020, 198; Wardle and Derakhshan 2017). These oft‐cited distinctions have been widely adopted across academic, journalistic, and policy contexts.

A shortcoming with these definitions, however, is that they are often applied in ways that neglect the broader communication contexts in which false and misleading information (messages) are conveyed or shared (communicated). Although the original authors are clear to underscore the central role of communication (e.g., Wardle and Derakhshan 2017), in practice, the definitions are frequently taken out of communication contexts, leading many researchers, journalists, and policymakers to unintentionally downplay, overlook, or even ignore core features of communication (Wardle 2023), and that communicating and informing are types of action and interaction (Manson and O'Neill 2007). This has contributed to a broader tendency to sideline both messengers and audiences, reducing complex communication processes to the binary of information truth or falsity, or what Manson and O'Neill (2007, 27) refer to as “the drift from agency.” Wardle (2023), for instance, has argued that “we researchers have become so obsessed with labeling the dots that we can't see the larger pattern they show.”

We posit an alternative starting point that recenters our attention on communication and the agency of messengers and audiences. We first distinguish clearly between three core features of any communication process: message falsity/truth, messenger intentions, and audience responses.1 Rather than beginning by defining misinformation as a standalone risk object, this reflects a communication‐ and agency‐based starting point for interpreting and contextualizing the problem of false and misleading information. We do not think that it would be feasible to examine all relevant features of communication; however, this article discusses these three because of their particular importance for understanding contemporary interpretations of misinformation.

Message falsity/truth is a quality or characteristic of verbal, written, or recorded communication that refers to the degree of false and/or true content contained in messages (Mayorga et al. 2020). Closely related are the concepts of (i) accuracy, where messages can fall on a continuum from more to less accurate (unipolar) rather than simply being false or true (bipolar), and (ii) validity where a scientific model may be valid, meaning that it logically follows from its premise or methods, yet still produce inaccurate predictions due to, for example, simplified assumptions or methodological coarseness.

Moreover, the term “messages” is commonly used over “information” in the communication literature to emphasize how messages are crafted, framed, and conveyed by messengers in a multiway process of exchange back and forth between interlocutors. In contrast, “information” tends to obscure the role and intentions of messengers and can reinforce a distorted conception of messages as part of a one‐way process of sending or spreading (mis)information (Section 6) (see Manson and O'Neill 2007, 34–49 for a discussion). Determining whether a message is false or true is not necessarily easy and may be disagreed upon by different actors over time. As Aven and Thekdi (2022, 1273) note, “…even highly trained scientists may disagree on the validity (or truth) of certain findings.” However, recognizing message falsity/truth as separate from the agency of messengers (e.g., intentions) and audiences (e.g., responses to messages) is essential, as it redirects our attention to long‐debated questions about how to distinguish truth from falsity and justified belief from opinion (a major topic in epistemology).

Messenger intentions refer to the implicit and explicit goals of the actors, that is, individuals, groups, and organizations, exchanging messages. Only messengers have intentions, for example, to deceive, amuse, confuse, or inform. Defining information, an inanimate object and conceptual entity, as having an intention or agency makes little sense (Section 4). For example, a messenger might share a false message with or without an intent to harm, but the information cannot have an intent. In practice, knowing a messenger's intentions may be difficult, and messengers are rarely entirely deceitful or truthful (Aven and Thekdi 2022). However, recognizing messenger intentions as separate from the falsity/truth of messages raises important ethical questions about how messengers ought to communicate and express themselves responsibly, including whether intentionally conveying false or misleading messages is ever morally justifiable (a major topic in moral philosophy) (Bok 1978, 1998; Manson and O'Neill 2007; Keohane et al. 2014).

These two core communication features can be usefully compared to create new categories that begin with the agency of messengers, rather concentrating intensely on different types of false and misleading information. Figure 2 shows a two‐by‐two matrix that combines whether messengers deceitfully or truthfully (moral domain) share false or true messages (epistemological domain) (Bok 1998). The matrix draws on the fundamental categorical distinction between the moral and epistemological domains, emphasized by philosophers such as Onora O'Neill and Sissela Bok.

Two‐by‐two matrix combining risk communication messengers’ deceitful or truthful intentions (moral domain) with the falsity or truth of their messages (epistemological domain) to create four categories.

Deceitful messengers may knowingly share false messages by lying outright (top‐left quadrant). For example, in an extensive court opinion, US District Judge Gladys Kessler documented how the tobacco industry lied for decades about the harmful effects of cigarettes (Smith et al. 2011). In other words, tobacco companies were found to knowingly share false messages (Smith et al. 2011). Alternatively, deceitful messengers may share true messages that intentionally mislead in other ways, such as by taking accurate statements out of context (i.e., quoting out of context); appealing to irrelevant, unqualified, or unidentified sources in support of a claim (i.e., false attribution); or withholding information that is material to decision‐making (i.e., non‐disclosure) (bottom‐left quadrant) (Bok 1998; Fischhoff 2011). For example, the pharmaceutical company, Merck, was accused of withholding or “failing to disclose” critical information about the drug Vioxx, including negative clinical trial data that highlighted an increased risk of cardiovascular events (Löfstedt 2007). These categories delineate between messengers who intend to deceive by conveying false messages and messengers who intend to deceive by other means (Lewandowsky et al. 2024).

In contrast, truthful messengers may unintentionally share false messages believing they are true such as if they are mistaken, uninformed, or inarticulate (top‐right quadrant). For example, truthful messengers may unintentionally oversimplify scientific results to the point of distortion, misrepresent key findings, omit critical context, or convey uncertainties poorly (see Aven and Thekdi 2022, 1275 for further examples). Finally, truthful messengers may share true messages intentionally by giving veracious2 accounts (bottom‐right quadrant) (Bok 1998). This final category emphasizes the obligations of truthful messengers not simply to avoid lying or deceiving audiences intentionally or unintentionally but to be as forthcoming and sincere as possible. “[A] scrupulously truthful person,” as Bok (1998) puts it, “is one who takes special care to try to counteract factors known to skew or distort communication.” Hence, truthful messengers that seek to convey true messages have important epistemic and ethical obligations required for communicating effectively (see Manson and O'Neill 2008, 50–67).

These four categories deliberately accentuate the important distinction between messengers, who can have deceitful or truthful intentions, and the messages they share, which can contain false and/or true information. They seek to recenter our focus on the agency of messengers, rather than attempting to add agency to (mis)information. However, distinguishing between the intentions of messengers and the falsity/truth of their messages does not tell us about the actual responses of audiences, a third core feature of communication. Audience responses refer to the impacts and consequences of false and misleading messages on message recipients, including any potential changes to their beliefs or behavior (Balog‐Way et al. 2020). A deceitful messenger that intends to harm an audience by conveying false messages will not necessarily succeed. Similarly, a truthful messenger that intends to inform an audience accurately may be unsuccessful. There are, of course, many reasons why a risk message may not be believed, understood, or accepted (Balog‐Way et al. 2020), and extensive empirical research has investigated the effects of false and misleading messages on audiences (Section 5) (Southwell et al. 2019; Besley and Dudo 2023; Altay et al. 2023).

By distinguishing clearly between message falsity/truth, messenger intentions, and audience responses, a communication‐based approach shifts our attention away from misinformation as a standalone risk object toward embracing the broader communication contexts and agency of messengers and audiences. The next section further explores the complexities of messenger intentions, emphasizing their essential role in shaping how messages are created, conveyed, and interpreted.

Messenger Intentions

When misinformation is interpreted as a standalone risk object, the agency of messengers and their intentions can be hidden or obscured altogether. This can unintentionally marginalize messengers’ essential role in the meaning‐making process. Information is not simply “passed on,” “spread,” or “transmitted” by senders to receivers who can readily make true or false judgments independently of the individuals, groups, and organizations that communicated messages (Manson and O'Neill 2007). Rather, communication occurs within a situational, historical, cultural, social, and political meaning‐making context (Boholm 2009; Boholm and Corvellec 2011, 2014), where audiences’ responses to messages are strongly shaped by their perceptions of and multiway interactions with messengers (Earle 2010; Besley and McComas 2014; Siegrist 2021). For example, decades of risk communication research show that the perceived trustworthiness of messengers, also known as source credibility, has a particularly strong influence on the extent to which audiences believe that messages are true, false, honest, fair, and reliable (Earle 2010; Besley and McComas 2014; Siegrist 2021). If messengers are perceived as deceitful or incompetent, their messages—whether true or false—are less likely to be believed.

Beyond obscuring the role of messengers, another tendency within the literature has been to implicitly concentrate on deceitful actors spreading misinformation (Figure 2, left quadrants) (Lewandowsky et al. 2024). Recent studies have provided important insights into how manipulative tactics such as fearmongering, impersonation, polarization, trolling, and conspiracy narratives can influence audience beliefs and behaviors (van der Linden 2023). These investigations offer valuable theoretical clarity, particularly by exploring cases that illuminate how misinformation operates under extreme conditions and informing strategies for helping audiences to avoid being duped by deceitful messengers such as prebunking (Compton et al. 2021). However, when these vivid examples are not sufficiently contextualized within a broader ecosystem of more mundane or ambiguous misinformation dynamics, there is a risk that the field becomes anchored to dramatic or emotionally salient portrayals. This includes people ingesting bleach to “cure” COVID‐19 (Reimann 2021), the demonstrably false belief that Planet Earth might be flat, and the extraordinary QAnon conspiracy theory that powerful actors in society are controlled by a group of Satan‐worshiping pedophiles and sex‐traffickers (van der Linden 2023). Such cases, while empirically important and revealing, can unintentionally fuel an availability bias (Tversky and Kahneman 1974), leading us to overestimate the prevalence and influence of deceitful messengers using manipulative message tactics. As Altay et al. (2023) argue, many of these extreme beliefs are held by only a small minority. The challenge, therefore, is to ensure that the field integrates this work into a representative and nuanced understanding of broader communication process dynamics.

A third issue arises when attention is diverted away from truthful messengers who may unintentionally convey false or misleading messages (Figure 2, top‐right quadrant). Although many truthful messengers share uncontroversial and veracious messages, some may unknowingly share false messages believing they are true, especially if they are mistaken, uninformed, or inarticulate (Bok 1978). Consider the 2009 L'Aquila, Italy earthquake (6.3 MW) where six Italian scientists and an ex‐government official were jailed for involuntary manslaughter after predicting, only days before, that there was “no danger” and it was “highly unlikely” an earthquake would occur (Johnston 2012). The scientists were truthful when giving their earthquake predictions; yet their messages later turned out to be false because they poorly conveyed uncertainty through “inexact, incomplete, and contradictory” messages that were “falsely reassuring,” according to prosecutors (Johnston 2012). In other words, the messengers believed that they were truthfully conveying true messages (Figure 2, bottom right quadrant) but were actually truthfully conveying false messages (Figure 2, top‐right quadrant).

This exemplifies how low‐quality or ineffective communication can be a critical contributor to “misinformation” by producing unintended, unwanted effects such as misinforming audiences (Salmon et al. 2014; Aven and Thekdi 2022). The L'Aquila example also highlights why concentrating intensely on misinformation as a standalone risk object has the potential to deflect attention away from other valuable approaches, such as efforts to improve the effectiveness of how messengers, like scientists or risk regulators, communicate uncertainty. Clearly a different approach is needed for improving responsible risk communication compared with responding to deliberately fabricated content intended to deceive audiences.

Truthful messengers also may communicate messages not to spread misinformation but because they disagree in good faith with even widely held truth claims. The long history of science has witnessed countless public debates and conflicts over the falsity and truth of scientific claims, including contemporary risk controversies over vaccines, nuclear power, future energy systems, climate geoengineering, and genetically modified organisms (McComas 2006; Balog‐Way et al. 2020; Pidgeon 2021). Take the recent scientific debate on the origins of COVID‐19. Only a few years ago, people were labeled conspiracy theorists for even suggesting that COVID‐19 may have originated in the Wuhan Institute of Virology, China. Yet, those once conspiratorial claims are being seriously discussed by scientists and national security officials (Cohen 2023). These examples alone highlight how responsible multiway risk communication among truthful interlocutors is essential, especially for reexamining and reaffirming claims of truth and falsity and meaningfully engaging public audiences in an open exchange about risk issues that affect them and their communities. Though these scenarios may lack the sensationalism of vivid, deceitful messengers spreading demonstrably false messages, they are no less critical for understanding and addressing misinformation within the broader communication ecosystem.

Audience Responses

Unlike messenger intentions, audiences have received substantial attention in the dominant interpretation of misinformation. Different observers like policymakers, academics, and journalists, however, have conceptualized audiences in different ways, which we broadly categorize as (i) hazard‐based approaches, (ii) impact‐based approaches, and (iii) context‐based approaches. Each approach rests on different assumptions and carries distinct implications for how the risk of misinformation is interpreted and addressed.

Hazard‐Based Approaches

Hazard‐based approaches concentrate on measuring the prevalence or spread of misinformation as a risk object or “hazard.” Studies typically stop short of evaluating the effects of misinformation on audiences, such as changes to beliefs or behaviors, treating the existence or circulation of false or misleading information as a proxy for harm. In an oft‐cited analysis of ∼136,000 stories distributed on Twitter (now X) from 2006 to 2017, Vosoughi et al. (2018) found that false versus true information spread significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly, especially when information was perceived as novel and evoked fear, disgust, and/or surprise. Although the authors were sure not to equate the spread of misinformation with harm, the study, published in Science, received global media coverage that was criticized for “quickly overgeneralizing” the authors’ findings by implying that the speed and reach of misinformation alone meant the public was highly misinformed (Altay et al. 2023, 4).

Numerous studies have since focused on measuring the prevalence and spread of misinformation online (Bovet and Makse 2019). In a systematic review of 69 studies on health misinformation, Suarez‐Lledo and Alvarez‐Galvez (2021) found that misinformation was most prevalent for smoking and illegal drugs. In a systematic review of 101 studies on COVID‐19 misinformation, Zhao et al. (2023) found that conspiracy beliefs and safety concerns were the most prevalent misinformation issues. These studies function like the initial hazard identification stage in human and ecological risk assessment (Löfstedt 2011). They detect potential sources of harm (i.e., false or misleading information) but stop short at conducting systematic risk assessments that include dose‐response assessment (e.g., What is the relationship between how much misinformation people consume and harm?), exposure assessment (e.g., How much, how often, and how long are people exposed to misinformation?), and risk characterization (e.g., What is the estimated risk of harm to people, and how certain is this estimate?).

The prevalence and spread of misinformation should not be conflated with actual harm to audiences. The empirical communication literature has consistently shown that audiences are not uncritical (mis)information recipients (Rains et al. 2015; Southwell et al. 2019; Altay et al. 2023). Individuals are not simply exposed to misinformation and then readily believe what they read and hear (Sperber et al. 2010; Lynch 2012). Although some may be more susceptible than others (Pennycook and Rand 2020), audiences are better understood as active, critical, and reflective (Southwell et al. 2019; Altay et al. 2023). In a five‐country survey, Roozenbeek et al. (2020) found that beliefs about COVID‐19 misinformation were “not particularly common” with most public participants finding false claims unreliable or not credible. This is neither to suggest that irresponsible messengers cannot successfully manipulate public opinion by knowingly sharing false or misleading messages (van der Linden 2023) nor that the prevalence of misinformation online is trivial (Wardle 2019; O'Connor and Weatherall 2019; Novaes and Ridder 2021; Lewandowsky et al. 2024). Rather, as Southwell et al. (2019, 283) put it, “The existence of misinformation is not the same as a large number of people being misinformed.”

Impact‐Based Approaches

Impact‐based approaches shift the focus from prevalence to the effects of misinformation on audiences (Pennycook and Rand 2021; Zhou and Shen 2022; Nan et al. 2022; Ecker et al. 2022; van der Linden 2023). This includes studies investigating the cognitive and social mechanisms that result in individuals holding false beliefs or being manipulated by deceitful messengers. In other words, impact‐based approaches investigate the causal relationship between the presence of misinformation (risk object) and the actual harm on audiences (objects at risk). Some of the central questions include: How do false or misleading messages influence beliefs and behavior? What type of audience is more susceptible to misinformation, and why? How can individuals be protected or defended from the negative effects of misinformation?

Impact‐based studies have been prominent in psychology and cognitive science, generating substantial research in a short time (Pennycook and Rand 2021; Zhou and Shen 2022; Nan et al. 2022; Ecker et al. 2022). Research has particularly examined the cognitive mechanisms underlying why some individuals may be more susceptible to false and misleading messages (e.g., Pennycook and Rand 2020), especially how manipulative tactics can cause individuals to be deceived (e.g., van der Linden 2023). This includes the influence of motivated reasoning, where group identities can lead to the dismissal of facts that contradict group norms; emotional appeals, where fear, anger, or surprise, for instance, can strongly influence audience responses; and cognitive processes like confirmation bias, where individuals seek out, interpret, and remember information in ways that confirm their existing beliefs or values (see van der Linden 2023 for a discussion). Notably, a troubling concern is that confirmation bias can entrench existing beliefs and undermine the possibility of establishing a shared reality, making it especially difficult for groups with differing values or identities to agree on what constitutes reliable knowledge (Jost et al. 2018). In turn, impact‐based approaches have informed a range of interventions designed to lessen the impacts of misinformation, especially by correcting false claims or reinforcing cognitive defenses through prebunking interventions (van der Linden et al. 2017; Wong 2015; Basol et al. 2020; Roozenbeek et al. 2020; Compton et al. 2021).

Context‐Based Approaches

Context‐based approaches have received much less attention but offer a distinctive starting point. Rather than beginning by concentrating on misinformation as a risk object (or hazard) to mitigate, they start with the broader communicative, social, and institutional ecosystems in which misinformation is encountered and interpreted by audiences in situ (boyd 2017; Marwick 2018; Donovan and boyd 2021; Wardle 2023). This approach focuses on people's lived‐in world of livelihood, engagement, values, experiences, histories, and collective identities (see Marwick 2018 for a discussion). From this perspective, the meaning and impact of misinformation are understood as context‐dependent, shaped by situational and relational factors like trust, identity, values, and the surrounding media environment (Boholm and Corvellec 2011; Marwick 2018).

Key questions in the context‐based approach include: What kinds of messages do people encounter and engage with in their daily lives? Who do they trust for credible information, and why? What social, cultural, and institutional factors shape their interpretations of competing claims? Starting from the perspectives of audiences, some authors have estimated, for instance, that misinformation comprises just 0.15% of the average American's daily media consumption and that reliable sources attract far more attention than unreliable sources (Altay et al. 2023). Most online behavior involves entertainment, work, or socializing, suggesting that widespread exposure to misinformation may be lower than hazard‐ and impact‐based studies might imply (Altay et al. 2023). These approaches often emphasize that we can only understand false and misleading beliefs in the context of wider media and online ecosystems (Marwick 2018).

Context‐based approaches therefore shift our attention away from the intense focus on misinformation‐as‐a‐risk‐object and the effects of false and misleading information on audiences toward broader patterns of situated cognition, engagement, and meaning‐making. By foregrounding audience agency, these approaches emphasize how people actively navigate, interpret, and respond to the evolving communication environment (boyd 2017; Wardle 2023). Rather than trying to eliminate harmful content or debunk false claims, context‐based approaches seek to first understand, inter alia, how audiences choose between trustworthy and untrustworthy sources, how platforms shape online environments and exposure to true and false messages, and what conditions support meaningful engagement. Context‐based approaches tend to adopt a more relational and agency‐centered understanding of risk, in which audiences do not simply receive harmful information but also co‐construct meaning within a complex communication ecosystem.

Communication Processes

Many misinformation interventions are undergirded by linear models that conceptualize communication as a unidirectional flow or transfer of information from senders to receivers. Content moderation aims to prevent harmful information from reaching recipients by removing messages from circulation. Debunking aims to reshape how receivers interpret messages by explaining why they are false or misleading. Prebunking has been inspired by McGuire's (1961a, 1961b) inoculation theory, which likens misinformation to a virus spreading and infecting people with false beliefs. First proposed in the wake of McCarthy‐era fears about Communist brainwashing (Williams 2023b), the theory contends that people can be “inoculated” against misinformation through cognitive “injections” that strengthen their judgment and make them less susceptible to manipulation (Compton et al. 2021). Although these interventions have merit, they are constrained by an overreliance on this outdated view of communication.

Linear conceptualizations of communication proliferated during the 20th century (Shannon and Weaver 1948; Reddy 1979; Gregory and Lock 2008). The virus metaphor, for example, is comparable to Shannon and Weaver's (1948) transmission model that conceptualizes communication as the transmission of an electric signal between senders and receivers, and Reddy's (1979) seminal critique of the conduit metaphor that conceptualizes communication as the flow of water between containers via a conduit. A related and similarly flawed conceptualization is the deficit model (Gregory and Lock 2008; Simis et al. 2016; Reincke et al. 2020). Public perceptions, according to the model, are understood to diverge from the expert or scientific consensus due to cognitive deficits stemming from ignorance, irrationality, innumeracy, or illiteracy. Deficits are erroneously viewed as the main or only reason why nonexpert views diverge from those of the experts. In much the same way, the dominant interpretation of misinformation assumes that exposing people to false and misleading information causes their views to diverge from expert and scientific “truths,” and members of the public are viewed as easily manipulable and deficient in accurate knowledge. Both the dominant interpretation of misinformation and the deficit model assume that the underlying problems stem primarily from people lacking accurate knowledge and/or critical thinking skills (Section 5).

Communication scholars have extensively critiqued these one‐way models for oversimplifying communication as a unidirectional flow of information between senders and receivers (Reddy 1979; Sturgis and Allum 2004; Gregory and Lock 2008; Simis et al. 2016; Reincke et al. 2020). One‐way models also tend to neglect critical dimensions of context and audience interpretation, interactive and iterative multiway exchanges, and that meaning is constructed through dialogue, not mere information transfer. The deficit model, specifically, overlooks how knowledge is only one factor that shapes how we think, feel about, and act on many risk issues (Sturgis and Allum 2004; Gregory and Lock 2008; Simis et al. 2016). An influential meta‐analysis (Allum et al. 2008), for instance, shows that scientific knowledge only accounts for a small fraction of the variance in how people form opinions about controversial areas of science. Equally important, research shows that trust in messengers and cognitive processes like confirmation bias can strongly shape how individuals receive and evaluate messages, often reinforcing existing beliefs rather than challenging them even in the face of contrary evidence (Zhou and Shen 2022). Extensive research also shows that knowledge often takes a backseat to value dispositions like ideology, partisanship, and religious identity; reasonable disagreements, where “what might seem like misinformed or unreasonable actions from the outside often turns out to be a reasonable response to people's social reality” (Zinn 2020, 5); and the trustworthiness of messengers (Section 3). For these and other reasons, communication scholars therefore have widely rejected one‐way models as distorted representations of communication processes.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This article sought to offer a constructive critique of the dominant interpretation of misinformation that has emerged since 2016. Our perspective is that isolating and intensely focusing on misinformation as a standalone risk object obscures the agency of messengers, responses of audiences, and complexities of communication processes. Our central recommendation is that a communication‐based approach offers a more nuanced and holistic foundation for addressing the underlying challenges associated with false and misleading messages. This underscores the importance of interpreting misinformation within a broader communication context and ecosystem, recognizing that how we interpret any issue profoundly influences both our understanding of the underlying problems and the strategies we employ (Slovic 1999; Boholm and Corvellec 2011). Building on this foundation, we outline four sets of recommendations to guide researchers and policymakers.

Resist Oversimplifying Communication

We need to resist conceptualizing communication as a one‐way transmission of false or misleading information to a passive audience. Such conceptualizations likely endure due to heuristic or practical affordance. As Simis et al. (2016) argue, one‐way conceptualizations like the deficit model persist partly because of their simplicity in informing policy design: Misinformation interventions pinpoint a simple problem (i.e., false and misleading information misinforms the public) and propose a simple solution (e.g., prevent the public from receiving false and misleading information, correct false and misleading claims, inoculate the public against manipulation). Oversimplifying communication processes, however, misrepresents the multifaceted nature of the risk of misinformation and limits the scope of viable policy options.

Rather, researchers and policymakers ought to engage with the full complexity of communication processes, including the agency of messengers and audiences. One‐way approaches tend to fixate on the transfer of “content” or false and misleading “information” as the primary cause of misbeliefs, neglecting the role of active agents who craft, frame, and convey messages. Not all deceitful messengers communicate demonstrably false or misleading claims that audiences can readily identify as misinformation, even the most epistemically vigilant ones (Sperber et al. 2010). Conversely, even well‐intended, truthful messengers may inadvertently share false and misleading messages that can mislead, as the L'Aquila earthquake case demonstrates (Section 4). This highlights the ongoing need to improve how well‐intentioned messengers—such as trustworthy scientists, regulators, and journalists—communicate about risk. Focusing solely on the hazard, that is, the prevalence of misinformation online, can obscure the significant role that well‐intentioned messengers play, underscoring the need for ongoing, adaptive improvement in risk communication practices.

In a communication environment flooded with mixed messages, truthful messengers must also seek evidence‐based ways to demonstrate their trustworthiness (O'Neill 2017). People need to know whom to trust, how to make sense of conflicting messages, and how they understand their own role in seeking credible advice about risks that affect them and their communities (boyd 2017). A large body of risk communication scholarship shows that risk perceptions are strongly shaped by trust in messengers in domains ranging from climate change and nuclear power to autonomous vehicles and food, chemical, and pharmaceutical regulation (Earle 2010; Besley and McComas 2014; Siegrist 2021). Addressing the problems associated with false and misleading messages therefore requires more than moderating content, debunking false claims, or showing people how deceitful messengers attempt to manipulate. People need trustworthy messengers who can counter confusion with clarity. In so doing, messengers need to actively demonstrate why they are truthful, competent, and fair sources of knowledge that are deserving of the public's trust (O'Neill 2017).

Engage With Multiway Processes

Resisting the lure of oversimplified one‐way processes also requires engaging with more complex, multiway communication processes, which depict communication as a dynamic, interactive, and multidirectional exchange where meaning is co‐constructed through dialogue, context, and active engagement. Multiway communication processes (i) emphasize the inclusive exchange of perspectives, where experts and nonexperts engage in meaningful interactions; (ii) recognize the value of alternative, diverse viewpoints; (iii) engage members of the public as active participants through multiple fora; and (iv) address disagreements constructively to resolve conflicts (Reincke et al. 2020; Balog‐Way et al. 2020; Pidgeon 2021). This requires shifting the emphasis from paternalistically “protecting” members of the public in a top‐down manner to constructively engaging with how people interpret, experience, and challenge misinformation in their situated social and cultural contexts (Wardle 2023).

These conceptual commitments have practical implications. Notably, policymakers must engage with the multifaceted factors undergirding audiences’ responses to true/false messages conveyed by truthful/deceitful messengers. To be sure, both accurate and inaccurate beliefs are important factors influencing public attitudes and behaviors (Siegrist and Árvai 2020). However, extensive research demonstrates that audience responses to risk messages are strongly shaped by a multitude of psychological, social, cultural, economic, institutional, and other situational factors beyond knowledge alone (Boholm 2009; Boholm and Corvellec 2011, 2014). Over‐relying on simplistic message‐transfer models can cause us to miss these deeper drivers (boyd 2017).

Multiway approaches are critical for understanding why false beliefs take hold in the first place and can generate new insights for policymakers. For Wardle (2023), seeking out and listening to the public's questions and concerns provides an essential first step for messengers like government communication staff, community organizations, and journalists. Moreover, part of the way forward will require engaging the public through both low‐ and high‐involvement activities ranging from surveys, focus groups, and interviews to public meetings and integrating “outsiders” into policy processes to directly influence decisions. Surveys, for instance, can serve as a starting point for meaningful engagement, acting as a form of low‐intensity dialogue or as a “conversation” between researchers and respondents (Tepper et al. 2022). Meaningful engagement can empower communities such as by enabling participation in defining, identifying, and responding to problematic content, not just relying on top‐down decisions made by platforms or governments (Gillespie 2018; Matias 2019).

Although one‐way thinking is likely to persist (Reddy 1979; Simis et al. 2016), researchers and policymakers must nevertheless engage with the complexities of multiway communication. The benefits of inclusive, dialogical approaches far outweigh their challenges, offering a stronger and more holistic foundation for addressing the underlying issues.

Build on Existing Scholarship

The dominant interpretation often portrays misinformation as a novel problem linked to the latest technologies like the Internet, social media, and AI. From a communication perspective, however, the core challenges posed by false and misleading messages are not entirely new. Risk communication scholars have investigated similar issues for decades, albeit without necessarily framing their work in the language of “misinformation.” For example, the falsified link between the MMR vaccine and autism (Boyce 2007), UK ministers misinforming the public over the risks of the BSE crisis (Powell and Leiss 1997), and tobacco companies lying about the dangers of smoking (Smith et al. 2011;) are just a few historical examples of what would now be categorized as misinformation.

Rather than starting from scratch, future research should strategically build on interdisciplinary insights from the risk communication literature (Cho et al. 2014; McComas 2006; Bostrom et al. 2018; Fischhoff and Scheufele 2014, 2019; Balog‐Way et al. 2020). This means identifying how insights on risk perception, strategic messaging, and meaningful public engagement, for instance, can be adapted to the latest challenges. To remain both practical and policy‐relevant, risk communication scholars must keep pace with the rapidly evolving communication and policy environment fueled by the latest technologies (Rickard 2019; Wardman 2014).

For practitioners, existing and emerging research provides practical lessons for improving communication in the current environment. These insights include developing ethically grounded, strategic communication approaches (Besley and Dudo 2023) and carefully evaluating messages to avoid unintended outcomes (Salmon et al. 2014; Fischhoff 2019). Effective messengers should not stop at being truthful but also employ evidence‐based communication practices to inform precisely, with audience relevance and process transparency, and while specifying uncertainties about conclusions (Keohane et al. 2014). This reflects Bok's (1998) argument that “scrupulously truthful” messengers must take “special care” to counteract factors in their own communication that might mislead audiences (Section 3).

Nurture Interdisciplinary Research

The dominant interpretation tends to reduce challenges posed by the latest communication technologies to “combating” the spread of false or misleading information. Common interventions like content moderation, debunking, and prebunking are designed to protect people from misinformation, but on their own, they are insufficient to address today's complex and evolving challenges. Members of the public are now awash with near instantaneously distributed messages (Mayorga et al. 2020); traditional information brokers like journalists and regulatory authorities must compete for attention as trusted sources of knowledge under challenging circumstances (Rains et al. 2015); and backchannel peer‐to‐peer communication has become more visible, influential, and interactive (Sutton and Veil 2017). Social media companies and their leaders have become powerful actors in society (Davies 2025), and many trustworthy and knowledgeable messengers need guidance on how to communicate effectively. These challenges are unlikely to be fully addressed by the dominant approach alone but require new approaches that both build on and go beyond current scholarship and practice.

We believe that meeting these challenges will greatly benefit from nurturing interdisciplinary research. Some of the greatest achievements in the field of risk communication have come from cross‐fertilizing research across disciplines while working closely with practitioners (Balog‐Way et al. 2020). For example, recent scholarship examining the systemic and infrastructural dimensions of online communication emphasizes that platforms must be held accountable for the design of systems and interfaces that shape user behavior (Gillespie 2018). Risk communication scholars and practitioners also must grapple with the politics of platform design and architecture (Gillespie 2010). Interdisciplinarity holds great potential for inspiring more expansive approaches to the enduring challenges posed by false and misleading messages in a rapidly evolving communication ecosystem.
