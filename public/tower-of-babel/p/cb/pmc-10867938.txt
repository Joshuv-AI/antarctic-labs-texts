Recognizing and Managing the Burdens of Chronic Breathlessness Alongside the Burdens of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
Authors: David C. Currow, Rajam Iyer, Joseph Clark, Sujeet K. Rajan
Published: 2023

To the Editor:

Florman and colleagues are to be commended for drawing attention to the overwhelming burden generated by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in low- and middle-income countries (1). Access to the resources for its diagnosis and treatment are extremely limited, reflecting the reality in Nepal, Peru, and Uganda. These limitations are even greater for people with the most challenged social determinants of health (2).

Understandably, there is much emphasis in epidemiology on quantifying the burden from individual disease etiologies and the subsequent ability to treat within the social and health contexts of the population. Sadly, there are few data on the chronic symptom burden that these diseases impose on people’s daily lives for years or even decades. The seminal article by Florman and colleagues mentions a “breathlessness action plan” for people whose modified Medical Research Council breathlessness scale score was ⩾2. Such plans reflect the experience of acute-on-chronic breathlessness for the majority of people with COPD severe enough to generate scores this high.

Chronic breathlessness, recently recognized with a distinct code in the World-Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision, is the cardinal symptom of COPD (3). Even with optimal treatment of COPD in a perfect world, many people with COPD are left with long-term exertion limited by breathlessness (chronic breathlessness) (4). Like the underdiagnosis and undertreatment of COPD in low- and middle-income countries, chronic breathlessness is poorly recognized by clinicians and, even when recognized, generates very poor clinical responses at a population level even in high-income countries (5).

Chronic breathlessness has well-documented impacts on every part of a person’s life, particularly later in the course of the disease as the symptom increases in severity. If people are of working age, workforce participation decreases with greater severity of chronic breathlessness (6). In most low- and middle-income countries, if a breadwinner has chronic breathlessness, this will increase the likelihood that their household will experience poverty. Exertion limited by chronic breathlessness when dressing or undressing leaves many people with few realistic employment options, especially if that is compounded by low levels of education.

Chronic breathlessness is strongly associated with psychological distress, reduced quality of life, and increased use of health services, the latter especially in acute-on-chronic breathlessness crises, adding to the costs of health care and disrupting households. People who care for a person with breathlessness describe how distress “spreads through the family” in the context of witnessing episodes of acute-on-chronic breathlessness, mostly without the means to support the person. As the severity of chronic breathlessness increases, there are also additional responsibilities for family and friends, especially in terms of physical care. Although self-care is the last domain of the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule to be compromised (S. Kochovska and colleagues, unpublished results), an appreciable proportion of people have breathlessness that is sufficiently severe to reduce their ability even of self-care (7).

Globally, it is only recently that chronic breathlessness has begun to receive any attention (especially given its prevalence and impacts). Treatment of the underlying causes of chronic breathlessness remains paramount. Acknowledgment by healthcare professionals of the impact that breathlessness has on individuals and families can itself be therapeutic. Reassurance that a level of breathlessness is normal may support people to continue activities that they are able to manage and helps to avoid a cycle of deconditioning and increasing social isolation that are both so pervasive in people with chronic breathlessness. Use of a handheld fan is a relatively low-cost option, can help to decrease recovery time from episodes of acute-on-chronic breathlessness, and can help people feel more “safe.”

Treatment of underlying etiologies in parallel with good symptom management is critical to optimizing patient outcomes. Hand-in-hand with measuring disease burden, it is imperative that population studies quantify the symptom burden generated. Only then is it possible to start to address more systematically the well-being and health of the population with evidence-based interventions to reduce this symptom’s burden and help people to live lives with less long-term suffering.
