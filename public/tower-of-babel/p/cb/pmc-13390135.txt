Spatial analysis of predictors of prostate cancer incidence in the united states using multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR)
Authors: Yang Liu, Peihai Zhang, Ziyang Ma, Jintao Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate cancer incidence varies markedly across the United States (U.S.), yet the broad contextual predictors associated with this variation and the spatial scales at which these associations operate remain insufficiently characterized within a unified spatial framework.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine annual state-level associations between prostate cancer incidence and selected contextual predictors across the U.S. from 2018 to 2022 using Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR).MethodsFive annual state-level datasets covering the 50 U.S. states were assembled for 2018–2022. After exploratory spatial analysis, correlation screening, collinearity diagnostics, theory-informed re-evaluation, and representative-year sensitivity analyses, six predictors were retained for the final annual models: elevation, rainfall, atmospheric pressure, obesity prevalence, the percentage of males aged 65 years and older among the male population, and the percentage of the Black or African American alone population. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR), and MGWR were fitted separately for each annual dataset. Model performance was compared using R², adjusted R², residual sum of squares, corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc), bandwidth diagnostics, and residual spatial autocorrelation.ResultsAcross all five annual analyses, MGWR showed the most favorable overall performance, with adjusted R² values ranging from 0.555 to 0.618 and AICc values ranging from 124.934 to 132.779. MGWR also identified variable-specific adaptive bandwidths ranging from 24 to 40 nearest neighbors, indicating spatial scale heterogeneity among predictors. Local coefficient surfaces showed clear spatial non-stationarity in both coefficient magnitude and, for some predictors, coefficient direction. Residual Moran’s I tests were non-significant for all five annual models, suggesting that substantial residual spatial autocorrelation had been reduced after model fitting. Multivariate clustering of standardized local coefficients further revealed recurring regional groupings in coefficient-pattern similarity.ConclusionAt the state level in the U.S., prostate cancer incidence was associated with geographically differentiated contextual patterns rather than a single spatially uniform relationship structure. In this setting, the main value of MGWR lay in its ability to characterize multi-scale and regionally varying association patterns across repeated annual spatial analyses. These findings may support geographically differentiated surveillance and future hypothesis generation while underscoring the importance of considering spatial heterogeneity in ecological studies of prostate cancer incidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27860-w.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Spatial heterogeneity, MGWR, Environmental factors, U.S

Introduction

Prostate cancer continues to represent a substantial public health challenge worldwide, alongside a steadily increasing burden among men globally [1]. According to global cancer statistics from 2020, it ranks as the second most frequent malignancy among men, and is the top cancer for men in 107 countries—accounting for more than half of the world’s regions [2]. Globally, there were an estimated 1.4 million new cases and 375,000 deaths, making prostate cancer a major contributor to male cancer-related mortality [3]. Notably, the disease exhibits substantial geographical variation, with the greatest age-standardized incidence rates recorded in Northern and Western Europe, North America, and Southern Africa [4]. Within North America, the United States (U.S.) bears a considerable share of this burden [5]. Despite its advanced healthcare infrastructure, the U.S. continues to face significant challenges in the prevention and control of prostate cancer [6]. It remains the most frequently diagnosed cancer among American men [7]. Between 2014 and 2019, incidence increased by approximately 3% annually, with late-stage diagnoses rising even more rapidly at 4.6–4.8% per year [8]. Regional disparities are also evident, particularly in the western U.S., which shows the highest mortality rates and increasing trends in late-stage diagnoses across various racial and age groups [9, 10].

The etiology of prostate cancer is multifactorial and remains incompletely understood [11]. In addition to established individual-level determinants such as age, race, and genetic predisposition, a growing body of literature suggests that geographic variation in prostate cancer incidence may be associated with broader contextual conditions, including environmental exposures, socioeconomic circumstances, healthcare access, and population structure [12]. Recent research has increasingly examined how environmental and socioeconomic context may be associated with geographic variation in prostate cancer incidence; for example, case-control evidence has linked higher ambient nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) exposure to elevated prostate cancer risk [13, 14]. Further supporting this, an ecological study across U.S. counties reported higher prostate cancer incidence in regions with colder and drier climates, suggesting that meteorological conditions may be related to geographic variation in incidence, possibly through differences in pollutant distribution [15]. Environmental exposure studies have suggested that air pollution may be associated with prostate cancer risk or with broader geographic variation in cancer incidence [16, 17]. Other evidence indicates that environmental context may also be relevant through factors such as ultraviolet radiation exposure and interactions between external exposures and underlying susceptibility [18, 19]. From a social and population perspective, socioeconomic inequality and racial disparities may also shape prostate cancer-related differences through complex pathways involving healthcare access, treatment patterns, and broader structural disadvantage [20, 21]. Taken together, these studies suggest that prostate cancer incidence may be associated with multiple contextual predictors rather than a single uniformly acting factor, and that these associations may differ across regions. However, the available evidence is heterogeneous and often derived from single-exposure studies, local case-control analyses, or geographically restricted investigations, making it difficult to evaluate multiple state-level predictors within a unified spatial framework. Accordingly, the present study considered a set of candidate state-level predictors selected a priori on the basis of literature relevance and data availability for 2018–2022. These predictors were intended to reflect macroeconomic conditions, healthcare context, environmental setting, metabolic and behavioral context, and demographic composition. All explanatory variables were aligned year by year with the corresponding annual prostate cancer incidence data.

Spatial epidemiology has increasingly been used to investigate cancer patterns and their contextual correlates [22]. For example, spatiotemporal clustering analyses have been employed in China to explore potential links between prostate cancer incidence and environmental exposures [23]. Regression modeling has identified associations between gross domestic product (GDP) and prostate cancer incidence [24], while spatial analyses in Virginia have examined racial disparities in disease burden [25]. Multilevel spatiotemporal models have been applied to examine the joint impact of various determinants on surgical outcomes, such as positive surgical margins [26], and regression-based studies in Spain have analyzed prostate cancer mortality risk in urban areas [27]. These studies have improved understanding of geographic inequality in prostate cancer burden, but several gaps remain. First, many analyses have focused on single determinants or specific regions rather than jointly evaluating multiple predictors across the U.S. Second, conventional global models assume spatial stationarity and may therefore obscure regional variation in the strength and direction of associations. Third, even when local spatial models are applied, it remains unclear whether different predictors operate at similar or distinct spatial scales in state-level prostate cancer incidence data. In addition, relatively few studies have examined whether broad spatial association patterns are reproduced across repeated annual analyses rather than inferred from a single cross-sectional dataset.

Conventional statistical models are widely used but often overlook local variations [28]. In contrast, Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) and its multiscale extension, Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR), offer more flexible frameworks by factoring in geographic variation in how variables relate [29, 30]. Compared with global models, MGWR allows different predictors to vary over potentially different spatial scales and may therefore offer additional interpretive value when multiple processes operate unevenly across space [31]. It has been applied to characterize spatial heterogeneity in studies of various cancers, including breast and lung cancer [32, 33]. The novelty of the present study does not lie in introducing a new statistical method, but in applying a comparative Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)-GWR-MGWR framework to prostate cancer incidence across the U.S. states in order to assess whether different predictors exhibit distinct spatial scales of association. This comparison is methodologically relevant because it evaluates whether MGWR provides added interpretive value beyond global OLS and single-bandwidth GWR in a state-level ecological setting. Its substantive relevance lies in describing broad regional association patterns that may support geographically differentiated surveillance and future hypothesis generation.

In this study, we analyzed five annual state-level datasets covering the 50 U.S. states from 2018 to 2022 to examine associations between prostate cancer incidence and selected contextual predictors. Specifically, OLS, GWR, and MGWR were fitted separately for each annual dataset to compare model fit, assess spatial non-stationarity, and evaluate scale heterogeneity among predictors. Our methodological aim was to assess whether MGWR offers additional interpretive value beyond OLS and GWR in this application. Our substantive aim was to characterize broad regional patterns of association that may inform geographically differentiated surveillance and future hypothesis generation. Because the regression framework did not include explicit temporal parameters, the study should be interpreted as a sequence of annual spatial analyses rather than as a formal spatiotemporal model. Given the ecological, state-level design, the findings are intended to describe contextual association patterns rather than support causal inference or individual-level conclusions.

Materials and methods

This study used a year-specific spatial analytical framework (Fig. 1). For each year from 2018 to 2022, annual state-level prostate cancer incidence data were linked to year-matched contextual predictors representing environmental, socioeconomic, healthcare-related, behavioral, and demographic domains. After exploratory spatial analysis, candidate predictors were preliminarily assessed using correlation analysis and the variance inflation factor (VIF). These preliminary statistical results were then considered together with prior literature, biological plausibility, and data availability to identify a parsimonious set of variables for further evaluation. Sensitivity analyses were subsequently conducted to assess which candidate predictors most consistently improved MGWR model performance. Finally, OLS, GWR, and MGWR were fitted separately for each annual dataset, and the resulting local coefficients, residuals, and clustering patterns were visualized and interpreted descriptively.

Study area and data sources

This study analyzed the 50 U.S. states as the spatial units of analysis for the period 2018–2022 (Fig. 2). The U.S. exhibits substantial geographic diversity, including marked variation in landforms, climate conditions, demographic structure, and socioeconomic context across states [34, 35]. The outcome variable was the annual age-adjusted incidence rate of prostate cancer. Candidate predictors were assembled a priori on the basis of prior literature and data availability to represent broad contextual domains, including macroeconomic conditions, healthcare context, environmental setting, metabolic and behavioral context, and demographic composition, rather than established individual-level risk factors alone (Table 1).

Outcome variable and candidate explanatory variables

Age-adjusted prostate cancer incidence rate data were obtained from the CiNA+ Online Database, a collaborative project of the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR). This comprehensive dataset integrates high-quality, NAACCR-certified information from multiple sources, including the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR), and the Canadian Provincial and Territorial Registries (CCCR).

Candidate predictor data were obtained from official U.S. public sources, including the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) for state annual GDP and GDP per capita, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration National Centers for Environmental Information (NOAA NCEI) for temperature, precipitation, and atmospheric pressure, the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB) maintained by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory for solar radiation, the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) for elevation, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency AirData platform for annual NO₂ concentration, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System for obesity prevalence, and the U.S. Census Bureau for demographic and insurance-related variables.

All variables were harmonized to annual state-level units before analysis. Year-specific predictor values were matched to the corresponding annual prostate cancer incidence data for 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, and 2022. Variables not originally reported at the state level were aggregated to annual state-level summaries before dataset integration; for example, annual NO₂ monitor-based observations were averaged within each state to derive annual state-level mean concentrations. A small number of missing state-year observations were addressed using source-consistent imputation based on adjacent years or closely related official records in order to preserve temporal continuity and dataset completeness.

Spatial analysis methods

Spatial clustering analysis and hot spot analysis

Exploratory spatial analyses were conducted in ArcGIS Pro 3.5. Global Moran’s I was first calculated for the mean prostate cancer incidence rate across 2018–2022 to assess whether the overall spatial pattern across the 50 U.S. states was significantly clustered, dispersed, or random. After the global pattern had been assessed, Anselin Local Moran’s I was applied to identify local clusters and spatial outliers, and the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic was used to detect statistically significant hot spots and cold spots. These analyses were used to characterize the spatial structure of annual prostate cancer incidence before regression modeling. Because these are standard spatial analytical procedures, detailed mathematical derivations are not reproduced here and the corresponding methodological references are cited instead [36, 37].

Variable screening and collinearity diagnosis

Variable screening was conducted in three stages using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. First, Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses were used as preliminary descriptive tools to identify candidate predictors showing relatively stable bivariate associations with prostate cancer incidence across the pooled state-year observations from 2018 to 2022. In parallel, multicollinearity among candidate predictors was evaluated using the VIF, with VIF values below 5 considered acceptable for further evaluation [38, 39]. These statistical procedures were used to inform, but not solely determine, model inclusion.

Second, the candidate variables were re-evaluated in light of prior epidemiological literature, biological plausibility, and data availability across 2018–2022. This step was introduced to avoid excluding potentially relevant predictors solely because they were not significant in initial bivariate analyses and to ensure that variable selection was not based on correlation coefficients alone.

Third, representative-year sensitivity analyses were conducted using the 2019 and 2021 datasets, which were selected as mid-series years with complete data to reduce potential endpoint sensitivity, to assess the incremental contribution of literature-supported candidate predictors to MGWR model performance. Starting from a parsimonious core predictor set, additional candidate variables were entered one at a time into alternative MGWR specifications. Their contribution was evaluated using an explicit comparative decision rule based on overall and consistent model improvement across both representative years, considering changes in corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc), R², adjusted R², residual sum of squares, and residual spatial autocorrelation. The candidate variable showing the most favorable combined performance across these indicators, while maintaining model parsimony, was selected for inclusion in the final annual models. Given the limited number of state-level observations in each annual dataset (n = 50), this one-at-a-time strategy was adopted to reduce the risk of over-parameterization and model instability. The final predictor set for the annual models was therefore determined jointly by preliminary correlation patterns, collinearity diagnostics, theoretical relevance, and the results of representative-year sensitivity analyses rather than by bivariate significance alone.

OLS was used as a global baseline model, GWR was used to assess spatial non-stationarity under a single bandwidth, and MGWR was used to assess whether different predictors operated at different spatial scales. Because MGWR is a cross-sectional spatial model and does not explicitly model temporal dependence, OLS, GWR, and MGWR were fitted separately for each annual dataset from 2018 to 2022 rather than as a single pooled spatiotemporal model.

All regression models were implemented using MGWR 2.2. Each annual analysis included the 50 U.S. states (n = 50 spatial units). In both GWR and MGWR, bandwidths were specified as adaptive nearest-neighbor bandwidths rather than fixed geographic distances. To ensure comparability between the two local models and to avoid using neighborhood sizes that were either too small or too large for the state-level sample, the bandwidth search range was set to 10–40 nearest neighbors with a step size of 1, and the optimal bandwidth was identified within this interval. Given the limited number of spatial units, this setting was intended to reduce instability from overly small local neighborhoods while also preventing bandwidths from approaching the full sample size and thereby approximating a near-global solution. Within this common search framework, GWR estimated one adaptive bandwidth shared across all predictors, whereas MGWR estimated variable-specific adaptive bandwidths. Therefore, the reported bandwidths should be interpreted as adaptive neighborhood sizes identified within the prespecified search interval rather than as fixed-distance measures.

Given this limited state-level sample size, final model specification was guided by parsimony considerations to reduce the risk of over-parameterization, instability, and overfitting. Model performance was compared within each year using R², adjusted R², corrected AICc, residual sum of squares (RSS), bandwidth diagnostics, and residual spatial autocorrelation. Sensitivity analyses were used to evaluate which candidate predictors most consistently improved MGWR performance before the final annual model specifications were determined. Model outputs were interpreted cautiously as exploratory evidence of spatially varying associations rather than definitive local effect estimates.

Spatial validation of model results

For each annual MGWR analysis, ArcGIS Pro 3.5 was used to visualize the spatial distribution of local R² values, local coefficient surfaces, and model residuals. These maps were used to describe regional variation in model fit and in the direction and magnitude of local associations. Global Moran’s I of the model residuals was then calculated for each year to assess whether substantial residual spatial autocorrelation remained after model fitting. Residual diagnostics were interpreted as evidence of how well the spatial structure of the annual data had been captured, rather than as direct evidence of temporal stability across years.

Cluster analysis of MGWR coefficients

To summarize broad regional patterns in the MGWR results, the standardized local coefficients from each annual MGWR analysis were further examined using the Multivariate Clustering tool in ArcGIS Pro 3.5. K-means clustering with optimized seed locations was used, and the number of clusters was set to five as an exploratory descriptive solution chosen to balance regional differentiation and interpretability in the coefficient-pattern maps. The resulting cluster groups were interpreted descriptively to compare combinations of local coefficient patterns across regions rather than to infer discrete causal regimes.

Results

Global and local spatial autocorrelation of prostate cancer incidence

Prior to the local spatial analyses, Global Moran’s I was used to evaluate whether the mean prostate cancer incidence rate across 2018–2022 exhibited overall spatial dependence across the 50 U.S. states included in this study. The result showed significant positive spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.306, z = 3.354, p < 0.001), indicating that the spatial distribution of mean prostate cancer incidence was significantly more clustered than would be expected under a random pattern (Supplementary Fig. S1). This finding supported the subsequent use of Anselin Local Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi* statistics to identify local clusters, outliers, and hot/cold spots.

Figure 3 depicts the geographic variation in the mean prostate cancer incidence rate across the U.S. Higher-incidence areas were primarily concentrated in the north and east, in contrast to the predominantly moderate-to-low incidence levels observed across much of the western U.S. (Fig. 3A). Hot spots of mean prostate cancer incidence were concentrated mainly in parts of the northern and eastern U.S., whereas cold spots were more evident in lower-incidence western areas (Fig. 3B). The clusters-and-outliers map further showed localized spatial anomalies, including low-incidence states embedded within higher-incidence surroundings and higher-incidence states embedded within lower-incidence surroundings (Fig. 3C).

Variable screening and collinearity diagnosis

Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses were performed to preliminarily examine the associations between prostate cancer incidence and the candidate predictors. Prostate cancer incidence showed significant negative correlations with elevation (Pearson’s r = -0.361, p < 0.001; Spearman’s ρ = -0.392, p < 0.001) and the percentage of males aged 65 years and older among the male population (Pearson’s r = -0.186, p = 0.003; Spearman’s ρ = -0.191, p = 0.002). Significant positive correlations were observed for rainfall (Pearson’s r = 0.276, p < 0.001; Spearman’s ρ = 0.202, p = 0.001), obesity prevalence (Pearson’s r = 0.248, p < 0.001; Spearman’s ρ = 0.243, p < 0.001), and the percentage of the Black or African American alone population (Pearson’s r = 0.439, p < 0.001; Spearman’s ρ = 0.316, p < 0.001). Solar radiation was negatively associated with incidence in the Pearson analysis (r = -0.204, p = 0.001), whereas atmospheric pressure showed a weak positive association in the Spearman analysis (ρ = 0.157, p = 0.013). Annual NO₂ concentration was negatively correlated with incidence in the Pearson analysis (r = -0.194, p = 0.002), but this association was not statistically significant in the Spearman analysis (ρ = -0.105, p = 0.099). State annual GDP, GDP per capita, temperature, and health insurance coverage rate were not significantly correlated with prostate cancer incidence in either analysis (Supplementary Table S1).

VIF analysis indicated marked multicollinearity in the full candidate predictor set. In particular, GDP per capita (VIF = 71.220) and state annual GDP (VIF = 70.880) showed extremely severe collinearity. Temperature (VIF = 16.648), solar radiation (VIF = 17.301), rainfall (VIF = 10.054), atmospheric pressure (VIF = 8.302), and elevation (VIF = 8.990) also exhibited relatively high collinearity (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, the five-predictor base set retained after preliminary screening showed substantially lower collinearity, with VIF values of 2.830 for elevation, 2.900 for rainfall, 1.301 for obesity prevalence, 1.237 for the percentage of males aged 65 years and older among the male population, and 1.894 for the percentage of the Black or African American alone population, indicating acceptable levels of multicollinearity before the subsequent sensitivity-analysis stage (Supplementary Table S3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted for the 2019 and 2021 annual datasets by adding candidate variables one at a time to the five-predictor base MGWR model (Supplementary Table S4). Using the explicit comparative decision rule described above, atmospheric pressure showed the most favorable combined and consistent model performance across both representative years, considering AICc, R², adjusted R², residual sum of squares, and residual spatial autocorrelation jointly. Compared with the five-predictor base model, adding atmospheric pressure improved all main model-performance indicators in both representative years, with AICc decreasing from 141.030 to 126.759 in 2021 and from 140.496 to 131.593 in 2019, R² increasing from 0.650 to 0.727 and from 0.648 to 0.698, adjusted R² increasing from 0.486 to 0.603 and from 0.485 to 0.562, and residual sum of squares decreasing from 17.480 to 13.661 and from 17.601 to 15.086, respectively. Although NO₂ concentration and insurance coverage improved some model indicators, their overall contributions were smaller or less consistent across the two representative years, whereas temperature improved some fit indices but did not improve overall model performance. By contrast, solar radiation, state annual GDP, and GDP per capita did not provide consistent overall improvement. On the basis of these representative-year sensitivity analyses, atmospheric pressure was added to the five-predictor base model as the sixth predictor in the final annual model specifications.

Model comparison results

Bandwidth diagnostics further distinguished the single-bandwidth GWR model from the multiscale MGWR model. Because each annual analysis was based on only 50 U.S. states, both local models were configured to search for the optimal adaptive bandwidth within a prespecified range of 10–40 nearest neighbors. Within this common search interval, the single-bandwidth GWR model consistently reached the upper bound of 40 neighbors in all five annual analyses, indicating a tendency toward a relatively broad spatial scale under the current state-level design. By contrast, MGWR identified variable-specific adaptive bandwidths ranging from 24 to 40 nearest neighbors across the annual models. The percentage of the Black or African American alone population consistently showed the smallest bandwidths (24–25 nearest neighbors), suggesting a comparatively more localized association scale, whereas obesity prevalence and the percentage of males aged 65 years and older were generally estimated at broader neighborhood scales. Elevation, rainfall, and atmospheric pressure were typically estimated at intermediate-to-broad spatial scales. Overall, these findings suggest that the retained predictors may not operate at a single uniform spatial scale, although several predictors were estimated at relatively broad scales in this state-level setting (Table 2).

Spatial heterogeneity visualization analysis

To further describe spatial heterogeneity in the annual MGWR results, the spatial distributions of local R² values, regression residuals, and local coefficients for the selected predictors were visualized. These visualizations were interpreted descriptively to characterize regional variation in model fit and in the direction and magnitude of local associations, rather than to imply causal effects.

Spatial variation in local model fit (Local R²)

The local R² surfaces showed clear regional heterogeneity across all five annual MGWR models (Fig. 4). Based on the annual model outputs, local R² ranged from 0.507 to 0.807 in 2018, 0.440 to 0.765 in 2019, 0.519 to 0.824 in 2020, 0.510 to 0.810 in 2021, and 0.475 to 0.792 in 2022. Higher local R² values were repeatedly observed in the western and southwestern U.S., whereas lower values were more common in the Upper Midwest and parts of the Northeast. These patterns indicate that model fit varied regionally across the study area, with the selected predictor set accounting for incidence variation more effectively in some regions than in others.

Spatial distribution and randomness of regression residuals

The spatial distribution of the MGWR residuals from 2018 to 2022 did not exhibit clear or persistent spatial clustering patterns (Fig. 5). Across the five annual models, positive and negative residuals were interspersed across states, and no obvious large contiguous zones of systematic overestimation or underestimation were observed. This visual pattern suggests that the MGWR models captured the major broad-scale spatial structure in the annual state-level data, although some local unexplained variation remained.

Spatial Distribution of Regression Residuals from the MGWR Model, 2018–2022

Global Moran’s I tests of the standardized residuals further supported this interpretation (Supplementary Fig. S2). Moran’s I values were − 0.037 in 2018 (z = -0.170, p = 0.865), -0.035 in 2019 (z = -0.146, p = 0.884), -0.073 in 2020 (z = -0.535, p = 0.593), -0.061 in 2021 (z = -0.420, p = 0.675), and − 0.078 in 2022 (z = -0.584, p = 0.559). All five tests were non-significant, indicating that the standardized residuals were close to spatial randomness and that no substantial residual spatial autocorrelation remained after model fitting. Taken together, these findings suggest that the MGWR models adequately accounted for the main spatial dependence in the annual datasets. However, these residual diagnostics reflect model adequacy within each year and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of temporal stability across years.

Spatial heterogeneity of local coefficients across annual analyses

Figure 6 and the subsequent annual coefficient maps present the MGWR local coefficient surfaces for the retained predictors. Overall, the local coefficients showed clear spatial heterogeneity in both magnitude and, for some predictors, sign. Across the five annual models, the broad spatial configuration of several coefficient surfaces remained descriptively similar, although the annual MGWR models were fitted separately for each year. Accordingly, these patterns should be interpreted as repeated annual spatial analyses rather than as evidence from a formal temporal trend model or a quantitative test of between-year stability. Spatial distribution of MGWR coefficients for elevation, 2018–2022

Across all annual local coefficients, elevation remained uniformly negative (Mean: -1.107, Min: -1.408, Max: -0.848), with annual means ranging from − 1.172 in 2020 to -1.083 in 2019. Stronger negative coefficients were generally concentrated in the western and southwestern U.S., whereas relatively weaker negative coefficients were more often observed in parts of the southeastern and north-central regions, depending on the year. This pattern indicates a spatially pervasive inverse association between elevation and prostate cancer incidence, with regional variation mainly in magnitude rather than sign (Fig. 6).

Rainfall coefficients were predominantly positive across the study period (Mean: 0.194, Min: -0.047, Max: 0.344), and the annual means ranged from 0.106 in 2022 to 0.256 in 2020. Only a few states showed slightly negative values in 2018, 2019, and 2022, whereas all states showed positive rainfall coefficients in 2020 and 2021. Spatially, weaker or near-zero coefficients were more common in the upper midwestern region, while stronger positive coefficients appeared in parts of the South, Southwest, and Northeast in different years, indicating that the sign of the association was largely stable but its magnitude varied geographically (Fig. 7).

Atmospheric pressure also showed uniformly negative coefficients in all states and all years (Mean: -0.813, Min: -1.240, Max: -0.197), with annual means ranging from − 1.069 in 2021 to -0.581 in 2018. More negative coefficients were recurrently observed in the upper midwestern and northern interior regions, whereas relatively weaker negative coefficients were found in the northeastern region. This pattern suggests a consistently inverse association with only modest between-year variation in the overall coefficient surface (Fig. 8).

Spatial distribution of MGWR coefficients for atmospheric pressure, 2018–2022

Obesity prevalence showed the greatest between-year variation in average coefficient sign among the retained predictors, although its broad spatial surface remained descriptively similar across years. Across all annual local coefficients, the obesity coefficient ranged from negative to positive (Mean: -0.032, Min: -0.177, Max: 0.326), with annual means shifting from slightly positive values in 2018–2020 (0.014, 0.007, and 0.027) to negative values in 2021 and 2022 (-0.109 and − 0.098). Positive or near-zero coefficients were more evident in the far western and Pacific regions, whereas more negative coefficients were concentrated in parts of the Northeast and the Great Lakes region. These findings suggest that the obesity-incidence association was spatially heterogeneous and showed greater between-year variation in average coefficient sign than the other retained predictors (Fig. 9).

Spatial distribution of MGWR coefficients for obesity prevalence, 2018–2022

The coefficient for the percentage of males aged 65 years and older among the male population was negative in almost all states throughout the study period (Mean: -0.216, Min: -0.434, Max: 0.116), with annual means ranging from − 0.277 in 2018 to -0.155 in 2022. The most negative coefficients were repeatedly observed in the southern region, whereas coefficients near zero or slightly positive values were confined to only a very small number of states. This pattern indicates a largely stable negative association with limited sign reversals (Fig. 10).

The coefficient for the percentage of the Black or African American alone population showed the clearest spatial sign heterogeneity, while also displaying broadly similar spatial patterning across years. Across the five annual coefficient surfaces, its values ranged from − 1.500 to 0.450 (Mean: -0.242, Min: -1.500, Max: 0.450), and the annual means ranged from − 0.322 in 2018 to -0.155 in 2021. Negative coefficients were concentrated mainly in parts of the northern, upper midwestern, and western regions, whereas positive coefficients were more common in the South and selected eastern areas. This persistent regional differentiation suggests that the association between racial composition and prostate cancer incidence was strongly spatially non-stationary (Fig. 11).

Spatial heterogeneity in influencing factors revealed by cluster analysis

To further summarize broad regional similarities in the local MGWR coefficient patterns, multivariate clustering was applied to the standardized annual local coefficients, yielding five regionally coherent cluster groups (Fig. 12). Overall, the cluster maps showed that local coefficient profiles were regionally organized rather than randomly distributed across the U.S., indicating that neighboring states tended to share similar combinations of standardized local coefficients. Although cluster membership varied modestly across years, the broad spatial structure remained regionally organized, providing additional descriptive evidence of spatial non-stationarity in the associations examined. Because the clustering was based on standardized local coefficients, these clusters should be interpreted as summaries of relative coefficient-pattern similarity rather than as discrete causal regimes or direct intervention categories.

Discussion

This study applied a comparative OLS-GWR-MGWR framework to examine annual state-level associations between prostate cancer incidence and selected contextual predictors across the U.S. from 2018 to 2022. Overall, the findings indicate that prostate cancer incidence exhibited clear spatial heterogeneity and that the retained predictors were not associated with incidence in a spatially uniform manner. Instead, the local coefficient maps and cluster analysis suggested that neighboring states often shared similar combinations of coefficient patterns, indicating that contextual association profiles varied regionally rather than following a single nationwide structure.

From a methodological perspective, the value of MGWR in this study lay less in a dramatic increase in explanatory power than in its ability to reveal predictor-specific spatial scales and localized association patterns that could not be represented by a single global model or a single-bandwidth local model. Although the differences in adjusted R² between GWR and MGWR were modest, bandwidth diagnostics showed that GWR consistently selected one common bandwidth of 40 nearest neighbors, whereas MGWR identified variable-specific bandwidths ranging from 24 to 40 nearest neighbors. In particular, the percentage of the Black or African American alone population was repeatedly estimated at the narrowest local scale, while some environmental predictors such as elevation, rainfall, and atmospheric pressure also operated at somewhat narrower scales than the broadest bandwidth. By contrast, state-level obesity prevalence and the percentage of males aged 65 years and older were generally estimated at the broadest local scale. These differences indicate that not all retained predictors operated over the same spatial extent, which helps explain the added interpretive value of MGWR in this state-level ecological setting. However, bandwidth estimates reaching the prespecified upper limit should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect weak spatial heterogeneity or insufficient spatial resolution at the state level rather than a clearly localized process. The broader interpretation of regional coefficient-pattern heterogeneity was further supported by the clustering results, which showed that states could be grouped into geographically coherent regions according to similarities in their standardized local coefficient profiles.

At the substantive level, the six retained predictors displayed different forms of spatial variability. Elevation and atmospheric pressure were characterized by predominantly negative local coefficients across states and years, whereas rainfall remained predominantly positive but varied geographically in magnitude. State-level obesity prevalence and the percentage of the Black or African American alone population showed greater heterogeneity in coefficient direction and strength, suggesting that these ecological associations were more context-dependent across the study area. The percentage of males aged 65 years and older also showed predominantly negative state-level coefficients; however, this finding should not be interpreted as contradicting the well-established individual-level association between older age and prostate cancer risk. Rather, in an ecological analysis such as this one, the coefficients more likely reflect broader state-level compositional and contextual differences, including demographic structure, screening practices, healthcare context, and other unmeasured influences.

These findings may also have cautious public health relevance. Because the observed coefficient patterns differed across regions, the results may support geographically differentiated surveillance and future hypothesis generation. In particular, regions showing similar local coefficient combinations may warrant closer comparative investigation of screening practices, healthcare access, environmental exposures, and demographic composition. At the same time, the ecological design and state-level scale of the study mean that these results should be interpreted as regionally varying contextual associations rather than as direct evidence of individual-level mechanisms or immediate intervention targets.

Strengths, limitations, and future research directions

A key strength of this study is that it applied a comparative OLS-GWR-MGWR framework to five annual state-level datasets from 2018 to 2022, allowing broad regional association patterns in prostate cancer incidence to be examined within a consistent spatial analytical setting. Rather than relying on a single global model, the study evaluated whether local spatial modeling and variable-specific bandwidth estimation could provide additional interpretive value. The use of year-matched predictor datasets, representative-year sensitivity analyses for candidate variable inclusion, and residual and clustering diagnostics further strengthened the descriptive assessment of spatial heterogeneity in the annual models.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the analysis was conducted at the state level, and this degree of spatial aggregation may obscure finer-scale heterogeneity and is subject to the ecological fallacy and the modifiable areal unit problem. Second, because the study area consisted of U.S. states, edge effects and the relatively small number of spatial units in each annual dataset may have influenced local coefficient estimation and the apparent stability of cluster boundaries. In addition, several MGWR bandwidth estimates approached the upper search bound of 40 nearest neighbors, suggesting that broader-scale processes may not have been fully distinguishable at this spatial resolution and sample size. Third, although variable selection was guided by theoretical relevance and representative-year sensitivity analyses, the screening process still partly relied on correlation patterns and collinearity diagnostics, which may have excluded potentially relevant predictors or retained only a parsimonious subset of broader contextual influences. Fourth, the annual MGWR models were fitted separately for each year; accordingly, the results should be interpreted as a sequence of annual spatial analyses rather than as evidence from a formal spatiotemporal model, and any cross-year resemblance should not be overinterpreted as strict temporal stability. Finally, some potentially important contextual factors, including more detailed measures of screening, healthcare access, environmental exposure, and social conditions, were not included because of data constraints, and source-based preprocessing or imputation may have introduced unavoidable measurement uncertainty.

Future research should extend this work in several directions. Analyses at finer spatial resolutions, such as the county or census-tract level, would help determine whether the observed regional patterns persist when within-state heterogeneity is better represented. Incorporating additional predictors, including more detailed environmental exposure metrics, screening-related variables, healthcare access indicators, and broader social determinants of health, may improve explanatory depth. Methodologically, future studies could also consider multilevel frameworks, formal spatiotemporal models, and other advanced approaches capable of handling nonlinearity, hierarchical structure, and temporal dynamics more explicitly.

Conclusion

Using five annual state-level datasets from 2018 to 2022, this study showed that prostate cancer incidence across the U.S. exhibited clear spatial heterogeneity and that its associations with the selected contextual predictors were not spatially uniform. The comparative OLS-GWR-MGWR framework indicated that local spatial models provided additional interpretive value beyond a single global model by describing regional variation in coefficient magnitude and, for some predictors, coefficient direction. Rather than supporting a formal spatiotemporal interpretation, the findings should be understood as a sequence of annual spatial analyses that recurrently showed spatial non-stationarity and broad regional resemblance in local coefficient patterns. In this state-level ecological setting, the main value of MGWR was not simply a modest improvement in model fit, but its ability to characterize multi-scale association patterns and to provide a more nuanced description of how contextual associations varied across regions. Overall, the study suggests that prostate cancer incidence at the state level may be better understood as reflecting geographically differentiated contextual association patterns rather than a single nationwide relationship structure.
