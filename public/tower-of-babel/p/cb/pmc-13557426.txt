Toward Next-Gen Cell Therapy for Pediatric Patients: Neonatal Hepatocytes Tolerate Electroporation-Mediated Gene Editing and Engraft in the Liver
Authors: Justin R. Gibson, Abishek Dhungana, Menam Pokhrel, Benjamin Bessah Arthur, Olumide Adebayo, Daniel Hossack, Renee N. Cottle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocyte transplantation (HTx) offers a safer, less invasive alternative to orthotopic liver transplantation for inherited metabolic liver diseases, especially in high-risk pediatric patients. Combining HTx with ex vivo gene editing is a promising autologous therapeutic strategy using the patient’s cells. We investigated the feasibility of this approach by applying CRISPR-Cas9 gene knock-out to neonatal mouse hepatocytes and comparing their engraftment potential with that of mature adult cells in the Fah−/− mouse model of hereditary tyrosinemia type I (HT1). Electroporation-mediated gene editing did not significantly impair the ability of neonatal hepatocytes to engraft in vivo. Quantitative histological analysis revealed comparable liver repopulation levels between recipients of gene-edited neonatal cells and adult cells after hepatoxicity-mediated selection, providing a benchmark for electroporation-mediated gene editing in neonatal hepatocytes, and supporting the development of genetically corrected neonatal hepatocyte products as a crucial long-term or bridge-to-transplant therapeutic strategy for pediatric liver disease.

Introduction

Inherited metabolic liver diseases (IMLDs) encompass a group of disorders caused by pathogenic mutations that affect hepatic enzymes or transport proteins, leading to the accumulation of toxic metabolites or deficiencies in essential metabolic byproducts. 1 In children, IMLDs contribute significantly to acute liver failure (ALF) as well as severe underlying metabolic defects in the absence of liver failure. Collectively, these disorders account for approximately 8–13% of all pediatric liver transplants.2,3 While orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT) remains the benchmark treatment for terminal cases, it faces considerable challenges in the pediatric population. Donor organ shortages lead to significant waitlist mortality, particularly for infants, and younger children experience higher rates of graft rejection, leading to the need for retransplantation.4,5 Because OLT is technically more challenging in infants, it is rarely performed in this age group; in fact, infants account for only 2% of all pediatric liver transplants. 6 Furthermore, the necessity for lifelong immunosuppression, long-term medical complications, potential post-transplant malignancies, and high costs limit the overall success of OLT in the pediatric age group, underscoring the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies.7–10

Hepatocyte transplantation (HTx) is a promising, less invasive alternative that involves the infusion of donor hepatocytes from discarded liver segments or whole organs into the recipient liver.11–14 Compared with OLT, HTx has lower surgical risks, a shorter recovery, and the potential to treat multiple recipients with a single donor organ.15,16 Advances in cryopreservation enable thorough screening and banking of primary hepatocytes, while cellular reprogramming techniques offer the potential to generate large quantities of iPSC-derived hepatocytes for transplantation.17–19 However, achieving the therapeutic threshold of engraftment for transplanted hepatocytes remains a significant hurdle, particularly in the absence of strong selective pressure. 20 Combining HTx with ex vivo gene therapy provides a powerful approach to overcome some of these limitations, allowing for the generation of genetically modified autologous hepatocytes taken from the patient’s own liver or engineered nonimmunogenic universal hepatocytes from discarded cadaver donor livers.21,22 Emerging gene editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, can be used to genetically modify hepatocytes to reprogram metabolic pathways, correct loss-of-function mutations, precisely insert therapeutic transgenes, and disrupt genes encoding immunogenic antigens.23–25 Furthermore, gene editing enables the engineering of hepatocytes with a competitive advantage for selection in vivo.20,22,26 While viral vectors have been explored for gene therapy for IMLDs, they are associated with risks of insertional mutagenesis and immunogenicity. Gene editing also introduces additional safety challenges, including the risk of off-target mutations and genotoxicity from nuclease-induced double-strand breaks, which increase with multiplex gene editing or with simultaneous delivery of a donor template for homology-directed repair. Given these inherent risks, it is critical to minimize toxicity associated with the delivery vehicle. Nonviral delivery approaches can reduce the immunogenicity risks associated with viral vectors. Lipid nanoparticle-mediated delivery has emerged as the leading strategy for in vivo gene editing to treat IMLDs. However, LNPs are limited by off-target organ transfection, reduced transfection efficiency, and the risk of allergic reactions.27,28 Electroporation emerges as a potentially safer, nonviral ex vivo gene delivery method that uses electrical pulses to permeabilize cell membranes, eliminating vector-associated risks and accommodating larger genetic cargos.29,30 In prior studies, we demonstrated the impacts of electroporation for treatment of IMLDs.21,31,32

The choice of donor cell source also critically influences HTx outcomes. Hepatocytes from younger donors, such as those derived from neonatal sources, present several advantages, particularly for pediatric applications. They exhibit enhanced resistance to ischemia and cryopreservation stresses and possess more immune-privileged characteristics than cells from older donor livers.33–35 Despite these inherent benefits, a key unanswered question is whether robust neonatal hepatocytes can withstand the significant electroporation stress required for gene editing while retaining high viability and engraftment potential. Addressing these questions through foundational validation is necessary before using hepatocytes from pediatric donors in HTx and is even more critical before autologous approaches may be considered for pediatric patients.

To address this gap, we investigated the feasibility of combining electroporation-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing and HTx using neonatal murine hepatocytes. We used the hereditary tyrosinemia type I (HT1) mouse model as a proof-of-principle because of the hepatotoxic effects from fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase (Fah) deficiency, which provides a strong selective pressure to study hepatocyte transplantation. Fah-expressing hepatocytes have the capacity to repopulate the liver in Fah−/− recipient mice up to over 90% of the liver mass. 31 While Fah is expressed systemically, including in the renal proximal tubules, the liver is the primary source of toxic metabolite production due to its high tyrosine catabolic activity. Consequently, while HT1 involves multiorgan pathology, liver-specific correction is sufficient to halt liver failure and the production of circulating toxic metabolites and resolve the lethal phenotype. 36

As a proof-of-principle study for HT1 therapy, we targeted the 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (Hpd) gene. In the absence of Fah, tyrosine catabolism produces accumulating fumarylacetoacetate and succinyl acetone. Inhibition of Hpd using the drug, 2-(2-nitro-4-trifluoromethyl benzoyl)−1,3-cyclohexanedione (NTBC), prevents the buildup of these metabolites and protects against organ damage (Fig. 1A). Similarly, genetic disruption of Hpd elicits nearly identical outcomes, establishing Hpd as the primary therapeutic target for this disorder. Here, we transplanted wild-type (C57Bl/6J) donor hepatocytes into Fah−/− recipient mice to avoid confounding effects attributed to donor-cell genotype or baseline health. Prior studies have assessed the cytotoxicity of ex vivo gene editing delivery methods regarding their significant impact on the viability of hepatocytes and subsequent engraftment after introducing CRISPR-Cas9. 32 Therefore, to rigorously isolate the impacts of electroporation on engraftment potential, it is best to use healthy, uninjured hepatocytes as the cell substrate for transplantation. This experimental design allowed us to rule out underlying donor-cell dysfunction and isolate the specific electroporation-associated impacts and subsequent CRISPR-mediated gene disruption on neonatal hepatocyte viability and engraftment potential. Targeting Hpd allowed us to quantify the efficiency of gene editing by Cas9 RNP at a clinically relevant target locus. We benchmarked the engraftment of neonatal hepatocytes to adult hepatocytes by quantifying the engraftment efficiency and distribution in grafted recipients. We hypothesized that neonatal hepatocytes would tolerate ex vivo gene editing and achieve engraftment efficiencies comparable to those of adult hepatocytes, thereby supporting the potential of cell-based gene therapy for pediatric liver diseases.

Materials and Methods

Animal care and husbandry

All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the regulations of the Clemson University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), under protocol D16-00435 (approved June 24, 2024). Hepatocyte donors were 3-week-old and 12-week-old male wild-type C57Bl/6J mice, maintained on a regular chow diet. Donor mice were group-housed at a density of three or fewer per cage. For hepatocyte transplantation, 6-week-old male C57BL/6J FahΔexon5 (Fah−/−) mice were used as recipients. These recipient mice were fed a high-energy chow diet. Their drinking water contained 8.0 mg/L of NTBC. Following transplantation, recipient mice were housed with no more than two animals per cage and were monitored daily for weight loss, activity, and general health.

Hepatocyte isolation and characterization

Hepatocytes were isolated from fresh liver tissue. The left lateral lobe was dissected and loaded into a GentleMACS perfusion tube, which stabilizes the tissue and facilitates automated ex vivo perfusion and subsequent enzymatic digestion using the GentleMACS Octo dissociator (Miltenyi, Cat# 130–134-029) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For subsequent nuclear staining and flow cytometry, freshly isolated hepatocytes were resuspended to a concentration of 1.5 x 106 cells/mL prior to incubation with 1.5 µg/mL of Hoechst 33342 (Fischer Scientific, Cat# H3570) and 5.0 µM reserpine (Thermo Fischer, Cat# AAL0350603) for 15 min at 37°C, then 5.0 µg/mL of propidium iodide (Thermo Fischer, Cat# P3566) for 2 min at 37°C. Samples were then analyzed using flow cytometry. Through the gating strategies, debris and clumped cells were first excluded from the target population. Following this, propidium iodide-positive hepatocytes were gated out to exclude dead cells. Then, Hoechst-stained hepatocytes were analyzed based on fluorescent intensity to determine DNA quantity and thus ploidy status. Hepatocyte size was measured through the average forward scatter intensity of the total hepatocyte population as well as of each ploidy population.

Electroporation of CRISPR-Cas9 RNPs into primary hepatocytes

The Hpd-targeting sgRNA (CAACCCAGAAGGTCACCGAG) was chemically synthesized by Synthego (Redwood City, CA) as previously described. 32 RNP substrate was prepared in strip wells by adding 1.5 µL Hpd sgRNA (20 µg/µL) to 4.9 µL of 63 uM V3 Cas9 Protein (IDT, Cat# 1081061) and incubated at room temperature to precomplex for at least 10 min. 100 µL of electroporation buffer solution (Lonza, Cat# V4XP-3024) was added to 2.4 x 106 cells. Then, 100 µL of the cell solution was added to the RNP substrate to dispense the cells into the substrate prior to being transferred into the nucleocuvette vessel. Then the electroporation was performed using program CL-150 in the Lonza 4 D Nucleofector (Lonza, Cat# AAF-1002X & Cat# AAF-1002B), and the cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature before 500 µL of high cytokine recovery media 32 was added to each of the nucleocuvettes and left to sit at room temperature for an additional 15 min. Post-electroporation viability was quantified with the trypan blue exclusion method, where hepatocytes were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with 0.4% trypan blue stain (Gibco, Cat # 15250061) and counted using a hemocytometer. Following this, all like reactions were pooled. For each transplantation performed, 500,000 viable cells within 120 µL of cell product was transferred to individual tubes and transported on ice to the operation room. With the excess cells, 500,000 cells were plated in 1.5 mL of HMX media on a 6-well collagen-coated plate (Corning, Cat# 62405-413).

Analysis of transfection efficiency

At 24 h after nucleofection, media was aspirated from the wells of the plated cells and fresh HMX media was added. A collagen gel matrix overlay was added to the wells which would be cultured more than 24 h. At 48 h after electroporation, from the wells that remained in culture, media was aspirated and fresh HMX media was added. At 72 h after electroporation, cells were detached and gDNA isolation along with subsequent PCR of the Hpd amplicon was performed. Gene editing indel efficiency was quantified using tracking of indels by decomposition (TIDE) analysis. 37

Hepatocyte transplantation and selection

Weight of the experimental group of mice and no surgery control was recorded 3 days prior to the hepatocyte transplantation, establishing a baseline. The experimental group of mice was then taken off NTBC and switched to dextrose water (32.5 g/L) while keeping the no surgery group on NTBC, and on the third day post NTBC withdrawal, the mice underwent HTx via intrasplenic injection. The weight of the experimental mice was taken every day, maintaining the 24-h cycle. The reduction of body weight postsurgery was monitored, and if any drop below 10% of the baseline was detected, the experimental group was switched to water containing NTBC and dextrose. Mice were kept on the NTBC and dextrose water until they regained their baseline weight. Once they reached their baseline weight, they were switched back to water with dextrose only, while tracking their weight. At 4 weeks post-surgery, mice were euthanized for the end of study.

Histology and metabolic analysis

For histological analysis, liver tissue sections were taken from the area of the left lateral lobe most central to the main left portal vein, from half the distance to the most peripheral region of the lobe, and from the most peripheral portion in livers of euthanized mice. From each lobe, ∼3 MM thick sections were prepared, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (Millipore Sigma, Cat# HT501640), and mounted to microscopy slides. The slide sections were then stained for Fah with an immunohistochemistry (IHC) polyclonal rabbit anti-Fah primary antibody and visualized with a biotinylated secondary antibody and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine chromogenic substrate as previously described. 32 Next, the stained slides were imaged with a laser microdissection microscope (Leica, LMD7), and all tiled microscopic images were stitched together to form full images of each total slide. For each section, total liver tissue section area and Fah-positive regional area as well as Fah-positive clonal nodule (CN) count and size were quantified using ImageJ. For each section, CN spatial analysis of nearest neighbor index (NNI) was conducted using geographical analysis software (QGIS, v3.40 LTR “Bratislava”). Blood serum was collected from the recipients, and biomarker concentration analyses were conducted on ALT, AST, ASP, ALB, TBIL, and GLU.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted on GraphPad Prism 10.5.0. The Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plot analysis were performed on all sample groups to assess distribution normality. For groups with a n of at least eight, the D’Agostino and Pearson test, Anderson-Darling test, and Komologrov–Smirnov test were also used to assess normality. For comparisons between groups, for groups that took a lognormal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA with Dunn’s multiple comparisons (or, for comparisons of just two groups, a Komologrov–mirnov T test) was performed, and for groups that tended toward a normal distribution, the Brown–Forsythe and Welch one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons (or, for comparisons of just two groups, a Welch’s T test) was performed. For paired comparisons of subgroups within treatment groups, if the subgroups took a lognormal distribution, the Friedman test was used, and if the subgroups took a normal distribution, the RM One-way ANOVA was used. For analysis of the correlation between multiple variables, multiple linear regression was performed. Statistical significance was denoted as * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, *** for p < 0.001, and **** for p < 0.0001.

Results

Characterization of hepatocyte populations

First, we confirmed the ploidy characteristics of hepatocytes from C57BL/6J mice of different ages as described in Duncan, Andrew W et al. 38 using flow cytometry analysis in freshly isolated hepatocytes. We analyzed the cell size and nuclear content in hepatocytes stained with Hoechst and observed hepatocyte polyploidization with aging. In 3-week-old neonatal mice, diploid hepatocytes constituted 60.6% of the total hepatocyte population. In contrast, adult mice (9–18 weeks old) exhibited markedly lower proportions of diploid hepatocytes, averaging 2.19 − 2.02%, whereas late adult mice (36 weeks old) displayed only 0.83% (Supplementary Fig. S1). There were significant differences in hepatocyte size across age groups, with hepatocyte size nearly doubling from 3 weeks to 9 weeks of age. Specifically, neonatal hepatocytes exhibited an average forward scatter intensity of 0.83 × 106, 9-week-old hepatocytes exhibited 1.60 × 106, and 36-week-old hepatocytes exhibited 2.00 × 106 (Fig. 1B). This age-related hepatocyte hypertrophy was not only due to a larger total cell population, with a greater percentage of polyploid cells that are innately larger in older donors, but also to hypertrophy within each ploidy class with the same DNA content. For example, the average forward scatter intensity increased by 16.1% in diploid 2C cells, 49.9% in 4C cells, 72.5% in 8C cells, and 84.4% in 16C cells from 3- to 9-week-old donors (Fig. 1C).

Electroporation-mediated ex vivo gene editing and transplantation of young hepatocytes into fah−/− recipients

We next assessed the engraftment potential of hepatocytes from different mouse age categories. Hepatocytes were isolated from 3-week-old neonatal and 12-week-old adult wild-type (C57Bl/6J) mice and subsequently delivered Hpd-aiming CRISPR-Cas9 RNPs via electroporation. The hepatocytes from the like treatments were pooled and, within 2 h following electroporation, transplanted into Fah−/− recipients via splenic injection. Immediately after electroporation, we confirmed that the treatment would not have a significant effect on cell size or nuclear content. Neonatal hepatocytes from 3-week-old donors showed an average forward scatter intensity of 1.13 × 106 and 1.03 × 106 for Hpd KO and untreated cells, respectively (Fig. 2A). Adult hepatocytes from 12-week-old donors showed an average forward scatter intensity of 1.68 x 106 and 1.47 x 106 for Hpd KO and untreated cells, respectively (Fig. 2A). Electroporation-mediated gene editing did not elicit any immediate or disproportionate effect on different ploidy populations. Neonatal hepatocytes had no more than a 0.93% difference in population size of their ploidy levels, and adult hepatocytes had no more than a 2.47% difference (Fig. 2B). Neonatal hepatocytes displayed greater fragility to isolation and subsequent treatment, where cell viability was 72.9% and 77.1% for Hpd KO and untreated cells, respectively, and adult hepatocytes had a viability of 80.2% and 86.7%, for Hpd KO and untreated cells, respectively (Fig. 2C). Using the same RNP concentrations previously optimized for adult mouse hepatocytes, 31 we observed no age-dependent differences in targeted gene editing outcomes. On-target indel efficiencies were 97.5% and 99.4% for neonatal and adult hepatocytes, respectively.

Assessment of engraftment of immature hepatocytes in fah−/− recipients

The transplant recipients were removed from NTBC until a 10% decrease in body weight was observed at 5 days following surgery for the induction of hepatotoxicity and elicitation of clonal expansion of transplanted donor hepatocytes. Then, NTBC was readministered until recipients gained body weight back up to baseline, and then it was withdrawn again. One instance of NTBC cycling was required to attain 3 weeks of drug independence (Supplementary Fig. S2). In order to observe the greatest differences in initial engraftment and short-term proliferation of grafted hepatocytes between the different conditions, recipients were euthanized 21 days following withdrawal from NTBC (4 weeks post-HTx). After dissection, the liver was weighed, and hepatomegaly was observed in all treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. S3). Liver tissue sections were taken from the area of the left lateral lobe most central to the main left portal vein, from half the distance to the most peripheral region of the lobe, and from the most peripheral region. Sections were then subjected to immunohistochemistry staining against Fah to quantify engraftment by microscopy analysis (Figs. 3 and 4). Neonatal donor hepatocytes showed comparable engraftment potential to adult donor hepatocytes. Although there were no significant differences in engraftment between treatment groups, mature hepatocytes saw significantly reduced engraftment in the peripheral region of the left lateral lobe compared with the central region, where there were 78.0% and 36.1% reductions in Hpd knock-out and untreated hepatocyte engraftment, respectively (Fig. 5A). Although neonatal donor hepatocyte engraftment showed similar trends, these reductions were not statistically significant. Similar but insignificant trends were observed with the concentration of donor hepatocyte CNs throughout the lobe (Fig. 5B). CN size was observed to decrease throughout the lobe for all treatments (Fig. 5C). Any differences observed in engraftment area, CN concentration, or CN size across regions of the lobe are unlikely to result from aggregation in the transplanted cell suspension, as CNs from all treatment groups exhibited comparable nearest-neighbor index scores (Fig. 5D). This suggests that the cell size of donor hepatocytes is a dominant determinant of the penetration depth of engrafted hepatocytes within the lobe.

Multiple linear regression analyses revealed a significant influence of both average CN concentration and average CN size (p < 0.0001 for both) on total engraftment area (Fig. 6A), with standardized coefficients indicating a comparable contribution from both factors (Concentration β = 0.64; Size β = 0.61) (Supplementary Table S1). However, regional analysis revealed distinct drivers of engraftment. In the central region, both factors remained strong predictors, though CN size exerted a greater relative influence (β = 0.63, p < 0.0001) compared with concentration (β = 0.56, p < 0.0001), likely reflecting the coalescence of adjacent colonies (Fig. 6B). Notably, in the intermediate region, CN size exerted the maximal relative influence on engraftment (β = 0.65, p < 0.0001), serving as the primary driver in this region, closely followed by concentration (β = 0.59, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6C). In contrast, for the most peripheral region, this dynamic was reversed; CN concentration had a significantly greater impact on the engraftment area (β = 0.66, p < 0.0001) than CN size did (β = 0.46, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6D). Serum liver and kidney damage biomarkers were analyzed to assess whether donor age impacted the disease phenotype; however, no significant differences were observed between the treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Conclusion

Based on our findings, we conclude that neonatal hepatocytes from 3-week-old C57Bl/6J mice maintained sufficient viability and functionality to robustly engraft in recipient liver parenchyma following electroporation-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing and subsequent transplantation. Compared with adult hepatocytes, neonatal hepatocytes had similar levels of on-target gene editing. Despite having lower post-treatment viability than adult hepatocytes, neonatal hepatocytes showed improved engraftment penetration through the liver parenchyma compared to adult hepatocytes (Fig. 5A). The data trends suggest that the small size of neonatal hepatocytes is a key attribute for their enhanced engraftment farther into the peripheral regions of the liver. We hypothesize that this smaller size allows a higher number of donor cells to travel through the portal vasculature and become engrafted deeper in the liver lobe, particularly in the peripheral regions. This location appears to be critical, as our results show that the concentration of the CNs, which indicates the frequency at which individual donor hepatocytes engrafted, was the dominant predictor of engraftment in these outer areas compared with the size of the CN in the histology sections (Fig. 6D). This finding suggests that access, rather than proliferative capacity, is the primary bottleneck to repopulating these distal regions, a claim that is further supported by our observation that neonatal hepatocytes, which showed better overall engraftment, consistently maintained higher average CN concentration throughout the entire liver lobe (Fig. 5B). We hypothesize that the reduced physical dimensions of neonatal hepatocytes are the key determinant that enables this improved access, allowing cells to traverse the hepatic vasculature and sinusoidal network and establish high seeding densities required for peripheral engraftment, though we cannot strictly rule out the contribution of age-related biological factors such as differential expression of adhesion molecules or cytoskeletal deformability.

Furthermore, our regression analysis highlighted a spatial shift in repopulation kinetics among all recipients. While engraftment in the central portion of the lobe was driven by a balance of CN concentration and colony growth represented by CN size (Fig. 6B), the intermediate region was characterized by the maximal relative influence of colony size on engraftment (Fig. 6C). This suggests that the intermediate region represents the active front of clonal expansion, where hepatocytes are undergoing rapid proliferation into unoccupied parenchyma, distinct from the more established engraftment in the central portion of the lobe. Future studies utilizing cell-cycle markers could serve to further validate these region-specific kinetic models and may necessitate larger sample sizes to strengthen associated donor age-related statistical justifications.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study demonstrates the first successful combination of electroporation-mediated gene knock-out therapy with subsequent HTx using immature hepatocytes. By achieving levels of engraftment in immature hepatocytes which are comparable with mature hepatocytes, our results validate the feasibility of electroporation’s use in the production of cell-based gene therapies for the treatment of liver disease in pediatric patients. The translational potential of this approach is further strengthened when considered alongside previous evidence that human neonatal hepatocytes exhibit greater resistance to warm ischemia and cryopreservation compared with adult cells, and that they possess more immune-privileged characteristics.16,33,39 While human hepatocyte maturation involves a less dramatic surge in polyploidization compared with the rapid transition observed in mice, the progression of cellular hypertrophy with age remains a conserved feature in humans.38,40 This suggests that the morphological and genomic advantages of neonatal hepatocytes, specifically smaller cell size and higher diploid fraction compared with adult hepatocytes, are as relevant to human clinical applications as they are in murine models. Our findings complement these advantages, suggesting that electroporated immature hepatocytes could form the basis of robust, potentially universal cell therapy products, which would be particularly valuable for pediatric patients with ALF or IMLDs. In this vulnerable population, standard treatments like OLT carry a disproportionately high risk of mortality, making less invasive options like HTx highly desirable. Beyond direct disease correction, electroporation-mediated gene editing offers further therapeutic possibilities. For instance, silencing pro-apoptotic genes could enhance transplanted cell survival, while targeting key immune pathways might reduce immunogenicity. These strategies could further improve engraftment outcomes, positioning engineered HTx as a powerful bridge-to-transplant or potentially even a definitive therapy.

One common challenge impacting HTx efficacy is suboptimal cell distribution, which can contribute to sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS). SOS involves acute portal hypertension resulting from transplanted cell clumping and hepatic vaso-occlusion, potentially leading to hepatomegaly, intrahepatic cholestasis, edema, and thrombocytopenia.41,42 In this study, we employed histological analyses to assess engraftment efficiency at varying distances from the main left portal vein, providing a snapshot of hepatocyte migration through the liver architecture. Although we did observe differences in engraftment dynamics between groups, these did not translate into significant differences in serum biomarkers at the study’s endpoint. However, we cannot rule out that these engraftment differences might influence the liver’s response to injury or the rate of repopulation at later timepoints.

Therefore, while this validation strongly supports the potential of electroporated immature hepatocytes for short-term outcomes, such as bridge-to-transplant strategies for pediatric patients, the study’s duration represents a notable limitation regarding long-term clinical goals where HTx might serve as the endpoint therapy itself. We concluded the study shortly after recipients achieved NTBC independence, a time corresponding to the peak of the selection process. Consequently, the hepatomegaly and dramatically elevated indicators of liver damage observed across all groups likely reflect such an intense selection pressure rather than differences related to cell engraftment patterns at this early stage.

Another limitation of this study is the divergence between the experimental and clinical cell infusion routes. While intrasplenic injection was utilized here as the standard surgical approach to access the portal circulation in mice, clinical HTx in humans typically employs direct portal catheterization in adults and catheterization through umbilical vein access in neonates, which is significantly less invasive. 43 Despite these different entry points, both methods rely on the portal venous system for cell delivery to the hepatic parenchyma. Therefore, while our results demonstrate the mechanical advantages of smaller neonatal cells in navigating the portal microcirculation, further validation in large animal models using clinical-grade infusion protocols is warranted to confirm these translatability benefits.

Future studies focused on mitigating SOS and understanding long-term outcomes should incorporate strategies like vasodilation alongside gene editing of immature hepatocytes. 44 These studies could assess engraftment dynamics over various endpoints, correlating them with specific metrics of SOS, such as edema and thrombocytopenia, as well as the overall rate of liver repopulation and disease correction. Furthermore, future work should bridge the gap between the known feasibility of using neonatal PHHs for HTx and our findings regarding the robustness of immature hepatocytes to electroporation-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 editing. A rational next step would be to validate the ability of cryopreserved neonatal PHHs to undergo electroporation knock-out therapy prior to infusion into relevant preclinical models, such as immunocompromised murine or porcine models. Incorporating advanced in vivo or in situ imaging to track hepatocyte dispersal throughout the entire liver could also provide valuable insights into engraftment efficiencies at the lobe peripheries and the influence of specific hepatocyte characteristics or tissue preconditioning regimens.45,46
