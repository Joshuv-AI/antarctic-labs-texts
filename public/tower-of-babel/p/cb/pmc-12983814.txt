A place for everything… and everything in its place?
Authors: Marianne Marchini Reitz, Yuri Affonso Nobori Sanchez Tanaka, Pedro Augusto Rodrigues Vinhas, Caique Moraes de Mendonça, Monique Maçais de Carvalho, Flávius Vinicius Teixeira de Toledo, Marcelo Augusto Fontenelle Ribeiro Jr.
Published: 2026

Introduction

We describe a critical decision scenario where an uncommon and unexpected thoracic injury caused by blunt trauma to the chest modified the anatomy, requiring fast-paced critical thinking.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old male was brought in by ambulance for being run over by a car after falling from his motorcycle on the highway. At the scene, the patient had blood on his upper airway, and breath sounds were diminished bilaterally. Blood pressure (BP) was 170/100 mm Hg, heart rate (HR) was 120 bpm, and he had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 8. The prehospital team performed rapid sequence intubation and bilateral needle chest decompression on the second intercostal spaces. A cervical spine collar was placed. On route, he received 1 g of tranexamic acid (TXA) and 1500 mL of saline solution.

Upon arrival, bilateral chest emphysema with multiple rib crepitus was noted, with an oxygen saturation (O2 Sat) of 70%. His skin was pale and clammy, with a soft abdomen and stable pelvis on palpation. E-FAST (Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma) was negative on the abdomen, positive for pneumothorax bilaterally, but equivocal on the pericardial view. He had a GCS of 3T, pupils were miotic bilaterally, and bone crepitus with deformity was present on the left arm. Chest tubes were inserted on both sides with immediate blood output (800 mL on the left and 500 mL on the right), in addition to continuous air outflow. His oxygen saturation dropped to 65%, BP went down to 70/50 mm Hg, and his HR was still 120 bpm, with a Shock Index of 1.7. Massive transfusion protocol was initiated, and he was immediately started on two units of whole blood. Calcium gluconate and another 1 g of TXA were given. A urine catheter was inserted, with clear urine output.

As the patient responded to volume resuscitation, with BP reaching 132/60 mm Hg and HR 113 bpm, and given the close proximity of the Trauma Bay to the computed tomography (CT) scanner, it was decided to take him to the CT. The patient was then taken back to the Trauma Bay, and a central line was obtained (right jugular vein), after which he presented bradycardia followed by asystole. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation with external chest compressions was performed. This decision was based on the CT results (described below), and the patient regained spontaneous circulation after five cycles. What we did and why

After comparing the CT images to the previous X-ray, any questions regarding possible dextrocardia were eliminated. In addition to multiple rib fractures, extensive bilateral pneumothorax, lung contusions, and insufficient lung expansion, the heart was observed to be in the right chest, with the left ventricle flipped over on top of the right ventricle. A left humerus diaphyseal fracture was also noted. No significant injuries were seen in the abdomen, retroperitoneum, or pelvis that could justify the patient’s instability.

At this point, both chest tubes continued to leak air, with no significant additional amount of blood output. The etiology of the shock was then thought to be less likely due to hemorrhagic shock and more probably due to obstruction caused by the rotation of the heart. Considering these findings and the patient’s clinical deterioration, the decision was made to take him to the operating room to perform a clamshell thoracotomy in order to restore the heart to its anatomical position and, secondarily, to check for suspected additional airway injuries.

Upon entering the chest cavity and evacuating a bilateral hematoma, it was observed that the pericardium was ruptured horizontally, involving more than 50% of its circumference, likely injuring both phrenic nerves (figure 3 and online supplemental video 2). The heart was completely herniated and rotated to the right side, presenting with bradycardia. A laceration of the left ventricle, close to the apex, was observed and sutured with 2–0 polypropylene. The heart was then put back into the pericardium sac, which was approximated with separate 2–0 polypropylene sutures (figure 4). No pericardial drains were placed, since no running sutures were used.

Bilateral lung contusion was present, with a bronchial fistula and parenchymal bleeding on the left lower lobe. Suture ligation of the inferior lobar vessels was performed, followed by left inferior lobectomy using a linear stapler. The chest tubes were repositioned under direct vision, and the chest was closed with number 5 polyester sutures.

During surgery, the patient received 1500 mL of Ringer’s lactate, three units of frozen plasma, seven units of pooled platelets, and seven units of cryoprecipitate. He was also started on vasopressors. Approximately three hours after surgery, during which vasopressors were progressively increased, he presented another cardiac arrest, initially in pulseless electrical activity, and then evolving to asystole. The total chest tube output was 200 mL on the right and 150 mL on the left, which spoke against hypovolemic shock at that moment. Considering all identified anatomic injuries had already been addressed, it was decided to maintain external chest compressions only. After 45 min, it was noted the patient had bilateral fixed mydriatic pupils, and he was pronounced dead.

Discussion and lessons learned

Acute traumatic cardiac hernias pose a challenge regarding both diagnosis and management. Not all pericardial injuries lead to acute physiological derangement, and patients might evolve with late symptoms, depending on the extent of the pericardial rupture and the degree of herniation.1 In the acute setting, when shock is present, other much more common etiologies are likely to be considered and treated. Although electrocardiogram (ECG) and auscultation abnormalities might be present, it is a very difficult injury to identify without imaging. E-FAST may be able to identify it, but lack of experience and the coexistence of hemothorax might render the exam equivocal. Chest X-ray can reveal from a loss of cardiac contour to an abnormal positioning of the heart on the right side. In the absence of past medical information and imaging, it can be easily mistaken for dextrocardia. In our case, comparison between the CT scan images and the chest X-ray from a previous visit proved pivotal.

The diagnosis may also be made incidentally during surgery. Conditions that might prompt a surgical decision include suspicion of active bleeding in the chest (demonstrated by continuous blood output through chest tubes) or of a bronchial fistula (if chest tube air leak does not resolve with suction or if there is worsening of subcutaneous emphysema). In hemodynamically stable patients, video-assisted thoracoscopy surgery (VATS) is an option. In hemodynamically unstable patients, regardless of the etiology, or when traumatic brain injury is also present, open chest surgery is a safer option, starting with an anteroposterior left thoracotomy. If only a partial tear and small herniation are present, either of these strategies might provide the necessary exposure to perform a repair.2 In case of complete rupture and cardiac herniation with dislocation of the heart, though, an open approach is needed, with extension of the incision into the right chest through a clamshell thoracotomy.

Important consideration must be given to intracardiac injuries. A transesophageal echocardiogram may be helpful to identify valve or septal damage in need of additional repair. Unfortunately, this option is not readily available at our institution, and there might have been unidentified and, thus, unaddressed injuries that contributed to this patient’s death. When the myocardium itself is damaged, acute cardiac insufficiency may appear. That may also be the case when lung resection is necessary, leading to hemodynamic impact due to an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance and decreased right ventricle function.3 In these cases, more advanced resources such as veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA ECMO) may provide hemodynamic and respiratory support, especially when other injuries are present, like extensive lung contusion.4

Also important is the location of the pericardial injury. In this case, the tear ran superiorly and horizontally, encompassing more than half of the pericardial circumference, in addition to a blunt left ventricle perforation (Grade IV injury based on the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma Organ Injury Scale), most likely transecting both phrenic nerves.5 In the acute setting in an intubated patient with ongoing ventilation support, this might not have a readily significant impact, but it is necessary to later address this issue, since diaphragm paralysis due to phrenic injury may lead to chronic respiratory insufficiency, and tracheostomy for ongoing ventilation support may be required. Later on, other options might be considered, including diaphragm pacers or nerve reconstruction surgery.6 7
