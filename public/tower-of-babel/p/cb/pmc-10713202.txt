The impact of Canadian-produced research on the global orthopedic literature: a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Julien Dartus, Patrick Devos, Bogdan A Matache, Luc Bédard, Stéphane Pelet, Etienne L Belzile
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Little is known about the quality and impact of Canadian-produced research relative to that of other developed nations. The purpose of this study was to determine the contribution of Canadian authors to the orthopedic literature globally and nationally as well as Canada’s research productivity in orthopedics. We hypothesized that Canada ranks among the most impactful countries in terms of orthopedic research productivity.Methods:We performed a bibliometric analysis to identify articles published between 2001 and 2020 in the category of orthopedics. We identified Canada’s global rank in terms of overall productivity and assessed the contributions of individual Canadian authors. We also examined the quality of publications as determined by category normalized citation impact (CNCI) and publication in the top quartile of journals (%Q1) in terms of impact factor. In addition, we calculated the percentage of Canadian publications that were in orthopedics.Results:We identified 10 821 orthopedic publications from 2001 to 2020. Canada placed sixth globally in terms of productivity in orthopedic research. The annual productivity of Canadian orthopedic researchers increased over the study period by a factor of 3.2. In terms of research quality, with a %Q1 of 36.5% and a CNCI of 1.22, Canada outperformed Asian countries and the United States; the latter country had a %Q1 of 35.3% and a CNCI of 1.14 over the study period.Conclusion:The body of Canadian orthopedic literature has grown consistently over the past 20 years. Despite the overall leadership of the United States and other developed nations such as China and Japan, Canada ranks among the most influential countries in terms of the quality and quantity of orthopedic research.

Abibliometric analysis is both a quantitative and a qualitative review of the scientific literature. A number of factors have been described to assess academic productivity using this model, in terms of both the volume and the scientific impact of the research produced. Bibliometric analysis allows for a broad and complete evaluation of the productivity and scientific impact of the research produced in a particular country or specific discipline (such as medicine or surgery),1–5 individual journals6–8 and studies presented at annual scientific meetings,9–11 as well as the impact of individual authors.3,12,13 Using this model, Saab and colleagues showed that orthopedic research originating in France has increased by more than 50% over the past 10 years.14 Similarly, Naziri and colleagues demonstrated that abstracts presented at the annual meetings of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons resulted in journal publication 56% of the time, while Erivan and colleagues found that only 47% of the abstracts presented at the annual meeting of the Société française de chirurgie orthopédique et traumatologique were later published.9,10

In France, bibliometric analysis has been shown to improve the quality of research produced in journals such as Orthopaedics & Traumatology: Surgery & Research by identifying strong researchers, evaluating the performance of various academic centres15 and classifying articles on the basis of their impact factors.6 Furthermore, this method is often employed to help determine which research grant recipients are most likely to convert allocated funds into scientific publications,16 and to classify journals on the basis of their impact factors.17

In recent years, there has been increased transparency in the disclosure of publication data in the field of orthopedics in some countries.1,2,6,18,19 However, little is known about how Canada ranks in terms of orthopedic research productivity on a global scale or about the contribution of orthopedic research to the overall scientific research output of the country. The purpose of this study was to determine the contribution of Canadian authors to the orthopedic literature both nationally and globally, productivity in orthopedic research in Canada, the productivity in orthopedics of individual academic centres and the level of networking and collaboration in Canadian centres. We hypothesized that Canada ranks among the countries with the most impact in terms of orthopedic research productivity.

Methods

Data source

The bibliometric analysis was performed using the InCites platform, which uses data from the Web of Science (WoS), a multidisciplinary database edited by Clarivate.20 In the WoS, journals are classified into 254 disciplinary fields called Web of Science categories. The orthopedics category contains all indexed orthopedic journals, which roughly doubled in number between 2001 and 2020, from 42 to 82 journals, according to the 2020 release of Journal Citation Reports, as well as orthopedic articles published in other medical journals, such as The Lancet and The New England Journal of Medicine.

Using InCites and the Emerging Sources Citation Index, 1 of the authors (J.D.) analyzed all articles indexed in the orthopedics category. Articles were defined as being of Canadian origin if at least 1 Canadian address was associated with the authors. An article of Canadian origin could therefore be either an article with a Canadian corresponding author or an article with 1 or more Canadian noncorresponding authors, for instance in international collaborations. We included only original and review articles in our analysis; we excluded all other forms of publication (such as editorial letters). For each publication, we assigned 1 point to the country (or countries) listed for each author; each country received a maximum of 1 point for each publication, even if several authors were from a given country. The same point allocation system was used for institutional analysis.

The InCites database is an analytical tool that provides aggregate data by discipline, country or institution. Using data obtained from the WoS, it allows the user to perform a broad search with various key search terms (indicators). We used the following indicators in this study: the number of publications per year; the percentage of articles published in the top quartile (%Q1) of journals, defined as the top 25% of journals in terms of impact factor in orthopedics; the category normalized citation impact (CNCI), which is defined as the ratio of the number of citations of an article to the average number of citations of a similar article published in the same year and in the same WoS category (e.g., an article with a CNCI of 2 has twice as many citations as the global average); and the specialization index (SI) for each country, which is defined as the national ratio of orthopedic publications to national scientific publications divided by the global ratio of orthopedic publications to global scientific publications (e.g., a SI of 1 indicates that a country’s orthopedic productivity is similar to the global mean).

Analyses

We performed the following analyses. We compared Canada’s overall orthopedic productivity with that of other developed nations. We analyzed Canada’s orthopedic productivity as measured by %Q1, SI and CNCI. We compared Canada’s productivity in orthopedics with its productivity in other disciplines, and we compared global productivity in orthopedics with global productivity in other disciplines. We conducted a bibliometric analysis of the indicators of interest in 5-year periods. We compared the productivity in orthopedics of Canadian academic centres. Finally, we conducted an analysis of network collaboration among Canadian orthopedic surgeons.

This was a purely descriptive study employing absolute values, means, minimum and maximum values and percentages. Advanced statistical analysis was not required nor performed.

Results

Contribution of Canadian authors to the national and global literature in orthopedics

Quantity of publications

There were 10 821 orthopedic scientific articles with at least 1 Canadian author published in the past 20 years, placing Canada sixth in terms of global orthopedic productivity (Table 1, Figure 1 and Figure 2). The corresponding author was Canadian in 7785 (72%) of these articles, and the articles were collaborations in 3036 cases (28%). Collectively, the annual productivity of Canadian orthopedic surgeon-scientists increased by a factor of 3.2 from 263 publications in 2001 to 840 in 2020. This trend is similar to that of other leading nations such as the United States (3.3), the United Kingdom (2.8), Germany (3) and Japan (3.3). China’s annual productivity increase far outpaced that of other countries, growing by a factor of 106 from 23 publications in 2001 to 2452 in 2020. Other Asian countries also saw rapid scientific growth, with South Korea increasing its annual productivity from 78 in 2001 to 951 in 2020 (a factor of 12) and India producing 656 publications in 2020 compared with 26 in 2001 (a factor of 25).

Quality of publications

Canada compared favourably with other developed nations in terms of the quality of its orthopedic publications. With a %Q1 of 36.5% and a CNCI of 1.22 in 2016–2020, Canada outperformed the US (35.3%, 1.14), the UK (32.3%, 1.21), Germany (26.6%, 0.96), China (22.9%, 0.90) and Japan (25.9%, 0.86), while falling short of the Netherlands (39.7%, 1.22) and Sweden (47.5%, 1.40) (Table 3). However, there was a downward trend in the quality of Canada’s publications over the past 20 years, with a decrease in %Q1 from 50.9% during the period 2006–2010 to 41.6% in 2011–2015 and finally to 36.5% in 2016–2020. Conversely, the UK, Germany, France, Brazil and Austria all experienced positive trends during this period.

Canada’s research productivity in orthopedics

Quantity of publications

A total of 1 327 046 scientific articles originating from Canada were published from 2001 to 2020, of which 478 969 were medical (classified under the medical and health sciences category) and 10 821 were orthopedic (Table 4). During the study period, orthopedic publications in Canada accounted for 2.26% of all medical publications, with little variation year over year (minimum 2.13% in 2020, maximum 2.44% in 2012). This compares favourably with the ratio of orthopedic to medical publications globally (2.61% in 2020).

Specialization index

The SI of the orthopedics category in Canada decreased over the study period, with a steep decline from 2015 onward, from 1.10 in 2001 to 0.81 in 2020 (Table 5 and Figure 3). In contrast, the SI of the medical and health sciences category increased over the same time period, peaking at 1.22 in 2020. This suggests that while Canada’s overall medical research productivity outperformed the global mean, its orthopedic research productivity underperformed.

Research productivity in orthopedics of individual academic centres

Quantity of publications

The 17 Canadian academic medical centres produced a total of 10 461 orthopedic publications in the study period, with an upward trajectory in terms of productivity across the board. The 3 most productive universities were the University of Toronto, the University of British Columbia and Western University, with 2675, 1459 and 1398 publications, respectively (Table 6). However, the universities demonstrating the most rapid growth in productivity were Dalhousie University (5 publications in 2001 v. 48 in 2020; r = 9.6), Université Laval (r = 6.6) and the University of Ottawa (r = 5.9).

Quality of publications

From 2001 to 2005, the mean %Q1 for orthopedic publications associated with Canadian universities was 47.9%, and the CNCI was 1.38 (Table 6). This was primarily due to the successes of 8 of the 17 universities (the University of Alberta, the University of British Columbia, the University of Calgary, McGill University, Queen’s University, Université Laval, Université de Sherbrooke, Université de Montréal), which all had a %Q1 greater than 50% and a CNCI between 2.11 and 4.64. However, there was a slight decrease in the quality of publications from 2016 to 2020 even though 8 of the 17 universities had a %Q1 and CNCI greater than the mean, with the mean %Q1 and CNCI being 36.8% and 1.21, respectively. The leading universities over this time period were the University of Toronto, Western University, the University of British Columbia, McMaster University, the University of Calgary, the University of Manitoba, the University of Alberta, the University of Ottawa, Queen’s University and Université Laval.

Network collaborations

Between 2001 and 2005, the largest collaborative network comprised 6 universities: the University of Toronto, McMaster University, Western University, the University of British Columbia, the University of Calgary and McGill University (Figure 4 and Figure 5). The 3 francophone academic centres (Université Laval, Université de Montréal and Université de Sherbrooke) comprised the second largest network. Although a similar trend was observed between 2016 and 2020, this period also saw an increase in collaboration on orthopedic publications between anglophone and francophone centres, thus producing a national network of collaboration that was previously not present.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that Canada ranks among the countries with the most impact in terms of research productivity in orthopedics, ranking sixth globally. We also found that the proportion of all medical publications on orthopedics in Canada was less than the global mean, indicating lower relative productivity for orthopedics than for other disciplines. The 3 most productive universities were the University of Toronto, the University of British Columbia and Western University; those demonstrating the most rapid growth in productivity were Dalhousie University, Université Laval and the University of Ottawa.

The findings of this study are in accordance with the current literature on the subject and show that all developed nations have increased their research productivity in orthopedics over the past 20 years. China, South Korea and India have seen exponential increases in productivity over this time period (r values of 106, 12 and 25, respectively), while the US has retained its top status in terms of overall productivity, with 86 504 publications.6 The US also leads in terms of the number of publications for each subspecialty, including trauma,21 foot and ankle,22 infections,23 hand and wrist24 and arthroplasty.21 Nevertheless, while Canada trails the aforementioned nations in overall orthopedic rsearch productivity, we showed that it actually ranks third in terms of qualitative metrics. With a %Q1 of 36.5% and a CNCI of 1.22, Canada actually outperformed the US, which had a %Q1 of 35.3% and a CNCI of 1.14 over the study period, and the Asian countries.

Few studies to date have performed qualitative analyses on a country’s orthopedic research productivity to better define its global impact. One such study by Saab and colleagues showed an increase in orthopedic research productivity in France over the past 10 years.14 France’s Société Française de Chirurgie Orthopédique et Traumatologique (SOFCOT) has 3979 members whereas the Canadian Orthopaedic Assocation has 1800 members, and thus France is a much larger publishing power. It is therefore important to highlight that a country’s academic productivity is nuanced by its publishing power and that a smaller overall number of publications does not necessarily indicate a lesser impact on the scientific community. Qualitative metrics may be a more useful measure of a country’s academic productivity. Furthermore, quality-based analysis serves as a check and balance to publication in 1 of a growing number of predatory and low-impact journals. For example, the number of predatory journals (journals that either do not or only in part adhere to the recommendations of the Committee on Publication Ethics and the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) has increased exponentially, from 18 in 201125 to 8000 in 2019.26 These journals encourage rapid publication at the expense of peer review in 50% of cases27 and have contributed to the dilution of the quality of the medical literature in orthopedics and other disciplines.

Our study also identified a somewhat exclusive collaborative network between orthopedic surgeons at 6 anglophone universities. Collaboration on multicentre research has been shown to produce studies with larger sample sizes, often allowing researchers to conduct studies on rarer conditions that would otherwise not have been possible using a single-centre design, increase the generalizability of conclusions and promote networking.28 Interestingly, while these 6 universities still had the highest level of jointly produced publications toward the end of the study period, they collaborated with a number of other Canadian academic centres, including the 3 francophone ones based in Quebec, thus growing the network of orthopedic collaboration to the national level. Going forward, this trend has the potential to further increase data sharing and improve the quality of orthopedic publications originating in Canada, where interacademic competition is less fierce than in countries with primarily private health care systems.

The strength of this study is its originality. To date, no study has evaluated the influence of Canadian universities and the collaborative networks between surgeons, to our knowledge. The themes studied correspond to those described in the literature, particularly for osteoarthritis and traumatology. We are also unaware of any study to date that has assessed Canada’s place among the major influential nations in orthopedics, evaluated the role of Canadian universities in the scientific research output of orthopedics or assessed Canadian publication networks in orthopedics.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. It included only publications in orthopedics and did not analyze the influence of orthopedic surgeons in other disciplines in the literature, such as infectious diseases, oncology and biomaterials. The number of orthopedic journals indexed in the Web of Science has increased substantially, but there are many journals that are not indexed; this poses a risk of information bias. Our study was descriptive in nature; it did not look for significant differences between the periods studied because they are fundamentally different owing to the exponential growth in publications over the last 10 years. We therefore focused on variations in market share and bibliometric impact parameters. Finally, we did not study the influence of university and provincial research funding and fellowship programs, which may vary among orthopedic departments and may be the cause of important differences between institutions in terms of both quantitative and qualitative analyses of research output.

Conclusion

The body of Canadian orthopedic literature has grown consistently over the past 20 years, and despite the overall leadership of the US and other developed nations such as China and Japan, Canada ranks sixth among the most influential countries in terms of quality and quantity of orthopedic research. Given the growing trend of national collaboration on orthopedic publications between academic centres, this rank is only expected to improve in the future with increased data sharing, networking and production of high-impact research.
