ABSTRACT
Key Points•mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination induces functional T helper 1–skewed CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses after HCT and CAR-T therapy.•T-cell responses did not differ between patients vaccinated earlier vs later after cellular therapy within the time frames studied.

Introduction

In a large multicenter prospective study (n = 466), we previously reported spike-specific immunoglobulin G, neutralizing antibodies, and T-cell receptor (TCR) responses after severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccination in patients after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (allo-HSCT), autologous HCT (auto-HSCT), and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell (CAR-T) therapy. A key finding was that vaccine immunogenicity was comparable between patients vaccinated <4 months vs 4 to 12 months after therapy,1,2 challenging the assumption that delayed vaccination results in more robust responses and confers superior immunity. As the largest prospective SARS-CoV-2 vaccine study in this population, it provided encouraging evidence to support early vaccination, which is especially important for patients in the early post–cellular therapy period who are at high risk of morbidity and mortality from coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19).3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Although TCR profiling provides insights into the breadth and clonality of the anti–COVID-19 T-cell response, it does not fully capture the functional capacity of vaccine-induced T-cell immunity. T-cell immunity is critical for viral clearance and modulating the severity of viral disease,8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 but T-cell responses to vaccination remain poorly described in this population.

Therefore, the motivation for this work was to evaluate antigen-specific functional T-cell immunity to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in patients undergoing cellular therapies vaccinated within the first year after treatment. Specifically, we aimed to quantify the magnitude of T-cell response to SARS-CoV-2 antigen in vitro and to define key T-cell subsets responsible for cytokine production. Additionally, we sought to explore correlations between functional T-cell responses, TCR repertoires, and antibody levels to provide a more comprehensive understanding of adaptive immunity in this high-risk population. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate functional, phenotypic, and repertoire-level analyses of vaccine-induced T-cell immunity in this setting.

Methods

Participants and study design

The study was a prospective, multicenter, observational study conducted by the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research and the Blood and Marrow Transplant Clinical Trials Network (Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research SC21-07/Blood and Marrow Transplant Clinical Trials Network 2101).1,2 We enrolled patients of any age who underwent an allo- or auto- HSCT or CAR-T therapy and for whom a first posttreatment messenger RNA (mRNA) SARS-CoV-2 vaccine within 12 months of treatment was planned. Type of SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, number of doses, and timing after treatment was at the discretion of the participating centers. To reflect real-world practice, patients with a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination before cellular therapy were also included. This analysis represents a substudy (n = 68) of our previously published prospective multicenter cohort of 466 patients who received SARS-CoV-2 vaccination after cellular therapy. Samples for this study were selected from patients who underwent TCR profiling, prioritizing those with a baseline sample and at least 2 follow-up time points, based on sample availability.

Sample collection

Blood was collected within prespecified windows: (1) within 2 weeks before the first vaccination (pre-V1), (2) at least 3 weeks after the V1 and within 1 week before the second (post-V1), (3 and 4) 1 to 5 weeks after the V2 and V3 (post-V2 and post-V3, respectively), and (5) at end-of-study (7-9 months after enrollment). Some participants received a V4 or V5 before the post-V3 or end-of-study time points. An overview of the sampling schedule is shown in supplemental Figure 1.

Binding and neutralizing antibodies and SARS-CoV-2–specific TCR

Methods for detection of binding and neutralizing antibodies and SARS-CoV-2–specific TCR, as well as results of these analyses were previously reported.1,2 Functional T-cell responses in this study were compared with the antibody responses and TCR sequencing results reported in the larger previously reported cohort. In the aforementioned study, TCR sequencing was performed at Adaptive Biotechnologies (Seattle, WA) using the T-Detect ImmunoSEQ assay classifier, which classified samples as positive, negative, or no call (insufficient TCR rearrangements) based on a predefined previously validated SARS-CoV-2–specific TCR signature. In addition, quantitative measures of TCR breadth (proportion of SARS-CoV-2–associated clones) and depth (extent of clonal expansion) were assessed.2

Baseline immunophenotyping

Baseline lymphocyte subsets were assessed by spectral flow cytometry in the subset of the original 466-patient cohort with available samples (n = 370) using cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs); full experimental details are provided in the supplemental Methods (supplemental Table 2).

T-cell stimulation

Recombinant spike glycoprotein receptor binding domain (RBD) from SARS-CoV-2 was acquired through BEI Resources, National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, National Institutes of Health. This RBD contains 223 residues of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, spanning amino acids 319 through 541, as defined in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) entries PDB 6VSB and the reference strain Wuhan-Hu-1 (PDB 6XR8). For T-cell stimulation, cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed, washed, and plated in 96-well U-bottom plates (Nunc) at a density of 1 × 106 PBMCs per well in complete medium (RPMI 1640 [Thermo Fisher Scientific] supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum [Gibco]). Cells were rested overnight before stimulation. The SARS-CoV-2 stimulation assay was based on previously described methods15,16 with cell concentration and stimulation times determined through experimental optimization. Cells were stimulated for 8 hours at 37°C, with brefeldin A (1 μg/mL; Sigma Aldrich) added for the final 5 hours. After the 8-hour incubation, cells were collected for flow cytometric analysis of intracellular cytokine expression. An equimolar dimethyl sulfoxide stimulation was included as a negative control.

Intracellular cytokine staining assay

After stimulation with SARS-CoV-2 protein, PBMCs were collected, washed, and pelleted. For surface staining, 1 × 106 PBMCs were resuspended in 100 μL phosphate-buffered saline and incubated with human TruStain FcX Fc receptor blocking solution (BioLegend) and LIVE/DEAD fixable blue dead cell stain (Invitrogen) for 20 minutes at room temperature (RT), shielded from light. After washing in flow wash buffer (RPMI 1640, no phenol red, plus 4% fetal bovine serum and 0.01% sodium azide), cells were incubated with the appropriate surface staining antibody mix for 20 minutes at RT in the dark, using Brilliant staining buffer (BD Biosciences) and CellBlox blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After surface staining, cells were washed in flow wash buffer, resuspended in fixation/permeabilization solution (BD Biosciences) and incubated at 4°C for 10 hours in the dark. Cells were subsequently washed twice with permeabilization buffer (BD Biosciences) and stained with intracellular antibodies for 20 minutes at RT in the dark. After staining, cells were washed with permeabilization buffer and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline for acquisition. All samples were analyzed on a Cytek Aurora 5L spectral flow cytometer. Details of the surface and intracellular antibodies, including clone and fluorophore, are listed in supplemental Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Antibody concentrations were optimized by titration. T-cell subset gating strategies are provided in supplemental Figure 2. Activation-induced marker–positive gates were defined relative to the unstimulated condition for each donor.

Statistical analysis

For each sample, cytokine expression was defined as the ratio of the proportion of cytokine-positive cells under activation to that under the unstimulated (negative control) condition. Log-fold changes (natural log) in cytokine expression were then calculated between the postvaccination time point and the prevaccination baseline (pre-V1). The postvaccination time point was preferentially selected as visit 4 (after 3 vaccine doses), if visit 4 data were unavailable, visit 3 was used instead. If neither visit 4 nor visit 3 data were available, the final available postvaccination time point (typically visit 5) was used. This selected postvaccination time point, used in the paired analysis with pre-V1, is hereafter referred to as Vlate. Patients lacking postvaccination data were excluded from subsequent analyses. Based on our definition of log-fold changes in cytokine expression, a positive log-fold change implies an increased expression of cytokine from before to after vaccination. To explore baseline immunological correlates of vaccine responsiveness, baseline CD19+ B-cell and CD4+ T-cell counts were log2-transformed due to their skewed distributions and correlated with log-fold changes in cytokine expression using Pearson correlation. For each patient group, log-fold changes in cytokine expression were tested against the null value of 0 using 1-sample signed-rank tests. Pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

To explore which immune cell subsets were associated with functional responses, we defined a subset of “responders” as patients who showed a statistically significant increase (P < .05 by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test) in all 4 cytokine readouts (interferon gamma [IFN-γ] and tumor necrosis factor α [TNF-α] in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) from baseline (pre-V1) to after vaccination (Vlate). This definition was used exclusively for correlation analyses with T-cell phenotypes and not to define clinical responses.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of the National Marrow Donor Program. All participants provided written or verbal informed consent, as appropriate. This study followed the STROBE (strengthening of the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology) reporting guidelines for observational studies.

Baseline immunophenotyping in the full cohort

At baseline, flow cytometry of unstimulated samples from the full cohort (n = 370) showed distinct distributions of lymphocyte subsets across treatment groups (supplemental Table 3). Patients after allo-HSCT had higher proportions of B cells compared with both auto-HSCT and CAR-T recipients, whereas CD8+ T cells were expanded in the latter 2 groups (adjusted P < .001). Auto-HSCT and CAR-T recipients exhibited higher frequencies of effector memory and terminally differentiated effector memory CD8+ T cells, whereas allo-HCT recipients had higher fractions of naïve and central memory (CM) CD4+ T cells. Regulatory T-cell frequencies were comparable across groups. Overall, these data illustrate expected differences in immune reconstitution between treatment modalities. Compared with the marked differences in lymphocyte subsets by treatment type, baseline immune profiles were largely similar between patients vaccinated <4 months vs 4 to 12 months after therapy (supplemental Table 4). The subgroup of 68 patients included in the functional T-cell analyses was representative of the overall cohort regarding baseline immunophenotypic composition (supplemental Figure 3).

Measuring changes in COVID-19–directed T-cell function before and after vaccination

Phenotypic origin of functional T-cell responses

In the overall cohort, CD4+ IFN-γ responses were primarily driven by CM T cells, whereas CD8+ TNF-α responses were dominated by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Among patients classified as T-cell vaccine responders, defined as those with a significant increase in cytokine levels from before to after vaccination for all 4 outcomes (CD4+ and CD8+ IFN-γ and TNF-α), the strongest correlations with cytokine production were observed for CD4+ CM cells and CD8+ CTLs. When stratified by treatment group, distinct patterns emerged: in the auto-HCT group, cytotoxic and stem cell memory T cells were most strongly associated with responses; in the allo-HCT group, CM subsets predominated; and in the CAR-T group, multiple subsets contributed, including CD4+ effector memory, and CD4+ naïve and CD8+ naïve T cells (supplemental Figure 4A-E).

T-cell responses in patients with previous antigen exposure

The log-fold changes in T-cell cytokine responses (IFN-γ and TNF-α) between before and after vaccination did not significantly differ between patients who were SARS-CoV-2–naïve and those with previous antigen exposure (via infection or vaccination) before cellular therapy. Although median log-fold increases tended to be numerically higher in the COVID-19–naïve group, the between-group differences were not statistically significant for either CD4+ or CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ or TNF-α (supplemental Figure 6). Baseline cytokine expression levels were also similar between groups (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study, we found that SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination induced increases in antigen-specific IFN-γ and TNF-α production by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells across all cellular therapy groups (allo-HSCT, auto-HSCT, and CAR-T), with no significant differences detected between participants vaccinated early (<4 months) and late (4-12 months) after cellular therapy. Importantly, the 68 patients included in these functional analyses were both clinically and immunophenotypically similar to those in the larger cohort. These T-cell responses were primarily driven by CD4+ CM cells and CD8+ CTLs. Functional T-cell responses correlated with postvaccination antibody responses and baseline B-cell counts, but not with postvaccination T-cell repertoire metrics. The absence of a healthy control group limits interpretation of the magnitude of functional T-cell responses. Accordingly, our analyses focused on within-patient changes after vaccination and relative comparisons across cellular therapy groups.

The predominant increase in TNF-α and IFN-γ responses after vaccination is consistent with a classic Th1-type immune activation and associated with effective antiviral responses.19,20 Previous studies using comparable intracellular cytokine stimulation assays in healthy individuals have demonstrated Th1-skewed IFN-γ and TNF-α responses after mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination,15,18 consistent with the qualitative pattern observed in this study, although direct quantitative comparisons across studies are not possible. In individuals who are immunocompromised, the preservation of IFN-γ and TNF-α responses is encouraging, because these pathways are often more robust than other cytokine axes.20,21

The observed postvaccination functional T-cell responses were phenotypically diverse, reflecting contributions from several T-cell subsets. In the overall cohort, increases in CD4+ IFN-γ were most strongly associated with CD4+ CM cells, whereas CD8+ TNF-α increases were dominated by cytotoxic CD8+ T cells; this pattern was most evident among stringently defined “responders” (concordant increases in all 4 cytokine readouts). Stratified analyses showed therapy-specific skews: auto-HSCT responses aligned with CTL and T-central memory stem cells (CMS) pools; allo-HSCT responses were CM-predominant; and CAR-T responses were broader, with contributions from CD4+ effector memory, CD4+ naïve, and CD8+ naïve subsets. Collectively, these data indicate that the dominant correlates of vaccine-reactive cytokine production are CD4+ CM and CD8+ cytotoxic compartments, with variation by treatment context.

In our previous papers, the main predictors of a robust antibody responses to SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination were previous COVID-19 infection, previous COVID-19 vaccination, and higher absolute B-cell counts,1,2 underscoring the role of preexisting humoral memory and adequate B-cell reconstitution. In contrast, in this study, functional T-cell responses did not differ between patients with or without previous antigen exposure; both groups exhibited similar log-fold increases after vaccination. This finding contrasts with previous reports showing that hybrid immunity (infection plus vaccination) augments not only antibody responses,22,23 but also T-cell responses.24 In immunocompromised hosts, recall antibody responses can be rapidly amplified by memory B cells, whereas T-cell expansion may depend on naïve or functionally exhausted populations that are less responsive to previous antigen encounter. The interval between priming and revaccination may also have permitted contraction of spike-specific T cells while sparing long-lived memory B cells, thereby reducing any observable priming advantage at the T-cell level. Overall, these data suggest an attenuated hybrid immunity signal for T-cell responses in this immunocompromised population.

Baseline circulating B-cell counts positively correlated with IFN-γ and TNF-α responses, likely reflecting more robust immune recovery. In contrast, no correlation was observed with baseline CD4+ T-cell counts. B cells can enhance T-cell responses through antigen presentation, costimulation, and cytokine secretion.25 Although B cells are not the dominant antigen-presenting cell population, particularly early after HSCT26 or CAR-T therapy, they contribute uniquely to high-affinity antigen presentation and to supporting T follicular helper cell function, which reinforces coordinated humoral and cellular immunity.27 The T- and B-cell compartments are therefore closely interconnected, with each shaping the other’s activation, expansion, and memory formation, yet they remain functionally distinct. Indeed, we observed cytokine boosting at relatively low B-cell counts, suggesting that other antigen-presenting cell subsets can compensate for quantitative B-cell deficits and that long-lived or rapidly inducible T-cell populations can respond to vaccination independently of B-cell help. This interplay highlights the adaptability of the immune system after cellular therapies, as well as the potential for functional T-cell immunity to emerge even before full humoral recovery.

We observed significant log-fold increases in functional T-cell responses even among patients failing to mount a protective antibody response. Although many studies report a strong correlation between B- and T-cell responses,28 several others have demonstrated detectable T-cell immunity in the absence of an antibody responses29, 30, 31, 32, 33 and some studies have even shown an enhanced cellular response in patients on B-cell depleting therapies,29 suggesting that the immune system may compensate for impaired humoral immunity through preserved or even augmented T-cell function.

Functional T-cell responses occurred independently of both classifier-based (T-Detect) and quantitative (breadth/depth) repertoire metrics. TCR assays are not functional and do not assess the ability of T cells to proliferate, produce cytokines, or execute effector functions. Additionally, it is possible that the TCR sequencing approach did not capture all SARS-CoV-2–specific T cells, particularly if relevant clones were not included in the reference database or were present at very low frequencies. Moreover, immunologic assays, including serologic and repertoire-based measures, do not consistently correlate with functional responses, especially in immunocompromised hosts.34, 35, 36 Together, these findings underscore the complexity of assessing T-cell responses to vaccination in patients who are immunocompromised as well as a lack of standardization in how such responses are measured and interpreted.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections were captured after the final sampling time point, event numbers were too small to link functional T-cell responses with clinical protection. The use of multiple cytokine readouts and the absence of established thresholds for functional T-cell responses limited the ability to define clear responder categories. Furthermore, we selected the study cohort based on availability of paired samples rather than being representative of the larger study; this limited statistical power for subgroup analyses and precluded adjustment for several potentially relevant clinical covariates, including immunosuppressive therapies, GVHD status, CAR-T target, and vaccine-related factors. Because this was an observational study, vaccination timing was determined by treating physicians, and comparisons between early and later vaccination groups may therefore be subject to residual confounding, particularly related to chronic GVHD and immunosuppression. Strengths of the study include its prospective, multicenter design and detailed functional immunologic characterization. Although the analyzed subset was clinically and immunophenotypically similar to the larger cohort, findings should be interpreted within the context of these limitations.

In summary, mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination elicited functional, Th1-skewed CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses after allo-HSCT, auto-HSCT, and CAR-T therapy, including in patients without detectable antibody responses or SARS-CoV-2–specific TCR signatures. Although clinical protection could not be directly assessed in this study, functional T-cell responses were not inferior when vaccination was initiated earlier after cellular therapy within the time frames studied, motivating future studies to delineate the optimal timing of vaccine initiation early after treatment.
