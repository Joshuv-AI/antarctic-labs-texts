Fecal microbiota succession in dairy calves is driven by age and modestly influenced by colostrum source
Authors: Gercino Ferreira Virginio Júnior, Cristiane Regina Tomaluski, Sophia Cattleya Dondé, Ana Paula da Silva, Ariany Faria de Toledo, Lucas William Mendes, Horácio Montenegro, Luiz Lehmann Coutinho, Carla Maris Machado Bittar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColostrum is crucial for the survival and health of neonatal calves, providing passive immunity and influencing early gut microbial colonization. This study evaluated the impact of three colostrum sources: fresh maternal colostrum (FMC), frozen maternal colostrum (FC), and colostrum replacer (CR) on the development of the fecal microbiota in Holstein calves. Fifteen newborn calves were randomly assigned to one of three treatments (n = 5 per group). Fecal samples were collected at birth (meconium), daily during the first seven days of life, and at weeks 2, 4, 8 (weaning), and 10 (post-weaning). Microbial composition was assessed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Alpha diversity increased over time (P < 0.001), with no significant differences observed among treatments. Microbiota succession followed a consistent trajectory across all groups, characterized by an early dominance of Escherichia/Shigella, which gradually transitioned to increased relative abundances of Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and Faecalibacterium over time. Notably, calves fed FMC showed a significantly higher fecal relative abundance of Lactobacillus during the first three days of life (excluding day 0, meconium) compared to those receiving FC (P = 0.046) or CR (P = 0.44), although the overall bacterial community structure was primarily influenced by calf age. These findings suggest that while colostrum source does not impact overall microbial diversity, it may modulate early colonization by beneficial bacteria. Introduction

The initial microbial colonization of the gastrointestinal tract in dairy calves plays a crucial role in shaping their health, immune development, and long-term productivity1. This early colonization refers to the establishment of pioneer microbial communities in the gut immediately after birth. One of the first and most critical factors influencing this process is colostrum feeding, provided shortly after birth, which contributes to seeding and shaping the neonatal gut microbiota2,3. In addition to providing immunoglobulins, colostrum contains a wide range of nutrients, growth factors, and microorganisms that support the development of the intestinal microbiota4,5. Fresh maternal colostrum is the most common and preferred source used in dairy industry colostrum management strategies, which may also involve the use of pasteurized colostrum, frozen pooled colostrum, or commercial colostrum replacers when maternal colostrum is not available or adequate4,6,7.

Bovine colostrum from healthy cows is predominantly composed of bacteria from the phyla Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteria8,9. Its core microbiome includes genera such as Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, Lactococcus, Streptococcus, Paraclostridium, and Romboutsia8,10, which contribute to early gut colonization and immune development in neonatal calves1,10. Early microbial exposure is essential, as it initiates gut colonization. Within hours after birth, calves that receive colostrum show increased colonization by beneficial commensals such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, whereas Escherichia coli is more abundant in colostrum-deprived calves5. Thus, colostrum acts not only as a vehicle for passive immunity but also as a microbial inoculum that helps shape the initial gut microbiota1.

Freezing colostrum is a widely used preservation method to guarantee high-quality colostrum availability on the farm, but it may damage immune cells and reduce the viability of probiotic bacteria through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, ice crystal formation, osmotic imbalance, and protein denaturation4,11,12. Crucially, studies have demonstrated that frozen colostrum, by eliminating viable maternal cells, impairs the balanced development of the innate and adaptive immune system13,14, and is associated with heightened systemic inflammation, reduced gut microbiome modulation, and a greater need for antimicrobial treatment compared to fresh colostrum15.In a companion study, the microbiological quality of fresh and frozen maternal colostrum was evaluated by total plate count and Escherichia coli, with no differences detected, although only these parameters were measured6. Although practical, the long-term impacts of frozen colostrum on gut microbiome development are still not fully elucidated.

Colostrum replacers are recommended when high-quality maternal colostrum is unavailable. These products are subject to rigorous manufacturing and quality control standards, which may reduce neonatal exposure to microbial contaminants4,16. However, differences in immunological and nutritional composition across colostrum sources may affect microbial colonization patterns in diverse ways4,17,18.

The interaction between colostrum and the neonatal gut microbiota is particularly critical during the first days of life, when the gastrointestinal system is still developing. Inadequate colostrum management has been linked to neonatal diarrhea and higher morbidity rates4,5. However, studies investigating the influence of colostrum on gut microbiota have primarily focused on the immediate postnatal period5,19–21. Limited information is available regarding the impact of different colostrum sources on the temporal dynamics of microbial development, particularly during and after the weaning period.

The temporal progression of the calf gut microbiome is critical for early development. From birth to weaning, the intestinal microbial community undergoes dynamic changes, especially during dietary transitions22–24. During the preweaning phase, when the rumen is still immature, the large intestine plays a key role in fermenting undigested nutrients and producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), amino acids, and vitamins essential for growth and gut development25,26. Consequently, fecal sampling provides a non-invasive method to monitor these microbial communities in the large intestine23,24,27,28.

In this context, we hypothesized that the source of colostrum administered to neonatal calves influences the establishment and longitudinal development of the intestinal microbiota. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of three colostrum sources, i.e., fresh maternal colostrum, frozen colostrum, and colostrum replacer, on the diversity, structure, and composition of the fecal microbiota in dairy calves during the first 10 weeks of life.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All procedures with calves were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations approved by ‘Luiz de Queiroz’ College of Agriculture, University of São Paulo, Brazil. All the experimental procedures were approved following the ethics by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in the “Luiz de Queiroz” College of Agriculture, University of São Paulo, Brazil (Protocol no. 2018.5.586.11.7). We confirm that this study was carried out in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Animals, experimental design, and management

The study was conducted from April to September 2019 at the “Evilásio de Camargo” Experimental Calf Unit, Department of Animal Science, Luiz de Queiroz College of Agriculture (ESALQ/USP), in Piracicaba, São Paulo, Brazil. Thirty-nine newborn Holstein calves (32.15 ± 1.97 kg BW) were enrolled. Performance, immunity transfer, and health data from this cohort study were reported by Tomaluski et al. 6.

Calvings were supervised to ensure immediate calf-dam separation to prevent colostrum ingestion before treatment allocation. Calves were weighed at birth with a digital scale (ICS-300, Coimma Ltda., Dracena, SP, Brazil), and had their umbilical cords disinfected with a 7% iodine solution.

A randomized complete block design was used, with calf sex, birth weight, and birth date as blocking factors. Calves were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups. Each calf received only its assigned colostrum source: Fresh maternal colostrum (FMC, n = 11): at 10% of birth weight, collected from its own dam (> 50 g/L IgG); Frozen maternal colostrum (FC; n = 15) at 10% of birth weight, obtained from a colostrum bank composed of individually collected high-quality maternal colostrum (> 50 g/L IgG); Colostrum replacer (CR; n = 13): 1.5 doses of a commercial product (SCCL®, Alta Genetics, Saskatoon, Canada), providing 150 g of IgG, as recommended by Godden et al29. All calves were fed within 2 h after birth to ensure timely passive transfer, and no calf received colostrum from another source.

Fresh maternal colostrum was collected immediately after calving, and a subsample was cooled to 22 °C for quality assessment using a colostrometer (Suprivet, Divinópolis, MG, Brazil), given its stronger association with IgG concentration than Brix refractometry30. Each calf, in this treatment, received the colostrum produced by its own dam when the measured quality exceeded 50 g/L IgG.

Before feeding, both fresh maternal colostrum that had cooled after collection and frozen colostrum from the bank were gently reheated in a water bath (maximum 50–55 °C), ensuring a delivery temperature close to physiological levels (approximately 38–40 °C). Frozen colostrum was additionally thawed in the same type of water bath and re-evaluated with the colostrometer prior to being offered to the calves6.

Commercial CR was prepared according to the instructions by mixing 705 g of powder in 1.5 L of potable water at 45 °C. The guaranteed composition of the colostrum replacer is listed in Supplementary Table S1. As indicated on the label, the formulation includes bovine colostrum powder along with polyethylene glycol monooleate, polyoxyethylene monooleate sorbitan, butylated hydroxytoluene, tocopherols, and calcium silicate6.

Calves in the FMC group consumed a mean of 304.77 g IgG, those in the FC group 292.57 g, and calves in the CR group 150.25 g (mean total IgG intake per calf; individual values only for the 15 animals used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table S2. For full details on the complete cohort, see Tomaluski et al. 6). The colostrum was initially offered using 4-L bottles. If the calf refused to drink or if any volume remained, the esophageal tube was used to ensure that the full required amount of colostrum was consumed. All feedings were performed by the same trained person, and detailed records of individual intake and tube-feeding events are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

From the second feeding onward, all calves received 6 L/d of whole milk divided into two meals (07:00 and 17:00). Milk was offered in open buckets, and calves were trained to drink from the bucket starting at 2 d of age. The commercial calf starter (“Bezerra Ag Milk,” Agroceres Multimix Nutrição Animal Ltda., Rio Claro, SP, Brazil) was provided ad libitum, freshened once daily, always after supplying the liquid diet. Water was offered ad libitum from day 1. The composition of the starter and milk diet is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Immediately after birth, calves were individually housed in suspended pens (113 × 140 cm), with wood shavings bedding, inside a ventilated barn until 14 days of age. They were then moved to outdoor individual wooden hutches. In both housing systems, calves were kept separate, preventing any physical or visual contact between animals.

The milk-feeding period lasted 56 days (8 weeks). Weaning started at 57 days of age with a gradual reduction of 1 L/day split into two meals. Complete weaning occurred at 62 days. Post-weaning calves were group-housed in a paddock with access to water, hay, and the same starter concentrate. The grass in paddock was maintained short through regular cutting, preventing grazing. The study ended at 70 days of age.

Calf health parameters, including fecal score, hematocrit, and days with fever, were monitored throughout the study following standard procedures (see Tomaluski et al. 6).

Animals enrolled in for Microbiome analysis

For fecal microbiome analysis, a subset of 15 calves (33.72 ± 2.33 kg BW at birth), comprising 5 calves per treatment, was randomly selected from the original cohort (see Tomaluski et al. 6). Selection was performed within five complete blocks from the study to ensure balanced representation of all treatments. Although the sample size is limited, previous studies from our group have successfully detected meaningful differences in calf gut microbial communities using similar or slightly larger numbers23,24,31.

Fecal sample collection

Fecal samples were collected at multiple time points: The first sampling occurred on day 0 (meconium, within approximately one hour after birth and before colostrum feeding), days 1–7 (daily), and later on days 14 (week 2), 28 (week 4), 56 (week 8; weaning), and 70 (week 10; post-weaning). Daily and weekly samples were collected in the morning, approximately two hours after milk feeding. For days 1–7, if a calf did not have feces at the scheduled sampling time, the sample was disregarded to avoid interference with the next day’s collection. For weekly samplings at weeks 2, 4, 8, and 10, a second attempt was made the following day if feces were not present; samples were disregarded if feces remained unavailable.

For analysis purposes, the first week (days 0–7) was considered separately to capture daily variation immediately after birth. Pre-weaning samples include weekly collections at weeks 1, 2, 4, and 8, while post-weaning samples correspond to week 10. This distinction allows evaluation of both early-life dynamics and longer-term changes in the calf gut microbiome.

Approximately 2 g of feces were collected directly from the rectum using clean gloves, which were changed between calves to prevent cross-contamination23,24. Samples were immediately transferred to sterile tubes and rapidly frozen when stored at − 20 °C.

DNA Extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the QIAamp® Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with a bead-beating step incorporated according to the modifications suggested by Yu and Morrison 32, to enhance cell lysis and DNA yield. DNA integrity was assessed using 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentrations were determined with a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA).

The V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced at the Center for Functional Genomic Research at ESALQ/USP using the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end) with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3. Amplicon libraries were prepared following standard Illumina protocols. The V4 region was selected because it provides robust taxonomic resolution for bacterial communities and is widely used in microbiome studies, allowing reliable comparisons with other research.

Primer sequences included overhang adapters: Forward: 5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG; Reverse: 5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC.

Raw sequencing data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under the BioProject accession nº: PRJNA639165.

Bioinformatic analyses

Bioinformatic processing was conducted using R packages from the Bioconductor project 33,34, following the workflow described by Callahan et al35.. Sequencing reads were demultiplexed and processed using DADA236, to remove low-quality reads (< Q20) and chimeras, generating high-resolution amplicon sequencing variants (ASVs). Taxonomic assignments was performed using DADA2’s implementation of the naïve Bayesian classifier37 trained on the SILVA v138 reference database38 with the assignTaxonomy() function (default parameters), followed by species-level assignment using addSpecies() when exact matches were available. Only assignments with bootstrap confidence ≥ 50% were retained, and unclassified ranks were labeled as “NA.” The resulting taxonomy table included hierarchical levels from Kingdom to Species. ASVs classified below the family level (i.e., not assigned to at least Family) were removed, and redundant ASVs representing the same species were collapsed.

Taxonomic assignments and abundance matrices were imported into phyloseq39 for α- and β-diversity analyses. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon index and observed ASV richness, and differences among groups were evaluated using ANOVA. For β-diversity, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on weighted UniFrac distances was used to explore the effects of treatment, week, and their interaction. Community composition differences among groups were evaluated using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA)40. Differential abundance analyses at the genus and ASV levels were performed using Welch’s two-tailed t-test followed by Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction in STAMP software41.

For early-life analyses, samples collected on days 1 to 3 were combined to reduce the high inter-individual variability typically observed immediately after birth and to provide a more stable representation of the initial microbial colonization. This consolidated early-life period (days 1–3) was then compared with day 7, which reflects the subsequent stage of neonatal microbial establishment.

To evaluate microbial dynamics in relation to the milk-feeding phase and the transition to solid feed, weekly samples were organized according to the feeding regimen. Weeks 1 to 8 corresponded to the milk-feeding period, and week 10 represented the post-weaning stage. Comparisons were performed between weeks 1 and 8 to capture changes from the beginning to the end of the milk-feeding period, and between weeks 8 and 10 to assess the effects of weaning.

Results

The first week of life (days 0–7)

Alpha diversity analysis revealed no significant differences among treatments in terms of fecal microbiota richness (observed ASVs; P = 0.8663) or diversity (Shannon index; P = 0.8448) at any of the evaluated time points (Supplementary figure S1a and S1b). Regardless of treatment, both richness and diversity were significantly higher at birth (day 0; P < 0.001), followed by a marked decline beginning on day 1. After this initial drop, alpha diversity indices remained relatively stable through day 7 (Fig. 1a and b).

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) ordination based on weighted UniFrac revealed significant effects of both treatment (PERMANOVA; P = 0.0226) and age (P = 0.0001) on the fecal microbiota composition (Fig. 2a). Although colostrum source influenced microbial community structure, age accounted for the most pronounced variation, with distinct clustering of samples becoming evident from day 3 onward (Fig. 2b). Notably, fecal samples from calves in the FMC group exhibited greater dissimilarity on days 3 (P = 0.049), 4 (P = 0.046), 5 (P = 0.049), and 7 (P = 0.041), while samples from the FC group also showed increased dissimilarity on day 7.

A total of 23 bacterial phyla were detected in fecal samples during the first week of life (Supplementary figure S2a). Firmicutes was the predominant phylum overall (43.82% ± 2.10), followed by Proteobacteria (35.74% ± 2.73) and Bacteroidota (16.27% ± 1.51). Temporal analysis (Supplementary figure S2b) revealed a sharp peak in Proteobacteria on day 1 (65.72% ± 8.75), followed by a steady decline to below 25% by day 5. In contrast, Firmicutes increased progressively, becoming dominant (42.38% ± 6.q51) by day 4. Bacteroidota gradually increased from day 3, reaching approximately 20% by day 7. Actinobacteriota and Fusobacteriota remained consistently low, with slight increases toward the end of the week.

A total of 423 bacterial genera were identified across 23 phyla, with 85 genera remaining unclassified, accounting for 5.83% of the total relative abundance (Fig. 3a). The most abundant genera were Escherichia/Shigella (25.72% ± 2.51), Bacteroides (12.02% ± 1.30), and Lactobacillus (11.25% ± 1.33). Temporal shifts in the relative abundance of key genera were evident during the first week of life (Fig. 3b). Escherichia/Shigella peaked at 50.13% ± 9.74 on day 1, followed by a steady decline to below 14% by day 6. In contrast, Lactobacillus increased progressively, becoming dominant by day 5 and maintaining a level of approximately 15% thereafter. Bacteroides also increased steadily, reaching levels similar to Lactobacillus by day 7. Faecalibacterium showed a delayed but consistent increase, reaching 17.97% ± 4.95 by day 7. Butyricicoccus remained consistently low, with minimal variation over time.

To assess temporal dynamics within each treatment group, we compared bacterial composition between early time points (days 1 to 3) and day 7 (Supplementary figure S3). Differential abundance analysis of ASVs revealed significant shifts in taxonomic composition over time across all treatments. In the FC treatment, ASVs classified as Streptococcus (P < 0.001), Escherichia/Shigella (P < 0.001), and various Clostridium clades (P < 0.04; Supplementary figure S3a) showed higher abundance in early samples. A total of 25 bacterial phyla were identified in fecal samples collected from birth through the post-weaning period (Supplementary figure S5a). Firmicutes predominated overall (38.94% ± 2.21), followed by Bacteroidota (34.65% ± 2.35) and Proteobacteria (12.85% ± 1.87). At birth, Firmicutes (42.69% ± 4.73) and Proteobacteria (32.88% ± 8.45) were dominant, while Bacteroidota (15.17% ± 3.44) and Fusobacteriota (4.16% ± 1.69) were less abundant (Supplementary figure S5a). Over time, Bacteroidota increased steadily, surpassing Firmicutes by week 4, and reaching 52.17% ± 3.85 by week 10 (Supplementary figure S5b). Firmicutes remained stable until week 8, then declined to 30.73% ± 2.51 by week 10. Proteobacteria declined progressively, falling below 4% by the end of week 4, but started to increase again by week 10 (8.31% ± 2.68). Fusobacteriota peaked at week 2 (25.30% ± 5.89) but dropped to < 1% post-weaning. Spirochaetota, initially rare (< 0.1%), emerged by week 8 and increased to 6.05% ± 2.48 by week 10.

Escherichia/Shigella was dominant at birth (17.64% ± 6.59) but decreased progressively to < 1% after week 4. Discussion

Impact of colostrum source on neonatal microbiota

Colostrum represents the initial functional interface between the external environment and the neonatal gastrointestinal tract, serving not only as a vehicle for passive immunity but also as a key modulator of early intestinal microbial colonization2,5,42. Its complex composition, rich in immunoglobulins, growth factors, immune cells, and commensal microorganisms, plays a critical role in establishing intestinal eubiosis, which has important implications for calf health, development, and long-term productivity4,43. Therefore, understanding how different colostrum sources influence the intestinal microbiota during the first days of life is essential for optimizing management strategies and ensuring a healthy start for calves.

Although microbial diversity or viability of the colostrum sources was not assessed, the companion paper showed no differences between fresh and frozen maternal colostrum in total bacterial counts or Escherichia coli. Likewise, the water used to reconstitute the colostrum replacer was not analyzed. However, given that calf age was the dominant factor shaping microbial succession, and that only minor, transient differences among colostrum sources were detected, it is unlikely that unmeasured microbial inputs from either colostrum or water substantially influenced the overall microbiota patterns observed.

This study evaluated the impact of three colostrum sources (i.e., fresh, frozen, and powder replacer) on the fecal microbiota of dairy calves during the pre- and post-weaning periods. Although some transient differences were detected in early life, such as minor shifts in Lactobacillus, the overall microbiota trajectories were largely shaped by calf age, with no consistent patterns attributable to the specific colostrum materials evaluated in this study.

Lactobacillus dynamics in the first days of life

Lactobacillus spp. are well-known early colonizers of the neonatal gastrointestinal tract and have been extensively studied for their potential as probiotics in ruminants5. These bacteria play a significant role in establishing a protective microbial barrier by producing lactic acid and antimicrobial compounds, such as bacteriocins and hydrogen peroxide, which inhibit pathogens like Escherichia coli5,44. Additionally, Lactobacillus supports gut homeostasis by modulating the host immune response, enhancing mucosal integrity, and stimulating the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines45. In calves, the early presence of Lactobacillus is associated with improved gut health, reduced incidence of diarrhea, and enhanced feed efficiency24,46,47. Their ability to ferment milk carbohydrates and adhere to intestinal epithelial cells allows effective colonization of the small intestine, creating a favorable environment for other beneficial microorganisms45,47.

Although differences in microbiota composition were not detected among treatments at later ages, the transient increase in Lactobacillus abundance observed early in life may still have contributed to functional benefits for calf performance. Consistent with the early increase in Lactobacillus observed in our study, a companion trial that followed the same cohort until weaning Tomaluski et al. 6, calves receiving fresh maternal colostrum showed greater average daily gain, improved feed efficiency, and better health indicators, such as fewer fever days and reduced diarrhea, compared with those fed colostrum replacer, whereas no significant differences were reported between the fresh and frozen colostrum groups.

To interpret these early differences, it is important to consider immunoglobulin intake. In that study, the authors attributed the superior performance of calves fed fresh maternal colostrum to their higher intake of immunoglobulins relative to calves fed colostrum replacer. Moreover, no differences in IgG mass were detected between fresh and frozen colostrum groups; however, freezing may reduce the viability of naturally occurring bacteria12, potentially limiting early microbial transfer and contributing to subtle differences in Lactobacillus establishment.

Although the benefits of Lactobacillus spp. are well recognized, their abundance is typically limited to the early weeks of life and gradually declines until weaning24,44, as observed in our data. Given this expected decline, strategies such as the use of prebiotics, probiotics, or synbiotic combinations have shown potential to enhance the establishment and persistence of lactic acid–producing bacteria in neonatal calves48–50. Management factors that reduce early-life stress may also favor microbial stabilization.

Fecal Microbiome diversity dynamics in dairy calves

In dairy calves, gut bacterial diversity and community composition evolve progressively with age, reflecting adaptations to dietary transitions, physiological development and the environment. These dynamics highlight the critical role of early microbial colonization in shaping gastrointestinal health and function1,42.

The most pronounced shifts in both alpha and beta diversity occurred during the first week of life, reflecting the highly dynamic nature of early microbial colonization. Although richness and diversity reached their lowest point on day 1, this pattern becomes particularly clear when the analysis is restricted to the first week of life; when the full experimental period is considered, this decline is less apparent. Early microbial trajectories can vary substantially depending on the temporal window analyzed: short-term patterns (days 0–7) reflect rapid and dynamic fluctuations immediately after birth, whereas longer-term patterns reveal the subsequent increase and stabilization that occur as the gastrointestinal ecosystem develops. Despite these temporal changes, the colostrum source had no significant effect on alpha diversity at any time point. In contrast, beta diversity analysis showed that both time and colostrum source influenced bacterial community structure during the neonatal period; however, only age remained a significant driver beyond weaning. Overall, these findings suggest that while colostrum modulates the initial establishment of the neonatal microbiota, its influence does not persist throughout the 10 weeks evaluated. The convergence of microbial profiles across treatment groups over time indicates that environmental factors play a predominant role in shaping microbial development. In previous studies from our group23,24,31, minimal differences in fecal microbiome diversity, structure, and composition were observed among dairy calves raised under similar housing conditions (wooden shelters). These findings highlight the need for further research to elucidate how environmental exposures shape the microbiome in dairy calves.

Temporal changes in fecal Microbiome composition

The fecal microbiome of dairy calves is a complex and dynamic bacterial community that undergoes significant changes from birth through the post-weaning period22,42. In this study, substantial shifts in fecal microbiota composition were observed over time, reflecting the progressive maturation of the gastrointestinal environment. During the first days of life, the bacterial communities were primarily composed by Proteobacteria, particularly Escherichia/Shigella, which declined sharply by the end of the first week23,24,51. These facultative anaerobes typically proliferate immediately after birth because the gut environment still contains residual oxygen52. Their rapid expansion contributes to the establishment of anaerobic conditions, enabling the subsequent increase in Firmicutes and Bacteroidota observed toward the end of the first week22,53. Although this succession aligns with previous literature, the specific factors determining the intensity of the early Escherichia/Shigella peak in our study cannot be precisely inferred.

From week 4 onward, Bacteroidota became the predominant phylum, surpassing 50% relative abundance by week 10, coinciding with the increased intake of starter concentrate and the onset of the weaning process6. At the genus level, a similar course was observed: the early dominance of Escherichia/Shigella was progressively replaced by increasing abundance of Bacteroides, Alloprevotella, and Faecalibacterium during the pre-weaning period. These genera are associated with carbohydrate fermentation, gut health, and SCFA production22,27,31. These compositional changes reflect a diet-driven bacterial succession and are consistent with previous findings that describe microbiota adaptation in response to the introduction of solid feed and ruminal development22,53,54.

This study provides a valuable glimpse into how early-life nutrition shapes gut microbiota development in dairy calves. A clear trend of microbial succession from facultative to obligate anaerobes appeared to occur, consistent with the expected establishment of an anaerobic gut environment alongside early physiological and nutritional changes. Colostrum source modestly influenced early microbial colonization, particularly the abundance of Lactobacillus, although these effects were not sustained beyond weaning. We acknowledge the limitations of the study, including the absence of microbiome compositional analyses of the initial diets (fresh maternal colostrum, frozen colostrum, and colostrum replacer) and the subsequent milk-feeding period (whole milk), which could have clarified dietary contributions to microbial patterns. The experiment was conducted under tightly controlled conditions with daily monitoring of animals, which is reflected in the growth performance reported in the companion study. While the small number of animals represents a limitation for microbiome analyses, such work is inherently resource-intensive, and even with this sample size we were able to identify meaningful trends in microbial succession. It is important to note that in commercial dairy settings, these effects may be influenced by factors not captured in the experiment, such as variable colostrum quality, environmental conditions, or less stringent health management. Overall, the results provide insights into early-life microbial dynamics that can inform the refinement of feeding practices to support gut health, growth, and resilience in calves, while highlighting avenues for future research integrating microbial, health, and performance outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that colostrum source is associated with a modest yet measurable influence on early gut microbial colonization in newborn dairy calves, particularly reflected in the increased relative abundance of Lactobacillus during the first days of life in calves fed fresh maternal colostrum. Despite these early differences, no significant long-term effects on overall microbial diversity or community structure were observed among treatment groups.

While the impact of colostrum on the fecal microbiota did not persist beyond the neonatal period, the transient enrichment of beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus may have functional implications for early-life health and performance, as suggested by complementary data on growth and disease resistance. These findings underscore the significance of early-life microbial programming. Furthermore, they emphasize the need for further research to elucidate how colostrum quality, microbial viability, and environmental exposures interact to influence microbiome dynamics and long-term outcomes in dairy calves.
