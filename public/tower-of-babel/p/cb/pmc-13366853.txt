Prehospital emergency medical services in Nepal: current status, challenges, and future directions
Authors: Sanjaya Karki, Ishan Sharma, Sagun Shrestha, Jasper Chell, Callum Bayliss, Ram Krishna Budhathoki, Sushma Thapaliya, Santosh Neupane, Praphul Kumar Jha, Vijay Chaudhary, Dhirendra Kumar Malla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNepal, a landlocked country in South Asia, is topographically diverse, consisting of mountains, hills, and plains (Terai). Geographic challenges impede EMS access, especially in remote, mountainous areas with substandard and deteriorated road infrastructure and Fragmented populations. While the nation’s healthcare system is gradually advancing, it remains largely centralized and underdeveloped. Prehospital medical care is still in its infancy and is virtually inaccessible to much of the population, especially in remote areas. Although various government policies have been introduced to address this issue, implementation has been inconsistent. This paper aims to provide an overview of the current state of prehospital medical care in Nepal, conduct an analysis of existing policy frameworks to identify key challenges and opportunities, thereby offering insights to guide the development and implementation of future policy initiatives and make recommendations for its future.MethodThis study is a narrative review and policy analysis which examines evidence by systematically searching PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and national databases for literature published between 2000 and 2025, supplemented by primary data and expert insights, and findings are thematically synthesized to assess the current status and challenges of prehospital emergency medical services in Nepal.ResultsPre-hospital emergency care in Nepal is still in a rudimentary phase. Analysis of the current system underscores that Inconsistent policy implementation, limited infrastructure, and a lack of standardized training are the key barriers to its development. Comparative cross- regional analysis shows that urban areas have relatively better access compared to rural regions, highlighting significant disparities.ConclusionAlthough progress has been made in pre hospital emergency care in recent decades, significant development is required to ensure timely access to emergency medical services. Notable obstacles to the development of pre-hospital care include insufficient infrastructure, scarce resources, and the lack of standardized formal training. Further research is essential to identify the most appropriate prehospital care model for Nepal’s distinct geographical and socioeconomic conditions. Ultimately, coordinated efforts among government agencies, non-profit organizations, and international partners are essential to ensure timely and equitable emergency medical services across the country.Keywords: Emergency medical services, Ambulances, Transportation of patients, Emergency responders, Prehospital care, Nepal

Introduction

Nepal has not yet established a comprehensive and efficient emergency medical services (EMS) system as evidenced by patient transport pattern at Patan Hospital, a tertiary care center in Kathmandu, only 9.9% of patients arrived by ambulance, while 53.6% came by taxi, 13.5% by bus, 11.4% by car, 5.4% by bicycle, and 6.2% by other means of transport [1].

The origins of ambulance services in Nepal are unclear but are believed to predate the country’s understanding of PHC. It is thought that the first ambulance was driven to Nepal overland from Germany in the early 1980s by Dr Bhawani Narsimha Rana [2]. In the early days of Nepali emergency medical services, very little is known about the number of ambulances operated, the number of patients transported, or the types of emergencies managed.

The 1990s saw a surge in healthcare privatization in Nepal, leading to the establishment of new hospitals and nursing homes [3], many of which acquired their own ambulance vehicles through private funding. These were soon joined by ambulances operated by the Nepal Red Cross Society, one of the pioneers of first aid services in Nepal [4]. In addition, other NGOs and INGOs, such as the Rotary Club, began offering ambulance services to the public [5].

The establishment of the Nepal Ambulance Service (NAS) in 2011 marked a significant milestone in the history of Nepalese ambulance services. NAS emerged as a pioneering initiative to provide standardized, community-based emergency medical transportation in Nepal which is a non-profit, non-governmental organization. It initially operated five ambulances in Kathmandu and Patan, later expanding to Pokhara and Chitwan districts, and now maintains approximately ten ambulances. Crucially, NAS provides prehospital medical care through emergency medical technicians (EMTs) who undergo a three-month training programme designed by the Stanford University School of Medicine [6]. This enables the delivery of a range of medical interventions, including basic and advanced life support (BLS and ALS), fracture splinting, spinal injury stabilization, bleeding control, airway management, and intravenous fluid administration.

As of 2022, the Nepal Ambulance Service (NAS) operates ten ambulances across Nepal and employs 27 EMTs, 20 ambulance drivers, six emergency medical dispatchers (EMDs), and five administrative staff. NAS functions under the toll-free emergency number 102 [1, 7].

A major step forward was taken in 2018, when Nepal hosted its first successful national-level conference on prehospital care [8]. In the subsequent year, the Government of Nepal introduced six strategic actions to implement its commitment to “ensuring access to basic emergency health services for all citizens,” as outlined in the National Health Policy of 2019 [9]. Among these strategies, the government pledged to provide at least one ambulance with minimum required facilities at each local level and to develop ambulance services meeting defined standards and classifications, equipped with modern technologies. Additionally, the policy includes the provision of air ambulances to rescue individuals in critical condition from ultra-remote areas.

Despite these commitments, there is a significant lack of data regarding the implementation and status of these initiatives [9]. It can be inferred that ambulances in many rural parts of Nepal remain largely inaccessible and unavailable (Fig. 1). With the rapid growth of private healthcare services—primarily concentrated in urban centres such as Kathmandu—the disparity in healthcare access, particularly prehospital care, between urban (Figs. 2 and 3) and rural populations is likely to widen in the coming years.

In 2020, the Nepalese government established a policy on ambulances and Pre-hospital care entitled, “Combined Ambulance and Pre-hospital care directives”. This is the first government policy in the country to comprehensively integrate ambulance services with Pre-hospital care, recognizing the need for well-equipped ambulances with necessary equipment and an ambulance driver who has received formal Pre-hospital care training. This directive also mentions the need to establish a single, centralized ambulance system by amalgamating various organizations, under a single, accessible helpline number “102” [10].

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a narrative literature review in conjunction with policy analysis to assess the current status, existing challenges, and potential way forward of prehospital emergency medical services in Nepal by using existing data from research articles, government reports, and other sources published between 2000 and 2025, in order to ensure relevance and comparability.

Search strategy

The electronic databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, as well as national databases, including government repositories. Our search used keywords related to emergency medical services, prehospital care, and patient transport, specifically: “Emergency Medical Services,” “Prehospital care,” “Ambulance services,” “Helicopter emergency medical services,” and “Nepal.” Additionally, targeted searches were performed on government portals and institutional repositories to identify unpublished or grey literature, with particular emphasis on policy documents from the Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP) and the Health Emergency Operation Center (HEOC). These sources were thoroughly analyzed to assess healthcare regulations, service accessibility, and workforce structure.

Data organization and scope

The main data were organized to identify key issues and areas for improvement in the system. A review was undertaken of studies and reports addressing prehospital emergency care in Nepal, commonly referred to as emergency medical services (EMS). The scope of the review encompassed ground ambulance services, helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS), the EMS workforce and its training, as well as the infrastructure and equipment supporting these services.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Included publications were required to provide substantive information on the availability of EMS services, the composition and roles of the EMS workforce, or the implementation of governmental policies related to prehospital emergency care. Conversely, studies focusing exclusively on in-hospital emergency care, broader health topics unrelated to prehospital services, duplicate publications, inaccessible documents, and materials published in languages other than English were excluded. Sources that did not meaningfully address the development, structure, or operational functioning of EMS in Nepal were also omitted from the review.

Quality appraisal and risk of bias

Given the narrative and policy-focused nature of this review, formal risk of bias assessment was not conducted. Instead, included studies and reports were evaluated for relevance, credibility, and alignment with the review objectives, emphasizing sources from peer-reviewed literature and official government documentation. Grey literature was critically appraised for authenticity and reliability.

Policy analysis

An informal, evidence-based approach was conducted to bring together insights from existing research studies, government reports, and policy documents. This approach was adopted because the available data was predominantly descriptive, which were fragmented across multiple sources, thereby constraining the suitability of a formalized policy framework.

Limitations of methodology

Given its narrative design, this study does not adhere to a formal systematic review methodology and may be subject to selection bias and incomplete data capture.

Results

Nepal has established national ambulance guidelines and directives but remained historically in draft form and not fully implemented and their execution varies considerably across the nation. The majority of ambulances deficit prerequisite equipment, such as oxygen, and are often staffed without trained Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs), which may reflect insufficient monitoring and assessment of service quality. In contrast, some ambulances are highly sophisticated, potentially including an onboard physician and advanced equipment such as ventilators, tailored to the patient’s condition. Helicopter transport is available nationwide, yet high costs substantially limit access. For instance, over an eight-year period, more than 1,700 helicopters landed at Nepal Mediciti Hospital, providing critical life-saving interventions; nonetheless, such services remain unaffordable for many. These examples illustrate the uneven implementation of national standards and underscore persistent gaps in emergency care provision across Nepal. Major issues include poor rule enforcement, no standard training programs, bad infrastructure, and little first aid education in communities.

Policy framework and multidisciplinary team composition

Government directive on ambulance and prehospital care

The “Combined Ambulance and Prehospital Care Directives” from 2020 helped recognizing a need of more organized and standardized approach to emergency medical services. At this point of time, ambulance services in Nepal is often fragmented, and there isn’t much coordination between different agencies providing emergency transport. The directive emphasizes the value of establishing a centralized ambulance system by integrating various organizations under a single, accessible helpline number: “102.” It also underscores the need for well-equipped ambulances and trained staff, which is crucial in saving lives, mainly in remote or rural areas where healthcare infrastructure seems limited. Proper training for ambulance drivers and emergency medical technicians (EMTs) is indispensable, as they can play a pivotal role in managing the patient’s condition before reaching a hospital.

Members of the multidisciplinary team (MDT)

Nepal’s prehospital care (PHC) services remain in a developmental phase, with a significant disparity between public and private sector capabilities. Only a limited number of ambulance services are staffed with emergency medical technicians (EMTs) or paramedics. In most cases, ambulances are operated by hospitals and are typically manned only by a driver without formal emergency medical training [11]. However, in hospitals which offer pre hospital emergency care such as Nepal Mediciti Hospital, Grande International Hospital, and HAMS Hospital, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) may include a driver, paramedic or nurse, and occasionally a physician. The composition of the retrieval team for any given mission depends on staff availability, patient condition, and resource allocation.

The driver is responsible for transporting patients from one location to another in a time-critical manner. Ambulance drivers in Nepal often lack formal qualifications. In response, the Nepal Ministry of Health issued a directive in 2021 stating that prospective ambulance drivers must hold a valid license for five years and undergo training from the National Health Training Centre to operate Category A and B vehicles.

Similarly, physicians on board should possess formal clinical qualifications and certified by the Nepal Medical Council.

The paramedic or EMT is often the most competent and skilled professional on scene, capable of performing procedures such as cannulation, oxygenation, and Basic Life Support (BLS). At present, Nepal lacks a formal EMT or paramedic training program, except for the sporadic EMT training conducted by the Nepal Ambulance Service (NAS) for its staff. For example, NAS offers a 12-week course developed by the Division of Emergency Medicine at Stanford University in collaboration with the Patan Academy of Health Sciences (PAHS) [6].

Ambulance service classification

A key section of the Combined Ambulance and Prehospital Care Directive categories ambulances into three grades, each meeting specific mechanical and technical standards. All ambulances share a standardized exterior design, predominantly red with green sides, and feature a yellow inverted chevron baton.

Category A (Ka): Fully equipped with advanced emergency equipment, including ECG monitors, oxygen supplies, intubation kits, and defibrillators. Staff requirements include a driver with five years of experience and BLS training, an EMT or experienced nurse, and—in severe cases—the capacity to deploy physicians.

Operational data and helicopter services in Nepal’s prehospital care system

Helicopter Emergency Medical Services (HEMS)

Since the introduction of helicopter services in Nepal in the 1960s for rapid casualty transfer, the concept of Helicopter Emergency Medical Services (HEMS) was formally established in 2013. That same year, coordination efforts were commenced with commercial airlines to streamline the retrieval and transport of patients to tertiary care facilities [14, 15].

Currently, hospitals such as Grande International Hospital, Nepal Mediciti Hospital, and HAMS Hospital provide HEMS with onboard medical facilities as part of their prehospital care services [15, 16]. Although Nepal lacks dedicated medical helicopters (Fig. 4), these services operate in collaboration with private helicopter companies, modifying commercial helicopters for medical use by reclining the co-pilot’s seat to accommodate patients and medical personnel (Fig. 5). If the patient is stable or multiple individuals require transport, they may be seated in the rear and treated en route.

This public-private partnership model has proven mutually beneficial: airlines generate revenue during off-peak seasons, while emergency medical access in remote regions improves. However, due to the absence of a comprehensive national insurance system, HEMS remains financially inaccessible to much of the Nepalese population despite high demand. Most costs are borne by patients through out-of-pocket payments, as formal insurance schemes rarely cover helicopter evacuations.

Exceptions exist for specific groups such as employees of the Nepal Electricity Authority, diplomatic staff, and certain INGO/NGO workers. Additionally, some NGOs have stepped in to support these efforts. Notably, the German organization Nepal Aid covers helicopter evacuation costs for critically ill patients from the Rasuwa district, ensuring timely access to advanced medical care [17].

As of July 2025, Nepal’s pre-hospital emergency care system can utilize a fleet of 31 helicopters, managed by 12 civilian aviation companies nationwide (Table 2) [18]. These aircraft - primarily Eurocopter AS350 B3 models - play a critical role in facilitating emergency medical evacuations, particularly in remote and mountainous regions where road access is limited or non-existent.

When civilian resources are unavailable or unable to fulfill a mission, the Nepalese Armed Forces may be called upon to provide support under government-led initiatives. Military pilots, with their advanced training and expertise in navigating difficult terrain and adverse flight conditions- including operations beyond standard Visual Flight Rules (VFR)- are often tasked with these missions. One notable example is the President’s Women Uplift Program, developed by the University of Newcastle (Australia). Since its launch in 2019, the program has reached 808 women across 44 districts, contributing to a 40% reduction in maternal deaths [19–21].

Against the backdrop of developmental evolution of Emergency care in Nepal, it has achieved notable advancements in emergency medicine education, encompassing Doctorate of Medicine (DM) programs, residency training, and fellowships centered on hospital-based care. In contrast, formal education in prehospital or ambulance-based emergency care remains scarce, with the exception of a one-year fellowship in Prehospital Care and Retrieval Medicine, accredited by the Nepal Medical Council and offered at HAMS Hospital which has started from 2026. As a result, ambulance staffing practices are heterogeneous, and there is currently no nationwide mandate for the presence of emergency medicine–trained physicians in prehospital services.

In April 2026, Nepal Mediciti Hospital partnered with the Scottish Ambulance Service to conduct the first-ever Emergency Medical Retrieval Services (EMRS) training in Nepal. This pioneering program saw 30 participants successfully complete training in advanced prehospital care, significantly boosting local capacity for safe patient transport, particularly via helicopter.

Discussion

This review examines Nepal’s prehospital emergency medical services, which remain underdeveloped with notable urban-rural disparities in access, workforce capacity, and system organization. Despite progressive policies, such as the National Health Policy 2019, implementation is inconsistent, and the EMS system is fragmented across public, private, and non-governmental sectors.

Urban areas like Kathmandu benefit from better ambulance availability and trained personnel, while rural regions rely on informal transport. The absence of a standardized emergency medical technician (EMT) training system and weak regulatory enforcement further limit system effectiveness, with many ambulances lacking trained staff.

While helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) offer improvements for remote areas, their reliance on private providers and out-of-pocket payments restricts access. The inconsistent enforcement of ambulance standards and fragmented governance further hinder EMS development.

To strengthen Nepal’s EMS, coordinated efforts are needed to improve workforce training, enforce regulatory standards, expand equitable financing, and integrate services across sectors to ensure timely, quality care and reduce preventable mortality.

Conclusion

Nepal has made good progress in understanding the need for emergency care before reaching a hospital, but there are still big problems with facilities, training, and fair access. To improve this, the country needs to create a national emergency medical service plan, start official training programs for emergency medical technicians and paramedics, increase insurance for emergency transport, and improve first aid skills in the community. It is important for the government, hospitals, NGOs, and international partners to work together to create a strong and fair emergency care system.

Recommendations

Enhancing prehospital emergency care in Nepal requires urgent action across training, policy, infrastructure, and public awareness. The following recommendations focus on closing these gaps to improve patient outcomes and system efficiency.

Develop formal prehospital care training programs Nepal currently lacks standardized education and certification pathways for Physicians, Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs), and paramedics. Aside from a 12-week (Now 10 weeks) training course offered sporadically by the Nepal Ambulance Service, no formal programs exist. Establishing nationally accredited and sustainable training programs is essential to develop a competent prehospital care workforce capable of meeting the country’s growing emergency medical needs.

3. Strengthen government policy and regulatory framework for prehospital care Robust and enforceable government policies are necessary to advance Nepal’s prehospital care system. Ambulances must be staffed by trained paramedics or EMTs and comply with mechanical and technical standards specified in the Combined Ambulance and Pre-Hospital Care Directive. Hospitals with more than 50 inpatient beds should be mandated to employ dedicated prehospital care physicians and maintain helipads or designated open spaces to facilitate air ambulance services. This recommendation supports ongoing efforts to develop national air ambulance guidelines.

4. Enhance public awareness and basic life support training Standardized First Aid and Basic Life Support (BLS) training, including CPR, is not routinely integrated into Nepalese school curricula or workplace safety programs. However, evidence shows that immediate bystander CPR can double or even triple survival rates following cardiac arrest [24]. Implementing mandatory, nationwide first aid and CPR education would greatly reduce preventable deaths and strengthen community preparedness during emergencies. Furthermore, Automated External Defibrillators (AEDs) should be installed in all high-traffic public areas such as airports, bus stations, shopping centers, parks, and similar gathering places.
