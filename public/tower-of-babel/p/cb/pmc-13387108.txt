Peptide LKLKLL is a more effective component of Akkermansia muciniphila which regulate glucolipid metabolism through GLP-1/GIP dual modulation
Authors: Yuan Li, Dasen Ye, Caiyan Fu, Xingli Huang, Qingqing Ma, Fan Zhang, Gengyue Chen, Pingping Song, Changqing Kong, Jiaxing Wu, Hao Wu, Jianjun Qiao, Juhong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ability of Akkermansia muciniphila (AKK) to ameliorate metabolic dysfunction has attracted increasing attention as a potential strategy for obesity and diabetes management. Moving beyond the use of live bacteria toward the identification of defined bioactive components may facilitate translational development. In this study, we found that the metabolic supernatant of AKK promoted GLP-1 and GIP secretion in STC-1 cells. Metabolomic profiling identified 54 small molecules in the AKK supernatant, among which a bioactive hexapeptide, LKLKLL, was identified. LKLKLL reduced body weight in high-fat diet–induced obese mice, improved glucose tolerance in diabetic models, enhanced GLP-1 secretion, and reduced GIP levels. Mechanistically, our data suggest that LKLKLL may interact with GPR119 and activate the Gαs/adenylate cyclase/cAMP signaling pathway, leading to increased intracellular Ca²⁺ levels. Together, these findings identify an AKK-derived peptide with GLP-1/GIP regulatory activity and provide a foundation for further investigation of its potential role in metabolic diseases modulation.

Introduction

The global burden of metabolic diseases demands innovative therapeutic strategies. 1 In this pursuit, the gut microbiome, and specifically Akkermansia muciniphila (AKK), represents a promising source of discovery. 2 Compelling evidence shows that AKK administration improves glucose homeostasis and reduces body weight, driving the search for its active components to overcome the limitations of live biotherapeutics. 3 Initial breakthroughs have successfully identified specific AKK-derived molecules that modulate glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) signaling, marking a significant step into a “post-bacterial era” of targeted drug discovery. 4 , 5

Recent clinical studies unequivocally demonstrated that dual agonists targeting both GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptors yield superior metabolic benefits, including profound weight loss and enhanced glycemic control, compared to single GLP-1-targeting agents. 6-8 Although studies have established that AKK can stimulate GLP-1 secretion, 4 a crucial question remains unanswered: Does AKK mediate a broader, dual-hormone regulation? Identifying the specific bioactive molecules responsible and deciphering their precise mechanisms of action have thus become imperative and urgent research priorities.

Here, we bridge this gap by reporting a transformative discovery: a dual-acting incretin modulator derived from AKK. Moving beyond conventional single-target approaches, we hypothesized the existence of such a molecule and successfully identified a novel AKK-derived peptide that co-regulates both GLP-1 and GIP. This finding represents a paradigm shift from cataloging single-effect molecules to developing multi-target therapies inspired by native microbial systems. Our work unveils the first bacterial component with dual-incretin activity, positioning it as a pioneering therapeutic candidate designed to harness the superior efficacy validated by modern clinical endocrinology.

Materials and methods

Culture of AKK

AKK (ATCC BAA-835) was revived from stock by inoculating 0.2 mL of the preserved culture into 20 mL of sterile brain heart infusion (BHI) medium (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., HB8478) in a conical flask. The culture was incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48–72 h until visible white sediment formed at the bottom of the flask. The suspension was then mixed thoroughly and subcultured for further use.

Growth curve analysis showed that AKK reached the stationary phase at 36 h, and the culture was adjusted to 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL for experimental use (Supplemental Figure S1). Various bacterial doses were co-cultured with STC-1 cells for 24 h to determine the optimal intervention concentration, which was set at 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL (Supplemental Figure S2).

Preparation of AKK-conditioned supernatant and pasteurized AKK

AKK (ATCC 835) was harvested by centrifugation, washed, and resuspended in antibiotic-free culture medium, followed by anaerobic incubation for various time periods. Cultures were then centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were collected, filtered, and stored at −20 °C or −80 °C until use.

For pasteurization, an identical volume of AKK culture was subjected to heat inactivation by incubation at 70 °C for 30 min in a water bath. The bacterial suspension was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, washed twice with sterile distilled water, and resuspended in complete culture medium. Pasteurized preparations were stored at −20 °C for short-term use or −80 °C for long-term storage.

No post-treatment viability assay (e.g., CFU plating) was performed; this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Fractionation of bacterial metabolic supernatant by molecular weight

The 2-h metabolic supernatant of AKK was fractionated using ultrafiltration centrifugal tubes (Millipore, Pall) with different molecular weight cutoffs. The supernatant was separated into the following fractions: >300 kDa, 100–300 kDa, 50–100 kDa, 10–30 kDa, 3–10 kDa, and <3 kDa. Each fraction was then used to treat STC-1 cells, and the concentrations of GLP-1 and GIP in the cell culture supernatants were subsequently measured.

The culture supernatant or fractionated samples were incubated with proteinase K (iNtRON Biotechnology) at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL at 55 °C for 1 h, followed by heat inactivation at 90 °C for 10 min before application to cells.

Cell culture

STC-1 cells were obtained from Beina Bio (Beijing, China) and maintained in complete DMEM medium (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, F8687) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, V900929). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Measurement of GLP-1 and GIP in STC-1 cells cultured by AKK-conditioned supernatant or pasteurized AKK

STC-1 cells were first exposed to high-glucose stimulation (55.5 mM) for 2, 4, 6, and 24 h, and GLP-1 concentrations in the culture supernatants were measured by ELISA (Elabscience, Cat. No. E-EL-M3012), with the highest levels observed at 24 h (Figure 1H). For subsequent experiments, cells were subjected to 1 h starvation followed by 24 h treatment with bacterial preparations, after which supernatants were collected for GLP-1 and GIP measurements. Intervention concentrations were determined using the CCK-8 assay to identify the optimal dose for each treatment condition (Supplemental Figures S3A–C and S5). Thereafter, GLP-1 and GIP levels in cell supernatants were measured by ELISA (Elabscience, Cat. No. E-EL-M3012 for GLP-1 and E-EL-M0539c for GIP), and the gene expression of GLP-1 and GIP in cells was analyzed by qPCR.

For ELISA assessment of hormone secretion, STC-1 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 500 µL per well and cultured overnight. The medium was removed and cells were gently washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, 500 µL of HBSS was added to each well for a 1 h starvation period. After starvation, HBSS was removed, cells were washed twice with PBS, and stimulation was initiated. Experimental wells received 500 µL of high-glucose complete medium containing the designated interventions, whereas control wells received 500 µL of high-glucose complete medium alone. Cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. At the end of stimulation, culture media were collected and centrifuged at 10 × g for 25 min, and the resulting supernatants were either used immediately for ELISA or stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and inguinal adipose tissues using an RNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit following standard protocols. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a Roche real-time PCR system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the internal reference gene for normalization. Primer sequences for all target genes are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Primer specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis to ensure single, specific amplification products. Unless otherwise stated, each experiment was performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from mouse tissues or cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd, China). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg per lane) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-GPR119 (Affinity, DF4892, 1:500), anti-GAPDH (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd, LF205, 1:5000), and anti-β-actin (Tianjin Simo Biotechnology Co., Ltd, M9003, 1:10000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, ChemiDoc MP). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin as the loading control.

Peptide synthesis

The peptide LKLKLL was chemically synthesized by Beijing Peptide BioTech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Its purity (>99.5%) was confirmed by HPLC, and its identity was verified by mass spectrometry (see Supplementary materials).

Establishment of obese mouse model

A total of 76 male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences (Hangzhou, China). After quarantine, the animals were housed in a specific pathogen–free (SPF) facility. Following one week of acclimation, mice were randomly assigned to five groups: normal control (NC, n = 12), high-fat diet (HFD, n = 16), A. muciniphila intervention (AKK, n = 16), LKLKLL intervention (LK, n = 16), and metformin treatment (Met, n = 16).

Mice in the NC group were fed a standard chow diet, whereas those in the HFD group received a high-fat diet containing 60% kcal from fat (Research Diets, D12492). The AKK group was administered lyophilized viable A. muciniphila (0.5 × 10⁸ CFU per mouse) suspended in sterile saline by oral gavage once daily. Mice in the LK group received LKLKLL (12.5 mg/kg), and those in the Met group received metformin (150 mg/kg), while continuing on the HFD.

All interventions were performed by daily oral gavage for 8 weeks. The overall experimental design is illustrated in Figure 3A.

Establishment of type 2 diabetic mouse model

A total of 38 male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences (Hangzhou, China) and divided into five groups: NC group (n = 6), type 2 diabetes mellitus group (T2DM, n = 8), AKK intervention group (AKK, n = 8), LKLKLL intervention group (LK, n = 8), and metformin group (as a positive group; Met, n = 8). All groups were first fed with a high-fat diet for 8 weeks to induce insulin resistance. Mice were then fasted for 16 h and weighed before intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg) dissolved in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer, administered once daily for 5 consecutive days to induce T2DM. NC mice received an equal volume of citrate buffer as a control. After STZ injection, random blood glucose levels were measured every 3 d. Mice with stable blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mmol/L were considered type 2 diabetes modeling successful. Following successful modeling, mice were continuously fed on a high-fat diet for an additional 5 weeks, with weekly blood glucose monitoring. The experimental design was shown in Figure 4A and B.

Metabolomics analysis

For metabolomic profiling, culture supernatants of AKK grown anaerobically in high-glucose complete medium at 37 °C were collected after 2 h and 6 h of incubation. The supernatants were filtered and concentrated, and metabolites were extracted by adding 1.5 mL of methanol containing 20% acetonitrile. Samples were vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. A 100 µL aliquot of the resulting supernatant was transferred into autosampler vials for analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Atlantis T3 column using 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and an injection volume of 3 µL.

20K proteome microarray binding assay

The peptide LKLKLL was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 0.5 μg/μL stock solution and diluted to a working concentration of 10 μg/mL using blocking buffer (PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.1% Tween-20). Human proteome microarrays (Wayen Biotechnologies, Shanghai, China) were transferred from −80 °C to −20 °C overnight and equilibrated at 4 °C for 1 h prior to the assay. The arrays were blocked in blocking buffer at room temperature in the dark for 2 h with gentle shaking (50 rpm) and then washed once with wash buffer (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20). The arrays were incubated with the LKLKLL working solution at room temperature in the dark for 1 h (50 rpm), followed by three washes with wash buffer (5 min each) and two washes with ddH2O (5 min each). Cy5-streptavidin (0.1%) was then applied and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 20 min (50 rpm). After washing and drying, the arrays were scanned at 635 nm using a microarray scanner. This experiment was conducted with assistance from Wayen Biotechnologies (Shanghai) Inc., China.

Protein structure prediction and molecular docking

Protein structure prediction and molecular docking analyses were performed to evaluate the interaction between the LKLKLL peptide and the GPR119 receptor. The three-dimensional structure of GPR119 was first predicted using AlphaFold, and the LKLKLL peptide model was generated accordingly. Both structures were subjected to preprocessing, including hydrogen addition and charge optimization, and were subsequently converted into PDBQT format. The potential active site of GPR119 was defined using AutoDockTools, and the docking grid was configured based on the predicted binding pocket. Docking simulations were then performed using AutoDock Vina, and the resulting binding conformations were analyzed to determine the most favorable interaction modes.

siRNA transfection (24-well plate)

To investigate the functional role of specific genes, siRNA-mediated knockdown was carried out in STC-1 cells. Twenty-four hours prior to transfection, cells were seeded into 24-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured until reaching approximately 60% confluence. For transfection, 15 pmol siRNA was mixed with 8.5 µL of transfection buffer to prepare a premix, after which 1.5 µL of Plus reagent was added and thoroughly combined to form siRNA/Plus complexes. The complexes were then added directly to the culture medium. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were collected for downstream analyses 48 h after transfection.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was assessed prior to statistical analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc multiple comparison tests where applicable. When variance heterogeneity was detected, Welch's ANOVA was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software).

Unless otherwise stated, in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicate. In vivo experiments were conducted with mice randomly assigned to appropriate groups; for molecular analyses, three mice per group were used, as indicated in the figure legends.

Results

AKK and its supernatant metabolites promote GLP-1/GIP secretion in vitro

Co-culture experiments revealed that AKK significantly increased both gene and protein expression of GIP, proglucagon, and the endopeptidase PCSK1/3 (p < 0.05) (Figure 1A–G). To further uncover the active components of AKK, we compared the effects of live bacteria, pasteurized bacterial cells, and metabolic supernatant of AKK on STC-1 cells. As shown in Figure 1I and J and Supplemental Figure S3D–I, the metabolic supernatant of AKK induced the most significant incretin secretion (p < 0.05), which were even higher than the live bacteria group (Figure 1R and S p < 0.05). Time-course experiments indicated that 2 h of incubation with metabolic supernatant increased GLP-1 and GIP secretion by approximately 2-fold and 6-fold, respectively (Figure 1K and L, Supplemental Figure S4). Our further experiments revealed that the <3 kDa fraction had the strongest stimulation on GLP-1 and GIP secretion. Partial inhibition of this effect by proteinase K suggested that small peptides or proteinaceous molecules may be the active components (Figure 1N–Q). Dose-response experiments further confirmed that the <3 kDa fraction stimulated incretin secretion in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 1M). Together, these results indicate that AKK stimulates incretin secretion through the release of small metabolites (<3 kDa).

A novel peptide of AKK that possess strong stimulation of GIP and GLP-1 secretion was identified

To identify the small molecules of AKK which stimulate GLP-1/GIP secretion, we performed untargeted metabolomic analysis of AKK metabolic supernatant and compared to blank culture medium. Multivariate analysis using OPLS-DA showed clear separation between the compared groups, indicating distinct metabolic profiles (Supplementary Figure S12). The corresponding model parameters (R²Y and Q²) are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Volcano and Venn diagram analyses identified 54 metabolites that were consistently upregulated in AKK supernatant (Figure 2A and B; Supplemental Figures S6 and S7). Based on the available evidence and the accessibility of the compounds, we subsequently selected 17 small molecules (highlighted in red in Supplemental Figures S6C and S7C and F) for functional validation of their incretin-stimulating activities. In addition, acetate—a representative short-chain fatty acid—and glutamine were included as positive controls, whereas DMSO served as the vehicle control. Subsequently, the optimal intervention concentrations were identified using CCK-8 assays (Supplementary Figure S8). Functional validation revealed that among the tested metabolites, 1-OctadecyI LPA, arabitol and the peptides WIW and LKLKLL exhibited potential stimulatory effects on GIP and GLP-1 secretion (Figure 2C–H). Considering feasibility for downstream investigation, WIW and LKLKLL were selected for further analyses. We observed that, compared with the control peptide WIW, peptide LKLKLL significantly upregulated the gene expression of GIP, proglucagon, and PCSK1/3 (Figure 2I–N, p < 0.0001) and promoted GLP-1 and GIP secretion in a concentration-dependent manner (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2O and P). Moreover, Supplemental Figure S9A confirmed the presence of LKLKLL in AKK metabolic supernatant. Additionally, 1-OctadecyI LPA increased GIP secretion in a dose-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure S9D–E), while arabitol at 2 mM enhanced GLP-1 but reduced GIP levels (Supplemental Figure S9F–G). Stability tests showed that LKLKLL exhibited less than 2% degradation in simulated gastrointestinal fluids, indicating high resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis and supporting its potential for oral administration (Supplemental Figure S9B and C). These results not only reveal the key metabolite profile through which AKK regulates incretin secretion but also identify LKLKLL as a core bioactive molecule.

LKLKLL improves HFD-induced obesity by promoting GLP-1 secretion but inhibiting GIP secretion

To investigate the effects of LKLKLL on obesity we used high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity mice. Mice were divided into five groups: normal control (NC), HFD, AKK-treated group (AKK), LKLKLL-treated group (LK), and metformin-treated group as a positive control (Met). After 8 weeks of intervention, LKLKLL treatment significantly attenuated body weight compared with the HFD group (Figure 3B–D, p < 0.05), with effects comparable to those of AKK and metformin. The body weight measurements for all individual mice are provided in Supplementary Table S3. LKLKLL also reduced adipose tissue weights, including eWAT and iWAT, with significant differences observed in specific comparisons (Figure 3E and F and Supplementary Table S5). Importantly, we found that mice in the LKLKLL group, the HFD group, the AKK-treated group, and the metformin positive-control group displayed no significant differences in food intake(Supplementary Figure S10D and E), indicating that LKLKLL did not affect feeding behavior. Moreover, no abnormalities were observed in locomotor activity or general appearance across groups, and none of the mice exhibited diarrhea throughout the intervention period. We further investigated the mechanisms underlying these effects, and found that LKLKLL treatment significantly upregulated key thermogenic genes in brown adipose tissue, including UCP1, PGC1α, and Cidea (Figure 3M–O), suggesting a dual regulatory effect through the “gut–adipose axis.” Consistent with these transcriptional changes, hematoxylin and eosin staining of adipose tissue demonstrated that LKLKLL reversed adipocyte hypertrophy and mitigated the whitening of brown adipose tissue (Figure 3L). Collectively, these results highlight the potential of LKLKLL as an anti-obesity intervention.

Interestingly, unlike the promotion role of GLP-1 and GIP secretion in vitro, we found that LKLKLL significantly increase plasma and ileal GLP-1 levels (Figure 3H and J, p < 0.05) while suppress jejunal GIP over-secretion induced by HFD (Figure 3I and K, p < 0.05). Histological analysis revealed that LKLKLL improved intestinal villus architecture and modulated GIP and GLP-1 expression, as assessed by immunohistochemistry (Figure 3G). Therefore, LKLKLL exhibits context-dependent dual effects on GIP: stimulating its secretion in vitro while suppressing its hypersecretion in the context of obesity in vivo. No hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity was observed in any intervention group (Supplemental Figure S10A–C).

LKLKLL ameliorates hyperglycemia and insulin resistance in a type 2 diabetes mouse model

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of AKK and its peptide LKLKLL in diabetes, we next examined key metabolic and hormonal markers in T2DM model mice. Compared with the T2DM group, LKLKLL treatment significantly reduced fasting blood glucose levels (Figure 4C, p = 0.0188), improved glucose tolerance (Figure 4D–F, OGTT AUC reduction, p = 0.0109), and alleviated insulin resistance (Figure 4H, HOMA-IR < 2.5). LKLKLL also exerted multiple beneficial effects on body composition, including reductions in visceral fat (eWAT and iWAT, p < 0.01), and increases in brown adipose tissue (BAT) and muscle mass (p < 0.05), while alleviating hepatic and renal burden (Supplemental Figure S11). Again, we found that LKLKLL enhanced postprandial GLP-1 level (Figure 4K) and suppressed the pathological higher secretion of GIP in T2DM mouse (Figure 4L). These effects were confirmed in intestinal tissues (Figure 4I and J). Histological examination showed that LKLKLL improved diabetes-induced damage to intestinal villi (Figure 4G) and modulated GIP and GLP-1 expression, as assessed by immunohistochemistry. These results demonstrate that LKLKLL effectively improves hyperglycemia and restores incretin homeostasis in a T2DM mouse model.

LKLKLL promotes incretin secretion by targeting GPR119/cAMP signaling pathway

To elucidate the potential mechanism by which the peptide LKLKLL promotes GLP-1 and GIP secretion, we first performed a protein interaction screening using biotin-labeled LKLKLL (LKLKLL-Bio; Figure 5B) on a HuProt human protein microarray (Figure 5A–C). A total of 196 candidate interacting proteins were identified (Supplementary Table S1). Proteins were ranked by Z-score and further filtered based on literature evidence, 9 , 10 leading to the selection of GPR119 for subsequent functional validation (Figure 5D and E). Molecular docking analysis predicted that LKLKLL may occupy the ligand-binding pocket of GPR119, with a calculated binding energy of –7 kcal/mol. The predicted interactions involved hydrogen bonds with Glu-118/Lys-115 and hydrophobic contacts with leucine residues (Figure 5G). STRING network analysis further suggested functional associations between GPR119 and incretin-related genes, including GIP and GCG (Figure 5F). Together, these findings supported the hypothesis that LKLKLL may act through a GPR119-dependent mechanism.

To determine whether LKLKLL-induced hormone secretion requires GPR119, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of GPR119 in STC-1 cells. Western blot analysis confirmed approximately 85% reduction in GPR119 protein expression (Figure 5H–J). Functionally, GPR119 knockdown attenuated the stimulatory effects of LKLKLL on GLP-1 (p = 0.0007) and GIP (p < 0.0001) secretion (Figure 5K and L), suggesting that GPR119 signaling may contribute to the hormone-promoting effects of LKLKLL.

Given that GPR119 is a Gαs-coupled GPCR known to activate the adenylate cyclase/cAMP signaling cascade, we next examined downstream signaling events. 11 , 12 LKLKLL treatment significantly upregulated GNAS (p < 0.0001) and ADCY5 (p = 0.0068) mRNA expression (Figure 5M and N), increased intracellular cAMP levels (p = 0.0162), and elevated cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentrations (p = 0.0003) (Figure 5O and P). These results suggest that the GPR119–Gαs–cAMP–Ca²⁺ signaling pathway may contribute to the incretin-promoting effects of LKLKLL. Collectively, these findings suggest that LKLKLL may regulate GLP-1 and GIP secretion, at least in part, through an GPCR-mediated signaling pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a peptide (LKLKLL) derived from AKK and explored its potential mechanism of action. Our findings provide evidence supporting the dual-hormone regulatory effects of AKK and suggest that defined microbial molecules may represent a promising approach for modulation metabolic diseases.

Our study AKK possess dual modulation of GLP-1 and GIP. AKK has been consistently shown to improve metabolic disorders, however the clinical application of live AKK is hampered by practical limitations, including poor stability and low colonization efficiency in the human gut. 3 , 13 Previous studies have suggested that protein components in AKK supernatants can stimulate GLP-1 secretion 4 ; however, activation of GLP-1 pathway alone is prone to compensatory escape mechanisms. Clinical evidence indicates that agents capable of concurrently targeting both GLP-1 and GIP achieve more robust metabolic improvements, highlighting the need for interventions with dual incretin modulation. 14 , 15 Therefore, we explored the possibility of AKK components in the modulation of both GLP-1 and GIP.

Under obesogenic and insulin-resistant conditions, K cells exhibit pronounced functional heterogeneity and are frequently characterized by a state of pathological hypersecretion of glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). 16 Although GIP functions as a major incretin hormone under physiological conditions by promoting glucose-dependent insulin secretion and postprandial glycemic control, 17 accumulating evidence indicates that excessive and sustained GIP secretion represents a hallmark of obesity and T2DM. Both enhanced nutrient sensitivity of K cells and increased K-cell density have been documented in high-fat diet–fed rodents and obese human subjects, providing a structural and functional basis for chronic GIP hypersecretion. 18-21 Moreover, genetic association studies linking GIPR loci to obesity risk further support the notion that dysregulated GIP signaling contributes causally to metabolic deterioration. 22-25

In this context, our findings suggest that the AKK–derived peptide LKLKLL does not simply inhibit GIP secretion, but appears to restore incretin homeostasis through indirect, system-level metabolic improvements, as supported by functional studies. One plausible mechanism is that enhanced GLP-1 secretion induced by LKLKLL improves insulin secretion and glycemic control, which may in turn exert negative feedback on K-cell activity, thereby suppressing pathological GIP overproduction. In parallel, improvements in intestinal lipid handling and reduced luminal lipid exposure may alleviate chronic nutrient-driven stimulation of K cells, lowering the secretory pressure characteristic of obesity-associated hyper-GIP states. Furthermore, attenuation of low-grade inflammation and proinflammatory cytokine signaling—such as interleukin-6, 26 , 27 which has been implicated in enteroendocrine cell plasticity—may contribute to a functional shift of K cells from a hypersecretory phenotype toward a more homeostatic secretory profile. Against this backdrop, our identification of LKLKLL as a bioactive peptide derived from A. muciniphila provides mechanistic insight into how gut microbiota–host communication may recalibrate incretin secretion. Unlike classical incretin-based therapeutics that primarily enhance hormone release, LKLKLL simultaneously promoted GLP-1 secretion while suppressing excessive GIP output in both obesity and T2DM models. Mechanistically, LKLKLL stimulates GLP-1 release in a GPR119-dependent manner, as supported by knockdown experiments, while normalizing K-cell functional output under metabolically stressed conditions. Importantly, these effects occurred without alterations in food intake or overt adverse outcomes, highlighting a favorable therapeutic profile.

Notably, the actions of LKLKLL differed markedly between in vitro and in vivo settings. Under reductionist culture conditions, LKLKLL predominantly activated GPR119-dependent signaling pathways, consistent with predictions from molecular docking analysis. In contrast, in vivo administration elicited broader systemic responses, including restoration of GIP secretion dynamics, enhancement of thermogenic gene expression in brown adipose tissue, and improvement of intestinal barrier architecture. These observations underscore the importance of physiological context in shaping the metabolic consequences of microbiota-derived peptides and suggest that host–microbe interactions extend well beyond isolated receptor activation.

A central controversy in incretin biology concerns whether GIP agonism or antagonism confers metabolic benefit, 28 and emerging evidence indicates that they exert their effects through distinct mechanisms. 29 On the one hand, dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists produce superior glycemic control and weight loss compared with GLP-1 monotherapy, as demonstrated in large-scale clinical trials such as SURPASS and SURMOUNT. 30 On the other hand, pharmacological or genetic inhibition of GIP signaling induces weight loss in preclinical models, potentially through attenuation of adipogenic signaling and modulation of central energy balance. 31 , 32

Our data support a context-dependent model of GIP action. Rather than uniformly promoting or inhibiting GIP signaling, LKLKLL appears to restore incretin balance by dampening pathological GIP hypersecretion while preserving or enhancing GLP-1 activity. This dual modulation likely engages distinct metabolic pathways depending on nutritional state, hormonal milieu, and tissue-specific receptor expression, thereby providing a mechanistic framework that reconciles the seemingly opposing metabolic benefits of both GIP agonism and antagonism reported in previous studies.

LKLKLL was identified as a bioactive peptide capable of stimulating GLP-1 and GIP secretion in STC-1 cells. Protein microarray screening and molecular docking analysis suggested a potential interaction between LKLKLL and GPR119, a Gαs-coupled receptor known to regulate incretin release. Functional validation demonstrated that siRNA-mediated knockdown of GPR119 markedly attenuated LKLKLL-induced GLP-1 and GIP secretion, supporting a GPR119-dependent mechanism. Consistent with canonical GPR119 signaling, LKLKLL increased intracellular cAMP levels and cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentrations, accompanied by upregulation of GNAS and ADCY5 expression, indicating activation of the GPR119–Gαs–adenylate cyclase–cAMP pathway. In addition, LKLKLL upregulated the mRNA expression of genes involved in enteroendocrine differentiation and hormone processing, including GIP, GATA4, PDX1, PCSK1/3, and PCSK2; however, these transcriptional changes likely represent downstream responses to receptor-mediated signaling rather than direct molecular targets of the peptide. Collectively, these findings support a model in which LKLKLL promotes incretin secretion via a GPR119-dependent GPCR signaling mechanism.

In the present study, we did not directly quantify enteroendocrine cell numbers in intestinal tissues. Although LKLKLL modestly promoted STC-1 cell proliferation in vitro under certain concentrations, the relatively short treatment duration in vivo (4 weeks) suggests that the observed changes in incretin levels are more likely attributable to enhanced secretory activity of existing enteroendocrine cells rather than a substantial expansion of these cells. This interpretation is consistent with the activation of the GPR119–cAMP signaling pathway observed in our study, which is known to stimulate incretin secretion without necessarily altering enteroendocrine cell numbers.

The metabolic effects of A. muciniphila appeared more pronounced than those observed with the single peptide LKLKLL. This difference likely reflects the multifaceted mechanisms by which live or pasteurized A. muciniphila exerts metabolic benefits, including modulation of gut barrier integrity, immune responses, microbial community composition, and production of multiple bioactive metabolites. In contrast, LKLKLL represents a single bioactive component derived from A. muciniphila. Despite potentially more modest effects, peptide-based interventions offer several advantages for therapeutic development, including defined molecular composition, improved safety, precise dosing, and easier quality control compared with live microbial preparations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although LKLKLL is highly resistant to enzymatic degradation in simulated gastrointestinal fluids, suggesting potential for oral administration, its in vivo pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and potential blood–brain barrier penetration remain to be fully characterized. Based on dose conversion from murine studies, the estimated human equivalent dose appears achievable for peptide therapeutics; however, comprehensive toxicological evaluation and formulation optimization are required to assess clinical feasibility. Given the heterogeneous expression of GPR119 across tissues, organ-specific effects and potential receptor desensitization under chronic exposure warrant further investigation. Finally, whether LKLKLL indirectly reshapes gut microbial ecology to amplify its metabolic effects remains to be determined. Although LKLKLL significantly increased GLP-1 secretion and improved metabolic parameters, the present study did not include pharmacological blockade or genetic disruption of incretin receptors. Therefore, the extent to which the metabolic effects of LKLKLL depend on GLP-1 receptor or GIP receptor signaling requires further investigation.

In summary, this study identifies a microbiota-derived peptide associated with incretin regulation and supports a potential role for LKLKLL in the modulation of GLP-1 and GIP signaling through GPR119-related pathways. By integrating mechanistic and functional analyses, our findings contribute to the understanding of microbiota–incretin–metabolism interactions and support further investigation of microbiota-derived molecules in metabolic disease regulation.

Conclusions

The microbial peptide LKLKLL, derived from AKK, improved metabolic parameters in mouse models of obesity and type 2 diabetes. LKLKLL was associated with reduced body weight and blood glucose levels, enhanced GLP-1 secretion, normalized GIP levels, and increased expression of thermogenic genes in adipose tissue, without observable hepatic or renal toxicity under the experimental conditions tested. Mechanistically, our findings suggest that LKLKLL may exert these effects, at least in part, through GPR119–cAMP–related signaling pathways, providing insight into its potential role in the regulation of gut hormones. Together, these findings support further investigation of LKLKLL as a potential metabolic modulator.
