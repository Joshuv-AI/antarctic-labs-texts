Unmasking FCGR2B as a high-grade serous ovarian cancer specific marker of immune suppression and tumor progression through multi-omics mining
Authors: Jialu Zhou, Tao Zeng, Mingxia Ye, Mingxia Li, Liang Wen, Xinyi Liu, Zhe Zhang, Yuanguang Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Genetically-informed map of subtype-specific protein-immune-EOC regulatory axes.•FCGR2B+ macrophages promote M2 polarization in high-grade serous ovarian cancer.•High FCGR2B expression is associated to poor clinicopathological features and shorter survival.•NF-κB inhibitors as a potential therapeutic strategy for FCGR2B-high tumors.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the third most common and most lethal gynecologic malignancy worldwide, with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) accounting for over 90% of cases and a 5-year survival rate of only 47% [1,2]. EOC comprises five major histological subtypes, including high-grade serous (HGSOC), low-grade serous (LGSOC), endometrioid (ENOC), clear cell (OCCC), and mucinous (MOC), which exhibit substantial genetic and clinical heterogeneity [3,4]. Despite advances in surgery and systemic therapy, most patients eventually relapse, and immunotherapy has shown limited efficacy [1,3,5]. A hallmark of OC is an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) enriched with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which promotes immune evasion and disease progression [[6], [7], [8]]. However, how subtype-specific immune regulation contributes to EOC progression remains poorly understood.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) analyses have revealed distinct genetic architectures underlying OC susceptibility [9,10]. Building on these GWAS insights, proteome-wide Mendelian randomization (PWMR) enables the identification of causal proteins and potential therapeutic targets [11,12]. However, genetic and proteomic associations alone provide limited insight into how these risk signals operate within specific cell types in the TME.

Bulk transcriptomic analyses obscure cell-type-specific signals, whereas single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables high-resolution profiling of immune and epithelial populations within the EOC TME [[13], [14], [15]]. Although scRNA-seq studies have revealed immunosuppressive cell states across EOC subtypes [16], they largely lack integration with germline genetic evidence, leaving the causal links between inherited risk and cellular immune regulation unresolved.

To address this gap, we developed an integrative multi-omics framework combining MR, mediation analysis, and scRNA-seq to delineate genetically-informed, subtype-specific protein–immune–EOC regulatory landscapes, followed by functional and therapeutic characterization. Using this approach, we identified FCGR2B+ macrophages as key immunosuppressive players in HGSOC, directly linking genetic risk to adverse clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and specimens

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues were collected from 20 patients with HGSOC. All patients underwent primary debulking surgery without prior neoadjuvant chemotherapy at the Chinese PLA General Hospital (PLAGH) between November 2023 and January 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before sample collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell lines and siRNA transfection

The HGSOC cell lines SK-OV-3 (ATCC® HTB-77™) and OVCAR-3 (ATCC® HTB-161™), as well as the human monocytic cell line THP-1 (ATCC® TIB-202™), were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. SK-OV-3 and THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin–streptomycin, while OVCAR-3 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 20% FBS, penicillin–streptomycin, and bovine insulin (10 μg/mL). Detailed information on reagents and instruments is provided in Table S1.

The siRNA was dissolved in RNase-free ddH₂O to prepare a 20 μM stock solution. THP-1 cells were transfected with siRNA at a final concentration of 50 nM using Omifection-R reagent. The efficiency of gene knockdown was verified 36 h after transfection. The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S2.

GWAS summary sources of EOC subtypes, plasma proteins, and immune cell traits

The overall study design is shown in Fig. S1. We used two GWAS summary statistics (GWAS sumstats) as the outcomes for the PWMR analysis (Table S3). The EOC discovery GWAS dataset [9] comprised HGSOC (Ncase = 15,588), LGSOC (Ncase = 2,749), ENOC (Ncase = 2,877), OCCC (Ncase = 1,427), MOC (Ncase = 2,587), and shared controls (N = 105,724). The EOC replication dataset [10] comprised HGSOC (Ncase = 13,037), LGSOC (Ncase = 2,966), ENOC (Ncase = 2,810), OCCC (Ncase = 1,366), MOC (Ncase = 2,566), and shared controls (N = 40,941). Both datasets were derived from individuals of European ancestry.

The GWAS sumstats of plasma proteins measured by SomaScan were retrieved from the deCODE database (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/). Only cis-protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) were defined as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within ±1 Mb of the transcription start site (GRCh38 coordinates) of each protein, consistent with previous studies [17]. The research included 18,084 pQTLs associated with 4,907 circulating proteins in 35,559 Icelanders.

GWAS sumstats for 731 immune cell traits measured by flow cytometry were derived from a cohort of 3,757 Sardinian individuals (European ancestry), with data publicly accessible in the GWAS Catalog (accession numbers GCST0001391-GCST0002121). Immune cell traits included absolute counts, morphometric parameters, median fluorescence intensity, and relative counts of B cells, dendritic cells, monocytes, T cell subsets, and natural killer cells.

For all GWAS sumstats, genetic instruments were selected using the following criteria: (1) genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10–8), (2) minor allele frequency > 0.01, and (3) linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r² < 0.1 within a 10,000 kb window), using the 1000 Genomes European (1000 G EUR) reference panel in PLINK v1.9. Furthermore, we harmonized the effect alleles and effect sizes between the exposure and the corresponding SNPs in the outcome GWAS sumstats to ensure alignment. Instrument selection criteria were applied independently to each exposure and outcome. Palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand alignment were removed.

In addition, we retrieved known genetic loci associated with EOC subtypes from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) to integrate previously reported susceptibility variants into our analysis.

Functional annotation of GWAS sumstats

Genomic annotation of GWAS summary statistics was performed using the SNP2GENE module in FUMA (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/). Independent significant variants were defined using a suggestive threshold (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) and linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (r² < 0.6), with lead SNPs identified under a stricter LD threshold (r² < 0.1) based on the 1000 Genomes European reference panel. Genomic risk loci were constructed by merging adjacent LD blocks if lead SNPs were located within 250 kb. High-confidence candidate genes were prioritized based on minimal GWAS association strength (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) and intolerance to loss-of-function variation (pLI > 0.9) [18].

MR analysis and mediation analysis

A three-stage MR framework was implemented using the TwoSampleMR package (v0.6.6) to investigate causal relationships among plasma proteins, immune cell traits, and five EOC subtypes. The use of plasma pQTLs and immune traits from population-based cohorts as instrumental variables is based on the premise of stable germline genetic regulation. This assumption is common in disease-risk MR and is intended to capture the etiologic role of cumulative, pre-diagnostic exposure. Stage 1 assessed causal effects of plasma proteins on EOC risk, Stage 2 evaluated causal associations between 731 immune cell traits and EOC subtypes, and Stage 3 examined protein–immune mediation effects by testing Stage 1–significant proteins against Stage 2–significant immune traits. Inverse-variance weighted (IVW) or Robust Adjusted Profile Score (RAPS) methods with random effects were applied as primary analyses, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (FDR < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses were performed using complementary MR methods (Wald ratio, simple/weighted median, MR-Egger), and associations were retained if they showed consistent effect directions and remained significant in both discovery and replication datasets. No sample overlap existed between exposure and outcome datasets, minimizing potential bias [19]. To evaluate the robustness of causal estimates, we performed sensitivity analyses including F-statistic, Cochran’s Q, and MR-Egger intercept tests. Instrument strength was assessed by the F-statistic (F > 10 indicating no weak instrument bias). Potential horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity were monitored using the MR-Egger intercept (P > 0.05) and Cochran’s Q (P > 0.05) tests, respectively.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

Bayesian colocalization was performed to assess whether GWAS lead loci identified by FUMA shared causal variants with cis-pQTLs. For each lead variant, a ±250 kb window was defined to capture the local cis-regulatory region. Variants were matched by genomic position with harmonized effect alleles, and analyses were conducted using the coloc package (v5.2.3) with default priors (p1 = 1 × 10⁻⁴, p2 = 1 × 10⁻⁴, p12 = 1 × 10⁻⁵). GWAS were modeled as case–control traits and pQTLs as quantitative traits. Colocalization was considered likely when the posterior probability for a shared causal variant (PP.H4) exceeded 0.6 and represented the highest posterior probability among the five hypotheses [20].

Functional enrichment analysis and protein-protein interaction (PPI)

Functional enrichment analysis of PWMR-identified proteins was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) annotations with the ClusterProfiler R package (v4.14.6), with significance defined as an FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. PPI networks were constructed using STRING (v12.0) and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.3). Based on evaluation of interaction score thresholds across subtypes, a confidence score of 0.37 was selected to balance network coverage and interaction reliability.

Re-analysis of scRNA dataset

MR-prioritized protein gene symbols were extracted for re-analysis in single-cell RNA-seq datasets of four primary EOC subtypes (HGSOC, ENOC, LGSOC, OCCC; GSE235931) [16] (Table S4A). Raw UMI counts were processed with Seurat (v5.1.0). Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 7500 detected genes, or >5% mitochondrial reads, were excluded; genes detected in fewer than five cells were removed. Data were normalized using LogNormalize (scale factor 10,000), and the 2000 most variable genes were selected. Scaling included regression against mitochondrial percentage and cell-cycle scores. Batch effects were corrected using canonical correlation analysis (2000 features, 40 dimensions, “vst” method). Principal components were chosen based on elbow plots (15 for HGSOC, ENOC, OCCC; 12 for LGSOC), and clustering resolutions were empirically set (0.1 for HGSOC and OCCC; 0.4 for ENOC and LGSOC). Cell identities were assigned using CellMarker2.0 and manually curated with canonical markers and subtype-specific signatures (Table S4B).

MR-prioritized proteins were mapped to immune cell types using a two-step filter: a permissive threshold for detectable associations (average expression > 0.1 and expressing-cell proportion > 3%) and a stringent threshold for high-confidence associations (average expression > 0.75 and proportion > 8%).

Bulk expression and survival analysis

Gene expression in OC versus normal tissues was obtained from GEPIA, with differential expression assessed using standard log2(transformed Transcripts Per Million + 1) transformation. Differential expression thresholds were |log2 (fold change)| > 1 and P < 0.01.

For progression-free survival (PFS) analysis, we assembled HGSOC RNA-seq datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE102073 [21], GSE155164 [22], and GSE162714 [23]; total n =102) and validated in a meta-cohort of 1,435 OC cases using KMplot (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). Patients were stratified by median expression, and Cox proportional hazards regression estimated hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Kaplan–Meier curves were used for visualization.

Immune infiltration analysis

Enrichment scores of 28 immune cell types in public HGSOC RNA-seq datasets were calculated using ssGSEA (GSVA v1.44.5). Differences between FCGR2B-high and -low groups were assessed using FDR-corrected p-values (q < 0.05). The abundances of the macrophage subtypes were further quantified using CIBERSORT (v0.99).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and clinical relevance analysis

Correlations between FCGR2B and all other genes were computed and ranked. KEGG gene sets from the msigdbr package (v7.5.1) served as the reference database. GSEA was performed on the ranked list (P < 0.05). Clinicopathological associations of FCGR2B in OC were assessed using the BEST database.

Druggable analysis

To identify potential therapeutic agents targeting the FCGR2B-associated microenvironment, we queried the DepMap for compounds whose sensitivity correlated with FCGR2B expression. Recognizing the predominant expression of FCGR2B in the myeloid cells, the analysis was specifically restricted to myeloid-lineage cell lines. Compounds exhibiting significant negative correlations (r < -0.3 and P < 0.05) between their AUC values and FCGR2B expression levels were identified as potential modulators of FCGR2B-driven myeloid biology.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

FFPE HGSOC tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by rinsing in distilled water. Tissue boundaries were marked, and sections were washed in PBS before antigen retrieval in citrate buffer using a pressure cooker for 5 min, then cooled to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked using hydrogen peroxide blocking solution for 15 min, and non-specific binding was minimized with 3% BSA for 30 min. Primary antibodies against FCGR2B (1:200, Mouse), CD14 (1:4000, Rabbit), and CD68 (1:1250, Rabbit) were applied overnight at 4 °C. Sections were equilibrated to room temperature, washed once in PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 and PBS, incubated with secondary antibodies corresponding to the primary antibody species for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, and subjected to Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) for 25 min. Sequential rounds of antigen retrieval, primary/secondary antibody incubation, and TSA were performed for each fluorophore. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 15 min, washed, dried briefly, and mounted with antifade medium. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

For the quantitative analysis of mIF staining, H-scores were calculated to represent the expression levels of FCGR2B, CD14, and CD68. Based on the median H-score of FCGR2B, patients were stratified into FCGR2B-High and FCGR2B-Low groups. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test (a non-parametric method) was employed to compare the differences in myeloid marker expression between these two groups. To assess the relationship between FCGR2B and other markers across the entire cohort, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Western blotting (WB)

Baseline FCGR2B expression in THP-1, SKOV3, and OVCAR3 cells was evaluated using WB. Additionally, THP-1 cells transfected with control or three different FCGR2B-targeting siRNAs were assessed to confirm knockdown efficiency at the protein level. Total protein was extracted using 2 × Laemmli buffer, quantified by BCA assay, separated on the SDS–PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; ZSGB-Bio, ZB5305, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-FCGR2B (1:1000; bsm-43,210 M, Bioss, China), anti-β-Actin (1:10,000; Ac004, ABclonal, China), and anti-tublin (1:5000; M20005F, Abmart, China). Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and analyzed with the Tanon imaging system.

Flow-based polarization and phagocytosis assay

THP-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL in 6-well plates and differentiated into M0 macrophages using the Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (120 ng/mL). For macrophage polarization and phagocytosis assays, SKOV3 cells were employed as the tumor model. SKOV3 is extensively utilized in immune-oncology studies to evaluate TAM reprogramming due to its robust interaction with myeloid cells in co-culture systems [24,25].

For polarization assays, one day before the end of macrophage differentiation, SKOV3 cells were seeded at 1.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL in the upper chamber of 6-well Transwell inserts. After macrophage induction, the inserts were transferred into the wells containing macrophages, and the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium for 48 h of co-culture. Following co-culture, macrophages were collected by gentle scraping. For flow cytometry, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and washed once with buffer. Cells were incubated with fluorescent-conjugated anti-CD68 and anti-CD206 antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed twice, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Comparisons between the control (Si-NC) and knockdown (Si-FCGR2B) groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test after confirming equality of variances via an F-test.

For phagocytosis assays, SKOV3 cells were labeled with CFSE at a ratio of macrophages to cancer cells of 4:1. Labeled SKOV3 cells were added to M0 macrophages and co-cultured for 4–6 h. After co-culture, macrophages were collected by scraping. For flow cytometry, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and washed once with buffer. Cells were then incubated with fluorescent-conjugated anti-CD68 antibody at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed twice, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Macrophages subjected to knockdown or non-targeting control were collected after co-culture. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Thermo, America). RNA samples were submitted to Decode Biology (Shanghai, China) for library preparation and sequencing. RNA-seq libraries were constructed, and 150 bp paired-end sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. Raw reads were processed using Fastp (v0.22.0) [26] to remove adapters and low-quality bases, then aligned to the reference genome (Hg38) using STAR (v2.7.106) [27]. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 (v1.40.2) [28], with genes satisfying |log₂ (fold change)| > 1 and P < 0.05 defined as significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.14.6) [29].

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were harvested via trypsinization, counted, and resuspended before being seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well in a volume of 100 μL. Blank control wells containing only conditioned medium (CM) without cells were included to calibrate the measurements. Each experimental and control group was performed in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability. After cell adherence, the medium was replaced with a mixture of macrophage supernatant and fresh culture medium at a 1:1 ratio. At specified time points of 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Omc-03, Omiget, China) was added to each well. The plates were gently agitated to ensure thorough mixing and then incubated for an additional 1 hour. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader for subsequent statistical analysis.

Flow-based apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis kit (AP101, MultiSciences Biotech, China). Treated SKOV3 cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 1 × Binding Buffer at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/ml. The cell suspension was incubated with 5 μl Annexin V-FITC and 10 μl PI for 5 min at room temperature in the dark. For each independent experiment, an unstained control and single-stained compensators were utilized to calibrate the flow cytometer. Fluorescence signals were acquired via the FITC (Ex/Em = 488/530 nm) and PI (Ex/Em = 535/615 nm) channels. Apoptotic cells were defined as the sum of early (Annexin V+/PI-) and late (Annexin V+/PI+) apoptotic populations.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.2). Unless otherwise indicated, comparisons between two groups were performed using the Wilcoxon test, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Functional annotation using FUMA identified 367 candidate genes, 91.55% of which showed subtype-specific patterns, and 8.45% were shared across multiple subtypes (Fig. 1C; Table S5). For example, HNF1B was annotated across HGSOC, LGSOC, OCCC, and MOC, while most genes exhibited subtype-specific associations (Fig. 1D). Bayesian colocalization with cis-pQTLs revealed five loci (PP.H4 >0.6) where GWAS signals likely influence EOC risk through circulating protein regulation, exemplified by rs6005807 near ZNRF3 in HGSOC (PP.H4=0.82; Fig. 1E-F; Table S6).

We next used PWMR to infer causal relationships between plasma proteins and EOC risk. A total of 76 proteins demonstrated consistent directional effects across discovery and replication cohorts, with 95% showing subtype-specific associations (Fig. S2A and C; Table S7A). Sensitivity analyses, including F-statistic, Cochran’s Q, and MR-Egger intercept tests, consistently demonstrated strong instrument strength (all F > 10) and no evidence of heterogeneity or directional pleiotropy (Table S7B). Representative examples included NSF, BGAT in HGSOC; Prekallikrein, ASPN in LGSOC; NSF, PERL in ENOC; IEX1, PSP in OCCC; and Protein C, Galectin-2 in MOC (Fig. 2A). We then performed MR of 731 immune cell traits in EOC, revealing predominantly subtype-specific immune signatures (Fig. S2B and D; Table S10). HGSOC and LGSOC were characterized by recurrent causal effects involving HLA-DR–related myeloid phenotypes, whereas ENOC, OCCC, and MOC displayed distinct Treg, CD8⁺ T-cell, and B-cell associations (Fig. 2C).

Identification of subtype-specific protein–immune axes across EOC subtypes

To establish causal protein–immune regulatory landscape, we conducted mediation MR to test whether significant proteins causally influence significant immune-cell traits. This revealed that 60.5% of candidate proteins exerted causal effects on immune-cell traits (Fig. S2E; Table S11), directly supporting immune-mediated disease mechanisms. To anchor these genetic associations to specific immune-cell populations, we interrogated the scRNA-seq data from EOC tumors (GSE235931 [16]). Among the candidate proteins, 15 genes passed initial expression filters and exhibited subtype-specific distributions across monocytes, T cells, and B cells (Fig. 2D; Fig. S3).

To exclude potential false-positive associations, we applied more stringent single-cell expression thresholds, resulting in twenty robust protein–immune–EOC axes (Table S12; Fig. 3). In HGSOC, FCGR2B emerged as the most central player, showing replicated associations with multiple immune-cell traits, including CD40 expression on CD14⁺CD16⁻ monocytes, CD40 on total monocytes, SSC-A on CD14⁺ monocytes, and CD20 on IgD⁺CD38⁻ B cells. High FCGR2B expression associates with adverse clinicopathological features in HGSOC

We focused on the proteins within twenty protein–immune–EOC axes, examining their associations with patient survival and their expression in tumor versus normal tissues. Bulk RNA-seq analysis indicated significantly higher FCGR2B expression in HGSOC tumors relative to normal ovarian tissue (Fig. 4A). To evaluate its prognostic relevance, we integrated multiple independent cohorts and observed that higher FCGR2B expression consistently associated with shorter PFS. In three GEO datasets (total n = 102), FCGR2B-high patients exhibited significantly worse outcomes (HR=1.75, 95% CI: 1.10–2.79, log-rank P = 0.017; Fig. 4B). This association was further confirmed in a meta-cohort of 1435 OC cases (HR=1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.3, log-rank P = 0.032; Fig. 4C). Notably, the remaining candidates did not show consistent associations with patient survival (Fig. S4), highlighting the unique prognostic relevance of FCGR2B. To elucidate the biological programs underlying FCGR2B's adverse prognostic impact, we performed GSEA analysis. Genes associated with high FCGR2B expression were significantly enriched in pathways related to cytokine signaling, hematopoiesis, chemokine signaling, and cell adhesion, indicating a potential role of FCGR2B in regulating immune functions in the TME (Fig. 4G; Table S13).

Notably, aggregation of previously reported EOC subtype–associated loci from the GWAS Catalog indicated that FCGR2B has not been reported as a susceptibility locus in any EOC subtype (Table S14), highlighting its novelty as a candidate gene in HGSOC.

To examine FCGR2B expression in macrophages within human HGSOC tissues, mIF staining was performed in 20 HGSOC samples, revealing that FCGR2B expression was enriched in TAMs, as indicated by strong spatial co-localization with the macrophage marker CD68 and the monocyte marker CD14 (Fig. 5C; Table S15). FCGR2B H-scores correlated positively with CD14 H-scores (Spearman rho = 0.70; Fig. 5D). Notably, FCGR2B-high tumors showed significantly higher CD68 intensity (Wilcoxon test, P = 0.004), and FCGR2B H-scores were strongly correlated with CD68 H-scores across samples (Spearman rho = 0.78; Fig. 5E). WB demonstrated markedly high FCGR2B abundance in monocytes and minimal expression in HGSOC cell lines (Fig. 5F). This expression pattern is consistent with the monocyte enrichment observed in scRNA-seq data and with monocytes being implicated as the downstream immune cell type in our MR analysis.

To further functionally validate the role of FCGR2B in macrophage polarization, flow cytometry was performed. Results revealed that FCGR2B knockdown in macrophage cells resulted in a significant reduction in M2 polarization (from 55.90% ± 1.20% to 38.70% ± 1.20%; n = 3; t = 14.35, df = 4; P = 0.0001) and a significant increase in phagocytosis (from 7.98% ± 1.11% to 16.83% ± 1.11%; n = 3; t = 7.976, df = 4; P = 0.0013) compared with negative control cells (Fig. 5G–I). These findings indicate that FCGR2B contributes to the maintenance of an M2-like, immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype.

Collectively, these analyses indicate that FCGR2B is enriched in TAMs and promotes an M2-like, immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype in HGSOC, as evidenced by both tissue localization and functional knockdown experiments.

Recognizing that FCGR2B is predominantly expressed in the myeloid cells, we specifically restricted our analysis to myeloid-lineage cell lines. Most notably, the MEK inhibitor ARRY-886 exhibited the strongest negative correlation (r = -0.61, P = 0.0017; N = 24; Fig. 6B), along with several other kinase inhibitors that showed similar trends although with smaller sample sizes (Table S16B). This suggests that targeting the FCGR2B/NF-κB axis could be a promising therapeutic strategy in FCGR2B-high HGSOC.

Macrophage FCGR2B promotes SKOV3 cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis

To further assess the functional impact of Si-FCGR2B on tumor cells, SKOV3 cells were incubated with CM derived from either Si-NC or Si-FCGR2B transfected macrophages. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that CM from Si-FCGR2B macrophages significantly inhibited SKOV3 cell proliferation compared to the Si-NC group (Fig. 6F). This inhibitory effect was observed starting at 24 h and further intensified at 72 h, where the mean OD value in the Si-FCGR2B group (0.635 ± 0.027) was markedly lower than that in the Si-NC group (1.525 ± 0.027; P < 0.001).

Additionally, the effect of Si-FCGR2B macrophage CM on tumor cell apoptosis was quantified via flow cytometry. Compared with the control group, treatment with Si-FCGR2B CM resulted in a significant increase in the percentage of apoptotic SKOV3 cells (10.50% vs. 4.01%; P < 0.0001; Fig. 6G). These findings demonstrate that the reduction of FCGR2B in macrophages effectively impairs SKOV3 cell viability by suppressing proliferation and promoting apoptosis.

Discussion

Our study systematically delineates the genetic, proteomic, and immunological landscapes of the five major EOC subtypes, revealing substantial subtype-specific features, including Fc receptor–related immune signatures in HGSOC, chaperone-dependent pathways in OCCC, and ECM remodeling in LGSOC. We utilized a three-stage MR framework integrated with scRNA-seq data to define protein–immune regulatory axes and map their specific cellular localizations. Among these subtype-specific signals, FCGR2B was prioritized for investigation because it was the only candidate that maintained clinical significance across all validation stages, encompassing both differential expression and prognostic impact. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses identified FCGR2B enrichment in TAMs, which was further validated spatially by mIF and WB assays. High FCGR2B expression was associated with increased infiltration of MDSCs, Tregs, and M2-like macrophages. Flow cytometry further confirmed that FCGR2B knockdown in macrophages reduced M2 polarization and increased phagocytosis, supporting its role in maintaining an immunosuppressive phenotype. Drug-response analyses, together with transcriptional evidence of altered NF-κB signaling upon FCGR2B knockdown, suggest a potential association between high FCGR2B expression and sensitivity to NF-κB pathway inhibition.

In HGSOC, subtype-specific proteins are enriched in Fc receptor-related immune pathways. Fcγ receptors (FcγRs) mediate antibody-dependent immune responses, including antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [30] and phagocytosis (ADCP) [31]. Among them, the inhibitory receptor FCGR2B limits ADCP and other effector functions by competing with activating FcγRs and promoting antibody internalization, thereby modulating immune suppression in the TME. Mechanistically, FCGR2B limits ADCP by competing with activating FcγRs for Fc binding and, more importantly, by recruiting the inositol phosphatase SHIP1 to its cytoplasmic ITIM motif to intercept activating signals. Recent evidence suggests that therapeutic resistance is often driven by a transfer of dominance within macrophages, where the inhibitory signaling of FCGR2B overcomes activating FcγR activity, thereby halting tumor cell killing [32]. Consequently, FCGR2B promotes antibody internalization and modulates immune suppression within the TME.

TAMs in OC predominantly exhibit an M2-like, pro-tumoral phenotype, promoting metastasis, angiogenesis, and chemoresistance [33,34]. Although the role of FCGR2B in OC TAMs remains under investigation, studies in aggressive tumors like glioblastoma show that its TAM-specific expression drives tumor recurrence and correlates with poor prognosis [35]. These findings, together with the established protumoral functions of TAMs, support a model in which FCGR2B expression on monocyte-derived TAMs restrains phagocytic activity and sustains an M2-like, immunosuppressive phenotype. Mechanistically, prior studies have shown that FCGR2B can be engaged by macrophage-derived ligands and transcriptionally regulated by hypoxic signaling, contributing to impaired antibody-dependent phagocytosis and an immunosuppressive TME [36,37]. NF-κB signaling, a central regulator of immune and inflammatory responses [38], may mediate some of these downstream effects. This pathway could link FCGR2B activity to macrophage polarization and immune evasion.

Consistent with our findings, FCGR2B expression correlates with M2-like macrophage polarization and immune evasion in HGSOC, supporting a model in which FCGR2B contributes to tumor progression by promoting an immunosuppressive TME and maintaining protumoral macrophage phenotypes. Taken together, these observations suggest that inhibitory signaling of FCGR2B limits FcγR-dependent antitumor immunity in HGSOC. These mechanistic insights highlight FCGR2B as a promising therapeutic target, offering potential opportunities for novel immunotherapy strategies.

Our therapeutic prediction using the DepMap database further revealed that high FCGR2B expression correlates with increased sensitivity to the MEK inhibitor ARRY-886. This aligns with the established role of MEK as a critical regulator through pathway crosstalk to amplify NF-κB activation to drive tumor progression [38,39]. Mechanistically, the cytoplasmic ITIM of FCGR2B recruits SHIP1 phosphatase, potentially modulating the MEK/NF-κB flux to sustain the pro-tumoral phenotype of myeloid cells, paralleling the role of FCGR2B-mediated signaling in driving immune evasion in other solid tumors [40]. These myeloid-specific models represent a clinically relevant context for FCGR2B+ TAMs, suggesting that targeting the FCGR2B/NF-κB axis could reprogram the TME by disrupting immunosuppressive myeloid signaling. However, the systemic inhibition of NF-κB carries a significant risk of off-target toxicity, as this pathway is vital for normal immune homeostasis. Future strategies should prioritize myeloid-targeted delivery, such as nanoparticle-based systems or bispecific antibodies designed to optimize selective FcγR engagement [[41], [42], [43]], to achieve precision TME reprogramming in HGSOC while minimizing systemic adverse effects.

The therapeutic potential of FCGR2B is exemplified by the development of BI-1206, a clinical-stage monoclonal antibody that antagonizes the receptor's inhibitory signaling. Blocking FCGR2B with BI-1206 not only prevents this internalization but also significantly sensitizes multi-resistant tumors to combinational immunotherapies [44]. These findings, coupled with ongoing clinical trials in solid tumors (NCT04219254), reinforce our MR-based conclusion that FCGR2B antagonism is a promising strategy to overcome immunosuppression and enhance treatment efficacy in HGSOC.

In OCCC, subtype-specific proteins are significantly enriched in chaperone-mediated protein folding, aligning with frequent ARID1A mutations [45,46], which render tumor cells dependent on molecular chaperones [47,48]. ARID1A loss activates the IRE1α-XBP1 ER stress pathway and upregulates HDAC6, thereby creating selective vulnerability to chaperone-targeted therapies [47,48]. Molecular chaperones, such as HSP70 and HSP90, maintain the stability of oncogenic proteins [[49], [50], [51]], and modulate the pathogenicity of cancer-predisposing mutations, exemplified by BRCA1 variants [52]. These observations suggest that OCCC may rely on chaperone-mediated folding, particularly in the context of ARID1A loss, highlighting therapeutic opportunities that target chaperone networks.

In LGSOC, subtype-specific proteins are enriched in ECM remodeling pathways, highlighting the central role of ECM in tumor progression. ECM remodeling influences cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and stromal-immune interactions, and has been implicated in therapy resistance in OC [53]. Overexpression of collagen VI (COL6A3) reorganizes the peritumoral matrix, enhances cisplatin resistance, and promotes tumor survival. Dynamic ECM remodeling correlates with disease progression and poor outcomes [54] and contributes to immunosuppressive microenvironments, potentially limiting the efficacy of therapy [55]. These observations underscore ECM remodeling as a key mechanism of LGSOC and a potential therapeutic target.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the GWAS summary statistics for EOC subtypes and immune traits were derived predominantly from cohorts of European ancestry. While the genetic architecture of complex traits is often shared across populations [52], the potential for cross-ethnic variation in immune regulatory landscapes necessitates further validation in diverse, multi-ethnic cohorts to ensure the global generalizability of the FCGR2B axis. Second, our reliance on pQTL and immune QTL data from healthy individuals assumes that germline genetic effects remain consistent across physiological states. Although germline variants represent lifelong, cumulative exposures that precede malignancy [19], context-specific genetic effects within the tumor microenvironment cannot be entirely ruled out. Any such interactions, however, would likely lead to a conservative approximation of the true causal effect due to attenuation bias. Finally, this study employed only cis-pQTLs, and although sensitivity analyses were performed, residual confounding from horizontal pleiotropy remains a possibility. Future research integrating trans-pQTLs and expanded, multi-omic scRNA-seq datasets will be crucial to further elucidate the causal mechanisms and enhance the translational precision of our findings.

Conclusion

Our integrative multi-omics analyses define subtype-specific protein–immune–EOC regulatory axes and highlight FCGR2B as a key macrophage-associated player in the immunosuppressive microenvironment of HGSOC. Elevated FCGR2B expression characterizes tumors enriched with immunosuppressive subsets and correlates with poor prognosis, functioning to facilitate M2-like polarization while sustaining tumor cell proliferation and suppressing apoptosis, making it a potential candidate for therapeutic targeting that requires further experimental and clinical validation.
