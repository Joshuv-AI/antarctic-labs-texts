Clinical characteristics and outcomes of COVID-19-Associated acute encephalopathy in pediatric patients during the Omicron wave in beijing: a single-center prospective study
Authors: SiYuan Huang, Jing Chen, Fang Li, Wei Li, Ning Li, YuChun Yan, Dong Qu, Shuang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Omicron variant has become the predominant strain globally, yet pediatric data on COVID-19-associated acute encephalopathy (AE) remain scarce. This study aimed to characterize the clinical features and outcomes of Omicron-related AE in a PICU cohort.MethodsWe prospectively analyzed 10 pediatric patients (aged ≤ 18 years) diagnosed with COVID-19-associated AE at Capital Center of Children’s health Capital Medical University from December 2022 to January 2023. AE diagnosis followed the Japanese Society of Child Neurology criteria. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, radiological, and outcome data were collected.ResultsMedian page was 35 months (IQR 17.0–62.5), with 80% ≤4 years old. All patients presented with fever and altered mental status; 40% had seizures. Cranial MRI revealed symmetric brain lesions in all cases: 70% acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE), 10% encephalitis with splenial lesions (MERS), and 10% acute encephalopathy with subcortical demyelination (AESD). Elevated inflammatory markers (procalcitonin 31.27 ng/mL [IQR 23.8–38.7], IL-6 21.6 pg/mL [IQR 12.3–34.9]) and CSF pleocytosis were common. Six patients (60%) died within 48 h of PICU admission, all unvaccinated and ≤ 4 years old. Survivors exhibited long-term neurological sequelae (quadriparesis, hypotonia).ConclusionsOmicron-associated AE in young children is characterized by rapidly progressive neurological deterioration, with 60% mortality in this cohort and persistent deficits in survivors. Symmetric MRI lesions, elevated procalcitonin, and CSF cytokine elevation may serve as early diagnostic indicators. Vaccination gaps and innate immune dysregulation likely contribute to severe outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05970-5.Keywords: COVID-19, Omicron variant, Pediatric acute encephalopathy, Critical care, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

The emergence of Omicron variants has redefined the epidemiological landscape of COVID-19, manifested by enhanced transmissibility coupled with attenuation in pulmonary severity compared to ancestral strains [1, 2]. Notably, compared to ancestral strains, Omicron exhibits reduced pulmonary severity in newborns and young infants—who were initially considered highly vulnerable to severe COVID-19—while showing increased extrapulmonary manifestations such as neurological involvement. This shift may relate to its altered tropism and immune evasion properties [3–5]with AE emerging as a predominant clinical concern [6]. Recent surveillance data reveal a concerning surge in severe neurological manifestations among unvaccinated children during Omicron-dominated waves [7]underscoring the urgent need for targeted pediatric research. This prospective cohort study conducts a systematic analysis of clinical phenotypes, neuroimaging correlates, and longitudinal outcomes in pediatric intensive care unit (PICU)-admitted patients with Omicron-associated AE. By elucidating disease mechanisms and clinical trajectories specific to this pediatric population, our investigation addresses critical knowledge gaps in pediatric critical care medicine and informs evidence-based management strategies for COVID-19-associated neurological emergencies.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study included 50 pediatric patients (29 days to 18 years old) admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) at Capital Center of Children’s health, Capital Medical University between December 2022 and January 2023 with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection by PCR testing of nasopharyngeal or lower respiratory specimens. Enrollment required fulfillment of all three criteria: (1) age between 29 days and 18 years at admission, (2) laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 per institutional protocols, and (3) diagnosis of COVID-19-associated acute encephalopathy according to International consensus definitions for infection-triggered encephalopathy syndromes [8]. Patients were excluded if they presented with coexisting central nervous system infections (e.g., bacterial meningitis, herpes simplex encephalitis), had a history of severe underlying neurological disorders including congenital malformations, intractable epilepsy, or progressive neurodegenerative diseases, or were receiving immunosuppressive therapy for non-COVID-19-related conditions. The study was approved by the Capital Institute of Pediatrics Research Ethics Committee on August 10, 2023(NO. SHERLL2023009), and informed consent was obtained from all guardians. This study involving human participants was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Data acquisition and analytical methods

Comprehensive clinical information encompassed demographic characteristics, vaccination history, and multi-dimensional parameters including: complete blood count, inflammatory markers (procalcitonin, interleukin-6, interleukin-8, sampled within 24 h of PICU admission before immunotherapy), liver function tests, coagulation profiles, cranial MRI/CT scans, chest imaging, cerebrospinal fluid analyses, and treatment outcomes[Immunotherapies were administered as follows: methylprednisolone and IVIG within 2 h of PICU admission; plasma exchange (n = 3) and tocilizumab (n = 1) at 24 h]. Viral genome sequencing for SARS-CoV-2 Omicron subvariants was centrally conducted at the Beijing Center for Disease Prevention and Control using the multiplex PCR method previously validated by Pan et al. (2022) [9]. Demographic variables were standardized according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) coding system, while laboratory parameters were analyzed using duplicate measurements from admission and peak severity stages. All image data underwent blinded review by two neuroradiologists specializing in pediatric infectious diseases.

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, the dominant SARS-CoV-2 Omicron subvariants in Beijing were BA.5.2 (n = 4) and BF.7 (n = 6). All enrolled pediatric patients tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 via PCR using nasopharyngeal swabs or lower respiratory tract specimens, though only 4 cases showed serological positivity. Among 50 hospitalized pediatric COVID-19 cases, 10 (20%,10/50) developed acute encephalopathy (AE). These 10 cases were systematically classified using the updated Immunoglobulin Therapy for Encephalitis (ITES) criteria [8]: 7 met criteria for acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE), characterized by bilateral thalamic lesions and rapid neurological decline; 1 was diagnosed with acute encephalopathy with biphasic seizures and late reduced diffusion (AESD), presenting with biphasic seizures and subcortical diffusion restriction; and 1 met criteria for mild encephalopathy with reversible splenial lesion (MERS), showing a reversible splenial lesion and mild encephalopathy (Supplementary Table 1). Baseline characteristics revealed a median age of 35 months (interquartile range [IQR], 17.0–62.5), with 80% of AE cases occurring in unvaccinated infants ≤ 4 years old (Table 1). Clinically, all 10 patients exhibited fever (100%) and altered mental status (100%), with additional features including seizures (40%) and pupillary abnormalities (80%). Seizure details were as follows: 3 cases involved brief seizures (< 5 min), 1 case involved prolonged seizures (5–30 min). Notably, altered mental status developed rapidly after ICU admission; however, no further electroencephalogram (EEG) was performed to confirm the origin of seizures.

Complications and laboratory findings

Nine patients had elevated serum procalcitonin (median 31.27 ng/mL, reference range 0–0.5 ng/mL), and seven had elevated C-reactive protein (median 13.15 mg/L, reference range 0–8 mg/L) (Table 2).

Cytokine storm

Proinflammatory cytokines were significantly elevated: interleukin-6 (median 21.6 pg/mL, reference range 0–8.1 pg/mL), and interleukin-8 (median 318.5 pg/mL, reference range 0–62.0 pg/mL), suggesting excessive inflammatory response (Table 2). IL-10 was elevated alongside proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8). While IL-10 is typically anti-inflammatory, its context-dependent proinflammatory role here may reflect immune dysregulation in severe COVID-19.

Immunosuppression

Total T-lymphocyte count (520.88/uL, IQR 411.10–832.78), CD4 + T-cells (212.99/uL, IQR 190.59–482.66), and CD8 + T-cells (198.78/uL, IQR 123.34–424.62) were markedly reduced, with Th1/Th2 imbalance observed in seven cases (Table 2).

​Cerebrospinal fluid analysis

Four of seven patients had elevated CSF protein (cell counts remained normal), Cerebrospinal fluid interleukin-6 (median 96.8 pg/mL) and interleukin-8 (median 412 pg/mL) levels were significantly higher than serum counterparts (Table 2).

Imaging features

Cranial MRI

Nine (90%) of 10 AE patients exhibited symmetric multifocal cerebral lesions involving the bilateral thalamus, brainstem, basal ganglia, and corpus callosum splenium. Subtype-specific MRI features according to the updated Immunoglobulin Therapy for Encephalitis (ITES) criteria included: 6 cases met criteria for ANE (Fig. 1), showed additional unique lesions in medial temporal lobe/amygdala, external capsules, and cerebellar grey matter; bilateral mesial temporal and occipital grey matter lesions were observed in imaging; 1 case fulfilled criteria for AESD with biphasic seizures and late reduced diffusion (Fig. 2), presenting with subcortical white matter DWI hyperintensity; and 1 case was consistent with mild encephalopathy with MERS (Fig. 3), showing splenium FLAIR hyperintensity. EEG was performed for two patients, with isolated generalized slowing observed for two of them (case 8 and case 10). The latter also presented focal epileptic discharges. Notably, 3 fatal ANE cases (P1, P3, P5) progressed rapidly, developing liver/kidney dysfunction and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) within 48 h—consistent with acute shock with encephalopathy and multi-organ failure (ASEM) phenotypes. Cranial MRI was performed within 48 h of onset, potentially before full cerebral edema (a hallmark of ASEM/acute fulminant cerebral edema [AFCE]) developed, explaining the absence of fully evolved edema patterns.

X-ray and computed tomography (CT) imaging

Chest X-ray was performed in all patients at admission, but no signs of lobar pneumonia were found. In addition, chest computed tomography (CT) was performed in three patients, with the results revealing mild pneumonia. Out of eight patients for whom brain CT scans were carried out, two showed alterations. In particular, diffuse bilateral thalamus swelling was noted in case 3, while case 10 showed hypoattenuation of the bilateral frontal lobe, temporal lobe and parietal lobe as well as a loss of the gray-white differentiation with diffuse cerebral edema.

Treatment and outcomes

Clinical outcomes

Among 10 pediatric patients with COVID-19-associated acute encephalopathy, 6 (60%) died during hospitalization. Mortality timing was acute: deaths occurred at a median of 28 h (range: 14–42 h) after PICU admission, with one additional patient dying on day 4 following treatment withdrawal due to poor prognosis. To better characterize risk, we applied the ANE-Severity Score (ANE-SS) [10] to the 7 patients with acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE), a subtype implicated in severe cases. Notably, 6 of these 7 ANE patients scored ≥ 5 (high-risk category), all of whom required intensive care and succumbed to the disease; the remaining 1 ANE patient scored 3–4 (moderate risk). Consistent with this high-risk profile, all 6 deceased patients (P1–P6) exhibited hypotension requiring catecholamine support and had ANE-SS ≥ 5, further validating the score’s prognostic utility. Key demographic features of deceased patients included young age (all ≤ 4 years old) and lack of vaccination, with no autopsy data available to clarify pathological mechanisms. Among the 4 surviving patients (40%), follow-up assessments ≥ 2 months post-discharge revealed divergent neurological outcomes: two achieved full recovery with no residual deficits, while two exhibited persistent impairments, including hypotonia and quadriparesis (Table 3).

Interventions

All patients received mechanical ventilation, norepinephrine (n = 5), hypothermia, and anticonvulsant therapy. Additional treatments included methylprednisolone (20 mg/kg, n = 10), intravenous immunoglobulin (2 g/kg, n = 10), plasma exchange (n = 3), and tocilizumab (n = 1). Immunotherapies were administered as follows: methylprednisolone and IVIG within 2 h of PICU admission; plasma exchange and tocilizumab at 24 h. No patients required antiviral treatments.

Immune mechanisms

Elevated cerebrospinal fluid interleukin-6 and interleukin-8 levels indicated blood–brain barrier disruption and central nervous system-specific inflammation.

Discussion

Acute encephalopathy—defined as CNS dysfunction secondary to diffuse cerebral edema without significant inflammation—is a critical complication of pediatric COVID-19 11. While encephalopathy is strongly associated with disease severity, its incidence increases proportionally with ascending clinical stages: emergency department studies report a 28% prevalence in mild-to-moderate cases versus 55% in critically ill pediatric patients [12, 13]. Furthermore, multicenter cohort analyses reveal a fivefold increase in adjusted in-hospital mortality risk among children with COVID-19-associated acute encephalopathy compared to those without [14]underscoring its prognostic significance. Emerging evidence suggests immune-mediated mechanisms, including cytokine storm and blood-brain barrier disruption, may drive this pathology [11, 15]. though the absence of neutrophilic infiltration in histopathological studies challenges traditional inflammatory paradigms. Cytokine measurements aimed to explore immune dysregulation in Omicron-associated encephalopathy. Our patients showed higher IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 levels than COVID-19 patients without encephalopathy [16]supporting a distinct profile.

While acute encephalopathy complicates 7–25% of pediatric COVID-19/MIS-C hospitalizations, its clinical spectrum varies from mild cognitive dysfunction to life-threatening encephalitis, with severity directly proportional to resource utilization (e.g., ICU admissions increased by 3.2-fold) and in-hospital mortality [11, 17, 18]. Notably, this study provides the first virological confirmation of BA.5.2/BF.7-associated encephalopathy in Chinese mainland pediatric populations, with MRI findings (symmetric thalamic/cortical lesions) aligning with cytokine-mediated neuroinflammation pathways. The absence of detectable SARS-CoV-2 RNA in CSF raises critical questions about blood-brain barrier permeability mechanisms in Omicron subvariants.

Key clinical features of pediatric SARS-CoV-2-associated AE included rapid progression from febrile prodrome to coma, with a median time of < 6 h, and severe cases often complicated by thrombotic microangiopathy (disseminated intravascular coagulation, DIC), catecholamine-induced shock, and multiorgan failure. Beyond cardiac involvement (hypotension requiring catecholamine support in P1–P6), 2 patients exhibited liver dysfunction, acute kidney injury, coagulopathy, and elevated ferritin—manifestations consistent with previously reported SARS-CoV-2 ANE cases. Mortality varied strikingly across AE subtypes: while ANE remained rare (2.8%, 95% CI 1.1–5.9) [11]it was uniformly fatal (100% mortality), in sharp contrast to acute encephalopathy with biphasic seizures and reduced diffusion (AESD, 34.0% mortality), where 65% of survivors developed epilepsy, and mild encephalitis/reversible splenial lesion (MERS, 19.3% mortality), associated with 28% residual cognitive deficits [19]. Immunopathogenic differences further distinguished these subtypes: AESD and MERS shared a common cytokine storm profile (IL-6 > 500 pg/mL), but ANE was uniquely characterized by systemic complement activation (C5a > 120 ng/mL) and absent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis [20]suggesting divergent immunopathogenesis. The uniformly fatal outcome of ANE contrasts with recent Delta variant studies reporting 40% survival in ANE cohorts [21]raising hypotheses about altered neurotropism or immune modulation specific to the Omicron variant.

Notably, MERS in some cases is linked to genetic variants [22]but our patients showed no identifiable genetic mutations. Whole-exome sequencing in 3 patients revealed no pathogenic variants in neuroinflammatory-related genes or mitochondrial diseases, suggesting Omicron-associated acute encephalopathy here is driven by viral and immunological factors rather than monogenic causes. Additionally, MERS pathogenesis involves inflammatory cytokine-mediated responses rather than direct viral invasion [23–25]with limited targeted therapies—current management relies on supportive care, highlighting the need for IL-6 antagonists or complement inhibitors to severe COVID-19 strategies.

Pathogenetically, ANE in Asian pediatric populations exhibits a distinct infectious trigger profile, with influenza historically linked to hyperpyrexia (≥ 40 °C), refractory seizures (> 30 min), and rapid neurological decline (median time 6–12 h) [26]. In this cohort, ANE patients demonstrated ​​3.2-fold elevated CSF protein​​ and ​​procalcitonin levels exceeding 25 ng/mL​​, mirroring influenza-associated ANE patterns [26]. Hyperlactatemia (≥ 2.5 mmol/L) and CSF procalcitonin emerged as early discriminators (AUC 0.89, 95% CI 0.82–0.96), predating MRI abnormalities by 12–24 h.

Lymphopenia, particularly the depletion of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, is a key indicator of immune dysregulation and correlates with disease severity in both adult and pediatric COVID-19 patients [27]. This immunological abnormality is closely linked to the cytokine storm induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection, which is characterized not only by lymphopenia (including CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell depletion) but also by suppressed interferon-γ production—findings that collectively correlate with clinical severity [28]. Beyond systemic immune dysfunction, emerging evidence highlights CNS involvement in a subset of patients. Although SARS-CoV-2 RNA is undetectable in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in most cases, CNS invasion was observed in 6% of COVID-19 patients. These individuals exhibited CSF abnormalities, including mild pleocytosis (defined as < 10 cells/µL) in 88% and elevated protein levels in 40% [29]. Notably, compartmentalized immune responses were suggested by striking elevations in CSF IL-6 and IL-8 levels—more than 10-fold higher than those in serum—which implicate blood-brain barrier disruption in facilitating immune cell infiltration or cytokine translocation.Consistent with these systemic and CNS immune perturbations, our cohort study further revealed that reduced CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell ratios were associated with more severe encephalopathy. This aligns with the concept that T-cell exhaustion, a hallmark of T-cell dysfunction in advanced COVID-19, exacerbates disease pathogenesis by impairing both adaptive immunity and immune regulation.

Genetic susceptibility to ANE remains elusive, with no associations identified between RANBP2, HLA, or mitochondrial variants and COVID-19-associated ANE in this cohort. Epidemiological trends indicate heightened neurologic complications in Asian populations during Omicron surges, implicating viral lineage-specific pathogenicity [6, 30].

Management strategies combining IVIG and methylprednisolone improved outcomes in MIS-C but failed to mitigate ANE mortality, particularly in children with brainstem involvement [30]. Early immunomodulatory interventions (< 24 h post-symptom onset) correlated with better prognosis in non-brainstem ANE cases40. Urgent development of targeted therapies—pharmacologic or immunomodulatory—is warranted [19].

This study underscores the strategic importance of COVID-19 vaccination in pediatric populations and emphasizes ongoing genomic surveillance for emerging variants. This study has limitations: small sample size (n = 10), single-center design, and retrospective data collection (potential selection bias), which limit generalizability. Future studies should include larger cohorts and long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively characterizes acute encephalopathy in previously healthy children infected with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2/BF.7, reporting a 20% (10/50) incidence rate and 60% (6/10) mortality among affected cohort members, with survivors frequently experiencing persistent neurological deficits. Key clinical insights identify acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE) and acute encephalopathy with biphasic seizures and late reduced diffusion (AESD) as critical sentinel syndromes in febrile pediatric patients, underscoring the urgency of early neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytokine analysis for timely diagnosis. Mechanistically, the immunopathology mirrors influenza-associated encephalopathy, featuring cytokine storms, T-cell lymphopenia, and blood-brain barrier disruption. Notably, despite immunomodulatory interventions, ANE mortality remains high at 85.7% (6/7), highlighting an unmet need for targeted neuroprotective strategies. These findings emphasize the critical importance of global Omicron surveillance, equitable vaccine access, and accelerated development of antiviral/anti-inflammatory therapies to mitigate pediatric neurological morbidity associated with SARS-CoV-2 variants.
