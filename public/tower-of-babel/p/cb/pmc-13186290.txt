Rationale and protocol for a randomized parallel intervention trial of two intermittent fasting approaches in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Felicia L. Steger, Joseph E. Donnelly, Robert N. Montgomery, Lauren Clark, Corby K. Martin, John M. Miles, Kristine Grdinovac, Annie E. Henderson, John P. Thyfault
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a progressive disease driven by insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, often exacerbated by excess body weight. Intermittent fasting (IF) approaches, including intermittent energy restriction (IER) and time-restricted eating (TRE), have shown promise for improving glycemic control and facilitating diabetes remission. However, the feasibility and effectiveness of these IF approaches in individuals with T2D remain underexplored.Objective:This study aims to compare the feasibility, acceptability, and metabolic effects of IER and TRE in a randomized parallel trial in adults with T2D.Methods:Adults with T2D (N = 56) and overweight/obesity will be randomized to one of two interventions: IER (550–800 kcal/day on 2–3 days/week) or TRE (caloric intake within an 8-h daily window). Both groups will receive a comprehensive, remotely delivered diabetes education and behavioral support program over 6 months, followed by a 6-month low-contact follow-up during which participants continued the intervention on their own. Primary outcomes include feasibility (recruitment, retention, adherence) and acceptability. Secondary outcomes include changes in HbA1c, insulin sensitivity, insulin response to a mixed-meal tolerance test, body composition, and diabetes remission. Continuous glucose monitoring, metabolic and behavioral phenotyping will inform predictors of response.Conclusion:This study is the first parallel trial to compare IER and TRE in patients with T2D and overweight/obesity. Results will provide critical data on the metabolic and behavioral responses to each IF approach, informing future strategies to personalize nutrition therapy in T2D.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D), affecting one in four adults, is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [1] characterized by insulin resistance and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction. [2] Lifestyle interventions resulting in significant weight loss (≥5–7 % of initial body weight) are recommended for patients with both T2D and obesity [3–5], and weight loss magnitude predicts metabolic improvements with T2D interventions [6]. Recently, modifying the timing of food intake via intermittent fasting has been explored as an alternative strategy.

Intermittent energy restriction (IER) and time-restricted eating (TRE) are two forms of intermittent fasting (IF) that produce modest to moderate weight loss [7–12]. These methods may provide an advantage for preventing and treating T2D with preliminary data suggesting that IF may improve metabolic flexibility [13,14], increase insulin sensitivity [15–19], reduce beta cell dysfunction [15,18,20], and increase fat loss [11,19,21–23], while preserving lean mass during weight loss [17,22,24]. In addition, the metabolic benefits of IF on insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis may be independent of weight loss or synergize with weight loss [25–27].

Despite enthusiasm for IF in T2D management, clinical trials in this population remain limited [28–34]. Further, no trials have tested different IF approaches in parallel in T2D. To address this gap, we will compare IER and TRE in patients with T2D. This trial aims to assess the feasibility and acceptability of each IF approach and to evaluate changes in glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, and other cardiometabolic and behavioral outcomes. We hypothesize that both interventions will be feasible and will result in clinically relevant improvements in glycemic control.

Methods

Study overview

Study design

This study is a two-arm, parallel randomized clinical trial designed to compare the feasibility, acceptability, and metabolic effects of IER and TRE in adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D). The trial will last 12 months, consisting of six-month active intervention phase followed by a six-month low-contact follow-up period.

Setting

The study will be conducted at the University of Kansas Medical Center (KUMC; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05860413). Behavioral intervention sessions and medical monitoring visits will be delivered remotely (Zoom Video Communications, Inc.). Anthropometric and laboratory assessments will be performed at the KUMC Clinical and Translational Science Unit (CTSU). Study data will be collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools [35] hosted at KUMC. The study protocol was approved by the KUMC Institutional Review Board.

Rationale, sample size, and hypotheses

The primary goals of this trial are to determine whether TRE and/or IER are feasible and effective for improving glycemic control in patients with T2D. The target enrollment of 56 participants was determined to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the interventions and to generate preliminary data for future trials (See Section 2.2). Retention rates are expected to exceed 80 %, based on our previous work and other intermittent fasting studies in similar populations. We hypothesize that both TRE and IER will be feasible, with at least 60 % retention at 12 months and significant improvements in glycemic control.

Participants

Adults (N = 56) with both overweight/obesity and T2D diagnosed within the last 10 years will be recruited. Eligibility criteria are listed in Table 1.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited from the Kansas City metropolitan area. Recruitment sites will be within the University of Kansas Medical Center in the Cray Diabetes Self-Management Center (CDSMC) and the KUMC Weight Management Program. We will also recruit from KUMC Endocrinology clinics, through fliers and campus advertisement venues, and through word-of-mouth referrals.

Screening and enrollment

Potentially eligible participants will be directed to an eligibility survey in REDCap with a brief study overview. Study personnel will schedule an information session for those who meet eligibility criteria and are interested in enrolling. Those who complete informed consent will complete a health history questionnaire and be scheduled for a screening assessment. The in-person screening assessment will include measurements of height, weight, and a point-of-care HbA1c assessment to confirm eligibility information. If preliminarily deemed eligible, participants will be scheduled for a baseline study visit where eligibility will be confirmed. In addition to providing the study devices, participants will be compensated for the completion of each visit. Payments for month 0 and 6 visits are $25 each and for month 6 and 12 visits are $75 each.

Interventions

Participants will be randomized 1:1 into one of two dietary intervention groups: Intermittent Energy Restriction (IER) or Time-Restricted Eating (TRE) for 12 months. Both interventions include a structured diabetes education program, which incorporates behavioral strategies, exercise recommendations, and self-monitoring tools to encourage long-term success and adherence.

Intermittent energy restriction (IER)

Participants in the IER group will follow a 4:3 or 5:2 modified fasting protocol, alternating between modified fasting and normal eating days. The decision to allow for 4:3 or 5:2 was made to increase flexibility and because increasing the number of fasting days beyond two days tends not to impact weight loss or metabolic changes [34]. During the weight loss phase, participants will consume 550–800 kcal/day on 2–3 modified fasting days per week. These fasting days will consist of portion-controlled shakes and entrees supplemented with one serving of fruit and four or more servings of non-starchy vegetables. On non-fasting days, participants will not follow specific caloric restrictions but will receive guidance on balanced eating according to American Diabetes Association guidance for type 2 diabetes [3]. During the weight maintenance phase (months 3–6) and low-contact follow-up period, fasting days will decrease to 1–2 days per week, as needed.

Time-restricted eating (TRE) with energy restriction

Participants in the TRE group will limit intake of calorie-containing foods and beverages to an 8-h eating window ending no later than 19:00 as often as possible or at least five days per week. During the fasting periods, participants will be permitted only non-caloric beverages such as water, black coffee, or unsweetened herbal teas. During the weight loss phase of the intervention only, TRE participants will also be counseled to follow a reduced energy diet of 1200–1800 kcal/day through a combination of portion-controlled meals, fruits, vegetables, and healthy eating guidelines, with instruction that energy restriction is secondary to the eating window guidance. After three months, TRE will adhere to the 8-h window only for weight loss maintenance.

Intervention components shared among groups

Both groups will participate in a diabetes education and lifestyle intervention. The intervention has three stages: Phase 1 (Months 0–3): Weight Loss; Phase 2 (Months 3–6): Weight Loss Maintenance; Phase 3 (Months 6–12): Low-Contact Follow Up. During Phases 1 and 2, all participants will be counseled to reduce energy intake or maintain weight loss through methods specific to each intervention group. Behavioral education will focus on healthy eating, exercise, behavioral strategies, and overcoming barriers. Sessions occur weekly during Phase 1, biweekly during Phase 2, and monthly during the low-contact follow-up period. Additional shared components follow.

Exercise recommendations.

Participants will aim for at least 150 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity weekly and will receive guidance on reducing sedentary behaviors. All participants will be provided with a physical activity monitor (Garmin Venu® Sq, Garmin Ltd., Olathe, KS, USA) to track exercise and steps.

Self-monitoring.

Participants will track body weight, intervention adherence (including class attendance, data submission, and meeting group-specific components of the eating window and modified fasting days), physical activity, barriers to adherence or implementation, and adverse events via REDCap. Self-monitoring surveys will be administered weekly during months 0–6 and monthly during months 6–12, with all reports asking about the week immediately prior to the reporting deadline. Participants are also provided with an excel sheet to track this data prospectively prior to submitting the REDCap reports.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM).

All participants will be provided with CGMs (Freestyle Libre 2; Abbott Laboratories, Illinois, US) for the 6-month active intervention. Glucose levels are measured every minute and stored on the device in 15-min intervals. Thereafter, study personnel will monitor CGM transmission biweekly and contact participants if data are not received. Using the raw CGM data, total time-in-range (a surrogate for HbA1c [36]), mean glucose, glycemic variability (MAGE), time above range, and time below range will be calculated for each two-week period. Changes in CGM metrics will be reported pre-post and also analyzed via longitudinal analysis [37].

Adherence, medical and safety monitoring, and medication adjustments.

The study endocrinologist will monitor participant progress and safety through medical monitoring visits, review of self-monitoring logs and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data. Medical monitoring visits will occur remotely with the study physician or a Certified Diabetes Care and Education Specialist at baseline and after week 6, 12, 24, and 52, as needed, and a review of medication usage and requirements will be conducted. If the participant loses 5 % of more of their body weight, the study physician will be notified, and an additional medical monitoring visit may be scheduled for medication adjustments. If the participant informs the study team of ≥2 instances of hypoglycemia in a one-week period, a medication review and/or additional visit may occur. Medications may also be adjusted outside of the medical monitoring visits as needed.

While most IF studies exclude participants taking insulin or other antihyperglycemic mediations, we will determine how IER and TRE impact medication needs, including insulin. We have developed a medication management protocol for antihyperglycemic medications and pharmacologic therapies for related conditions, such as hypertension. We based our initial medication adjustment protocol on that developed by Carter et al. [32] and modified it for the differences in our fasting regimens. For those in IER, they may either reduce or eliminate some antihyperglycemic medications on days they are following a very-low energy diet. Thereafter and for all participants, medication changes will be guided by continuous glucose monitoring data, weight change, dietary adherence, and any adverse events.

Outcomes and assessments

Participants will undergo a series of assessments to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and metabolic effects of the dietary interventions. These assessments will be conducted at: baseline (week 0), at the end of the weight loss phase (week 12), at the end of the weight loss maintenance and active intervention period (week 24) and at the end of the low-contact follow-up period (week 52). In-person assessments will be conducted after a minimum 12-h, water-only fast and the time of the last food or drink consumed will be recorded. Questionnaires will be sent prior to the testing visit and will be collected during the visit if not complete. An overview of the outcomes and data collection schedule is in Table 2.

Primary outcomes: feasibility and acceptability

The primary outcomes, feasibility and acceptability, will be assessed using both objective and subjective measures. We are primarily interested in whether IER and TRE are feasible in those with T2D. The feasibility of recruitment will be measured as (# screened / # enrolled), with an acceptable threshold of no more than 3:1. If this threshold is not met, the inclusion/exclusion criteria will be modified to improve external validity for this population. Retention for each group is measured as (# completed / # randomized) in the 6-month study and ≥ 60 % retention is considered acceptable. Feasibility will also be evaluated via attendance at intervention sessions, and adherence data from self-reported logs and CGM use. Acceptability will be determined using satisfaction surveys and qualitative feedback collected through a questionnaire created for this trial, which will be sent to participants via REDCap after the 6- and 12-month testing visits. Study-specific questions were developed to capture participant perceptions of feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of the intervention. Items assessed satisfaction, sustainability, barriers, credibility, and willingness to recommend (conceptually analogous to the Net Promoter Score [38]). These domains align with validated implementation outcome measures [39], though the exact validated tools were not administered.

Glycemic control

Changes in glycemia will primarily be assessed via Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) which will be measured at all time points (0, 3, 6, 12 months). Secondarily, we will assess glycemic control via continuous glucose monitoring (continuously months 0–6) and a mixed meal tolerance test (0, 3, 6 months) to assess glycemia in both free-living and lab-based settings. Glycemia metrics will be reported with and without adjustment for medication usage as appropriate.

Continuous glucose monitoring

Continuous glucose monitoring provides a more accurate assessment of acute changes in glycemia that occur in the free-living environment relative to HbA1c, a lagging indicator. We will offer CGMs to all participants over the 6-month active intervention period in this trial. We will train participants on CGM placement and use at the baseline assessment visit. Thereafter, study personnel will monitor CGM transmission weekly and contact participants if data are not received. Using the raw CGM data, total time-in-range (a surrogate for HbA1c [36]), mean glucose, glycemic variability (MAGE), time above range, and time below range will be calculated for each two week period and analyzed via longitudinal analysis. [37]

Mixed meal tolerance testing (MMTT)

We will conduct a mixed meal tolerance test at 0, 3, and 6 months. After the fasting blood draw, a mixed meal providing ~8 kcal/kg will be administered. Participants will be instructed to consume the meal consisting of juice, breakfast crackers, and a breakfast bar within 15 min, or as soon as possible thereafter (See Supplemental Materials). Postprandial blood collection will occur at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min after the fasting sample. Fasting and postprandial samples will be analyzed for glucose, insulin, c-peptide, glucagon, appetite regulators (ghrelin, leptin, pancreatic polypeptide, peptide YY), incretins (GLP-1, GIP) inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, MCP-1), and CXCL10. In addition to the assessment in postprandial samples, fasting samples will also be analyzed for HbA1c, a cardiometabolic panel (CMP), and a lipid panel (comprising total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, VLDL+LDL-cholesterol, and triglycerides). The CMP, HbA1c, and lipid panel assays will be conducted by The University of Kansas Hospital laboratories (Kansas City, KS, US). The blood sample for analysis at the CTBB Core will be collected using chilled BDTM P800 tubes which contain DPPIV inhibitors to preserve incretin peptides. The CMP will be performed on the Beckman Coulter AU680 (Brea, California, US), the lipid panel will be performed on the Beckman Coulter AU5822 (Brea, California, US), and the HbA1c assay will be performed on the Trinity Premier Hb9210 HbA1c Analyzer (Trinity Biotech, Wicklow, Ireland). All other assays will be performed by the Cells, Tissue, Bioinformatics, and Biorepository Core at KUMC using the Milliplex ® Human Metabolic Hormone Panel V3 and performed on the Luminex200 multiplex system (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, US).

Estimates of insulin sensitivity and insulin secretion

Insulin sensitivity, insulin resistance, and β-cell function will be estimated using both fasting and postprandial measures of glucose, insulin and c-peptide. The Homeostatic Model Assessment (HOMA-IR and HOMA-β) will be used to assess β-cell function and insulin resistance (IR) from fasting glucose and insulin. HOMA equations have been reported in over 500 publications, which will allow us to compare our data to prior literature [40]. HOMA2-βc-peptide has a lower coefficient of variation relative to HOMA-β and will be calculated for another estimate of β-cell function [41,42]. The mixed meal tolerance test (MMTT) replicates typical postprandial exposure to glucose, gut, and vagal hormones [43], and postprandial measures of glucose, insulin, and c-peptide can be used to more accurately estimate insulin sensitivity and β-cell function. To assess insulin secretion, we will use AUCC-Peptide0–120/AUCGlucose0–120 as our primary estimate. We will calculate the insulinogenic index to assess early-phase insulin secretion [44], and the Matsuda Index to estimate whole body insulin sensitivity [45].

Medication requirements and diabetes remission

We will calculate medication effect scores to determine whether fewer medications were needed to manage blood sugar during the intervention. Estimates for the resulting impact of common diabetes medications on HbA1c will be multiplied by an adjustment factor based on maximum dosage [46]. Medication effect scores for each medication will be summed to result in a total medication effect score (MES) for each participant. These total MES values will then be added to HbA1c to give an adjusted HbA1c value. To test for diabetes remission after lifestyle changes, HbA1c is measured at least six months after starting the intervention and 3 months after cessation of any pharmacotherapy [47]. Therefore, our study physician will assess eligibility for medication withdrawal after 12 and 24 weeks. Participants with good glycemic control based on >85 % time-in-range by continuous glucose monitoring and who are adherent to the intervention will be evaluated for discontinuing any remaining antihyperglycemic medications. All participants who remain without diabetes medications for at least six months at the end of the intervention and who have a HbA1c <6.5 % will be classified as in diabetes remission [47].

Body weight and composition

Body weight will be measured to the nearest 0.1 kg (Seca Bariatric Scale, model 644) after a 12-h fast. Body composition will be measured via dual x-ray absorptiometry in accordance with standardized procedures at the KUMC CTSU. In addition to reporting fat mass, lean mass, and absolute (kg) and relative (%) fat loss, we will also report fat loss as the percent of weight lost as fat. Percent weight loss from fat will be calculated as 100 % × [Δ fat mass / Δ weight loss] for all participants who lose at least 2 % of initial body weight.

Blood pressure

Blood pressure will be measured at the start of the in-person visit following the AHA/ACC guidelines after a 5-min resting period (Care-scape V100, GE Healthcare). Two readings will be taken, with a 2-min break between the first and second reading. If the two readings differ by 5 mmHg, a third reading will be taken, and the closest two readings will be used for analysis.

Dietary intake, nutrition literacy, and dietary behavior

Dietary intake

We will utilize the Remote Food Photography Method© using the SmartIntake™ mobile application to collect dietary intake data for five consecutive days at each assessment period (months 0, 3, 6, 12). [48] Prior to beginning the photo assisted food record, participants complete a lifestyle interview to gather information related to typical meal timing, food choices, and eating patterns to assist in identifying foods from the photos. Participants are instructed on how to use the mobile application, how to take high quality images of meals, snacks, and beverages, and what additional information is useful for evaluating the photos. Photos are monitored daily during collection to gather additional details and reduce recall bias. Data will be analyzed via Nutrition Data System for Research (NDSR) for macronutrient and micronutrient intake. We will also use NDSR to evaluate diet quality, including the Healthy Eating Index–2020 (HEI-2020) score [49,50].

Diet behavior

We will assess eating behaviors and disordered eating using the EAT-2 [51], Three-factor Eating Questionnaire [52], Eating Examination Questionnaire [53], and Binge Eating Scale [54].

Physical activity

Physical activity readiness will be assessed prior to enrollment [55]. A physical activity monitor (Garmin Venu® Sq, Garmin Ltd., Olathe, KS, USA) will be offered to all participants to track activity and steps during the intervention. Steps and moderate-vigorous exercise minutes will also be self-reported via REDCap.

Sleep

Self-reported sleep quality will be measured using the Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Duration and quality of sleep the night prior to the testing visit will also be reported via Likert scales (1, horrible – 10, excellent) prior to the fasting blood draw.

Appetite

In addition to the appetite hormones collected during the mixed meal tolerance test (See Section 3.2.2), hunger will be assessed via a Likert scale (1, Extremely hungry; 5, Neither hungry nor full; 10, Extremely full) prior to the fasting blood draw, immediately after completion of the mixed meal, and after 1, 2, and 3 h.

Other behavioral and psychosocial factors

We will also assess other behavioral and psychosocial factors that may be related to intervention compliance, and response. These include the BRFSS Diabetes and Perceived Diabetes Self-Management Scale, social determinants of health, cognitive function, health literacy, facilitators, barriers, and satisfaction with intervention components and effects [56].

Data analysis

Our primary aim is to determine whether a definitive RCT to assess the effectiveness of IF on glycemic control is feasible. Baseline demographic characteristics will be reported by group, with means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Quantitative feasibility outcomes will be summarized with point estimates and 95 % confidence intervals. Given common RCT recruitment challenges (rates of RCTs that fully recruit are near 50–60 %), we will estimate the Bayesian posterior probability of recruiting and retaining a range of sample sizes that will then be used to further inform power analyses for a definitive RCT [57,58].

Our secondary aims are to estimate change in glycemic control (HbA1c and CGM data), body weight, body composition, and insulin sensitivity. Linear mixed models will be used to assess differences between groups throughout the intervention. Models will include fixed effects for group, time and the group time interaction and random effects for each participant. The model estimated changes from baseline 24-weeks will be used to assess preliminary efficacy. For each model, residual diagnostic plots, and fitted vs. observed plots will be used to assess model assumptions and fit. Appropriate transformations or alternative models will be used as necessary.

Exploratorily, we’ll identify factors associated with intervention feasibility/adherence and improvements in glycemic control. We’ll use regression analyses and best subset analysis (using Mallows’ CP) to examine how metabolic (e.g., insulin resistance, medication score) and behavioral factors (e.g., diet quality, mood, scheduling flexibility) impact glycemic control at 24 and 52 weeks. This will also help identify factors influencing retention and adherence to guide future intervention modifications.

Expected outcomes

A formal hypothesis test of the effectiveness of TRE or IER on glycemic control will not be conducted as we are primarily interested in feasibility [59]. Therefore, a traditional power analysis was not conducted. Instead, we used simulations to assess our ability to detect preliminary efficacy, defined as at least half the clinically significant change in HbA1c (a reduction of >0.15 %), with the 95 % CI including a 0.3 % reduction. With this approach, we estimated a 96 % probability of detecting a clinically relevant change if it occurs in either TRE or IER.

We expect both IER and TRE to be feasible and lead to clinically relevant improvements in HbA1c (with and without medication adjustment), as well as improvements in insulin action and sensitivity.

Discussion

Most type 2 diabetes cases are attributable to modifiable lifestyle factors, including excess caloric intake, poor dietary quality, and sedentary lifestyles. Recent research has highlighted not only what we eat, but when we eat, as a critical determinant of metabolic health. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and metabolic effects of two intermittent fasting interventions in adults with T2D: Intermittent Energy Restriction (IER) and Time-Restricted Eating (TRE). Both approaches are promising dietary strategies that utilize metabolic flexibility to enhance glycemic control, alongside other health benefits. Despite the interest in these strategies, their comparative effectiveness and practicality is not fully understood.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a pilot study aimed primarily to assess the feasibility of IER and TRE in patients with T2D. Therefore, we did not include a control arm and are not powered to detect between-group differences or pre-post differences in tertiary outcomes. We also acknowledge that our study-specific acceptability and feasibility assessments are subject to bias and limit comparability across trials. Second, inclusion is limited to individuals diagnosed within the prior 10 years and without major comorbidities, which may reduce generalizability to more progressed or complex patients. Third, although a standardized protocol guides medication adjustments, pharmacologic changes could still confound interpretation of glycemic changes. We address this with a medication effect score, but residual confounding is possible. Lastly, the 12-month follow up period may not be adequate to understand the long-term sustainability of these interventions.
