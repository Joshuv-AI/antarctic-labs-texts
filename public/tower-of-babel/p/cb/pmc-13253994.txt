Lingual Dyskinesia as the Presenting Feature of Acquired Demyelinating Syndrome: A Case Report and Review of Differential Diagnoses
Authors: Briana Davis, Ahmed Kaki, Eppie M Yiu, Emma Macdonald‐Laurs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Learning Points Acquired demyelinating syndromes may present with movement disorders, including lingual dyskinesia.The differential diagnosis for abnormal tongue movements includes: acquired demyelinating syndromes, structural lesions involving the basal ganglia, brainstem or cerebral cortex, stroke, tics, adverse drug reactions, systemic autoimmune conditions including Sydenham's chorea, and genetic conditions.In the absence of an alternative explanation, MRI brain should be undertaken in children presenting with new onset abnormal tongue movements.

Introduction

Abnormal tongue movements are an uncommon presenting feature in children with neurological disorders, and are associated with a broad range of differential diagnoses. We report a 4‐year‐old boy who presented to the Emergency Department with unusual writhing tongue movements who was subsequently diagnosed with an acquired demyelinating syndrome.

Case Report

A previously healthy 4‐year‐old presented with 5 days of abnormal tongue movements, poor oral intake, slurred speech and drooling. He was mildly lethargic at presentation, but this resolved the following morning, prior to treatment. There was no history of swallowing difficulty, gagging or choking. There was no history of foreign body ingestion or accidental poisoning. There were no other neurological symptoms; in particular, no difficulties with balance and coordination, no urinary or bowel symptoms, or vision changes. Three weeks prior to presentation, he had an illness with fever, coryza and diarrhoea from which he had fully recovered.

Neurological exam revealed dysarthria, intermittent sialorrhea and rolling and writhing tongue movements. Lingual dyskinesia was constant and was present with the tongue at rest in the oral cavity (Video S1) and when protruded. Voluntary horizontal and vertical tongue movements and tongue protrusion were normal. There were no other abnormal movements of the face, upper or lower limbs. No other focal neurology was present and he was not encephalopathic.

Brain MRI showed hyperintensities on T2 and FLAIR sequences in the left putamen, external capsule, subcortical and white matter of the posterior insula with a similar but smaller focus in the right putamen (Figure 1A,B). There was no diffusion restriction or gadolinium enhancement. Spine MRI showed an equivocal region of T2 hyperintensity in the thoracic cord. The brainstem and cranial nerves had a normal appearance. Cerebrospinal fluid testing (CSF) showed no pleocytosis or elevated protein (0.27 g/L). Identical patterns of trace oligoclonal bands were present in both CSF and serum. Serum myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies (live cell‐based assay, Churchill Hospital, Oxford) and anti‐aquaporin 4 antibodies were negative. CSF and serological testing for herpes simplex viruses, cytomegalovirus, varicella zoster virus, Epstein–Barr Virus, mycoplasma, toxoplasmosis and bartonella were negative. Antinuclear antibodies, anti‐double stranded DNA antibodies and complement levels were within normal limits.

MRI performed at the time of presentation demonstrated patchy, poorly defined hyperintensities involving the bilateral (left more than right) putamina as shown in T2‐weighted (A) and FLAIR (B) axial images. Follow‐up MRI performed 4 months after presentation demonstrated resolution of these findings (C, D).

Based on the MRI and CSF findings he was diagnosed with a monofocal acquired demyelinating syndrome. He was treated with high‐dose intravenous methylprednisolone (15 mg/kg) for 3 days followed by a tapering course of oral corticosteroids over 4 weeks. His lingual dyskinesia resolved within 1 week of steroid treatment. Repeat brain and spine MRI at 4 months following presentation showed resolution of previous bilateral basal ganglia changes and normal appearance of the spinal cord (Figure 1C,D).

Discussion

Lingual dyskinesia is defined as involuntary, repetitive but nonrhythmic, irregular movements of the tongue causing functional impairment [1]. Lingual dyskinesia often has an undulating quality similar to choreiform movements. It may occur in isolation or in association with dyskinesia of the labial and mandibular musculature, as seen in tardive dyskinesia [1]. Although lingual dyskinesia, or other abnormal tongue movements including tremor, dystonia, myoclonus or chorea, occur infrequently in children, they are associated with a broad differential diagnosis (Table 1).

Our patient presented with lingual dyskinesia and demyelinating lesions on MRI. Abnormal tongue movements are rarely reported in acquired demyelinating syndromes; however, other movement disorders, including choreoathetosis and dystonia, have been reported in paediatric cohorts [2]. The patient did not meet the full diagnostic criteria for acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) due to the absence of definite encephalopathy and polyfocal neurological deficits. Nonetheless, given the acute symptom onset in a previously well child and the presence of demyelinating lesions on MRI, a diagnosis of an acute demyelinating syndrome was made, with an excellent clinical and radiological response to steroids.

The differential diagnosis for abnormal tongue movements is outlined in Table 1. In addition to acquired demyelinating syndromes, lingual tremor, dyskinesia and dystonia have been reported in association with structural lesions, for example, tumours or infarcts involving the low precentral or subcentral gyri, basal ganglia, thalamus and brainstem, particularly involving the pons [1, 7].

Abnormal tongue movements may also occur in the absence of MRI abnormalities, for example due to tics. Immune‐mediated encephalitides or systemic autoimmune diseases may also cause abnormal tongue movements. In children, anti‐N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor encephalitis is by far the most common, this frequently presenting with a hyperkinetic orofacial and limb movement disorder, along with seizures, sleep–wake cycle disturbance, autonomic instability and neuropsychiatric symptoms [3]. Sydenham's chorea, occurring secondary to rheumatic fever, may present with a ‘darting tongue’ where choreiform movements occur upon tongue protrusion giving the appearance of the tongue darting in and out of the mouth [9]. Less commonly genetic or neurometabolic conditions such as paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia [5], Rett syndrome [6], Wilson's disease [1] and Lesch Nyhan syndrome may present with dystonic, dyskinetic or ‘galloping’ tongue movements, although rarely in isolation.

Finally, lingual dyskinesia may occur as an adverse effect of commonly prescribed paediatric medications including antiemetics, anticholinergic agents, anticonvulsants and particularly antipsychotics. Although atypical antipsychotics carry a lower risk than first‐generation agents, risperidone has been reported to cause tardive dyskinesia both at treatment initiation or following abrupt withdrawal [1, 8].

Although uncommon, abnormal tongue movements in children have a broad and varied differential diagnosis. In the absence of a causative lesion on MRI, and depending on clinical context, immune‐mediated encephalitides, systemic autoimmune diseases, genetic or neurometabolic conditions and medication side effects should be considered.
