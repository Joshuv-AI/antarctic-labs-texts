Rare Gain‐of‐Function Variants in IDH1 and IDH2 Confer Increased Risk of Immune‐Mediated Rheumatic Diseases
Authors: Mahmut S. Kaymakci, Mehmet Hocaoğlu, Kenneth J. Warrington, Amr H. Sawalha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGain‐of‐function variants in IDH1 and IDH2 are enriched among individuals with coexisting myeloid neoplasms and autoimmune diseases. However, the association between IDH1/IDH2 variants and rheumatic diseases independent of hematologic neoplasms remains unclear. We investigated the association between rare gain‐of‐function and loss‐of‐function genetic variants in IDH1 and IDH2 and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases.MethodsIndividuals with gain‐of‐function and loss‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 were identified by whole genome sequencing in the UK Biobank and the All of Us Research Program. These individuals were matched 1:10 to controls without IDH1 or IDH2 variants by age, sex, and ancestry. The frequency of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases in the presence and absence of IDH1/IDH2 variants was compared using chi‐square or Fisher exact tests.ResultsFifty‐five individuals with IDH1 or IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants without pre‐existing hematologic neoplasms were identified in the UK Biobank. These individuals had a significantly higher frequency of incident immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases compared to matched controls (11 of 55 [20%] vs 20 of 550 [4%], odds ratio [OR] = 6.6, P = 2.7 × 10−5), mainly polymyalgia rheumatica (6 of 11) and rheumatoid arthritis (5 of 11). Replication in the All of Us cohort identified 65 individuals with gain‐of‐function variants and confirmed an increased odds of incident immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases compared to controls (OR = 7.7, P < 5 × 10−4), predominantly rheumatoid arthritis. There was no association between loss‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases in either cohort.ConclusionRare gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 are associated with increased risk of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases, particularly rheumatoid arthritis and polymyalgia rheumatica.

INTRODUCTION

Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) and isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2) catalyze the conversion of isocitrate to α‐ketoglutarate, a metabolite that plays a central role in epigenetic regulation. 1 Gain‐of‐function mutations at IDH1 R132 as well as IDH2 R140 and R172 result in the accumulation of the oncometabolite (R)‐2‐hydroxyglutarate 2 and lead to DNA and histone hypermethylation, cell differentiation blockage, 3 and alterations in the immune system, such as innate immune system activation. 4

Somatic hotspot gain‐of‐function mutations in IDH1 and IDH2 have been shown to be implicated in various types of cancers in humans, including myeloid neoplasms. 2 These mutations are also observed in clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), a precursor state for hematologic neoplasms characterized by somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cell–derived blood clones with a variant allele frequency (VAF) > 2%, in the absence of hematologic neoplasms or disorders. 5 , 6 CHIP is a risk factor for autoimmune diseases, 7 , 8 , 9 and CHIP‐associated mutations are most commonly located in DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 genes. IDH1 and IDH2 are less commonly affected, and there are limited data on the association between IDH1/IDH2 mutations and autoimmune diseases.

Myeloid neoplasms are associated with a high frequency of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases, affecting around 25% of patients. 10 Furthermore, genetic studies have demonstrated a higher frequency of IDH1/IDH2 mutations in individuals with coexisting myeloid neoplasms and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases compared to those with myeloid neoplasms alone. 4 , 10 These data suggest a pathogenic role for IDH1/IDH2 mutations in autoimmunity.

Previous studies have primarily focused on the occurrence of rheumatologic diseases among patients with IDH‐mutated hematologic neoplasms. Indeed, the frequency and characteristics of systemic immune‐mediated rheumatologic diseases among individuals with IDH1/IDH2 mutations independent of hematologic neoplasms remain unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate the association of functional genetic variants in IDH1 and IDH2 with immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases, leveraging whole genome sequencing (WGS) data from two large independent population‐based cohorts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Our study population included individuals recruited in the UK Biobank and the All of Us Research Program. The UK Biobank enrolled around 500,000 individuals aged 40 to 69 years in the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 and followed the health care outcomes of enrolled individuals longitudinally. 11 WGS was performed in all individuals, 12 and genetic data were linked to electronic health records, which include inpatient hospital records and cancer registrations for all individuals. CHIP data are available for approximately 450,000 individuals and were derived from whole exome sequencing data. 5

The All of Us Research Program is a prospective cohort study that anticipates enrolling 1 million individuals from diverse backgrounds in the United States. 13 The latest data release (version 8) includes short‐read WGS data from 414,830 participants, of whom 317,969 have electronic health records available. 13

Study design

Cases were defined as individuals with gain‐of‐function or loss‐of‐function genetic variants in IDH1 or IDH2. Controls were defined as those individuals without functional variants in these two genes. Cases were matched in a 1:10 ratio to controls based on age, genetic sex or self‐identified sex, and genetic ancestry or ethnic background. Individuals with hematologic neoplasms before or within six months of DNA sequencing sample collection were removed from the gain‐of‐function variant analysis, because gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 are known to be associated with an increased risk for hematologic neoplasm. For the analysis of the CHIP mutation cohort, individuals with IDH1/IDH2‐mutant CHIP in the UK Biobank were matched in a similar manner to those without CHIP mutations and without hematologic neoplasms before or within six months of blood sample collection. In the All of Us Research Program, matching was conducted via genetic sex and genetic ancestry, whereas in the UK Biobank, self‐reported sex and ethnic background were used. Ethnic backgrounds in the UK Biobank are categorized as follows: British, Irish, White, any other White background was grouped as White; Indian, Pakistani, any other Asian background were grouped as Asian or Asian British; Caribbean, African, any other Black background was grouped as Black or Black British; and the remaining ethnic backgrounds were grouped as Others. In the All of Us Research Program, Middle Eastern and American genetic ancestry populations were grouped after matching and reported as Others.

Gain‐of‐function variants

Gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1 were defined as those variants resulting in amino acid substitution at R132 to H, C, G, S, L, and I; and in IDH2 at R140 to Q, W, L, and/or at R172 to K, G, S, W, M, R, and T 14 , 15 (Supplementary Document 1). Both in the All of Us Research Program and in the UK Biobank, gain‐of‐function variants were identified by WGS and required to have a Phred Genotype Quality (GQ) Score of ≥10 and depth of ≥10. In the All of Us Research Program, variants of interest were extracted from short‐read WGS data (version 8) using the Genomic Extraction Tool and DRAGEN version 3.7.8 germline variant calling pipeline. Similarly, UK Biobank WGS data were processed using the DRAGEN version 3.7.8 germline variant calling pipeline.

Secondary analysis for IDH1/IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants identified by a somatic caller in the UK Biobank was conducted using CHIP data. The characteristics of CHIP data in the UK Biobank are described elsewhere. 5 Briefly, somatic mutation caller Mutect2 was used, and variants were required to have a total allele depth of ≥20, minimum allele depth of ≥3, and VAF ≥2%, and both forward and reverse sequencing reads should be available. 5 Individuals with hematologic malignancies before or within six months of enrollment were excluded. 5

Loss‐of‐function variants

Putative loss‐of‐function variants in IDH1 and IDH2 were identified from WGS data and were defined as variants resulting in frameshift, splice acceptor, splice donor, stop gain, or start loss 16 (Supplementary Document 1). Both in the UK Biobank and All of Us Research Program, loss‐of‐function variants were required to have a Phred GQ Score of ≥10, depth of ≥10, and VAF ≥0.20.

Definition of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases

Immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases included in this study were giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, Takayasu arteritis, polyarteritis nodosa, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, microscopic polyangiitis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Behçet disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, cutaneous lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, Sjögren disease, dermatomyositis, polymyositis, relapsing polychondritis, systemic sclerosis, and sarcoidosis. Incident cases were defined as individuals diagnosed with any of these aforementioned diseases at least six months after sample collection.

Study patients were required to have at least one or more International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 and/or 10 codes for immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases or hematologic malignancies to be considered to have an immune‐mediated rheumatic disease or a hematologic malignancy, respectively (Supplementary Documents [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for the analysis of age, sex, and race. To compare the frequency of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases between cases and controls, a chi‐square test or Fisher exact test was used, depending on whether the expected cell counts were ≥5 or <5, respectively. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated and a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using Python.

RESULTS

IDH1 / IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants

In the UK Biobank, 55 individuals with gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 without prevalent hematologic neoplasms were identified (Table 1). Compared to age, sex, and ancestry matched controls, individuals with gain‐of‐function variants had a higher frequency of incident immune‐mediated rheumatic disease (11 of 55 [20%] vs 20 of 550 [4%], OR 6.6 [95% confidence interval (CI) 3.0–14.7], P = 2.7 × 10−5). The most common diagnoses were polymyalgia rheumatica (6 of 11) and rheumatoid arthritis (5 of 11).

A secondary analysis was conducted using CHIP data from the UK Biobank. Seventy‐eight individuals with IDH1/IDH2‐mutant CHIP were identified (Table 2), of whom 44 overlapped with the 55 individuals included in the primary analysis. The mean (SD) VAF of IDH1 and IDH2 was 0.22 (0.07) and 0.24 (0.09), respectively. The most frequent CHIP comutations with IDH1/IDH2 were in SRSF2 (28 of 78 [36%]), followed by ASXL1 (8 of 78 [10%]) and DNMT3A (7 of 78 [9%]). Compared to controls, individuals with IDH1/IDH2‐mutant CHIP had a higher frequency of incident immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases (10 of 78 [13%] vs 34 of 780 [4%], OR 3.2 [95% CI 1.5–6.8], P = 0.0042). The most common were polymyalgia rheumatica (4 of 10) and rheumatoid arthritis (4 of 10).

In the All of Us Research Program, 65 individuals with gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 and no prevalent hematologic neoplasm were identified (Table 1). Similar to the UK Biobank, individuals with gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 had higher odds of developing immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases compared to controls (OR 7.7, P < 5 × 10−4), with rheumatoid arthritis being the most frequent (~60%).

Overall, in the combined UK Biobank and All of Us cohorts, gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1 and IDH2 were associated with a markedly increased risk of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases compared with controls. One‐third of affected individuals carried IDH1 variants, whereas two‐thirds carried IDH2 variants. Comparative characteristics of the individuals with IDH1 versus IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants in the UK Biobank can be found in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

IDH1 / IDH2 loss‐of‐function variants

In the UK Biobank, 357 individuals with loss‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 were identified (Table 3). There was no significant association between loss‐of‐function variants and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases (13 of 357 [4%] vs 151 of 3,570 [4%], OR 0.85 [95% CI 0.48–1.52], P > 0.05). In the All of Us Research Program, 262 individuals with loss‐of‐function variants were identified, and consistent with the UK Biobank findings, no significant association was observed between these variants and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases (26 of 262 [10%] vs 218 of 2,620 [8%], OR 1.2 [95% CI 0.8–1.9], P > 0.05) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

We investigated the association between functional variants in IDH1/IDH2 and the development of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases through an unbiased, genotype‐first approach in approximately 1 million individuals using two independent population‐based cohorts. We found that individuals with gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 had approximately seven times the odds of developing immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases, primarily rheumatoid arthritis and polymyalgia rheumatica. In contrast, there was no association between loss‐of‐function variants in these two genes and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases. These results support the hypothesis that IDH1/IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants are associated with autoimmunity independent of hematologic neoplasms.

The mechanism by which gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 might lead to the development of immune‐mediated diseases remains incompletely understood. Gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 result in the production of an oncometabolite (R)‐2‐hydroxyglutarate, 2 which inhibits TET2, leading to DNA and histone hypermethylation 3 and innate immune system activation. 4

Previous studies have focused on IDH1/IDH2 variants associated with immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases in individuals diagnosed with a hematologic neoplasm. 4 , 17 Patients with IDH‐mutated hematologic neoplasms and immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases have elevated markers of inflammation such as interferon γ (IFNγ), interleukin (IL)‐13, and Fas Ligand compared to those with wild‐type IDH‐hematologic neoplasms. 17 Sirenko et al investigated the molecular consequences of somatic gain‐of‐function mutations in IDH1/IDH2 in individuals without hematologic neoplasms using single‐cell sequencing of IDH‐mutant monocytes and found upregulation of IFNγ response, IL‐6/JAK/STAT3, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) signaling pathways. 18

In our study, polymyalgia rheumatica and rheumatoid arthritis were identified as the most common immune‐mediated rheumatic disease associated with IDH1/IDH2 gain‐of‐function genetic variants, which is consistent with previous reports in patients with concomitant hematologic neoplasms. 4 , 17 IL‐6 and TNFα play an important role in the pathogenesis of polymyalgia rheumatica 19 and rheumatoid arthritis, 20 and upregulation of these pathways in IDH‐mutant monocytes may provide a mechanistic explanation for this association.

Although somatic gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1/IDH2 are well recognized in cancer biology, germline gain‐of‐function variants in these genes are rarely reported and are often lethal in utero. 1 , 21 Notably, the majority of individuals (80%) identified by the germline caller in our study were also detected by the somatic caller in the UK Biobank (CHIP), supporting a predominantly somatic origin of IDH gain‐of‐function variants. Nevertheless, in the absence of a second nonhematologic tissue, the possibility that some gain‐of‐function variants are germline cannot be excluded in our study.

The strengths of this study include an unbiased genotype‐first approach that enabled identification of individuals with gain‐of‐function variants independent of hematologic neoplasms, the large population size, and replication of findings in two independent cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, the identification of immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases and hematologic neoplasms relied on ICD codes, which may introduce misclassification bias. Second, consistent with the rarity of IDH1/IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants, the number of variant carriers was limited (n = 120 in total), which may reduce statistical power and generalizability. Finally, this study is observational in nature and lacks functional experiments to directly elucidate the molecular mechanisms linking IDH1/IDH2 gain‐of‐function variants to immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases.

In conclusion, gain‐of‐function variants in IDH1 and IDH2 confer an increased risk of incident immune‐mediated rheumatic diseases, predominantly rheumatoid arthritis and polymyalgia rheumatica, highlighting a potential mechanistic link between altered IDH function and autoimmunity.
