Systemic Inflammation Decreases Initial Brain Injury but Attenuates Neurite Extension and Synapse Formation during the Repair of Injured Brains
Authors: Sushil Gaire, Haijie Yang, Manisha Dumre, Eun Jeong Lee, Sang-Myun Park, Eun-Hye Joe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we explored the impact of systemic inflammation on initial brain injury and repair processes, including neurite extension and synapse formation. For this purpose, we established a brain injury model by administering adenosine triphosphate (ATP), a component of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), through stereotaxic injection into the striatum of mice. Systemic inflammation was induced by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS-ip). Bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses and immunostaining for microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) showed that LPS-ip led to a reduction in initial brain injury, but inhibited neurite extension into the damaged brain. LPS-ip upregulated expression of defense response genes and anti-apoptotic genes, but decreased expression of genes associated with repair and regeneration. In addition, LPS-ip reduced levels of vGlut1 and PSD95 (markers for excitatory pre and post synapses, respectively), but had little effect on vGAT and gephyrin (markers for inhibitory pre and post synapses, respectively). Taken together, these findings suggest that systemic inflammation reduce initial damage but impede subsequent repair process.Keywords: Systemic inflammation, Brain injury, Neurite extension, Synapses, Repair

INTRODUCTION

Systemic inflammation refers to inflammation that occurs throughout the entire body in response to infection, injury, or other harmful stimuli. Certain diseases, including arthritis and diabetes, are considered systemic inflammatory diseases [1, 2]. These systemic inflammatory diseases are suggested risk factors of neurodegenerative diseases [3-5]. In addition, it has been suggested that systemic inflammation can exacerbate symptoms of neurodegenerative diseases and accelerate disease progression [6-8]. There are various pathways through which a systemic inflammatory response can relay signals to the central nervous system [9, 10]. These include circumventricular organs, endothelium, sensory afferents of the vagus nerve, and direct active transport across the blood-brain barrier (BBB).

LPS-induced systemic inflammation affects cells in both intact and injured brains [11-14]. In intact brains, systemic inflammation changes the morphology of microglia and astrocytes, alters the expression of inflammatory cytokines [11, 15]. Microglia activated by systemic inflammation phagocytose synapses and lose their capacity to synchronize local neural circuits, resulting in cognitive deficits [16, 17]. Activated microglia may increase BBB permeability through phagocytosis of astrocytic end-feet [18]. In response to brain injury, acutely induced systemic inflammation is known to play a protective role in initial damage [15, 19]. However, persistent systemic inflammation may change neural repair [20, 21]. In addition to microglia and astrocytes, blood monocytes directly affected by LPS-induced systemic inflammation infiltrate injured brains also play for repair through phagocytosis of damaged neurons, expression of neurotrophic factors, and production of exosomes for neurite extension and astrocyte process elongation [22-27]. However, the underlying mechanisms of initial protective effects and attenuated repair processes are not yet clearly defined.

Here, we found that LPS-induced systemic inflammation reduced the initial brain injury and repair processes. LPS-ip upregulated genes related to defense response and anti-apoptosis, but decreased expression of genes associated with repair and regeneration. Particularly, LPS-ip reduced levels of excitatory synapse more than inhibitory synapses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and LPS-ip administration

Male mice aged 8~10 weeks from the C57BL/6N strain were used in this study. The mice were maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water. All experimental procedures were carried out following approved animal protocols and adhering to the guidelines set by the Ethics Review Committee for Animal Experiments at Ajou University School of Medicine.

A group of animals was challenged with LPS (1 mg/kg) (L7770, sigma, sourced from salmonella enterica serotype enteritidis) intraperitoneally on alternate days, commencing three days before brain injury and continuing until the day of sacrifice.

Stereotaxic injection

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injections of tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg; Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus (David Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA). Brain injury was induced by unilateral administration of ATP (500 mM, 0.8 μl; Sigma), a DAMPs component, into the striatum (AP, +1.0 mm; ML, -2.0 mm; DV, -3.2 mm from bregma) based on stereotaxic coordinates from The Atlas of the Mouse Brain (Paxinos and Franklin, 2nd edition), as explained in previous work [28]. ATP was prepared by dissolving it in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A total of 0.8 μl of ATP solution was infused at a rate of 0.2 μl/min using a Hamilton syringe equipped with a 30-gauge needle connected to a syringe pump (KD Scientific, New Hope, PA). The entire injection process, comprising 4 minutes of ATP infusion and an additional 6-minute waiting period before withdrawal, was implemented to prevent leakage or outflow.

Tissue preparation

Mice were anesthetized, and transcardial perfusion was performed initially with PBS containing 0.5% sodium nitrate and heparin (10 U/ml), followed by perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB; pH 7.2). The brains were then stored at 4°C in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PB for 1 day before being transferred to a 30% sucrose solution in PBS until fully submerged. Six separate series of 35-μm-thick coronal sections were obtained using a cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and stored in an antifreeze stock solution (PB containing 30% glycerol and 30% ethylene glycol, pH 7.2) at 4°C until utilized for immunostaining.

For protein sampling, brains were collected after transcardial perfusion with PBS. Brain slice (2 mm thickness) of the needle injection site was prepared using an Alto mouse brain slicer matrix (Roboz Surgical Instruments, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Tissue blocks (2×2×2 mm3) were obtained and stored at -70°C until use.

Immunostaining and image analysis

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were first washed with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (PBST) and treated with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). They were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a combination of primary antibodies. After rinsing three times with PBST, the sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were visualized by counterstaining with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma). Sections were embedded in Fluoroshield Mounting Medium (Abcam, Cambridge, Great Britain). Images were captured using LSM800 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and analyzed with ZEN software (Carl Zeiss). Damaged volumes of injured brain sections immunostained with MAP2 were reconstructed and assessed using Neurolucida software (MBF Bioscience, Williston, VT, USA). Higher magnification images of synapses were acquired with a Leica TCS SP8 microscope and analyzed with Leica Las-X software. Imaris software (Bitplane, Belfast, UK) was used to quantify the number of colocalized synapses with the “Spots” module.

Western blotting

Brain lysates were prepared on ice using RIPA buffer (10 mM PB (phosphate buffer) pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) with a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA). Lysates were boiled for 5 minutes at 95°C in sample buffer (6.25 mM Tris pH 6.8, 12.5% glycerol, 2.5% SDS, 0.025% bromophenol blue, and 5% β-mercaptoethanol). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk (Seoul Dairy Coop, Seoul, Korea), the nitrocellulose membrane was sequentially incubated with primary antibodies, peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Koma Biotech, Seoul, Korea), and enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Daeil Lab Services, Seoul, Korea). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was served as the loading control. For vGlut1 and VGAT analysis, brain lysates were not boiled. Band intensities were quantified using Image J.

Bulk RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total cellular RNA was extracted using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Illumina-compatible libraries were generated with the TruSeq RNA library preparation kit (Illumina, CA, USA), as per the manufacturer's guidelines. mRNA was purified through polyA selection from total RNA, chemically fragmented, and converted into single-stranded cDNA using random hexamer priming. RNA libraries were then constructed through PCR amplification, and quantification was performed using quantitative PCR (qPCR) with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, following the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (Agilent Technologies Inc., San Diego, CA). The cDNA library underwent sequencing on the HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina), and the sequence was analyzed (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea). Quality verification of the sequences was done using FastQC v0.10.0. Raw data were log2-transformed and subjected to analysis using R Studio software. The packages used in this study are RColorBrewer, ColorRamp2, ComplexHeatmap, and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). Transcriptomic analysis utilized samples from four mice (n=4).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA). An unpaired t-test was used to compare two groups. Details of the specific statistical tests and corresponding p-values are provided in the figure legends. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All reported values are expressed as means±standard errors of the mean (SEMs) from at least three independent experiments.

RESULTS

To investigate the impact of systemic inflammation on brain injury and repair, we induced systemic inflammation by intraperitoneal (ip) injection of LPS (1 mg/kg) every other day, starting 3 days before inducing injury, shown in Research design (Fig. 1). Stereotaxic striatal injection of ATP, a component of DAMPs, induces damage acutely without secondary damage, which makes quantitative analysis appropriate [28-30]. Brain damage was analyzed with immunostaining for MAP2 and TH at day 1 (Fig. 2a), and damage areas were reconstructed based on MAP2 images using Neurolucida 3D modeling (Fig. 2b). As expected from the preconditioning effect [19, 31, 32], initial damage volume at 1 d was smaller in the LPS-ip brains than control brains (Fig. 2).

To investigate the protective effect of LPS-ip on initial damage, we examined the expression of genes altered by LPS-ip in intact brains after two LPS injections using RNA-seq analysis. LPS-ip increased the expression of genes in gene ontology (GO) terms such as immune response, defense response, response to biotic stimulus, inflammatory response, and myeloid leukocyte migration (Fig. 3a). As expected, the expression of genes associated with the positive regulation of defense response, such as Tlr2, Irf7, S100a8/a9, and Cxcl10 [33-37], was higher in +LPS brains (Fig. 3b, c). In addition, LPS-ip enhanced anti-apoptotic genes such as Bcl2a1a and Bcl2a1b [19, 38] in the intact brain, although LPS-ip slightly increased the expression of positive regulators of cell death, such as Bax and caspases (Fig. 3d). The ratio of Bax/Bcl-2a1 was decreased in the intact brain following LPS-ip treatment (Fig. 3e). These results suggest that the reduction in initial damage observed in LPS-ip brains (Fig. 2) may be attributed to the upregulation of defense response genes and anti-apoptotic genes.

LPS-ip attenuated neurite regeneration and synapse formation

Next, we examined the time-dependent effect of LPS-ip on the repair of injured brains. Transcriptomic analysis showed that even in intact brains, LPS-ip decreased expression of genes related to the GO terms associated with repair and regeneration, such as axon extension, axonogenesis, synapse organization, and oligodendrocyte differentiation etc., although their expression was decreased at 3 and 7 d in both brains with and without LPS-ip (Fig. 4a). Accordingly, the extension of neurites into the injury sites with MAP2 immunostaining showed that in LPS-ip brains, the intensity of MAP2 near the damage core was slightly lower at 14 d, and the difference significantly increased at 21 d (Fig. 4b, c).

Synapse formation is crucial for the functional repair of injured brains. In the transcriptomic analysis, LPS-ip treatment altered the expression of various synaptic genes encoding transporters (Slc6a11, Slc6a1, Slc1a2, Slc1a3, Slc17a7) and receptors (Gabbr1, Gabbr2, Grin2c, Grik5, Gria1) (Fig. 5a). Most of these genes were downregulated by LPS-ip in intact brains. LPS-ip decreased expression of more genes related to excitatory synapse than inhibitory synapse: LPS-ip downregulated 24 genes among 25 glutamatergic genes in intact brains (24/25), and 17 genes in all time points of intact, 3, and 7 d (17/25); downregulated 15 inhibitory genes (15/25) in intact brains, and 11 genes in all time points (11/25) (Fig. 5b~d). LPS-ip decreased Slc17a7 (storage and release of glutamate), Gria1 (glutamate receptor), and Slc1a2 (glutamate transporter that clears glutamate from the synaptic cleft), and Slc6a1 (GABA transporter gene), but had little effect on Slc32a1 (vesicle loading and release of GABA), and Gabra1 (GABA receptor) (Fig. 5e, f). In western blot, levels of both excitatory (vGlut1 and PSD95) and inhibitory (vGAT and gephyrin) synaptic markers gradually declined by 7 d, and then slightly increased (vGlut1 and PSD95 in both -/+ LPS brains) or maintained (vGAT and gephyrin in both brains) (Fig. 5g). Interestingly, the levels of PSD95 were lower in intact +LPS brains compared to -LPS brains (Fig. 5g). Expression of synaptic pruning genes such as Ngef, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, and Trem2 was higher in +LPS brains in intact state but became similar in both groups at 3 and 7 d (Fig. 5h).

We further analyzed synaptic markers and MAP2 using immunostaining and the colocalization of pre- and post-synaptic markers with IMARIS (Fig. 6a). Both excitatory and inhibitory synapses were analyzed in two regions: the penumbra near the core (R1), and the penumbra near the intact region (R2). R1 was located in the penumbra near the core, determined by the combined observation of MAP2 and PSD95 signals for excitatory synapses, and MAP2 and Gephyrin for inhibitory synapses. R2 was located farther from the injury edge toward the intact region. In analysis of excitatory synapses, the numbers of both individual and colocalized vGlut1 and PSD95 were lower in intact +LPS brains than in -LPS brains in accordance with transcriptome and western blot analysis (Fig. 6b, d, Supplementary Fig.1). In both R1 and R2, the numbers of colocalized excitatory synapses decreased at 7 d post injury and increased at 21 d in both groups (Fig. 6b, d). Particularly, in R2 regions at 21 d, the numbers recovered to levels similar to those in intact brains, although the numbers were lower in the +LPS brains than in -LPS brains (Fig. 6b, d).

In analysis of inhibitory synapses, the numbers of both individual and colocalized vGAT and gephyrin was not different in intact brains of -LPS and +LPS mice, but significantly reduced in R1 similarly in both groups at 7 d post injury (Fig. 6c, d, Supplementary Fig.1b, c). At 21 d, in both groups, the number in R1 increased, particularly, more in -LPS mice (Fig. 6c, d). However, the number of colocalization in R2 at 7 and 21 d post injury was similar to that of intact regions in both groups (Fig. 6c, d). These findings suggest that LPS-ip reduces the repair of synapses, particularly excitatory synapses, in the injured brain.

DISCUSSION

The results in this study revealed LPS-induced systemic inflammation reduced initial brain injury but attenuates repair processes. LPS-induced systemic inflammation initially induced expression of genes associated with defense response and anti-apoptosis, but thereafter decreased expression of genes involved in repair and regeneration, ultimately leading to reduced neurite extension and synapse formation.

Systemic inflammation changes the brain microenvironment by increasing the expression of cytokines in microglia and GFAP in astrocytes, etc. [11, 13, 15]. In injured brains, the effect of systemic inflammation may become more important because of monocyte infiltration from blood [15, 39-41]. It has been reported that LPS and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) exert preconditioning effect [31, 42]. LPS preconditioning provides protection against ischemia, traumatic injury, and even depression [31, 32, 42, 43]. Our transcriptomic data show that LPS upregulated expression of defense response genes in the intact brains including Tlr2, Irf7, S100a8/a9, and Cxcl10. A TLR2 specific ligand, Pam3CSK4, attenuates loss of tight junction protein, occluding, and preserves BBB function [36]. There is little preconditioning effect in mice lacking IRF3 or IRF7 [37]. Endothelial cells also play for precondition through expression of CXCL10 [34]. While S100A8/S100A9 are commonly considered proinflammatory, growing evidence indicates they can also play diverse roles, including anti-inflammatory and immune regulatory functions. S100A8 has been shown to facilitate angiogenesis [33]. Additionally, S100A9 knockout mice showed increased fibrosis following ischemia/reperfusion injury [35] .

Injured brains and spinal cords are repaired through the growth of neurites from the penumbra region [22, 24, 26, 44, 45]. Systemic inflammation affects repair of injured brains through the altered functions of brain cells as well as blood monocytes. Microglia and monocytes contribute to repair of injured brains through expression of genes related to synapse formation, such as Snca, Syt17, and Syn2 [46], in addition to phagocytosis of dead cells and production of neurotrophic/regeneration factors [21-27].

Synaptic loss and dysfunction are key features of neurodegenerative diseases, closely linked to cognitive decline [47]. In the hippocampus, LPS induced a substantial decrease in glun2b, a glutamatergic receptor gene, and moderate changes of other glutamatergic receptor genes including glun1, glun2a, and glua1 [48]. We also observed in intact brains LPS-ip decreased the number of colocalization of pre- and post-excitatory synaptic proteins with an increase in synaptic pruning genes such as Ngef, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, and Trem2, consistent with previous findings [49-51]. LPS-ip reduced the level of PSD95 significantly, vGlut1 slightly, but not vGAT/gephyrin. Previously, it has been reported that astrocytes phagocytose excitatory post-synaptic density [52]. Based on this information, we speculate that systemic inflammation may alter synaptic pruning effects of astrocytes.

The present study showed that LPS-induced systemic inflammation attenuates the repair of injured brain. Particularly, the striatum is a region critically involved in motor control and is associated with various neurodegenerative diseases. Therefore, LPS-induced systemic inflammation may initiate or exacerbate neurodegeneration through the abnormal repair process in this region.
