Novel phenotypic clusters of adult-onset diabetic kidney disease based on static and dynamic clustering algorithms - a data-driven multi-center study
Authors: Kun Li, Lingling Wei, Ruili Yin, Miao Xu, Qiubo Zhao, Jing Ke, Longyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Recent epidemiological data reveal demonstrates a marked 173.6% increase in Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) attributable to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)-related diabetic kidney disease (DKD) over the past three decades, rising from 4.123 million to 11.279 million years [1]. In China, systematic meta-analysis has reported a pooled DKD prevalence of 21.8% (95% CI: 18.5%-25.4%), imposing a substantial burden on both individuals and public health systems [2]. Effective risk stratification and management strategies are therefore critical to prevent or delay DKD progression in people with diabetes [3, 4].

Classically, DKD is diagnosed based on persistent albuminuria followed by a progressive decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [5]. In clinical practice, however, DKD exhibits substantial phenotypic heterogeneity: some patients present with albuminuria without significant eGFR reduction, while others develop impaired eGFR in the absence of marked albuminuria. Over the past decades, several alternative phenotypes have been recognized, including regression of albuminuria, rapid eGFR decline, and non-albuminuric DKD [6]. Nevertheless, existing classification frameworks rarely integrate broader patient characteristics, particularly those related to metabolic and diabetic phenotypic profiles [7]. Furthermore, the rate of DKD progression varies widely; not all patients advance to end-stage renal disease [8].

Previous studies have identified subtypes of adult-onset diabetes that are associated with DKD risk [9]. Classification derived at diabetes onset reflects differences in baseline metabolic status and can stratify the risk of subsequent diabetic complications. However, as diabetic phenotypes evolve dynamically over the disease course, such baseline classifications may not accurately represent the clinical state of patients at DKD diagnosis, limiting their utility for predicting individual disease trajectories.

To address this gap, we applied two complementary clustering approaches to identify potential phenotypic subtypes of DKD [10–12]. This work aims to generate hypothesis-driven subgroup stratification that may inform future personalized prognostic evaluation.

Methods

Study design and population

This multi-center cohort study enrolled participants with T2DM from three hospitals in China, located in North China (Beijing), Central China (Hebi), and East China (Ningbo). All patients underwent standardized physical examinations, venous blood sample collection, and oral questionnaire interviews. The study protocol has been published previously [13].

As the derivation cohort, data from Beijing and Ningbo were used to form the DKD clusters. The data of Hebi were used to verify the results.

T2DM was defined by any of the following: self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes, current use of antidiabetic agents, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-h postload glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%. Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) age 18–80 years; and (2) Diagnosis of DKD. Exclusion criteria were: (1) current pregnancy or lactation; (2) confirmed diagnosis of malignant tumor (regardless of treatment status); (3) presence of acute diabetic complications (e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state); (4) missing data for critical variable. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection

Data was collected at each study center according to the standardized protocol. An Electronic Data Capture System (EDC) was used to collect and store oral questionnaire data including smoking and drinking status, educational attainment, duration of diabetes, recent diet and exercise frequency. The follow-up schedule is shown in Fig. 1A. Participants were generally followed up every 12 to 24 months.

Research schematic diagram. A: The follow-up calendar of the DKD cohort. Overall, the participants were followed up every 12 to 24 months. B: Statistical analysis procedure of the present study. C: The data cleaning procedure of this study

Smoking status was defined as “yes” for participants who smoked daily or almost daily. Drinking status was defined as “yes” for those who consumed alcohol weekly or nearly weekly. Educational attainment was categorized as < high school or ≥ high school.

Physical examination included measurements of height, body weight, and waist circumference. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula: BMI = weight (kg) / [height (m)]². Fasting blood samples were collected in the morning. Estimate glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [14].

Definition of DKD

DKD was defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73m2 and/or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)≥ 30 mg/g, attributable to diabetes and persistent for ≥ 3 months.

Statistical analysis

Statistical description

The missing key variables for clustering, including eGFR, UACR, diabetic retinopathy (DR), uric acid (UA), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides (TG) and glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) were excluded, and the missing variables of education level (n = 3, 0.20%), sleep quality (n = 31, 2.04%) and physical activity (n = 32, 2.10%) were imputed by multiple imputation methods.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range (IQR)] and categorical variables as frequency (%). Trends across clusters were analyzed using the Cochran–Armitage trend test and linear regression analyses for categorical and continuous variables across the groups, respectively.

Cluster analysis

Two distinct clustering approaches were applied to identify putative phenotypic subgroups of DKD.

First, a static clustering analysis was performed using cross-sectional data obtained at the time of DKD diagnosis. The k-means algorithm, a centroid-based partitioning method, was used to divide the cohort into k clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance. The k-means algorithm, a centroid-based method, was employed to partition the dataset into k clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance. The optimal number of clusters was justified by elbow method and silhouette score. Euclidean distance was adopted as the distance metric, and random initialization was used for algorithm execution [15, 16].

The variables were chosen based on: (i) recognized pathophysiological domains of DKD and metabolic disease; (ii) prior literature on DKD heterogeneity; and (iii) clinical accessibility. Variables entered into the static clustering model included age, sex, diabetes duration, HbA1c, BMI, eGFR, UACR, UA, HDL cholesterol, hypertension, and DR.

Second, a dynamic clustering analysis was conducted using longitudinal data spanning from each participant’s first follow-up visit to DKD diagnosis, based on the group-based multi-trajectory model (GBMTM). Given that GBMTM accommodates only continuous variables, this analysis focused on metabolic trajectories, including FPG, HDL, TG, UA, albumin (ALB), systolic blood pressure (SBP), waist circumference (WC), total cholesterol (TC), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP). The number of latent trajectory groups was set from 2 to 5, and each trajectory was fitted with a second-order polynomial function. The optimal model was selected according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The data cleaning process is summarized in Fig. 1C. After excluding participants with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) or gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), as well as those with missing key clustering variables either before or after DKD diagnosis, a total of 1520 participants were included in the static clustering analysis. Of these, 32.53% were female, and the mean age at DKD diagnosis was 54.4 ± 12.1 years. The median follow-up duration was 40 months (interquartile range: 24–58 months). Detailed characteristics are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

For the dynamic clustering analysis, 1392 participants with at least three follow-up visits prior to DKD diagnosis were included; their baseline characteristics are summarized in Supplemental Table 2. In addition, an independent cohort of 523 participants was retained after data cleaning for subsequent result validation.

Characteristics of the DKD clusters in static and dynamic clustering analysis

DKD clusters and rapid progression of DKD after diagnosis

Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between baseline metabolic DKD clusters and rapid progression of DKD after diagnosis.

As shown in Fig. 4I, static clustering revealed varying risks of rapid DKD progression across groups. Compared with the NATURAL cluster (Cluster 4), the ORs for rapid progression were 1.71 (95% CI: 1.25–2.35) in the BALANCE cluster (Cluster 1), 2.08 (95% CI: 1.52–2.86) in the YOUTH cluster (Cluster 2), and 4.36 (95% CI: 3.49–6.00) in the TYPICAL cluster (Cluster 3). In the dynamic clustering analysis (Fig. 4J), compared with Trajectory 3, the ORs for rapid progression in Trajectory 1, Trajectory 2 were 2.42 (95%CI: 1.86–3.14), 1.56 (95%CI: 1.14–2.11), respectively. Detailed results of the logistic regression models are provided in Supplemental Tables 6 and 7.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess potential effect modification by education level, smoking, and drinking status (Supplemental Fig. 3). No significant interaction effects were observed between cluster membership and these factors (P for interaction > 0.05 for all). Compared with the respective reference cluster, participants in Cluster 2–3 (static clustering) as well as those in Trajectory 1 and 2 (dynamic clustering) consistently showed a higher risk of rapid DKD progression across all subgroups (stratified by education, smoking, and drinking status).

The relationship between the static and dynamic clustering subgroups is illustrated using a Sankey diagram (Supplemental Fig. 4). The clustering results obtained from the validation cohort were consistent with the derivation cohort (Supplemental Fig. 5). Discussion

In this multicenter cohort study, we leveraged serial follow-up data collected before and after DKD diagnosis to characterize longitudinal trajectories of key clinical markers. Using two complementary clustering approaches, we identified phenotypically distinct DKD subgroups with potential clinical interpretability. The static k-means clustering model defined cross-sectional metabolic–renal profiles at DKD diagnosis, whereas the dynamic group-based multi-trajectory model reconstructed pre-diagnostic longitudinal patterns of metabolic biomarkers.

Over the past decade, multiple studies have derived phenotypic subtypes of adult-onset diabetes [9, 18]. Classifications based on new-onset diabetes reflect initial metabolic states and can predict future complication risks [19]. However, metabolic and renal phenotypes evolve substantially with diabetes progression, and the clinical state at DKD onset may differ markedly from that at diabetes diagnosis [20]. Consequently, diabetes-onset classifications may not accurately represent the current status of DKD patients, limiting prognostic precision. Our study addresses this gap by providing a dual clustering approach for more personalized assessment.

The static model identified four distinct DKD clusters with unique clinical and pathophysiological profiles. The BALANCE cluster, characterized by moderate age and diabetes duration and relatively normal metabolic parameters, may represent a subgroup with lower genetic susceptibility or effective disease management. DKD in this group may reflect the classic hyperglycemia-glomerular hyperfiltration-albuminuria pathway, albeit with slow progression and minimal inflammation [19].

The YOUTH cluster—putatively a youth-onset, metabolic-dysfunction–driven subtype—consisted of the youngest participants, who presented with obesity (elevated BMI) and hypertriglyceridemia. We speculate that severe insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation, rather than diabetes duration alone, are the predominant drivers of DKD in this subgroup [21]. Proposed mechanisms include hyperinsulinemia-mediated glomerular endothelial hyperpermeability, lipotoxic injury from free fatty acid accumulation in renal parenchymal cells, and pro-inflammatory cytokines derived from adipose tissue; these pathways may collectively induce early glomerular and tubular damage, manifesting as marked albuminuria without significant eGFR impairment [22]. Consistent with prior reports, this metabolic profile has also been associated with elevated cardiovascular risk [14, 23, 24], highlighting the need for vigilance regarding cardiorenal metabolic (CKM) syndrome progression in these patients [25].

The TYPICAL cluster represents a severe metabolic disorder subtype, with the longest diabetes duration, the poorest metabolic control (e.g., highest FPG), the highest prevalence of DR, and comprehensive renal dysfunction evidenced by both albuminuria and eGFR decline. This profile aligns with the traditional conceptualization of DKD, where long-term, severe combined effects of sustained hyperglycemia-induced advanced glycation end-product (AGE) deposition, lipid toxicity, hypertension, and microvascular complications (e.g., DR) likely leads to widespread glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, and vascular disease [26]. In addition, the NATURAL cluster, an elderly renal dysfunction subtype, exhibits well-controlled metabolic parameters but a significant decline in eGFR, with albuminuria being less prominent. Renal impairment in this group may be shaped by two main factors: (1) age-related physiological renal decline, including glomerulosclerosis and tubular atrophy [27]; (2) chronic microvascular injury attributable to long-term but relatively stable diabetes. Consequently, renal dysfunction in this cluster is mainly characterized by reduced filtration [28, 29].

Previous work by Megumi et al. described a DKD subtype characterized by eGFR decline without significant prominent albuminuria [6]. In our study, both the NATURAL and BALANCE clusters shared this feature, but differed in terms of age, gender, hypertension, and uric acid levels. Thus, our findings extend prior DKD subtyping efforts by uncovering heterogeneity within the “eGFR-loss-without-albuminuria” phenotype and suggest that a more granular classification system, integrating demographic and metabolic covariates could facilitate tailored disease management [30].

We observed a moderate overall prevalence of DR among DKD patients (31.61% had moderate or worse retinopathy), with DR being concentrated in the TYPICAL cluster. Although previous studies and clinical guidelines have highlighted the association between DR and DKD risk [31–33], our data also underscore the importance of proactive monitoring patients without DR, particularly younger individuals with elevated uric acid levels and metabolic dysregulation.

The dynamic clustering model, constructed using continuous variables relevant to DKD diagnosis and prognosis, revealing distinct subgroups based on longitudinal trajectories of metabolic indices in the years preceding DKD diagnosis. Notably, patients in trajectory 3 consistently maintained a good state of FPG, blood pressure, total cholesterol, and uric acid, yet still developed DKD.

This observation suggests that, in addition to routine management of blood glucose, blood pressure and blood lipids as well as regular estimation of the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), combined detection of DKD biomarkers (such as urinary kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), or plasma cystatin C) is necessary for the timely identification of early individual renal injury [34–36]. Owing to the limited availability of these biomarker data in our cohort, we were unable to evaluate their utility for risk stratification across DKD subtypes, which represents a notable study limitation.

Furthermore, accumulating evidence underscores the critical involvement of epigenetic and molecular regulatory mechanisms in DKD progression. Dong et al. systematically summarized core epigenetic alterations implicated in renal inflammatory cascades, encompassing chromatin remodeling, DNA methylation, histone modification, non-coding RNAs, and RNA modification [37]. Likewise, the work by Chen et al. highlights the necessity of elucidating the biological pathways underlying heterogeneous DKD subtypes to inform the development of phenotype-specific targeted therapeutic strategies for clinical practice [38].

The clustering results obtained from the validation cohort were consistent with the derivation cohort, which indicated the robustness of our results. Limited by the duration of follow-up in the validation cohort, only the results from static clustering were validated. More derivation cohorts are needed to verify the stability of dynamic clustering.

A key strength of this multi-center study is the use of the National Metabolic Management Center network, which ensures standardized patient management and comprehensive risk factor collection, enhancing the generalizability of our findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, although we adjusted for major lifestyle and clinical covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out. Key unmeasured variables include dietary sodium and protein intake, medication adherence, and detailed history of other primary renal diseases. Notably, baseline prescription of renoprotective medications such as RAAS inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors was not systematically captured in our dataset. Differential use of these agents according to disease severity could potentially bias estimates of progression risk and alter phenotypic clustering patterns, representing an important source of residual confounding. In future studies, we will include more variables that may affect the clustering results and evaluate the stability of the clustering based on prospective cohorts.

Second, the cohort was followed for a median of 40 months; longer observation would allow assessment of harder renal endpoints (ESKD) and validation of the classifications across different health-care settings. In the future study, we will construct a prediction model based on a prospective cohort and evaluate the predictive value of DKD classification.

Third, in our cohort, UACR was measured less frequently than eGFR, preventing a robust analysis of UACR dynamics over time. As a result, the definition of rapid DKD progression was primarily on a rapid decline in eGFR.

Fourth, inter-center heterogeneity in clinical practice may introduce residual confounding. Participating centers differ in geographic location, patient population characteristics, and regional lifestyle patterns, which may translate into variations in routine laboratory monitoring frequency, blood pressure and glycemic treatment targets, thresholds for nephrology referral, and adherence to cardiorenal protection guidelines. Owing to limited sample size, we were unable to perform formally stratified or center-adjusted sensitivity analyses. Future studies with larger, more geographically diverse multicenter samples are needed to address this limitation.

Fifth, some effect estimates exhibited wide confidence intervals (e.g. the HR of progressing to CKD G4-G5), likely reflecting limited sample size in certain subgroups and inter-center heterogeneity. This statistical imprecision indicates that findings for these subgroups should be interpreted with caution, and replication in larger cohorts is warranted.

Finally, this study was conducted in Chinese adults. External validation was limited to a single hospital. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating our cluster definitions to other ethnic groups or healthcare systems. Multi-ethnic, multi-national prospective studies are required to confirm the robustness and clinical utility of these clusters in broader contexts. We have therefore avoided claims of universal applicability and instead frame the clusters as a hypothesis-generating framework for further investigation. Future studies should include more participants from different regions and different health care settings (e.g., primary care) to validate the stability of clustering.

Conclusion

This study identifies novel, clinically interpretable sub-phenotypes of DKD using both static and dynamic clustering approaches. We also evaluated the risk of DKD progression in each cluster. Thereafter, we expect to conduct prospective intervention studies to promote the development of disease management tools.
