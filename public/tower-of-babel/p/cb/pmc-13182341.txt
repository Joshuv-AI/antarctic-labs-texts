Low SARS-CoV-2 Positivity in a Large, Diverse US-Based Study of Persons Affected by HIV
Authors: Suzanne Siminski, Brad Karalius, Yanling Huo, Kathleen Malee, Renee Smith, Tzy-Jyun Yao, Deborah Kacanek, Kathleen M Powis, Kunjal Patel, Emily A Barr, Katherine Tassiopoulos, Elizabeth J McFarland, Ellen G Chadwick, Nagamah Deygoo, Denise L Jacobson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere are limited data on SARS-CoV-2 testing practices and positivity among children and adults affected by HIV. The Pediatric HIV/AIDS Cohort Study enrolls children, youth, and adults with perinatally-acquired HIV (PHIV), non-perinatally-acquired HIV (NPHIV), perinatal HIV exposure but uninfected (PHEU), and infants/children with PHEU across the United States and Puerto Rico. A Health Status Survey was developed and administered from April 2020 to November 2021 to assess cumulative incidence (%) of self-reported (1) SARS-CoV-2 testing uptake and results by HIV status, age, and pandemic period; (2) moderate and severe respiratory illness; and (3) hospitalization. At least one survey was completed for 2,289 participants (PHIV = 444, NPHIV = 644, PHEU = 1201) through November 2021; 62% identified as Black and 33% as Hispanic. Participants’ ages (years) ranged from 18–42 in PHIV, 19–57 in NPHIV, and < 1–28 in PHEU. Among PHIV, NPHIV, and PHEU, respectively, 43%, 35%, and 16% reported SARS-CoV-2 testing; of these, 18%, 16%, and 11% testing positive. The proportion tested was similar by race, Hispanic ethnicity, education, and income. Overall, 4% (87/2289) were hospitalized for any illness. 8% (174/2289) reported a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold,” of whom 44% (76/174) reported SARS-CoV-2 testing, 49% (37/76) testing positive, and 13% (23/174) were hospitalized. The cumulative incidence of both SARS-CoV-2 positivity and severe COVID-19 disease as of November 2021 was lower than expected among persons affected by HIV. Testing access, uptake, and mitigation strategies in this population may explain these findings and inform strategies to prevent acquisition of respiratory viruses.Trial Registration Not applicable; this is not a clinical trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10461-026-05088-4.Keywords: HIV, SARS-CoV-2 testing, COVID pandemic, Children, Adults

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2 testing became available in Spring 2020 in the United States (US) across hospitals, clinics, and community testing sites [1, 2]. This population-based testing strategy aimed to decrease viral transmission by monitoring SARS-CoV-2 test positivity across regions and socioeconomic groups, addressing unmet needs, and encouraging those testing positive to self-isolate and those who were exposed to seek testing and take appropriate precautions [3, 4]. Access to testing varied across populations. Most notably early in the pandemic, some studies found a lower percent tested in Black individuals [4, 5], and lower [6] and higher [5, 7] percent in Hispanic individuals. In addition, communities with higher social vulnerability had a lower percent tested [4, 5, 8].

Several factors may contribute to differential SARS-CoV-2 test positivity in persons with HIV (PWH) compared to those without HIV, as indicated in several studies conducted in the US. The location, sample size, and major findings of the cited references are summarized in Online Resource 1, “Summary of Referenced Studies.” Briefly, Black and Hispanic persons are disproportionately affected by HIV [9], and Black [4] and Hispanic [5, 7, 10] communities had a higher community exposure and prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection [5–7]. Within 5 of 6 large cohorts, the percent tested was higher in those with HIV, but there was no consistent pattern across studies regarding positivity in PWH compared to those without HIV [11]. A year into the pandemic, a similar percentage of both groups was testing positive [11]. While Spinelli et al. [12] observed a lower incidence of SARS-CoV-2 positivity in PWH, PWH were more likely to be hospitalized and have severe disease compared to persons without HIV. Among persons hospitalized for COVID-19 in New York City, disease severity and mortality were similar between PWH and those without HIV [13].

We examined SARS-CoV-2 testing practices and outcomes across diverse age groups and HIV status among participants of the Pediatric HIV/AIDS Cohort Study (PHACS), a network of prospective observational studies of adults with perinatally-acquired HIV (PHIV) and non-perinatally-acquired HIV (NPHIV), as well as infants, children, adolescents, and young adults with perinatal HIV exposure but uninfected (PHEU). PHACS studies enroll participants at 21 sites across the US, including Puerto Rico. PHACS provided a unique opportunity to evaluate self-reported SARS-CoV-2 testing practices and test positivity among a relatively young population of persons affected by HIV as the COVID-19 pandemic evolved over the first two years.

Methods

SMARTT and AMP Up Protocols

The Surveillance and Monitoring for ART Toxicities study (SMARTT) of PHACS enrolls pregnant people (≥ 18 years) with HIV and their PHEU children (< 18 years) to evaluate in utero antiretroviral exposure on outcomes across multiple clinical domains [14]. The Adolescent Master Protocol Up series (AMP Up) of PHACS examines HIV status and antiretroviral treatment (ART) on health, behaviors, and psychosocial functioning of young adults with PHIV compared to young adults with PHEU (≥ 18 years) [15]. These protocols were approved by the IRB at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and each clinical site (Online Resource 2, “PHACS clinical sites by institution, state, and region and protocol participation”). Participants ≥ 18 years provided informed consent to participate; the parent/guardian provided consent for children < 18 years and children provided assent according to site regulations.

For analyses in this paper, participants were classified into three study cohorts defined as PHIV (includes young adults with PHIV from AMP Up and biological mothers with PHIV from SMARTT); NPHIV (includes biological mothers from SMARTT); and PHEU (includes infants, children, and adolescents with PHEU from SMARTT and young adults with PHEU from AMP Up).

Health Status Survey

The Health Status Survey, introduced in April 2020, was designed to facilitate communication between PHACS participants and their clinical site during care disruptions throughout the pandemic and to assess physical health, SARS-CoV-2 testing, disease prevalence, and severity. It enabled PHACS site staff to maintain a connection with participants to address their ongoing concerns and questions. Site staff invited participants on study as of January 1, 2020 who were due for their scheduled in-person follow-up visit (+/−6 months) to take part in the survey interview via phone after providing verbal consent, in addition to assessments in regular follow-up visits that could be performed remotely. When in-person visits resumed as the pandemic progressed, the survey was administered by phone or in-person. Biological mothers in SMARTT were interviewed about their well-being and served as a proxy to report on their child’s well-being. In a few cases, a caregiver was interviewed on behalf of an AMP Up young adult when they could not respond for themselves.

The survey recall period was cumulative from January 1, 2020 until the day of the interview. Questions about SARS-CoV-2 testing and illness included whether the participant had experienced any respiratory illnesses “worse than the common cold.” If participants answered yes, follow-up questions related to that illness queried about testing and results for SARS-CoV-2, and hospitalization(s). Next, participants were asked if they had been tested for SARS-CoV-2 for reasons unrelated to a respiratory illness, along with SARS-CoV-2 test dates and results, and any hospitalization during the recall period, whether or not it was related to COVID-19. All surveys conducted between April 1, 2020 through November 30, 2021 (before December 1, 2021, the start of the Omicron period) were included in analyses.

Definitions

The pandemic period definition was based on the COVID-19 epidemic curve and timing of vaccine availability in the US as: the first wave (Jan 2020-May 2020), the summer lull (June 2020-October 2020), the second wave (November 2020-January 2021), the vaccine rollout (February 2021-June 2021), and the Delta wave (July 2021-November 2021) [16]. As surveys were completed at different times during the pandemic, the length of the recall period since January 1, 2020 varied across participants; that is, each participant’s recall period could only contribute information for the current and previous pandemic periods from January 2020. For example, participants who responded during the first wave could only contribute information pertaining to the first wave. Those who responded to the survey during the summer lull could contribute information pertaining to the summer lull and the first wave. Sociodemographic data were based on the PHACS study visit just prior to the pandemic, including self-reported race and ethnicity, income, and education. Household income was self-reported for SMARTT mothers, and, among AMP Up participants, personal income was reported if household income was not available; age at the last survey was also calculated. Maternal education was used for SMARTT children.

Outcomes

Study outcomes included cumulative incidence (%) of self-reported (1) SARS-CoV-2 testing and positivity; (2) any respiratory illness “worse than the common cold,” and SARS-CoV-2 testing, SARS-CoV-2 positivity, and hospitalizations related to the illness; and (3) hospitalization for any reason.

Analysis

This is a descriptive study, and no statistical tests were performed. We report the number of PHACS participants who completed a survey among those currently on study as of January 1, 2020 and sociodemographic characteristics by survey completion. We displayed characteristics across study cohorts (PHIV, NPHIV, PHEU). The percent of participants with at least one SARS-CoV-2 test and percent testing positive over the entire follow-up period were also summarized by study cohort, sex, age, race, ethnicity, income, and education. Some participants had multiple surveys because the study period covered multiple scheduled PHACS protocol visits. In that case, we report any history of testing and any positive result across all surveys, that is, information collected from January 2020 to the period of the last survey. The percent who had a “respiratory illness worse than the common cold” across follow-up was determined. Within that group, the percent tested for SARS-CoV-2 during that illness, the percent testing positive, and the percent hospitalized were determined. Lastly, the overall percent of survey respondents who were hospitalized for any illness was calculated. Within each pandemic period, we calculated the percent tested for SARS-CoV-2 and positivity overall and by study cohort and participant age at the end of each pandemic period.

Results

Characteristics

Among 2,478 attempted contacts with unique participants, 2,425 were reached (see Online Resource 3, “PHACS participants who completed the Health Status Survey (N = 6563)”); 2,312 completed the survey through November 2021. We excluded participants with duplicate surveys where the participants (older youth) reported for themselves and their caregivers also reported for them (N = 23), resulting in 2,289 participants with at least one completed survey during the follow-up period. For a few participants, the survey was administered more than once (N = 2,109 one survey, N = 169 two surveys, N = 9 three surveys, N = 2 four surveys). Analysis of the respondents compared to non-respondents showed no differences for sex at birth, age, education, and income (see Online Resource 4, “Characteristics by analysis population inclusion status (N = 6563)”). The percent living in the Northeast US was slightly lower for respondents.

Among the 2,289 respondents, 536 were surveyed in the first wave, 1,119 in the summer lull, 208 in the second wave, 465 during vaccine rollout, and 137 in the Delta wave. Survey data were collected on 444 PHIV, 644 NPHIV, and 1201 PHEU (Online Resource 3). Ages (yr) ranged from 18–42 in PHIV and 19–57 in NPHIV, with 72% of PHIV vs. 12% of NPHIV between ages 18–29. Of PHEU, 38% were < 1–5, 36% 6–10, 20% 11–17, and 6% 18–28 years of age. Overall, 62% self-identified as Black, 31% as white, and 33% as Hispanic ethnicity. Approximately one-third had income < $10,000/year.

Respiratory Illness and Hospitalizations

Across the whole study period, 8% (174/2289) reported experiencing a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold,” 44% (76/174) were tested for SARS-CoV-2, 49% (37/76) testing positive, and 13% (23/174) were hospitalized (Table 3). The percent with a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold” and the percent tested during their illness were lowest in participants with PHEU (including children) compared to those with PHIV and NPHIV, and the highest percent positive was among participants with PHIV. The percent hospitalized for an illness “worse than the common cold” was very similar across cohorts, as was the percent with hospitalization for any illness. Among participants with PHEU < 18 years of age, 5% had a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold” and 12% were hospitalized during the illness (Table 3). Overall, 4% (87/2289) of participants reported being hospitalized for any illness; specifically, 5% (23/444) of PHIV, 6% (37/644) of NPHIV, and 2% (27/1201) of the PHEU cohort.

Discussion

The Health Status Survey offered unique insights into SARS-CoV-2 testing and positivity and hospitalization in persons with and affected by HIV during the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic in the US. Overall, 27% of PHACS participants reported testing between January 2020 and November 2021, with 15% of those testing positive. The percent tested was highest in PHIV, followed by NPHIV participants; the percent positive was similar between groups. Participants with PHEU had the lowest testing percentage and, among those, percent testing positive compared to the other two cohorts, however among PHEU ≥ 18 years the percent tested was similar and percent testing positive was lower than among PHIV and NPHIV who were ≥ 18 years of age. The proportion tested for SARS-CoV-2 was similar by race, Hispanic ethnicity, education, and income. However, of those tested, white participants were more likely to test positive (24%) than Black participants (11%) and Hispanics (23%) more than non-Hispanics (11%). A small and similar proportion of participants across the cohorts reported a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold,” and few in any cohort were hospitalized. For participants with a notable respiratory illness, PHIV and NPHIV were more likely than PHEU to be tested and to be positive.

Findings from this cohort should be interpreted in the context of a well-characterized longitudinal population with sustained engagement in clinical care and an established rapport with their study team members. Compared with large population-based reports, PHACS cohorts demonstrated relatively high testing rates with lower proportions of SARS-CoV-2 positivity and hospitalization. PHACS participants with PHIV were relatively healthy overall, with 74% having a viral load ≤ 400 copies/mL and a median CD4 count of 571 cells/mm3 in May 2021. Many of the PHACS participants with PHIV and PHEU have participated in the PHACS study for over a decade and are accustomed to ongoing monitoring and health care by study staff, and this may have contributed to greater risk awareness, compliance with mitigation strategies, and routine practice of protective health behaviors in their households.

PHACS SARS-CoV-2 testing trends are similar to that observed in other US studies. Alexander et al. [1] reported that less than 0.1% were tested in May 2020 in the US and this increased to ~ 0.8% in November 2020, with variations across regions [1, 17]. One study in New York City and another in Phoenix, Arizona showed peak testing in December 2020 and January 2021, decreasing thereafter through Spring 2021 [3, 18].

In the US, testing patterns of persons with versus without HIV vary across studies. In 5 of 6 large diverse cohorts followed between March 2020 to December 2020, a higher percent of individuals with HIV tested (range 19.6%−40.5%) compared to those without HIV (range 14.8%−29.4%) [11]; however, in one of the six studies, percent tested was similar between groups (34% vs. 33%) [19]. In PHACS, from January 2020 through November 2021, the percent of PWH and PHEU ≥ 18 years of age tested (PHIV: 35%; NPHIV: 30%; PHEU: 29%) was within the range of 5 of the 6 studies [11].

In PHACS, SARS-CoV-2 positivity was highest in the second wave and lowest in the vaccine rollout wave, concordant with other reports across the US [1, 18, 20, 21]. The percent positive we observed in the first wave, summer lull, and second wave, respectively, in PHACS (15%, 10%, 25%) was higher than that reported for New York City and its surrounding area (7.8%, 1.6%, and 8.7%) [3]. In contrast, the 11.3% positive reported between December 2020 and April 2021 in Arizona (≥ 12 yrs of age) [18] was similar to the 15% positive we observed among PHACS participants from January 2020 to November 2021. SARS-CoV-2 testing and exposure vary by race/ethnicity, age, and income, which may partially explain the differences above [3]. Overall, 62% of PHACS participants self-identified as Black and 31% as white, and 60% reported low household income, in contrast to the New York City study at CityMD urgent care clinics where 10% were categorized as Black, 25% white-Hispanic, 33% white non-Hispanic, and 23% unknown [3]. Race/ethnicity and income characteristics of PHACS participants may be similar to those of participants tested in Arizona, where mobile testing vans were sent to underserved areas and based on case numbers and percent positive [18].

Testing and positivity for SARS-CoV-2 in PWH versus those without HIV differ across studies, but several studies report greater disease severity in PWH. From Spring 2020 to Fall 2020, the percent tested was similar in PWH and persons without HIV in the MACS/WIHS Combined Cohort study, but positivity rates were higher in PWH [19]. In San Francisco, PWH were more likely to be tested, but the incidence of COVID-19 was higher in those without HIV, while the risk of hospitalization and severe COVID-19 disease requiring supplemental oxygen was higher in PWH [12]. In-hospital COVID severity and risk of mortality were similar in PWH compared to persons without HIV in New York City, but among PWH, mortality was higher among those with low CD4 count and not on ART [13]. Several factors may contribute to differential percent SARS-CoV-2 positivity by HIV status, including a greater percent of Black and Hispanic individuals with HIV, and greater community exposure to SARS-CoV-2 in Black and Hispanic communities early in the pandemic [5–7], possibly due to in-person work, travel by public transportation, or living situation. On the other hand, those affected by HIV may have actively practiced mitigation strategies to decrease risk and sought advice from health care providers due to heightened concern about poor COVID-19 outcomes due to their underlying disease, including immunosuppression.

PHACS participants with PHIV and NPHIV had a higher cumulative incidence of self-reported “respiratory illnesses worse than the common cold” and were more likely to be tested for SARS-CoV-2 during that illness compared to PHEU (including children). Percent positivity was highest in the PHIV compared to NPHIV and PHEU cohorts, but the percent hospitalized for a self-reported “illness worse than the common cold” was slightly higher in NPHIV and PHEU, suggesting slightly higher severity for this illness in these two cohorts. Regardless of illness, PHEU were the least likely to be hospitalized. Among PHACS PHEU < 18 years of age, 5% had a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold,” and related to that illness, 25% were tested, 29% testing positive, and 12% were hospitalized. In comparison, among children (< 18 years of age) reported in Georgia from March 2020 to June 2021 who were tested in Fulton County and with results sent to the State Surveillance System, 4% tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 and 1.3% of those testing positive were hospitalized (hospitalization reasons were not included) [22].

The percent tested for SARS-CoV-2 in the US during the early period of the pandemic was often lower in Black [4] individuals, but studies in Hispanic individuals report both lower [6] and higher testing [5, 7]. Testing was lower in communities with higher social vulnerability [4, 5, 8]. Certainly, there were disparities in access to testing across locations and in minoritized populations. Some, but not all, studies found higher positivity rates in communities with the above-mentioned characteristics [3–5, 7, 8, 23–29]. In contrast, the proportion of PHACS participants tested for from January 2020 to November 2021 was similar by race, Hispanic ethnicity, education, and income, with a greater proportion of those self-identified as white and those who identified as Hispanic testing positive. Higher rates of hospitalization due to SARS-CoV-2 were observed in Hispanic or Black individuals in some reports [5, 29, 30], and some studies show increased mortality in Black or Hispanic patients [5, 23, 29]. In PHACS, 13% of those with a respiratory illness “worse than the common cold” were hospitalized compared to that reported by Dai et al., where 15.6% of those testing positive were hospitalized between March and December 2020 [29]. Our approach of using descriptive statistics aligns with reporting standards of descriptive observation studies and provides an important foundation for future hypothesis-driven studies in which confounding factors can be accounted for systematically.

Although individuals who were HIV-exposed in utero but uninfected (HEU) have demonstrated increased infectious morbidity in infancy [31], the older PHACS cohort evaluated here showed low positivity and illness rates. This observation suggests that early immune vulnerabilities attenuate with age. However, detailed immunologic assessments were beyond the scope of this study, and the absence of increased clinical illness should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of equivalent immune function. Future longitudinal studies incorporating immune profiling will be important to clarify long-term immune vulnerabilities among individuals who are HEU.

While our study had many strengths, a few limitations to our study are worth noting. Namely, because participants were surveyed at different times throughout the pandemic, not all participants have data from every pandemic period due to the timing of their protocol scheduled visits during the study period. Further, a smaller proportion of young adults were surveyed due to their visit schedule. Testing and positivity were self-reported, so the exact month of occurrence may be misclassified, although participants would likely remember correctly that they were tested and their test results, given concerns people had about the pandemic and its possible health consequences. We also acknowledge illness perception may differ in a cohort living with lifelong HIV. Responses to “an illness worse than the common cold” likely reflect subjective framing rather than a standardized clinical benchmark. Although surveys were interviewer-administered to promote consistent understanding, these findings should be interpreted with caution as perceptions of illness burden may not correlate well with objective measures of disease severity. Although it may have been informative to collect essential worker status, participant employment details were not collected. Finally, 95% CIs around some estimates were wide due to small numbers.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was an abrupt change in how individuals sought and received medical care. Before the pandemic, many PHACS participants had a regular schedule of medical appointments and study visits. Since the PHACS study relies heavily upon the close relationship between participants and site health care providers, there was concern that quarantine protocols might endanger the bond with study staff and accessibility to their providers. Although care was disrupted in the early stages of the pandemic, we believe that the Health Status Survey supported greater interaction and connection between participants and health care providers than that experienced by the general public, and it facilitated collection of SARS-CoV-2 data. Our data suggest that patterns of SARS-CoV-2 testing and positivity in PHACS participants with HIV were generally similar to US adults living with HIV [12], and to a general population study with similar socioeconomic characteristics [18]. Percent SARS-CoV-2 testing, positivity, and hospitalization are slightly higher in our PHEU compared to other studies in children. Comparisons to other populations for severe infection and hospitalization vary depending on time period and population characteristics. Examination of testing uptake and positivity offered valuable insights into the impact of COVID-19 on individuals affected by HIV. Our findings could inform prevention strategies for present and future pandemics, benefiting the general populace and those with HIV.
