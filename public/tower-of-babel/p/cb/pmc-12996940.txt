Enhanced wound healing in rat cheeks treated with lavender and ginger oils: A histological study
Authors: Enas F. Kadim, Zena Wally, Rasha Dosh, Julfikar Haider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDelayed or impaired skin wound healing can adversely affect cell proliferation, collagen formation, and granulation tissue. This study evaluated the effectiveness of daily topical treatment with lavender and ginger oils in promoting wound healing, by assessing the inflammatory response, re-epithelialization, and wound contraction in rat cheek wound area.MethodsA surgical wound of full skin thickness, 2 cm wide and 0.6 cm deep, was made on the cheeks of the animals. Forty albino rats were randomly divided into four experimental study groups of ten rats each: a control group, in which the wounds were left untreated, and three experimental groups in which the wounds were treated with 1 μL lavender oil, or 1 μL ginger oil, or a combination of 0.5 μL lavender oil and 0.5 μL ginger oil. After 5 and 10 days histological examination was conducted, and the mean number of inflammatory cells, epithelial thickness, and degree of wound contraction were measured in the treated and untreated groups. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test was used to identify differences among groups. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsHistological examination revealed faster wound healing in the experimental groups than the control group. Lavender flower oil and ginger oil significantly decreased inflammation, increased epithelial thickness, and promoted wound contraction.ConclusionsThe study results revealed a noticeable decrease in inflammatory cell counts, with increased epithelial tissue, and improved wound contraction in the groups receiving treatment, compared with the untreated control group. This supported the aim that treatment effectively improves wound healing in albino rats cheeks.Clinical significanceOur findings highlight the potential of the natural, accessible biomaterials of lavender and ginger oils to enhance wound healing in rat cheek tissues, and may pave the way for future research aimed at developing evidence-based therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

The skin, the main protective structure covering the external surface of the entire human body,1 has many functions, including protection, water regulation, electrolyte balance, and sensation.2 The skin also serves as a physical barrier protecting against external harmful microorganisms and environmental changes.1 Skin injury of the cheek is a common clinical manifestation of diverse causes including trauma, sunburn, and cancer,3 as well as recurrent pressure and friction caused by masks or goggles.4 Chronic wounds or delayed healing are common in people with diabetes or immunodeficiency disorders, for which currently available treatments are limited.5,6 Therefore developing effective wound-healing therapies that promote proper tissue repair and regeneration is an important research goal. Studies have been conducted to simulate the healing process, and regenerate and repair skin tissue.7

Dental and facial trauma frequently affects the teeth, bones, soft tissues, and skin of the face. Facial injuries can occur alone or in conjunction with other injuries. Local and systemic consequences may occur, aside from the site of trauma. Trauma can damage soft and hard tissues, thereby resulting in inflammation, pain, soreness, swelling, and decreased functionality. The skin at injury sites may become abraded, lacerated, or destroyed, thus leading to broken or displaced bones and teeth.1 Several biomaterials, including essential oils, have been found to cure facial and oral dental diseases.7 Nevertheless, further research examining essential oils for skin healing remains to be conducted.

Wound healing is a biological regeneration process involving a specific cellular functional mechanism for restoring tissue architecture and function after injury.1 Wound repair typically refers to parenchymal and connective tissues and surface epithelial healing.8 Healthy organs can repair or regenerate their tissues and consequently restore their functions.9 The wound-healing process involves a cascade of biochemical processes to repair injured tissue.6 Skin wound repair is a highly organized and dynamic process involving cellular, humoral, and molecular mechanisms that begins immediately after injury and may persist for years.1

Skin healing includes coagulation, hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling at the wound site, thereby resulting in scar tissue deposition.10 In the thrombotic phase, platelet-released growth factors stimulate and attract macrophages, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts to wound sites. During inflammation, neutrophils and macrophages attack foreign bodies, bacteria, and dead tissue from the wound area. In the next stage, proliferation, fibroblast migration, collagen synthesis, angiogenesis, and granulation tissue formation occur. New epithelial tissue and the extracellular matrix subsequently develop in the remodeling phase.11 Perturbation of one or more of these phases can cause improper or delayed wound healing.5,6 Silver sulfadiazine is a standard chemical treatment used to treat or prevent infection during wound healing, but its main limitation is microbial resistance.12 As alternatives to chemical agents, various plant molecules have been tested for their ability to promote wound healing.6,13

Essential oils, such as lavender, clove, oregano, and peppermint, have been used in dental and medical applications, because of their anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial,6 and sedative properties.14 These biomaterials have been used to treat dental diseases such as periodontitis,15 aphthous ulcers, toothaches, and teething. However, much work remains necessary to examine the use of essential oils in skin healing.

Biomaterials such as essential oils are safe and nontoxic when used at low concentrations. Essential oils’ natural composition and pleasant aromas have made them attractive candidates for incorporation into many cosmetic products. The molecular compositions of these oils differ according to the plant material and extraction method. Natural oils can permeate skin membranes faster than chemical medications, because of properties including their fat solubility and small molecular size.16 However, local application of essential oils to the skin surface can cause skin sensitivity or irritation and other symptoms, as a result of variable chemical structures17 or the application of excessive amounts.16

Lavender is a versatile and frequently used essential oil in aromatherapy. It is collected via steam distillation from Lavandula angustifolia. It promotes relaxation, and is used to treat anxiety, allergies, depression, eczema, insomnia, nausea, and bacterial infections. Lavender oil consists of 25–47 % linalyl acetate, 45 % linalool, 8 % terpinen-4-ol, 1.5 % camphor, 1 % limonene, and 3 % 1,8-cineole, according to the ISO standard.17

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) is a plant compound whose rhizomes, ginger root or ginger, are widely used as a food flavoring18 and in traditional medicine for rheumatic conditions.19 Ginger oil contains bioactive components such as gingerol and shogaol,20 which have been shown to have antifungal effects on oral Candida albicans.21,22 Ginger oil combined with spearmint, peppermint, or cardamom has been found to effectively decrease nausea after surgery.19 In a biological study, 6-gingerol nanophytosomes have been shown to exhibit antiproliferative activity and downregulate the expression of inflammatory markers and cytokines, thereby enhancing wound healing.23 However, in a clinical trial, the application of ginger ointment did not improve pain or the healing rate of episiotomy wounds.24 Nevertheless, ginger oil has been used for various medical treatments, but the literature provides no clear evidence of the required healing times for wounds. Although lavender and ginger oils have demonstrated some benefits against microbial growth and have been used for various therapeutic purposes, the evidence of their effects on skin wound healing is not robust, particularly regarding their combined effects and synergistic action.24, 25, 26 Herein, we used a new approach to examine whether lavender and ginger oils, individually or in synergistic combination, might influence the wound-healing process. This study was aimed at assessing the effects of topical application of lavender oil, ginger oil, or both in combination on inflammatory cell numbers, epithelial tissue thickness, and the extent of skin wound contraction in the cheeks of rats over 5-day and 10-day periods. The study hypothesis was that applying lavender and/or ginger oil would shorten the inflammation period and accelerate skin healing.

Materials and Methods

Experimental design and methods

We performed an in vivo study in an animal model (albino rats). The experimental trials were conducted according to the regulations for use of animals in scientific research (Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 22 September 2010) and received ethical approval from the University of Baghdad (reference number 584). Lavender flower (Lavandula angustifolia, USA) and ginger (Zingiber officinale, Aura Cacia) oils were purchased from Amazon. The essential oils were stored in dark airtight bottles at room temperature before use. Forty male albino rats 6–8 months of age, weighing 300–400 g, were obtained from the University of Baghdad, Iraq. They were housed in plastic cages in the recommended environment under a 13-h light/11-h dark cycle, and were given commercial chow and distilled water.

An incisional wound of full skin thickness (width 2 cm and depth 0.6 cm) was made on the cheeks of the animals with a sterile scalpel. The animals were randomly divided into four groups, each containing ten rats: five treated for 5 days and the other five treated for 10 days. In the control group, the wounds were left untreated and allowed to spontaneously heal, whereas the experimental groups were treated daily with local application of natural oils (1 μL of lavender flower or ginger oil, or a combination of 0.5 μL of each). A preliminary trial was conducted in two rats from each group to determine the optimal dose. A 1 μL amount was found to be sufficient to cover the entire wound area. In the combination group, 0.5 μL of each oil mixture was selected to maintain a total volume of 1 μL. The experimental design of the study groups is shown in Table 1. Histological testing

Data were collected from 40 animals used in the histological study. Each group consisted of ten rats (five rats per 5-day or 10-day period). In a preliminary study, two rats per experimental group were used to assess the appropriate sample size. The maximum sample size was determined to be ten rats per group, with a mean difference of 0.26 and a standard deviation of 0.14 at 80 % power. Tissue samples were fixed in 10 % formalin and embedded in paraffin wax. Serial sections 5 μm thick were prepared for each sample and mounted on clean glass slides for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.27 The sections were deparaffinized, incubated with hematoxylin (1 % w/v) for 10 min, and washed with tap water. Next, the samples were incubated with eosin (1 % w/v) for 5 min, washed with tap water, dehydrated with alcohol, and mounted in DPX mounting medium. To minimize bias, the histological slides were coded, and a researcher blinded to the treatment groups performed the cell counts and epithelial thickness measurements. Histopathological re-examination was performed by counting inflammatory cells in the histological sections (H&E stained). Four microscopic fields were examined under a light microscope at × 40 magnification. ImageJ software version 1.53 was used to measure the epidermis thickness from the inner edge of the basal cell layer to the surface of the corium under 40 × magnification.27 Measurements were made in micrometers. For wound contraction assessment, the length of each wound was measured on days 5 and 10 with electronic Vernier calipers.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate the effects of lavender or ginger oils individually or in combination on cheek wound healing in rats. The data are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses of the experimental compared with control values were performed in GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 software (Boston, MA, USA). The threshold for a statistically significant difference was p < 0.05. The normality of the data was assessed with a Gaussian distribution and the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Because the data were found to be normally distributed, parametric tests were performed for all data. Statistical comparisons were performed with two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test.

On day 5, histological examination of the cheek wounds in the control group revealed granulation tissue infiltrated with inflammatory cells and an irregular arrangement of collagen fibers and fibroblasts (Figure 1a). The histological images of lavender flower-oil-treated rats revealed fibrous tissue replacing granulation tissue, the presence of scattered fibroblasts, complete epithelialization, new blood vessel formation, and compressed collagen fibers with signs of remodeling (Figure 1b). By contrast, the ginger-oil-treated group showed complete epithelization with regular arrangement of collagen fibers and fibroblasts (signs of healing); however, infiltration of inflammatory cells still occurred (Figure 1c). All 5-day experimental groups treated with combined lavender flower and ginger oil, showed complete epithelization, and fewer inflammatory cells, and accelerated wound contraction (Figure 1d), compared with the control group.

On day 10, the tissue samples from the control group showed epithelialization, with regular orientation of collagen fibers, new blood vessels, and fibroblasts (Figure 2a). Tissue sections from the animals treated with lavender flower oil showed complete epithelialization with thin or no rete edge epithelium, and regular orientation of collagen fibers and fibroblasts with newly formed blood vessels (Figure 2b). The ginger oil group exhibited complete epithelization without rete pegs, budding of hair follicles, regular orientation of collagen and fibroblasts, and numerous blood vessels (Figure 3c). Discussion

Wound healing is a complex systematic process involving a response to tissue damage to restore the integrity of skin tissue.21 This process involves highly organized and interdependent stages of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling that occur sequentially. Inappropriate treatment or disruption of the healing phase can adversely affect cellular proliferation, collagen formation, and granulation tissue, thus decreasing wound contraction and scar formation.28, 29, 30 A common problem with commercially available wound-healing chemical treatments is microbial resistance. Various plant particles have been shown to act as antimicrobial agents.31 However, few studies have assessed the effectiveness of lavender oil for skin healing, and no studies have used it in combination with ginger oil to examine wound healing in the facial skin in a rat model. This study was aimed at assessing how the topical application of lavender or ginger oil, or a combination thereof might affect the inflammatory cell count, epithelial tissue thickness, and wound contraction of cheek skin wounds in rats over treatment periods of 5 and 10 days. Lavender and ginger oils were chosen because of their known antimicrobial properties.

The results revealed an overall decrease in the number of inflammatory cells, and an increase in epithelial tissue thickness and wound contraction, in the treated groups compared with those in the untreated control group. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Histological examination revealed that the topical application of lavender oil and ginger oil, alone or in combination, markedly decreased the number of inflammatory cells in the cheek wound areas of Wistar rats over 5-and 10-day periods, compared with the untreated group (Table 2 and Figure 3). Interestingly, the combined treatment group showed the most pronounced decrease in inflammatory cell infiltration after both 5 and 10 days, indicating potential synergistic effects. A longer duration of treatment with a mixture of lavender and ginger oils might increase the ability of essential oils to inhibit microbial activity at wound sites. The anti-inflammatory activity of lavender flower extracts may be related to their chemical composition, which is mainly composed of Oxygenated Monoterpenes.32 Ginger also contains phenolic substances which are known to have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and consequently might act as anticancer and antibacterial agents.32 Oxidative monoterpenes, such as linalool and linalool oxide, are major components of lavender flower extracts with antimicrobial and antioxidant activity, as demonstrated in several studies.26,33

The linalool component has been reported to be responsible for the antifungal and antimicrobial activity of lavender oil.34 The biochemical structure of lavender oil affects cytokines and modulates proinflammatory gene expression.35,36 Previous in vitro studies have shown that lavender oil effectively inhibits microbial growth.25,37 In one study, lavender oil at 0.80 mg/plate decreased the number of histidine-independent revertant colonies by approximately 66.4 %.25 ISO 3515, 2002, and European Pharmacopoeia, 2014, specified that medicinal grade lavender oil should comprise 25–47 % linalool acetate, 20–45 % linalool, 0.1–8.0 % 4-terpineol, <2.5 % eucalyptol, <1.2 % camphor, <2.0 % α-terpineole, >0.2 % lavandulol acetate, and >0.1 % lavandulol.37 The chemical composition of the lavender oil used in this study met these requirements.

Ginger molecules have been found to effectively lead to the creation of new blood vessels in inflamed and damaged skin tissues.38 The anti-inflammatory effect of ginger has been demonstrated in several studies.39,40 Several mechanisms have been suggested, such as the inhibition of 5-lipooxygenase, cyclooxygenase, and nitric oxide production, and the expression of genes encoding proinflammatory cytokines involved in inflammatory reactions.38 Ginger extract has also been shown to improve skin function and structure, thus potentially decreasing the formation of chronic skin wounds.41

Our study indicated that local application of both lavender and ginger oils significantly thickened the epithelial tissue at wound sites. This finding might have been due to the oils’ stimulation of specific extracellular proteins or factors, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), which is well known to regulate collagen, fibroblasts, and the production of granulation tissue.42 In 2016, Mori et al., in a study examining the molecular mechanism underlying the effect of lavender oil on the wound-healing process, reported that local treatment of skin wounds with lavender oil in a rat model promoted fibroblast differentiation, collagen production, and subsequent upregulation of TGF-β.42 Another investigation has revealed that lavender oil treatment enhances the skin wound closure process, through increasing platelet-derived growth factor levels.43 Other growth factors, such as epidermal growth factors, insulin growth factors, and fibroblast growth factors, have also been shown to play roles in the wound-healing process. These factors may control cell motility and the production of extracellular matrix proteins, which are crucial for granulation tissue development and therefore promote wound shrinkage.44,45

As expected, in this study, the facial wound closure of the treated and untreated rats significantly increased over time. Although greater wound contraction was observed in the ginger oil group than the lavender oil group after both treatment durations (5 and 10 days), the treated groups exhibited more effective wound shrinkage than the untreated control group. The anti-inflammatory action of these natural molecules might help remove bacteria, foreign particles, and dead tissues from the wound area. Consequently, fibroblasts, collagen, and granulation tissue might develop into epithelium and close the wound area.11 In another in vivo study, local treatment with lavender oil stimulated type I and III collagen expression 4 days postinjury in Sprague–Dawley rats.18

The strength of the present study is its demonstration that topical application of lavender flower and/or ginger oils significantly affected the inflammatory cell count, epithelial tissue thickness, and wound contraction in rat cheek wounds. The combination of lavender and ginger oil resulted in the fewest inflammatory cells, and the greatest epithelial tissue thickness and wound contraction. Several prior trials have reported mixing of lavender with other plant extracts. The combination of lavender and chamomile has been tested in a direct drop application for wound healing and was found to effectively accelerate the healing process.46 Ginger extract has also been combined with curcumin and was found to ameliorate skin injury in corticosteroid-impaired hairless rat skin.41 In another study, a combination of ciprofloxacin and an ethanolic propolis extract, as well as ciprofloxacin and propolis nanoemulsion, showed better therapeutic effects than either agent alone.47 Further local treatment with natural agents such as fisetin was also found to be effective in improving cutaneous wound healing by modulating oxidative stress.48 We observed that, among tested treatments, the combination of lavender and ginger oils had the most significant effects in decreasing inflammatory cell numbers, thickening the epithelial tissue, and decreasing the contraction of facial wounds. Several compounds, such as probiotics,49 myrrh oil,50,51 curcumin powder,52 Opuntia ficus-indica,53 eucalyptus oil,54 krill oil,55 lysates, postbiotics, and a combination of postbiotic and chlorhexidine,56 have been reported to markedly influence the oral microbiota. The oral environment is composed of many types of bacteria that can form biofilms on teeth or dental implants. This biofilm can lead to gingivitis, periodontitis, and peri-implantitis.57 Therefore, these products might also be considered in future studies. Limitations of the present study included that the experimental period was too short to allow for complete skin wound healing, and that lavender and ginger oils might have had no effect on re-epithelialization for wound closure. Therefore, further investigations should consider the influence of essential oils on various physiological stages of wound healing, to evaluate their long-term effects. Detailed studies on the effects of such natural plant extracts on the expression of associated growth factors and their impact on other wound-healing steps might be valuable. Another limitation of this study was that rat cheek skin histology differs from that of humans, which might lead to different responses. Therefore, other animal species more similar to humans should be considered in future studies before trials are conducted on human skin tissue. Furthermore, detailed investigations of the effects of such natural plant extracts on the expression of associated growth factors and their impact on other wound-healing steps are not warranted. Future studies should also examine the effects of oils on bacterial growth in wound areas. Moreover, the effects of these essential oils in comparison with that of standard wound-healing treatments should be considered in future research.

Conclusion

The local application of a combination of lavender flower and ginger oils was demonstrated to effectively improve wound healing in the cheeks of Wistar rats, as demonstrated by decreased inflammatory cell count, increased epithelial thickness, and enhanced wound contraction. These findings suggest the clinical potential of these essential oils, particularly in combination, in promoting skin wound healing. This study might potentially inform new strategies for wound management that use natural, easily accessible biomaterials.
