Pseudouridine increases ribosome stability in a thermophilic eukaryote
Authors: Klemens Wild, Marius Klein, Alicia Burkard, Virginie Marchand, Nikola Kellner, Antonio Paez, Stefan Pastore, Tamer Butto, Yuri Motorin, Mark Helm, Irmgard Sinning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA modifications alter stability, folding space, and interaction network of RNA molecules. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) modifications stabilize the structure of ribosomes and cluster around functionally important sites such as the peptidyl transferase center, ribosomal subunit bridges, and the polypeptide tunnel. Here, we investigate the rRNA modifications of the thermophilic fungus Chaetomium thermophilum (ct), a model organism for eukaryotic thermophily and structural stability. Using LC-MS/MS, orthogonal second and third generation RNA-sequencing and high-resolution cryo-electron microscopy, we describe a cross-correlating method to assign and quantify all ct rRNA modifications. Overall, a doubling of rRNA modifications to 4% explains ribosomal thermostability with an extended distribution towards peripheral functional sites. The 2.4 Å structure of the idle ct60S ribosome, retaining nascent chains and including metal ions, polyamines, and water molecules, allows for a comprehensive structure-function analysis. Comparison with mesophilic ribosomes from Chaetomium globosum, yeast, and human highlights the significant increase of pseudouridines (Ψs). The number of Ψs linearly correlates with growth temperature, suggesting statistical modification. A ct-specific Ψ substitution forming a ‘Ψ-turn’ at the polypeptide tunnel exit close to the third constriction exemplifies mechanistic adaptations of the ribosome at elevated temperatures.

Introduction

RNA modifications enhance the biochemical and biophysical spectrum of RNA, and changes within the modification pattern impact gene expression and translational control [1–3]. Their alterations also give rise to severe human diseases and cancer [4–6] but at the same time enable new strategies for therapeutic approaches. As a prominent example, artificial mRNA modification by pseudouridylation (Ψ for pseudouridine, C5-glycosidic isomer of uridine) was found to diminish immune response while at the same time increasing biological stability; attributes that were critical for COVID-19 vaccine development with N1-methyl-pseudouridine analogs [7]. The tRNAs are the most heavily decorated RNAs with up to 25% modified nucleotides, both stabilizing the cloverleaf structure as well as tRNA and mRNA interactions [8, 9], and tRNA ‘modopathies’ impact on mitochondrial diseases, neurological disorders, and cancer [6]. Within ribosomes, RNA modifications emerge as a source of physiological ribosome heterogeneity, and their variation is a hallmark of pathologic traits [10, 11]. For example, hypo-modification of the unique and strictly conserved m1acp3Ψ modification within 18S rRNA is implicated in about 50% of all colorectal carcinomas [12] or loss of two 28S rRNA Ψs due to mutations in the pseudouridine synthase gene DKC1 results in familial dyskeratosis [13]. The variety of RNA modifications is limited in rRNA, reflecting the fact that base modifications (methylation and acetylation) are underrepresented and account for less than 10%. Thus, 2′-O-methylations and pseudouridylation are the major players accounting together for about 2% of all nucleotides in eukaryotic ribosomes, with increasing numbers from protozoa and fungi to metazoa and mammalia [2]. Modifications confer increased stability to the ribosome [14] and especially to functionally important and sensitive interactions either within rRNA or with ribosomal proteins, tRNA, mRNA, or other factors and ligands [15]. While the specific functions of some rRNA modifications have been described, as for conserved Ψs in the A-site or B2a subunit bridge [16, 17], most of them are thought to work in an incremental manner, and it is the collectivity that makes the difference.

Different strategies have been described for ribosomal stress adaptation and especially to temperature variations or persistent extreme habitats. First, strategies for elevated temperatures apart from modifications include the increase of the GC-content [18] as well as of GC base pair triples, so-called homoiterons [19]. The latter have been found especially enriched in the rRNA expansion segments exposed on the ribosomal surface [20]. As a surplus, the expansions, as well as the whole rRNA, are generally reduced in length in thermophilic species. Inversely to the GC content, the number of uridines within thermophilic and psychrophilic prokaryotic 16S rRNA was found to anti-correlate with optimal growth temperature [21]. In line, thermophilic archeota of the phylum Thermoproteota replace two conserved A-site uridines with cytidines, conferring thermal stability [22]. As a second strategy for stress adaptation, rRNA modification patterns can be adapted as reported for prokaryotes. In a comprehensive study including cryo-EM and quantitative cross-evolutionary mapping, it was shown that high levels of cytosine acetylation (ac4C) have a marked impact on thermoadaptation of ribosomes in the hyperthermophilic archaea of Thermococcales [23]. A less specific but rather overall increase in modifications being beneficial at higher temperatures has been described for the hyperthermophile Sulfolobus solfataricus, here with the greatest net changes in 2′-O-methylations [24].

However, rRNA modification strategies in eukaryotic thermophiles are not yet characterized, and it is thus unknown if they correlate with prokaryotic adaptations. Eukaryotic thermophiles are rare due to the lower tolerance of their organellar membranes to higher temperatures [25], and the highest tolerable temperature was found at 62°C [26]. Thermophilic eukaryotes evolved in hydrothermal systems [25] but, like the filamentous fungi class of Sordariomycetes, also in compost habitats [27, 28]. The fungus Chaetomium thermophilum (C. thermophilum, ct) is growing at optimal temperatures around 50°C and was the first sequenced thermophilic eukaryote [29] that subsequently evolved as a unique biochemical-biotechnological tool and model system for structural studies and eukaryotic thermophily [30]. For example, due to protein rigidification and reduction of flexible surface regions and intrinsic disorder, crystal formation necessary for X-ray crystallography is generally enhanced, and atomic movements are reduced. Likewise, in cryo-EM studies as widely explored in nuclear pore complexes [31], ribosome biogenesis [32] and mature ribosomal structures [33], overall resolution is increased and peripheral factors are stabilized. The same holds true for ct80S ribosomes, where antibiotic puromycin treatment is not sufficient to release the nascent chain (NC), and otherwise flexible regions, like a third conserved constriction at the polypeptide tunnel exit (PTE), are stabilized [20].

Taking advantage of thermophilic organisms together with the recent resolution revolution [34] now allows for cryo-EM structure determination of large biological assemblies, including all-atomic models with sufficient precision for a mechanistic interpretation. Thus, ligands, metal, and water coordination can be assigned and distinguished from post-transcriptional modifications (PTMs), especially for RNA moieties. While base modifications and 2′-O-methylation are relatively easy to assign due to their extra functional groups (mostly -CH3 methyl groups), this is not the case for uridine isomerization to pseudouridine. The correct assignment of pseudouridine sites is of special importance as they are highly abundant [35] and significantly reduce RNA flexibility, albeit based on weak energy differences [36]. In Ψs, the now protonated N1H is only slightly enlarged with respect to the C5H in uridines, and a resolution below at least 2.5 Å is necessary for their distinction. While N1H hydrogen-bonding is a clear indication for pseudouridine, in most cases, orthogonal methods are absolutely necessary for final validation of RNA modifications. Fortunately, these methods were dramatically improved either in mass spectrometry, like the SILNAS method (Stable Isotope-Labeled riboNucleic Acid as an internal Standard) [37], or in RNA-Seq of the second generation (or next-generation NGS), like elaborated chemical modification strategies combined with Illumina sequencing [38]. Additionally, the emergence of third-generation Nanopore sequencing (Nanopore-Seq) with its long-read direct RNA sequencing (DRS) capability enables the analysis of various RNA modifications directly from single-molecule native transcripts [39, 40].

Here, we combine recent advances in both RNA-seq by NGS and Nanopore-Seq with high-resolution cryo-EM to assign and validate rRNA modifications of C. thermophilum ribosomes. The assignment of pseudouridines is based on a refined strategy combining information from state-of-the-art NGS (BID-Seq and HydraPsi-Seq), DRS applying the latest available basecalling strategies, and cryo-EM structure determination of the ct60S subunit at 2.4 Å resolution, allowing for an all-atom model building. The comprehensive comparison with mesophilic ribosomes of Chaetomium globosum, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Homo sapiens allows for a detailed structure-function analysis and for the description of ct-specific thermophilic adaptations of translational importance. Overall, the doubling in rRNA modification is mainly based on the temperature-dependent increase in pseudouridylation, which emerges as a concept for eukaryotic thermophily.

Materials and methods

Purification of 80S ribosomes and rRNA extraction

C. thermophilum (DSM 1495) and Chaetomium globosum (DSM 1962, C. globosum, cg) wildtype strains were obtained from DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany. Fungal mycelium was propagated on CCM medium as described [41] at 50°C, 42°C, or 35°C (ct) and 30°C (cg), respectively. 2 L cultures were grown for approx 22 h, harvested, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently ground to a fine powder in a cryogenic cell mill (Retsch MM400). 80S ribosomes were prepared as published previously [20]. rRNA was extracted from purified ct80S or cg80S ribosomes by adding 0.5 mL TES buffer (pH 7.5) and 0.5 mL acidic phenol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to approximately 100 µg ribosomes per batch. TES buffer contains 0.06 g Tris (Carl Roth), 0.18 g EDTA (Carl Roth), and 0.25 g SDS (Carl Roth) in 50 mL ultrapure water. The sample was incubated for 1 h at 65°C and vigorously vortexed every 15 min. Afterwards, the sample was placed on ice for 5 min before centrifuging for 5 min at 4°C and 3000 g to induce phase separation. The upper aqueous phase was transferred into a clean microcentrifugation tube, and 0.5 mL of acidic phenol was added. The mixture was vortexed for 20 s and subsequently kept on ice for 5 min before centrifuging again for 5 min at 4°C and 3000 g. This step was repeated once. After centrifugation, the upper phase was transferred to a new microcentrifugation tube, and ethanol precipitation was performed. Therefore, the sample volume was adjusted to 90 µL with ultrapure water, and 10 µL 5 M ammonium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to a final concentration of 0.5 M. After the addition of 1 µL glycogen (5 mg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific), the mixture was vortexed before adding 300 µL 100% ethanol (Carl Roth). After mixing again, the sample was stored at -20°C overnight. Pellets were generated by centrifugation for 1 h at 15 000 g and -4°C. After removing of supernatant, the pellet was washed with 300 µL cold 75% ethanol and centrifuged for 30 min at -4°C and 15 000 g. Supernatant was removed, and the RNA pellet was air-dried before dissolving RNA in ultrapure water. rRNA integrity and purity were checked via agarose gel electrophoresis.

An agarose gel was cast (15 cm length and 0.5 cm thickness) using 1.3% SeaKem® LE agarose (Lonza) and 1x TBE buffer (Carl Roth) and run in 1x TBE buffer at 120 V for 90 min. The gel was pre-stained with SYBR®-Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:10 000 during pouring the gel), and 250 ng extracted rRNA in denaturing loading dye was loaded into the pockets. Visualization of the band was done with Typhoon Trio+ (GE Healthcare) with laser excitation of 488 nm and emission filter of 520 nm.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The Agilent 1260 Infinity system, combined with an Agilent 6460 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer with electrospray ion source (ESI), was used for analysis. Nucleosides were chromatographically separated via a C18 reverse HPLC column (Synergi™ 4 µm Fusion-RP 80 Å, LC Column 250 × 2 mm, Phenomenex) with a constant flow rate of 0.35 mL/min and a column temperature of 35°C. Eluent solvents consisted of 5 mM ammonium acetate buffer at pH 5.3 as mobile phase A and acetonitrile as mobile phase B (LC-MS grade, Fisher Scientific). A linear gradient was used for elution, starting with 10 min 0–8% and 10 min 8–40% solvent B. This was followed by a 3-min decrease of eluent B from 40% to 0% and starting conditions were achieved again with 100% eluent A for 7 min. The ionization source had a gas temperature of 350°C and a gas flow of 8 L/min. Nebulizer pressure was 50 psi, and nozzle and capillary voltage were 500 V and 3000 V. Sheath gas temperature was 350°C with a sheath gas flow of 12 L/min.

3.5 µg of extracted rRNA per sample was digested to nucleoside level with a mixture of several enzymes to measure 1000 ng RNA per Injection. This mix consisted of 0.6 U nuclease P1 from Penicillium citrinum (Sigma–Aldrich), 0.2 U snake venom phosphodiesterase from Crotalus adamanteus (Worthington), 0.2 U bovine intestine phosphatase (Sigma–Aldrich), 10 U benzonase (Sigma–Aldrich), 200 ng pentostatin (Sigma–Aldrich), and 500 ng tetrahydrouridine (Merck-Millipore). Digestion enzymes were buffered at pH 8 with 5 mM Tris and 1 mM MgCl2 and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Cytidine, uridine, guanosine, and adenosine were detected photometrically at 254 nm wavelength via a diode array detector, and modifications were detected via a Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer. From Agilent's MassHunter software, dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (dMRM) was used, and MS was operated only in the positive mode. For quantification, external calibration for UV detection was performed with 0.5, 5, 50, and 500 pmol, and for MS detection with 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000, and 5000 fmol nucleosides. MS calibration solutions each contained equal amounts of stable isotope-labeled internal standard (ISTD). Absolute quantification was performed as described [42].

Summarized in brief, the same amount of 13C-labeled internal standard from digested Saccharomyces cerevisiae total RNA as in the calibration solutions was added to the sample after digestion. For LC–MS/MS analysis, 100 ng ISTD per 1000 ng RNA sample was injected. Peaks of the mass chromatograms of all analyzed modified nucleosides and their 13C-labeled counterparts were integrated to receive the peak areas. The ratio of the 12C and 13C signals was determined for the calibration curves and plotted to the amount of 12C reference to calculate the slope of the linear fit for each modification. For the samples, the amount of modified nucleoside was calculated by dividing the 12C-to-13C ratio of the peak areas by the slope of the linear fit of the respective calibration curve. This was not possible for the Um modifications due to non-evaluable 13C signals. For Um calibration, the peak area of the 12C signal was directly plotted to the 12C reference amount, and its linear fit was used as a divider for the sample's peak area to obtain the absolute amount of Um. As the last step, normalization of modifications total amounts to the amount of adenosine per sample was made. Analysis was performed in triplicate.

Analysis of rRNA 2′-O-methylation (Nm) by RiboMethSeq (RMS)

Isolated 18S and 26S rRNAs (∼100 ng) from Chaetomium thermophilum 80S ribosomes were subjected to random fragmentation by alkaline hydrolysis in 50 mM sodium-bicarbonate, pH 9.2, at 96°C for 14 min. The reaction was stopped by ethanol precipitation using 3 M sodium acetate (NaOAc), pH 5.2, and 15 μg GlycoBlue. After centrifugation, the RNA pellet was washed with 80% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. RNA fragments were end-repaired as previously described [43] and purified using RNeasy MinElute Cleanup kit (Qiagen) with 675 µL of 96% ethanol for the RNA binding step. Elution of purified RNA fragments was performed in 9 µL of nuclease-free water.

Analysis of rRNA modifications (m7G, m3C and Dihydrouridine) by AlkAnilineSeq

Isolated 18S and 26S rRNAs (∼100 ng) from Chaetomium thermophilum 80S ribosomes were subjected to random fragmentation by alkaline hydrolysis in 50 mM sodium-bicarbonate, pH 9.2, at 96°C for 5 min [44]. The reaction was stopped by ethanol precipitation using 3 M NaOAc, pH 5.2, and 15 μg GlycoBlue. After centrifugation, the RNA pellet was washed with 80% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. RNA fragments were dephosphorylated by Antarctic phosphatase (NEB ref M0289L, USA) at 37°C for 1 h and precipitated using 3 M NaOAc, pH 5.2, and 15 μg GlycoBlue. After centrifugation, the RNA pellet was washed with 80% ethanol, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 M aniline, pH 4.5, and incubated at 60°C for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped by ethanol precipitation using 3 M NaOAc, pH 5.2, and 15 μg GlycoBlue. The pellet was washed twice with 80% ethanol, dried, and resuspended in 3.5 µL of nuclease-free water.

Analysis of rRNA pseudouridylation by HydraPsiSeq and BID-Seq

For HydraPsiSeq, isolated 18S and 26S rRNAs (∼50–100 ng) from Chaetomium thermophilum 80S ribosomes were subjected to hydrazine treatment (50% final concentration) for 45 min on ice. The reaction was stopped by ethanol precipitation using 0.3 M NaOAc, pH 5.2, and 15 μg GlycoBlue and incubated at −80°C for at least 30 min. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed twice with 80% ethanol and resuspended in 1 M aniline, pH 4.5. The reaction was incubated in the dark at 60°C for 15 min and processed by ethanol precipitation as described above. RNA was dephosphorylated at the 3′-end using 10 U of T4 PNK in 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.5, 100 mM MgOAc, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol and incubated at 37°C for 6 h. T4 Polynucleotide Kinase was inactivated by incubation for 20 min at 65°C. RNA was extracted by phenol-chloroform and ethanol precipitated. The pellet was resuspended in 3.5 µL of nuclease-free water.

For BID-Seq, isolated 18S and 26S rRNAs (∼100 ng) from Chaetomium thermophilum 80S ribosomes were subjected to BID-Seq/PRAISE treatment as published [45, 46].

Analysis of m5C modifications by BisulfiteSeq

Isolated 18S and 26S rRNAs (∼50 ng) from Chaetomium thermophilum 80S ribosomes were subjected to bisulfite treatment according to published protocols [47, 48].

Library preparation, quantification, and sequencing

RNA was converted to a DNA library using the NEBNext Small RNA Library kit (NEB, UK). The DNA library was quantified using a fluorometer (Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, Invitrogen, USA) and qualified using a High Sensitivity DNA chip on Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Libraries were multiplexed and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq2000 instrument with a 50 bp single-end read mode.

RMS and AAS bioinformatic pipelines and scores

High-quality raw sequencing reads of C. thermophilum rRNA (>Q30) were subjected to trimming using Trimmomatic v0.39 [49] with the following parameters: MINLEN: 08, STRINGENCY: 7, AVGQUAL: 30. Trimmed reads were further processed to keep only short reads with fragmentation-defined 3′-extremities. Selected trimmed reads were aligned to the C. thermophilum rRNA reference sequence using bowtie2 v2.4.4 [50] in end-to-end mode (–no-unal –no-1mm-upfront -D 15 -R 2 -N 0 -L 10 -i S,1,1.15 as other bowtie2 parameters) and only uniquely mapped reads in positive orientation were retained for further analysis. 5′- and 3′-extremities of the reads were counted for each RNA position in the reference, and a cumulative 5′/3′-protection profile was established as described in the R/R-studio environment [43, 51].

RMS specific scores

Specific RMS scores (mean, A, B, and C = MethScore [51, 52]) were calculated in ± 2 nucleotide intervals for all RNA positions and extracted for positions of known Nm residues in target RNA. Potential Nm positions in RNA sequence were predicted using a medium stringency combination of score mean > 0.92 and ScoreA > 0.5, as this combination generally gives the best balance between the sensitivity and selectivity in de novo Nm detection [53]. Quantification of 2′-O-methylated residues was done using MethScore (previously called scoreC [51, 52]. This score is used for quantification of the modification level since it keeps a linear calibration curve between protection and the molar ratio of 2′-methylated residue in RNA.

AAS-specific scores

Due to a -1 shift in the sequence position (ligated 5′-P extremity is at the N + 1 nucleotide in the RNA sequence), this reads' count was used as the measure for intensity of cleavage at a given position. Four scores were used for analysis of AAS raw data [44, 54]: Normalized cleavage (NCleavage), normalized count (NormCount), normalized G count (NormGcount), and stop ratio. Normalized cleavage corresponds to 1000x proportion of reads starting at a given position to the total number of reads mapped to a given RNA sequence. This score is less noisy but also less sensitive than others and is well-suited for the detection of major cleavage events in RNA. In contrast, the stop ratio (closely derived from Ψ-ratio used for analysis of Ψ-seq data [55] is calculated as a ratio of reads starting at a given position to the total number of reads passing (covering) it. This score is relatively sensitive, but also noisy. The last two scores used, NormCount and NormGcount, use local normalization to the median of cleavage signals in a ± 5 nucleotide window around of analysed position. NormCount uses all cleavage signals in the window, while signals corresponding to G residues are excluded from the calculation of the normalization median for NormGcount. These scores represent the intensity of the cleavage at a given nucleotide compared to the local cleavage background in the adjacent RNA region. None of the AlkAnilineSeq scores show linear dependence between the score’s value and stoichiometry of RNA modification, stop ratio has a linear segment at very low modification levels (<5%), while the three other scores better represent the modification stoichiometry at higher modification levels in RNA (5–50%).

HydraPsiSeq scores were calculated on the basis of the U-cleavage profile, considering that only unmodified U residues in RNA are sensitive to hydrazine cleavage. From the U-cleavage profile, RiboMethSeq-like scores (mean, A, B, and C = MethScore [51, 52]) were calculated and used for the prediction of potentially protected U residues (corresponding to Ψ residues).

BID-Seq data analysis was performed by determining the deletion score for all RNA positions. This was done via full mpileup format, followed by analysis of the match string. Deletions/jumps correspond to bisulfite-modified Ψ residues. Background value of the deletion score (mock-treated RNA sample) was subtracted.

BS-Seq analysis of m5C locations was performed using alignments of the sequencing reads to a C-to-U converted reference sequence, by determining the residual C non-deamination. The proportion of residual non-deaminated C (UtoC score) corresponds to the molar ratio of m5C at a given location. The UtoC score was calculated from the full mpileup format, as described above for BID-Seq analysis.

In vitro polyadenylation and Nanopore library preparation

Around 5 µg of isolated ribosomes were in vitro polyadenylated using Poly(A) Tailing of RNA using Escherichia coli Poly(A) Polymerase (NEB #M0276) according to manufacturer instructions. For direct RNA libraries, 1.5 µg of polyadenylated ribosomes were taken for library preparation and prepared using the SQK-RNA004 kit (ONT) as previously described [56]. For direct cDNA libraries, 1 µg of polyadenylated ribosomes was taken for library preparation and prepared using ‘Direct cDNA Sequencing Kit’ (SQK-DCS109) coupled with ‘Native Barcoding Expansion 1–12 kit’ (EXP-NBD104-NBD114), as previously described [57]. Samples were loaded on our MinION device (ONT) and sequenced for 72 hours using ONT software MinKNOW (v. 23.11.7). Samples were basecalled using super-accurate basecalling (ONT software Dorado v.7.2.13).

Analysis of data

The C. thermophilum genome was obtained from the Chaetomium thermophilum genome resource [58]. The C. globosum genome was obtained from Ensemble genomes database (GCA_000143365.1). ‘fastq_pass’ files were aligned to ‘C_thermophilum.annotation.v2.4.scaff.fa’ or ‘Chaetomium_globosum_cbs_148_51_ gca_.dna.toplevel.fa’ using epi2me-labs/wf-alignment (Epi2Me, ONT). For direct RNA and direct cDNA, the ‘minimap2’ preset was set to ‘rna’ and ‘dna,’ respectively.

DRS pseudouridine detection analysis

Pseudouridine detection was performed by using Dorado version 7.2.0 with the super accuracy RNA004 model version 5.0.0 using the ‘pseU’ modification calling model version v1. The resulting unaligned ‘bam’ file was converted into ‘fastq’ format using the samtool suite [59]. The fastq file was aligned with minimap2 [60] using the ‘map-ont’ long read alignment flag together with the ‘MD’ flag. Throughout these steps, the remainder of the modification information was ensured by setting respective flags. Subsequently, the aligned bam file was processed using pysam https://github.com/pysam-developers/pysam, last accessed date 2025/06/25). For each possibly modified nucleotide on a read, aligned bam files contain a modification probability. A threshold of 95% modification probability was chosen to determine modifications.

Cryo-EM grid preparation

Before ct80S ribosomes (400 nM) vitrification, grids were glow-discharged for one minute in an O2 atmosphere using a Solarus plasma cleaner (Gatan, Inc.). Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI Company) was used to freeze 3 μL of sample in liquid ethane on 2/1 Quantifoil Multi A holey carbon grids (Quantifoil, Multi A, 400 mesh). Sample freezing was performed with Whatman #1 filter papers at 4°C and 100% humidity with a blot force of 5 and 10 s wait time. Sample grids were stored in liquid nitrogen until data collection.

Cryo-EM data collection

Data collection on ct80S ribosomes was performed on a 300 keV Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher/FEI Company) with a K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan, Inc.) that collected movies at a magnification of 105k and a pixel size of 0.868 Å/pixel. The total dose per micrograph was 42.7 e−/Å2. Data collection was set up and monitored with the Thermo Fisher internal software EPU3.

Cryo-EM data processing

Detailed flow-charts describing cryo-EM data processing can be found in the Supplementary Data (Supplementary Fig. S4). Briefly, movies were imported into cryoSPARC [61] and motion corrected. After patch – Contrast-Transfer-Function (patch-CTF) estimation, particles were picked and binned during extraction. Three rounds of 2D classification were followed by ab initio reconstruction into three classes to obtain a preliminary 3D structure. Subsequent rounds of ‘Heterogeneous Refinement’ were done to further polish the particle set and to separate ct80S ribosomes by their translocation state and separate ct80S from ct60S subunits. Each class was subjected to a separate round of 2D classification, followed by ‘Homogenous Refinement.’ Reference-based motion correction and local CTF estimation were performed on the final particle set. Homogenous Refinement was done to obtain a high-resolution structure of the ct60S subunit. A mask was generated to encompass the ct40S subunit and used to initialize a particle subtraction job. Subtracted particles were refined in a final Homogeneous Refinement, and a local resolution estimation job was run to assess the quality of the resulting map.

Cryo-EM model building

Model building, real-space refinement, and validation were done in iterative cycles using Coot [62] and the PHENIX suite [63]. Figure preparations were done with PyMOL [64].

Results

Assignment of rRNA modifications in ct80S ribosomes by RNA-sequencing

To comprehensively map the RNA modification landscape of C. thermophilum rRNA, we employed a multi-approach framework integrating several orthogonal methods. These included LC-MS/MS, advanced NGS techniques, long-read Nanopore sequencing, and cryo-EM for the deciphering of a validated set of rRNA modification in the ct60S LSU (and also ct40S SSU with ‘limited’ cryo-EM at 2.8 Å [20]).

For LC-MS/MS, we used a quadrupole mass spectrometer with an electrospray ion source (ESI) combined with external 13C-isotope labeling for analysis. The analyses of ct80S ribosomes in comparison with ribosomes of mesophilic S. cerevisiae revealed a first hint for a strong increase of pseudouridylation and a slight enhancement of 2′-O-methylations, while at the same time the numbers of uridine modifications are decreased and other base modifications are unchanged (Fig. 1A and Supplementary File S1). In order to exclude a Sordariomycetes class-specific effect, we also analyzed rRNA within the Chaetomiaceae family of the mesophilic mold Chaetomium globosum (C. globosum, cg). LC-MS/MS clearly showed that the modifications are only slightly enhanced in C. globosum compared to S. cerevisiae, and there is no pseudouridine doubling-boost as in C. thermophilum. However, when C. thermophilum was grown at 35°C, modification quantities matched with C. globosum. We thus also performed analyses for C. thermophilum at an intermediate temperature of 42°C and found pseudouridylation to linearly correlate with the temperature increase (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Having quantified rRNA modifications using LC-MS/MS, we next applied NGS RNA-seq for the specific assignment of detectable modifications. In detail, RiboMethSeq was applied for 2′-O-methylations [43], BID-Seq [45] and HydraPsiSeq [65] for pseudouridylation, AlkAnilineSeq for m7G and BS-Seq for m5C. Additionally, we exploited RT-signatures from RiboMethSeq and HydraPsiSeq to map RT-arresting base modifications [38] (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S2). AlkAnilineSeq analysis revealed only a single strong signal for 18S-G1571, which can be confidently interpreted as m7G1571 representing a highly conserved rRNA modification in eukaryotic 18S rRNA [2, 66]. Other AlkAnilineSeq-detectable residues (m3C/D and ho5C) are not reported for eukaryotic rRNAs, and, in agreement with this, no other plausible AlkAnilineSeq signals were found in ct rRNAs. BS-Seq, designed to detect m5C (and eventually m4C) modified RNA nucleotides, confirmed the absence of non-deamination signals in 18S rRNA and pointed out two highly intense signals for 26S-C2241 and 26S-C2829. These two signals were identified as m5C residues and also highly conserved in eukaryotes [67, 68]. The RiboMethSeq protocol measures relative protection of the Nm phosphodiester bond 3′-adjacent to cleavage and thus also allows quantification of the relative methylation level. However, certain RNA 2D and 3D structures may also affect cleavage and, together with ligation and amplification biases at the library preparation step, also contribute to irregularities and the complex character of the protection profile. Despite improvements in the protocol, de novo mapping is not that straightforward, and the candidate list may be contaminated by false positive (FP) hits, and some partially modified locations may not be detected (false negative (FN) hits). Since alternative validation protocols (Nanopore sequencing and cryo-EM data) were also included in this study, the prediction of potential Nm sites in ct rRNA was performed with a moderately stringent combination of mean2 and A2 scores to capture the maximum of potential candidates. Prediction with combination (mean2> 0.92, A2> 0.5) gave 44 predicted Nm sites in 18S rRNA and 57 in 26S rRNA, while more strict parameters (mean2> 0.95, A2> 0.5) reduced this prediction to 25 18S-rRNA sites and 39 in 26S rRNA. These predicted numbers of potential Nm residues roughly correspond to expectations for lower eukaryotes like S. cerevisiae, which has 18 well-characterized Nm sites in 18S rRNA and 37 Nm positions in 25S rRNA. It should also be noted that Ψ residues are well-known RiboMethSeq FP hits, since they also might protect RNA phosphodiester bonds from cleavage. Thus, some of the mapped potential Um residues may be in fact Ψs, and cross-validation with HydraPsiSeq data (and other methods, when available) is generally required. For validation of Nm residues, we also attempted a 2′-OMe-Seq protocol (NGS method using low [dNTP] extension conditions [69], but the overall performance of this method was worse than RiboMethSeq in Nm de novo mapping, so only very few locations could be validated this way (data not shown). Out of the extended list of Nm residues mapped, three Um sites correspond in fact to Ψ residues (see below), ∼30 potential Nm were also validated by Nm-Seq, and 42 are conserved in S. cerevisiae rRNAs.

Inspection of RT arrests, mismatches, and deletions in the Low[dNTP]Seq, RiboMethSeq, and HydraPsiSeq datasets pointed out the following ct rRNA residues with particular behavior upon RT reaction. Strong signals for 26S-U2593 and 26S-U2802 correspond to two relatively well-conserved m3U2593/2802 residues in LSU rRNA, RT stop at 26S-A634 is generated by m1A633, and the signals at 18S U1188/C1189 originate from m1acp3Ψ1188 in 18S rRNA. The signals at 18S-A1777/78 likely correspond to universally conserved m66A residues at the 18S 3′-end [70].

Last, and also considering elevated Ψ content found by LC-MS mapping of ct rRNAs, we attempted mapping of Ψ residues by two orthogonal techniques: BID-Seq and HydraPsiSeq. Our in-house BID-Seq protocol follows the same principle as the previously published BID-Seq [45] and PRAISE methods [46] and is based on the formation of a covalent adduct between Ψ residues in RNA and bisulfite ions under acidic conditions. This adduct is detected by RT primer extension as a deletion (jump) event leading to base skipping in cDNA. BID-Seq is suitable for screening of long RNAs and generally gives a low level of FP hits, but an elevated number of FN hits (non-detection of the modified site). The HydraPsiSeq protocol measures the resistance of Ψ residues to hydrazine cleavage and is conceptually similar to RiboMethSeq, thus allowing precise quantification of the modification level. However, like RiboMethSeq, HydraPsiSeq is rather prone to FP hits due to a highly irregular and complex cleavage profile. Combined analysis of HydraPsiSeq and BID-Seq profiles revealed altogether 73 positions validated by one or both techniques and/or also conserved in S. cerevisiae rRNAs.

Finally, using Nanopore-Seq, we performed both direct RNA-sequencing (DRS), which allows the sequencing of native RNA with its deposited RNA modifications, as well as direct cDNA sequencing to identify base-disrupting modifications [71] (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S3A). Following sequencing, over ∼400K reads aligning to the rRNA template were obtained (both cDNA and native RNA) (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Using the ONT pseudouridine model (v1) of the Dorado basecaller (v7.2.0), the prevalence of pseudouridine on the ct rRNA landscape was evaluated. Additionally, the U-to-C mismatch ratio was evaluated, as Ψ modifications have been reported to cause the mismatches during basecalling [56, 72]. Here, applying a modification frequency of > 10%, a total of 171 pseudouridines across the three rRNA species were identified (18S: 68, 5.8S: 6 26S: 97), including the U-to-C mismatches, and without those 129 modifications remain (18S: 54, 5.8S: 3 26S: 72) (Supplementary Fig. S3C and Supplementary File S2). Additionally, using cDNA data, the location of base-disrupting modifications such as m1acp3Ψ1188 (18S) and m1A663, m3U2593, and m3U2802 (26S rRNA) could be confirmed (Fig. 1D,E and Supplementary Fig. S3D).

Assignment of rRNA modifications in ct80S ribosomes by cryo-EM

In order to use a cryo-EM structure for the assignment of specific rRNA modifications, it needs to be of high quality and at a resolution better than about 2.5 Å. This should allow for a correct interpretation and to avoid wrong assignments due to structural ambiguities, with e.g. bound ligands like water molecules or magnesium ions. Having all RNA-seq data in hand, we cross-correlated them with our cryo-EM structure of the ct60S [20] re-refined with new data collected at 2.4 Å resolution (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Table S1). While the coordinates and validation statistics are virtually identical, the high-resolution allowed for building a complete atomic model (Fig. 2) including metal ions (268 Mg2+(H2O)x; 187 K+), more than 1500 water molecules and bound ligands such as spermines and spermidines (two of each), nascent chain (built as poly-Ala model at PTC and PTE), and E-site tRNA (only CCA-3′ modeled).

While differences to our previously determined structure at 2.8 Å resolution are small, we have now built in 124 of in total 125 rRNA modifications in the LSU (26S and 5.8S rRNA), including 66 pseudouridines, 53 2′-O-methylations, and 6 base modifications (Table 1 and Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). One rRNA modification occurs in the flexible L1-stalk, which is not included in the model. For pseudouridine assignment, we mainly focused on maximal correlation between Nanopore-Seq and cryo-EM data. Here, a 20% modification frequency cut-off of the relative Ψ-signal was found to be reflected by good visibility (mostly hydrogen-bonding) in the cryo-EM map with an overall correlation of 94%. With the acceptance lowered to a 10% Nanopore-Seq cut-off and including NGS data (when present in both BID-Seq and HydraPsiSeq with high confidence), both combined with simultaneous cryo-EM validation, we could complete the assignment (remaining 6%). Although re-refinement also improved map quality and resolution of the SSU, due to the intrinsic 40S movements, the quality was not good enough to convincingly visualize rRNA modifications. Therefore, by applying identical validation criteria as for the LSU and assuming the same correlation level of 94% for the Nanopore-Seq data, we estimated the total number of 46 Ψs for the SSU, resulting in a final number of 112 high confidence Ψs for the entire ct80S ribosome. 2′-O-methylations (in total 33) and base modifications (in total 7) for the SSU were assigned when significant by NGS data and mostly confirmed (when not too flexible) by our previous cryo-EM structure at 2.8 Å [20].

In summary, the overall number of rRNA modifications in ct80S ribosomes is 211, corresponding to 3.9%, which is the highest value reported to date and corresponds to almost a doubling of the typical values of protozoan ribosomes of around 2%. Most significantly, the number of pseudouridines is drastically increased (in total 2.07%), corresponding to 53% of all modifications, while the increase of 2′-O-methylation is less significant (1.59%), and base modifications are not changed at all with respect to the compared mesophilic 80S ribosomes.

Pseudouridines are highly increased in C. thermophilum rRNA

Together with 2′-O-methylations, pseudouridylation is known as the most common eukaryotic rRNA modification, outnumbering base modifications almost 10-fold [2]. However, rRNA modification strategies in eukaryotic thermophiles are not yet characterized. Pseudouridines are believed to play a key role in ribosome stabilization [73]. Given the elevated number of pseudouridine modifications identified in C. thermophilum (ct), we next sought to determine whether a similar pattern is observed in the mesophilic C. globosum (cg).

To address this question, we isolated rRNA from C. globosum and performed DRS followed by pseudouridine-specific basecalling as previously described. Our analysis revealed a reduced number of pseudouridine modifications with a total of 56 sites (overall 1.02% modification, modification frequency > 20%), compared to the 112 sites identified in C. thermophilum. The intra-family Nanopore-Seq-based comparison within Chaetomiaceae (ct and cg) thus confirms our LC-MS/MS data that the twofold Ψ increase in C. thermophilum is independent of phylogenetic relation. Overall, pseudouridines are even further enriched when compared to yeast (2.41x) and also to human rRNA values are elevated (1.51x). Enrichment is lower for 2′-O-methylations (1.56x compared to yeast) or absent compared to human ribosomes (0.98x). The synopsis of rRNA modifications for C. thermophilum and the ribosomes of mesophilic C. globosum (cg), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (sc), and Homo sapiens (hs) is given in Table 1. These findings suggest that the elevated pseudouridine levels observed in C. thermophilum are distinct and necessary for the stabilization of its rRNA.

Interestingly, while around one-third of the Ψ modification sites are found to be conserved in our comparison, especially around the PTC or the B2a bridge, most of them are species-specific. Many of the conserved sites are in the subunit interface, which correlates with the fact that they are important for correct conformational dynamics in the translation cycle as deduced from yeast ribosome cryo-EM structures devoid of pseudouridines [74]. The otherwise low conservation reflects the notion that only a few dedicated structure-function assignments are described, while the plethora of modifications is thought to play a more pleiotropic role in overall ribosome stabilization and has thus diverged during evolution. However, an astonishing feature of ct-specific rRNA modifications is their accumulation in the 5′-region (domains I and II) of 26S rRNA (Table 1 and see below), while e.g. human 28S rRNA shows a strong increase at the very 3′-end (domain VI) with respect to the protozoan ribosomes.

Further hyper-modified regions for ct26S rRNA occur around nucleotides 1900 (intersubunit space, H62), 2550 (E-site), and also in the Factor Binding Site (FBS) and at the PTE (see below). Corresponding additions are also found in the 18S rRNA around nucleotide 900 (bridges and E-site, H23) and also 550 (mRNA-entry, H17 and H18). Thus, especially peripheral substrate delivery or product release sites seem to be affected with a prevalent increase of Ψs in the additional regions.

Characterization of pseudouridine sites in C. thermophilum rRNA

Our orthogonal combination of state-of-the-art technologies identified the increase of pseudouridines as a major contributor to rRNA stability in C. thermophilum. Overall, rRNA modifications cluster around functionally important regions including the decoding center and tRNA-binding sites (A-, P- and E-site), the PTC and the polypeptide tunnel, as well as the intersubunit bridges, with the B2a bridge. The usual modification landscape is known for centering around the PTC and a gradual radial expansion towards the outside from prokaryotes to protozoa and metazoa [13]. The Ψ distribution and density within ct60S follows the general landscape, however, at different scales (Fig. 3). Thus, values are increased about threefold compared to yeast and almost twofold compared to human ribosomes. The single exception is the B2a bridge subunit at the decoding center (see below), which in all species comprises a high number of pseudouridines (5 in ct and hs, 4 in sc rRNA). Like in the human ribosome, modifications expand towards the exterior with distances of pseudouridines from the PTC reaching 100 Å. Around the PTC, Ψ density reaches a value of 0.22 Ψs/1000 Å3 corresponding to almost one Ψ nucleotide per 10 Å radius with a surprisingly even distribution.

With their carbon-carbon bond between ribose and base and their hydrogen-bonding capability of the N1-nitrogen, pseudouridines are known to stabilize RNA secondary structures [36]. While this is well established, a detailed description of their conserved interaction patterns in the context of the ribosome is still lacking. The high-resolution structure of ct60S (compared to yeast: 2.23 Å (8p8u), human: 1.67 Å (8glp) [76]) allows for a comprehensive evaluation of pseudouridine structure and function (Fig. 4). Most obviously, the N1 nitrogen is utilized in establishing multiple hydrogen-bonding networks. Thus, it can be employed in base triples with the pseudouridine as bridging nucleotide, as exemplified for Ψ758 (conserved in sc25S rRNA) bulged-out from 26S rRNA helix H31, bonding U2678 and C2696 within helix H86 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S5A and B). More indirectly RNA-stabilizing, it can coordinate water or metal ions that are omnipresent in the ribosome, as shown for Ψ948 (conserved) in contact with a cluster of hydrated potassium ions (Fig. 4B). Indeed, the majority of rRNA pseudouridines are found to be involved in N1 hydrogen-bonding, directly allowing for their cryo-EM identification. The highest Ψ density is found in the B2a bridge (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S6) protruding like a stalk (H69) from the 60S bulk and tightly interacting with the minor groove of H44 18S rRNA directly at the decoding center (ct: A1751, A1752). The conserved B2a bridge modifications are essential for ribosome biogenesis and translation [16], and ct26S rRNA contains five pseudouridines within a stretch of 13 nucleotides (Ψ2217, Ψ2221, Ψ2223, Ψ2227, Ψ2229). Here, pseudouridine function is not primarily hydrogen-bonding but rather secondary and tertiary structure stabilization within and across the ribosomal subunits. Most strikingly, the H69 stem-loop providing the bridge forms an extended linear interaction surface at a 90° angle with respect to H69. Ψ2217 (not conserved in sc25S rRNA) in the center of the stem-loop kink is tied to the opposing strand (non-WC bp with A2225 and ribose bonding) and provides a cross-strand stack for G2224 that itself binds to the phosphoribose backbone. Obviously, pseudouridines are installed in RNA-RNA tertiary interactions involving otherwise flexible loop regions. This principle also holds true for the exposed stem-loop of 26S rRNA helix H89 that protrudes into the center of the Factor Binding Site (FBS) between P-stalk and A-site (Fig. 4D). Here, ct60S establishes two adjacent modifications (Ψ2801, m3U2802) whereas the mesophilic ribosomes have only one (sc: m3U2843, hs: Ψ4420). The stem-loop is further rigidified by a multitude of charged interactions with eL40 and serves as a docking platform (or ‘battering ram’) for both the P-stalk and the eEF2 translocation factor during ribosomal ratcheting.

Another striking feature is the accumulation of pseudouridines in or next to tandem mismatches and especially tandem wobble base pairs. Wobbling apparently demands enhanced stabilization in thermophilic RNA helices like the Ψ807-G882/Ψ881-G808 tandem in helix H33 that is not modified in the compared mesophilic ribosomes (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S5A). While the N1-nitrogens are not exploited here, the typical base pair asymmetry allows for enhanced ligand coordination (potassium and water), stabilizing the arrangement.

Finally, one of the best characterized and conserved pseudouridylation occurs in the very center of the ribosome directly at the PTC [77]. Here, Ψ2882 of ct26S rRNA in the so-called A-loop (H92) at the A-site base-pairs with the penultimate cytidine at the CCA-3′ end of A-site tRNA. Although this tRNA is not present in our structure, Ψ2882 is perfectly aligned for interaction by a complex network including two more modified nucleotides. Most importantly, Ψ2882 is tilted by steric interference with the methyl-group of Um2880, and the N1-nitrogen is further fixed by hydrogen-bonding to U2261. While all these pseudouridine functions are not surprising, a major structure-function relationship so far is not described, and to our knowledge was not previously assigned: a feature we delineate as ‘Ψ-turn’ motif.

The ‘Ψ-turn’ is a unique RNA motif of pseudouridines

Uridines stabilize sharp turns in RNA structures in a motif known as U-turn [78] where the base hydrogen-bonds with a phosphate in a so-called base-phosphate (Bph) interaction of unusual strength [79]. U-turns induce a change in backbone direction, with the N3-nitrogen of the single-stranded uridine nucleotide forming a hydrogen-bond with the phosphate group of the N + 3 nucleotide, and often are involved in RNA tertiary interactions. Thus, the U-turn always involves a turn on the 3′-side of the uridine. However, in pseudouridine, the N1-nitrogen is also available for bonding back to the phosphoribose backbone. While this back-bonding is well established to occur in a water-mediated manner, the direct Bph-interaction is not described. Here, we define a ‘Ψ-turn’ with direct Bph hydrogen-bonding of the N1-nitrogen to the 5′-proximal phosphate of the N-1 nucleotide (9Bph nomenclature [79]) (Fig. 5A). Thus, the turn is even sharper than the U-turn and results in an immediate reversal of the backbone and the exposure of the N-1 nucleotide in a very rigidified manner. Characteristic for the Ψ-turn, the α-angle (around P-O5' bond of Ψ) cannot adopt the A-RNA typical value of -60°, but rotates either by 120° (α=60°) or by 240° (α=180°). As an example conserved in eukaryotic ribosomes (Table 1), the hairpin-loop closing ct26S rRNA H37 comprises a CΨCA sequence with Ψ942 bonding back to the exposed C941 nucleotide. In the context of the ct60S subunit, the CΨCA-loop is extremely tightly embedded in between the P- and the E-site, and the ‘phosphate-rich’ surrounding is packed with spermi(di)nes (Fig. 5B), metal ions (Mg2+ and K+), and water molecules (Supplementary Fig. S7). Interestingly, the exposed C941 nucleotide forms the platform for an interhelical single-stranded base stacking orthogonal to H37 with G42 (H11) and A2760 (H74) that is preserved between species. Stacking of Ψ942 with A2760 is reinforced by a magnesium ion bridging two phosphate groups. Finally, the Ψ-turn is stabilized by a tertiary interaction of the Ψ-phosphate with the G919 base and the N-terminal loop region of eL28 involving three subsequent histidines (H39-41) completing Ψ-turn coordination.

A ct-specific Ψ-turn is close to the polypeptide tunnel exit

While this Ψ942-turn is conserved, a second Ψ-turn (Ψ1453) is present only in ct26S rRNA and therefore seems ct-specific (Figs 6 and 7A). This Ψ-turn is included in 26S rRNA H47 and its complex closing loop involves five unpaired nucleotides and a Watson-Crick (WC)-Hoogsteen base pair (U1446-A1550). In contrast to the Ψ942-turn, Ψ1453 is part of a WC base pair with A1445 (three-times increased stacking with the WC-Hoogsteen pair compared to regular A-RNA according 3DNA web-server [80]), and instead of a single nucleotide being exposed, two stacked bases (A1451, C1452) are bulged-out forming a platform orthogonal to H47. Within the H47-loop, G1447 and G1449 form bifurcated hydrogen-bonds with the opposing backbone leaving a stacking-void in between G1449 and A1450. Both the platform and the void are occupied by exposed bases of helix H50 (G1492 and U1494, Fig. 6A and see below). In contrast to ct26S rRNA (Fig. 6B), human 28S rRNA contains a uridine (U2384) in an identical environment (Fig. 6C). However, the lacking Ψ-turn rearranges the turn (α-angle changes from + 60° to -60°) and the U-phosphate is retracted from the turn now hydrogen-bonding to the O3' of the N-3 nucleotide (A2381) of the Hoogsteen base-pair that is in C2'-endo conformation.

Why are these details important to describe? The Ψ-turn in ct26S rRNA re-localizes the exposed bulged-out nucleotides of the platform in respect to the non-modified turn as observed in yeast and human ribosomes. As mentioned above, H47 is in very tight interaction with H50 and besides the stacking, a set of hydrogen bonding defines a H47-H50 structural unit with almost perpendicular stacking around the exposed Ψ-phosphate (Fig. 7B). Most importantly, Ψ1453 is located close to the PTE and H50 refers to a peculiar motif that has been defined as ‘nested base-tiple knot’ [20] (Fig. 7C). This motif in ct26S rRNA includes the G1485 nucleotide that is bulged-out of H50 and restricts the PTE by one half (to 12 Å) providing tight interactions to the very N-terminus of the nascent chain before it loses contact in the funnel formed beyond the PTE. In fact, a continuous base-stacking of in total nine nucleotides between the Ψ-turn platform (A1451, C1452) and G1485 connects the Ψ-turn to the PTE constriction.

In contrast, the H47-H50 interaction in human 28S rRNA is remodeled as the U2384 phosphate is retracted towards H47 and G2416 (corresponding to ctG1485) is predominantly found in an H50-in position releasing the PTE constraint and apparently also releasing the nascent chain (upon Puromycin treatment in idle ribosomes) (Fig. 7D, E). This conformational change coincides with a break of the continuous H47 platform – H50 base-stacking. Stunningly, the retraction of the phosphate in human 28S rRNA is compensated for by 2′-O-methylation of the opposing nucleotide (Gm2424) of H50 filling the void and of Um2415 restricting the ‘in-out’ trajectory (Fig. 7F). Finally, the tight structure-function relationship is underlined by an additional conserved 2′-O-methylation (ctCm1491, hsCm2415) stabilizing a CC base pair with the Ψ-platform cytosine (ctC1452, hsC2383) and rigidifying the H47-H50 unit.

Taken together, the 80S ribosome of the thermophilic fungus Chaetomium thermophilum is heavily stabilized by an elevated number of pseudouridines that increase the rRNA modification density not only around the known functional sites but also in a ct-specific manner towards peripheral factor arrival and departure zones like FBS, E-site and PTE. Our orthogonal methodology including state-of-the-art NGS, Nanopore-Seq and cryo-EM methods allows for an explicit validation of all pseudouridine sites and for the definition of a tight Ψ-turn as RNA structural motif combining rigidity with ribosome functionality at elevated temperatures.

Discussion

The discovery of RNA modifications and especially of the fifth nucleotide pseudouridine Ψ occurred already in the early days of biochemistry [81], however their description in a mechanistic structure-function context and their impact in health and disease is only now emerging. While base modifications including the addition of extra-functional groups are relatively easy to ‘read’, there are less descriptions for a specific read-out of 2′-O-modification and pseudouridylation, which constitute the bulk of about 90% in rRNA modifications. The occurrence and structure-function relationships of 2′-O-methylations have been comprehensively reviewed for eukaryotic ribosomes [2] and especially for the human ribosome their general stabilizing effect on the RNA-typical C3'-endo ribose conformation [82] and for mice their impact on development dynamics has been reported [3]. Even more attention has been paid on pseudouridylation [73, 83], which by its overall stabilizing effect by hydrogen-bonding and backbone rigidification in duplex RNA, is important especially on exposed stem-loops of rRNA interaction sites as described for the hyper-pseudouridylated helix H69 in the B2a bridge to the 40S subunit [16] and for the conserved A-loop within the PTC [77] base-pairing with A-site tRNA.

Also, dysregulation of pseudouridylation has been repeatedly reported to be linked to human diseases [5]. However, it is mostly correlated with mutations in stand-alone pseudouridine synthases (PUS enzymes) with tRNA, mRNA or snRNA substrates and does not impact on rRNA pseudouridylation that is performed by the snoRNPs during early ribosome biogenesis within the nucleolus [84]. The isomerization of uridine is catalysed by the Box H/ACA snoRNPs harbouring a separate class of snoRNAs comprising ‘pseudouridylation pockets’ (bulges) within two stem-loop structures adjacent to the single-stranded H and ACA Box consensus motifs at their 3′-end. While their complete set is annotated e.g. for Saccharomyces cerevisiae (28 guiding snoRNAs for 44 Ψ sites) [85] this is not the case for Chaetomium thermophilum. The assignment is not straightforward and cannot be easily deduced from the Ψ-surrounding sequences, as the selective target-guide RNA base-pairings are non-linear and the guiding sno-RNAs fold in alternative conformations resulting in a degenerated code. Thus, the cause for the increased pseudouridylation in C. thermophilum rRNA remains unclear as it is not known whether the quantity and/or the activity of snoRNPs is increased. However, the elevated Ψ level might just reflect the changed physical properties and a higher ‘statistical’ probability due to higher temperature, as suggested for increased ac4C acetylation in Thermococcales [23]. The second interpretation fits our observation that pseudouridine levels within ct80S ribosomes lineally increase with temperature. Elevated temperatures destabilize RNA secondary structures and pre-rRNAs might therefore be better substrates for snoRNPs during early ribosome biogenesis.

In this sense, it is interesting to look at early folding intermediates in pre-60S biogenesis as available by cryo-EM studies for Chaetomium thermophilum (Fig. 8). In the first described pre-60S folding intermediate, the so-called Mak16 state (Fig. 8A), only rRNA domains I and II are folded corresponding to a scaffolding outer corset of the 60S below the central protuberance (still missing the 5S RNP) leaving the functional sites unfolded and highly accessible for pseudouridylation. Of special attention, the 5′ region of the pre-26S rRNA is solvent exposed and also the PTE region is uncovered, which would allow for enhanced pseudouridylation if this state would be stabilized or show a longer lifetime. Modification must occur latest in this pre-60S state (if not already earlier in context of the 90S SSU-processome), as in the subsequent ‘primordial 5S RNP state’ (Fig. 8B) the 5′-region is covered by the incoming 5S RNP (in its non-rotated state) and also the PTE is now hidden by the attached domain VI of pre-26S rRNA, as it is also in the mature 60S subunit (Fig. 8C). Of note, there are also indications that ribosome biogenesis in Chaetomium thermophilum might differ from e.g. the human mechanism as observed for the 90S SSU-processome [32], where a reverse 3′ to 5′ order of folding was observed that would fit to the observation that it is the 5′-region of the 26S rRNA revealing the most ct-specific modifications.

Applying state-of-the-art orthogonal NGS and Nanopore-based RNA-Seq technologies and cryo-EM our study allowed for a validated synopsis and structure-function analysis of all rRNA modifications and especially ct60S pseudouridylation. Of interest, despite applying a rather restrictive frequency cut-off of 20% for the Ψ-signal in our Nanopore analyses, we identify an extremely high correlation of 94% with Ψs unambiguously detected in the cryo-EM map. Thus, the introduction of single molecule long-read sequencing methods [10] and especially of advanced basecalling models [87] sets new standards in RNA modification detection that even by themselves yield in very robust data. While our equally restrictive cut-off criteria for NGS data only allowed for a correlation of 53%, a less stringent assignment would drastically increase this number to 75%, the data however could not have been used in an unequivocal manner without cryo-EM map inspection. The overall correlations as applied in our studies are summarized in a Venn-diagram (Fig. 9).

Venn diagram for orthogonal Ψ assignment in ct60S. Four methods are compared: BID-Seq (green) and HPS-Seq (red) for NGS, nanopore sequencing (20% frequency cut-off) for Nanopore-Seq (violet), and cryo-EM map interpretation (cyan). The respective Ψs are given according to their identification. Nanopore-Seq and cryo-EM correlate with 94% of all identified sites according our cross-correlation scheme (including significant data from all methods).

Starting from our validated set of rRNA modifications we comprehensively analyzed the structure–function relationship as deduced by structure analyses focusing on the Ψ modifications that were found to be dramatically increased in respect to the mesophilic ribosomes. While the overall spatial distribution is still the same, the density as analyzed for the 60S subunit is increased to about one Ψ per 10 Å3 around the PTC and the upper part of the polypeptide tunnel and is also extended towards the exterior up to 100 Å away from the PTC. Most strikingly, the substrate delivery sites (FBS and mRNA-entry) and the product release sites (E-site, PTE, mRNA-exit) are enriched in Ψ modifications. One obvious explanation would be that especially in interaction sites on the ribosomal surface increased stability is necessary to maintain specificity and kinetic proofreading [88] at higher temperatures. As not previously investigated for rRNA in a comprehensive manner, we summarized specific Ψ structure-function relations including the Ψ-typical H-bond formation via the N1-nitrogen (to ligands and in base triples), stabilization of the PTC, or occurring within exposed RNA stem-loops like 26S rRNA H69 forming the B2a bridge or helix H89 within the FBS forming a ‘battery ram’ for both translocation factor eEF2 as well as for the P-stalk during ribosomal ratcheting. Moreover, we identify a strong correlation of tandem base mismatches and especially tandem GU wobble base pairs with at least one Ψ-modification either in or directly adjacent to the mismatch. Whether this mismatch stabilization is purely ct-specific or a general thermophilic principle remains to be seen.

Finally, we define the Ψ-turn as RNA motif including a strong direct base-phosphate (Bph) H-bond of the protonated N1-nitrogen to the 5′-phosphate of the N-1 nucleotide. This motif is reminiscent of the classical U-turn, which however is wider and forms a Bph H-bond with its N3 to the N + 3 phosphate. The tightness of the turn and its angular restrictions expose the single-stranded N-1 nucleotide in a rigidified manner perfectly suited for RNA tertiary interactions by stacking on other single-stranded nucleotides (as seen for the conserved Ψ942 interaction) or on orthogonal helices as detailed for the ct-specific Ψ1453 within 26S rRNA H47 at the PTE. The latter is found at the center of an extremely tight inter-helical packing of H47 with helix H50 and a conserved structural RNA motif forming the 3rd and ultimate constriction of the polypeptide tunnel next to the PTE. H50 forms a stem-loop including the so-called ‘nested base-triple knot’ that harbors a guanosine (ctG1485) exposed on a platform and in direct contact with the N-terminus of the nascent chain, and while the respective nucleotide is flexible in human ribosomes and ribosome – nascent chain complexes, it is fixed in the C. thermophilum ribosome in an out-conformation cutting the tunnel diameter by half. Apparently, differences in triple-knot conformation (G-in and G-out) are directly communicated to H47, which in human 28S rRNA is a uridine that cannot form the tight turn and is thus not stabilizing its N-1 nucleotide, which is relocated.

Thus, we postulate a direct structure-function relationship of the Ψ-turn with the PTE restriction geometry and thus on the stability of ribosome – nascent chain complexes as thermophilic adaptation strategy. Further comprehensive biochemical, biophysical and in silico studies will be necessary to uncover and classify general and species-specific rRNA modification – thermophily relationships in all domains of life.
