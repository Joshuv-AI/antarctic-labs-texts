Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infections in the Northern Territory of Australia 2017–2022
Authors: Sara Davison, Aliya Bryce, Duncan Campbell, Robert Baird, Thomas Ewin, Shahid Ullah, Sonja Janson, Danny Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMethicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) bloodstream infections (BSIs) cause significant morbidity and mortality. In Australia's Northern Territory (NT), MRSA BSI prevalence is disproportionately high. Vancomycin remains standard therapy despite toxicity and complex monitoring requirements.AimTo evaluate demographics, clinical management and outcomes of patients with MRSA BSI in the NT, with a focus on health inequities.MethodsThis multi‐centre retrospective observational study analysed MRSA BSI episodes in hospitalised adults between 2017 and 2022 at the Royal Darwin and Alice Springs Hospitals. Key data were extracted from electronic medical records. Composite treatment failure was defined as follows: outcome of all‐cause 90‐day mortality, persistent bacteraemia (>7 days) or 90 day relapse.ResultsOf 182 identified episodes, MRSA accounted for 40% of all S. aureus BSIs. First Nations Australians were disproportionately affected, representing 69% of episodes, with an incidence rate 3.3 times higher than non‐Indigenous residents. The composite treatment failure rate was 18%, and 90‐day mortality was 13%. Age ≥ 50 years was the only independent variable associated with treatment failure. Notably, 19% of patients required an antimicrobial switch from vancomycin, frequently due to adverse drug reactions. Vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing showed high discordance between VITEK2 and E‐Test.ConclusionMRSA bloodstream infections remain a significant challenge in the NT, largely due to social and structural factors disproportionately affecting First Nations Australians. While strategies to prevent invasive bloodstream infections should be prioritised, the high incidence of vancomycin‐related adverse events and MIC discordance underscores the urgent need for stable, effective oral antimicrobial alternatives and culturally safe, community‐led prevention strategies.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infections (BSIs) are serious infections associated with high mortality, morbidity and significant healthcare costs. 1 , 2 Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) BSIs, first reported in the 1960s, remain a major clinical challenge. MRSA accounts for 16.9% of S. aureus BSI isolates nationally; however, in the Northern Territory (NT), this proportion is more than double, ranging from 43% to 48%. 3 , 4

The NT is home to a large population of First Nations Australians, who experience a disproportionate burden of invasive S. aureus infections. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 This disparity is complex, driven by social and structural determinants of health, including overcrowded housing and inequitable access to primary care, factors which contribute to high rates of skin and soft tissue infections. 8

Managing MRSA BSIs in this setting presents unique challenges. Intravenous vancomycin remains the standard first‐line treatment despite its narrow therapeutic window and known nephrotoxicity. 9 , 10 In the NT, the requirement for prolonged intravenous therapy and intensive therapeutic drug monitoring 9 is complicated by the fact that many patients reside in remote settings without consistent access to secondary medical facilities. There is a growing body of evidence in support of alternative agents and early oral antimicrobial switch with agents such as linezolid; however, inclusion criteria in prospective studies are selective for low‐risk groups, which may not account for our affected population, while some are also limited by retrospective data. 11 , 12

MRSA isolates are classified as ‘susceptible’ to vancomycin when the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is ≤2.0 mg/L. 13 Historically, and in centres including the NT, vancomycin therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for invasive MRSA infections is performed using trough serum concentrations. 14 This dosing strategy assumes the MRSA isolate has a MIC of 1 mg/L with no adjustments for higher or lower values. This ‘one‐size‐fits‐all’ approach to dosing has recently come under scrutiny, with updated guidelines advocating for area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) relative to the MIC (AUC/MIC) dosing, a more nuanced and individualised approach to TDM. 14 , 15 Higher AUC/MIC levels are more closely related to nephrotoxicity. 16 However, this method relies on expertise, access to specialist software and accurate MIC reporting. While broth microdilution (BMD) is the gold standard for MIC determination, it is labour‐intensive and rarely performed outside reference laboratories. 17 More commonly used methods, such as VITEK2 (bioMérieux) and Epsilometer Test™ (ETEST) (bioMérieux), are quicker and more accessible, but discordant results have been reported. 14 , 17 , 18 It is unclear to what extent this correlates with clinical outcome.

This study aims to describe the demographics, clinical features and outcomes of adult patients with MRSA BSI in the NT using a strength‐based approach that recognises structural health inequities. Secondary objectives include evaluating vancomycin therapy complications and assessing correlations between MIC testing methods to support more effective, culturally safe management strategies.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted across two public tertiary referral centres in the NT of Australia, the Royal Darwin Hospital and Alice Springs Hospital. These hospitals and associated microbiology services serve a collective population of approximately 250 000 people across the entire Top End and Central Australian regions in the NT. 19 The service catchment area is geographically expansive, exceeding 2.5 million km2 with both centres providing care to urban, semi‐remote and remote communities. 20 First Nations Australians comprise 26% and 40% of the catchment of the respective hospitals. 21 The microbiology laboratories serving these two tertiary referral centres process more than 90% of blood cultures taken in the NT.

All blood cultures isolating MRSA between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2022 in patients ≥18 years were identified from the local microbiology laboratories. Unique patient identifiers were used to extract demographics, outcomes, treatment and microbiological data from hospital records and laboratory databases. First Nations status was determined based on self‐identification recorded within the electronic medical record. Repeated cultures within a 90‐day period were analysed as a single episode to account for persistent or relapsed infection.

MRSA was defined as S. aureus with a cefoxitin MIC ≥ 8 μ g/mL as determined by VITEK2 in accordance with antimicrobial susceptibility testing guidelines by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. 13 Blood cultures are incubated for 5 days using the Bact/Alert 3D system (bioMérieux, France) and cultured using standard microbiological methods. For each isolate, a VITEK2 (bioMérieux, France) and ETEST (bioMérieux, France) was performed as per the manufacturer's guidelines.

The incidence of MRSA BSI in adult patients was calculated based on 2021 Census data (183 050 overall adult population, 46 055 of whom were identified as First Nations Australians). 21 Acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) were defined using the 2012 kidney disease: improving global outcomes (KDIGO) criteria. 22 Immunosuppression was defined as people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), steroid use equivalent to a daily dose of ≥20 mg of prednisolone for >4 weeks or chemotherapy or immunomodulators at the time of BSI. Device‐related infection was defined as indwelling prosthetic material deemed associated with the infection; this included venous and arterial access, as well as cardiovascular, orthopaedic and other implants. An antimicrobial switch was defined as cessation of vancomycin and commencement of an alternative antimicrobial agent or agents. The primary outcome was treatment failure, defined as a composite end point of all‐cause mortality at 90 days, persistent bacteraemia at 7 days or relapse with a further positive culture for MRSA within 90 days.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024). The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess the significance of differences between continuous variables. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, and chi‐squared test was used to examine the relationships between patient characteristics and group classifications. Logistic regression models were used to test for associations of patient characteristic variables in different patient groups against clinical outcomes and all‐cause 90‐day mortality. To further explore the effect of MIC on outcomes, logistic regression was repeated only on patients treated with vancomycin with multivariate models incorporating age, sex, First Nations status, diabetes mellitus (DM), haemodialysis (HD) and either VITEK2 or ETEST MIC (as continuous variables). Multivariate models for treatment failure incorporated First Nations status as a key variable to assess its independence from other clinical and social determinants. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, with P‐values >0.05 indicating adequate fit. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the AUC were also used to assess model performance, with AUC values of 0.70 or higher indicating a good fit.

Ethics approval was obtained through the NT Human Research Ethics Committee (Project code: 2023‐4501), which includes First Nations representation.

Results

A total of 182 episodes of MRSA BSI (40% (182/462) of all adult S. aureus BSI) were identified in 175 patients during the study period. The estimated population incidence of MRSA BSI was 16.6/100 000 adult person‐years across the NT.

Demographics

Median age was 52 years (interquartile range (IQR): 42–64 years), with similar numbers of BSIs across both sites (Table 1). We noted a high rate of comorbidities: 61% (111/182) of patients had DM; around one quarter were receiving HD (43/182, 24%). The most commonly documented primary site of infection was skin and soft tissue (SSTI), followed by osteomyelitis/septic arthritis (41% and 14% respectively). Around 10% had pneumonia documented as a suspected primary site of infection.

Sixty‐nine per cent of episodes (126/182) occurred in First Nations Australians. A significant disparity in MRSA BSI incidence was observed; the rate among First Nations Australians (45.60/100 000 person‐years) was 3.3 times higher than in non‐Indigenous people (13.74/100 000 person‐years). First Nations Australians were younger (50 years vs 66 years, P < 0.001) and more commonly female (51% vs 34%, P = 0.04). Reflecting the regional burden of non‐communicable diseases, First Nations patients had higher documented rates of DM (75% vs 31%, P < 0.001) and end‐stage renal disease requiring HD (30% vs 11%, P = 0.007).

Clinical outcomes

Median length of hospital stay (LOS) during MRSA BSI was 20 days (IQR 9–40), and 23% (42/182) of patients received care in the intensive care unit (ICU). AKI during BSI affected 53/139 (38%) of non‐dialysing patients. Thirty‐two patients (18%) met the composite outcome for treatment failure, which included all‐cause 90‐day mortality (24/182, 13%), with MRSA BSI lasting ≥7 days (5/182, 3%), or relapse with further positive blood culture for MRSA within 90 days (3/182, 2%) (Table 1). First Nations patients had a significantly shorter median LOS compared to non‐Indigenous patients (14 vs 28 days, P = 0.02), with no significant difference in treatment failure rates (24% vs 15%, P = 0.2).

Age ≥ 50 years was the only independent variable associated with the composite treatment failure outcome on univariate analysis (Table 2) (odds ratio (OR): 3.21 (confidence interval (CI): 1.37–8.45), P = 0.01). The all‐cause 90‐day mortality rate was higher in those receiving HD (OR: 2.49 (CI: 1.01–6.12), P = 0.05). DM appeared to trend towards protective to all‐cause 90‐day mortality (OR: 0.43 (CI: 0.17–1.04), P = 0.06) on univariate analysis, although not the composite failure outcome.

Complications of vancomycin treatment

Of the 182 episodes of MRSA BSI reviewed, most patients (174/182, 96%) were empirically commenced on vancomycin. In the remaining eight, one patient was commenced empirically on daptomycin and clindamycin, five had unclear regimens documented and two left hospital without treatment. One hundred and forty patients (77%) received vancomycin monotherapy; however, in 10 (7%) the course was abbreviated due to discharge against medical advice (n = 5), unclear reasons (n = 2), possible drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) (n = 1) or neutropenia (n = 2).

Thirty‐four patients (19%) initially commenced vancomycin, then underwent an antimicrobial ‘switch’ during therapy. Safety concerns with the current standard of care were evident, as adverse drug reactions, including AKI and cutaneous reactions, accounted for 62% (21/34) of all switches from vancomycin (Table 3). The most common alternative agents used were linezolid (n = 13) and daptomycin (n = 11).

VITEK2 and ETEST vancomycin MIC of MRSA BSI isolates

Of all MRSA BSI isolates, 55% and 61% had a MIC of 1 mg/L reported on ETEST (82/150) and VITEK2 respectively (105/171). VITEK2 MICs were generally lower than ETEST (mean 0.9 mg/L vs 1.43 mg/L, P < 0.01). In total, 5/171 (3%) isolates had a VITEK2 MIC ≥2 mg/L and 66/150 (44%) on ETEST. Both an ETEST and VITEK2 MIC were available for 142 isolates (78%) (Table 4), only 52 (37%) had concordant MICs between testing modalities. In logistic regression models of patients with MRSA BSI who were treated with vancomycin alone (n = 140), age was the only variable associated with the composite treatment failure outcome (Table S1). Neither ETEST nor VITEK2 MIC correlated with treatment failure or all‐cause 90‐day mortality.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine, characterise and describe the burden of MRSA BSI in the NT of Australia with a focus on clinical outcomes, including adverse reactions to first‐line vancomycin therapy. Additionally, we used data to explore variability in isolate vancomycin MIC when utilising different laboratory testing modalities.

In line with recent reports, 40% of all S. aureus bloodstream isolates were methicillin‐resistant. This is far greater than the Australian national average of 16.9%. 3 , 4 The median LOS of 20 days and our finding of nearly one‐quarter of patients requiring treatment within the ICU setting demonstrate the significant use of healthcare resources and substantial morbidity these infections exert on patients. MRSA BSI remains a challenging entity to treat, with almost one in five patients meeting the composite outcome for treatment failure. Age ≥ 50 years was the only variable independently associated with all‐cause 90‐day mortality in line with previous findings. 23 Interestingly, the 30‐day all‐cause mortality rate within our population was 7%, half the reported national average of 15%, 3 likely due to the younger age of onset observed in our cohort.

The disproportionate impact of MRSA BSI, particularly upon First Nations Australians, was substantial, occurring at about three times the incidence of the overall population. No significant difference in composite outcome was identified between First Nations and non‐Indigenous people; however, it is notable First Nations patients were on average 16 years younger at the time of BSI.

The high burden of non‐communicable diseases such as DM and CKD reflects systemic inequities in access to early intervention and preventive primary care within remote communities. The association between DM and invasive bacterial infection has been well described and may occur as a consequence of poor wound healing and functional immunosuppression facilitating angioinvasion of skin commensals such as S. aureus. 24 , 25 , 26 A 2019 consensus reported a five times greater prevalence of DM among First Nations Territorians at 22.7% versus 4.4% in non‐Indigenous Territorians, with this disparity even more pronounced in younger age groups. 27 Community‐focused and culturally sensitive care optimising risk factors and management of non‐communicable diseases, particularly DM, may therefore be a potential strategy to reduce MRSA BSI in this high‐risk population.

Almost a quarter of MRSA BSIs occurred in patients receiving HD with First Nations Australians overrepresented within this group (30% vs 11%). The use of indwelling intravenous catheters and regular contact within the healthcare setting increases the likelihood of acquiring healthcare‐associated commensals such as MRSA and may predispose to increased risk of BSI. Our data additionally demonstrated higher all‐cause 90‐day mortality among all patients receiving HD (Table 2). This likely remains a key population for infection prevention strategies.

First Nations Australians had a significantly shorter length of stay compared with non‐Indigenous patients (14 days vs 28 days), although this did not appear to correlate with poorer outcomes. Several factors may explain this difference. As a younger cohort overall, First Nations patients may have experienced more rapid recovery with less deconditioning, facilitating earlier discharge from the hospital. As previously mentioned, we noted a high rate of HD dependence in this group. Line‐associated infections as a source of MRSA BSI in this population may have been recognised earlier given frequent healthcare interactions and may be amenable to source control procedures. In addition, MRSA BSI antimicrobials can be commenced after HD in dialysis centres, streamlining services required prior to discharge.

The majority of MRSA BSIs were documented as originating from SSTIs. Optimising skin health as a protection barrier against invasive bacteria is paramount to preventing BSI. 28 This is well described in children in the NT, with high rates of skin sores, tinea and scabies leading the dysbiosis of bacteria such as Streptococcus pyogenes and S. aureus, with downstream effects of invasive infections. 29 , 30 In older adults, ageing‐associated skin fragility and breakdown may also predispose to higher rates of SSTIs as a cause of MRSA BSI. 23 MRSA pneumonia was identified as the most likely primary cause of infection in 10% of the study population. This is a surprisingly high value but has been correlated with MRSA skin colonisation and recurrent skin infections. 31

Socio‐economic factors are also likely contributors to the increased incidence of MRSA BSI in First Nations Australians, such as overcrowded housing and lower overall household income leading to health, education and employment inequities. 8 , 32 Of note, in our study, MRSA BSI affects males and females equally among First Nations Australians, in contrast to the male predominance observed in non‐Indigenous populations and reported in prior studies. 33 Female overrepresentation has also been described when observing demographics of populations affected by skin infections such as crusted scabies and Group A Streptococcus in the NT. 29 , 34 In addition, there are high rates of employment within the healthcare, education and social services in women between 25 and 59 years of age in the NT. 35 These roles increase the likelihood of encountering healthcare‐associated skin commensals such as MRSA.

While vancomycin remains the recommended first‐line antimicrobial for MRSA BSI, 9 , 10 our data show higher‐than‐expected rates of vancomycin cutaneous reactions, neutropenia and AKI, with around one in seven patients switching or ceasing therapy early due to adverse effects. For patients in remote settings, the toxicities and complex monitoring requirements of vancomycin create substantial barriers to care, often separating patients from their communities for prolonged periods.

Daptomycin is a well‐established alternative agent to vancomycin in severe MRSA infection 9 , 10 , 36 and was the most commonly used alternative intravenous agent in our cohort. However, daptomycin still requires daily dosing, an indwelling line and close monitoring of blood tests, particularly creatinine kinase. In addition, inactivation by pulmonary surfactant limits the utility of daptomycin in patients with lung involvement, 37 which is particularly relevant in our cohort given the high rates of pneumonia observed. Long‐acting glycopeptides such as dalbavancin are a promising alternative, particularly in our rural population requiring once‐weekly intravenous dosing, which could be administered in the community. 38 Lincosamides have also shown potential as alternative intravenous agents in retrospective studies, although prospective evidence is needed before they can be adopted in routine clinical practice. 12 Oral agents, particularly linezolid and clindamycin, may be acceptable options in this setting. However, numbers of MRSA BSIs in oral switch trials have generally been small, and eligible patients with uncomplicated infection are carefully selected, impacting generalisability. 11 While clindamycin has over 90% oral bioavailability, 39 making it a reasonable step‐down option, there are no completed trials evaluating the non‐inferiority of early oral step‐down to clindamycin in MRSA bacteraemia. In this real‐world cohort, 16% of patients with MRSA BSI received an oral agent. This may reflect growing confidence in oral agents but equally may reflect that in geographically sparse regions like the NT, patients often transfer to tertiary care from extremely remote settings where ongoing access to intravenous infusions may not be feasible. This highlights the need for evidence‐based, well‐tolerated, non‐inferior oral alternatives for treating MRSA BSI to address disparities in access and outcomes in patients from rural and remote settings.

For patients who receive vancomycin, recent dosing recommendations individualised to the patient and MRSA isolate are dependent on reported MIC. However, as in previous studies we noted high discordance between VITEK2 and ETEST MICs, with ETEST averaging significantly higher values, 17 , 40 making accurate AUC/MIC estimation challenging. While this may have clinical implications, with higher MICs reported to correlate with worse outcomes, 41 in this study, neither MIC testing modality correlated with the composite treatment failure outcome or all‐cause 90‐day mortality.

This retrospective cohort study has provided insights into demographics, outcomes, microbiology and real‐world treatment approaches of a large cohort of adult patients with MRSA BSI in the NT. However, the study has several limitations. Data were manually extracted from clinical records and are therefore reasonably complete; however, interpretation of retrospective data points relied on the accuracy of the documenting clinical team and data collectors. We contrasted ETEST and VITEK2 MICs as these are the two commonly performed assays in the NT; however, our findings would have been strengthened through comparison with the gold standard of broth microdilution.

Conclusion

MRSA BSI remains a critical public health issue in the NT, requiring a shift to evidence‐based, well‐tolerated and non‐inferior oral or long‐acting alternatives. These options are essential to improving health equity for residents in rural and remote areas. Urgent prospective research is needed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of these regimens in this unique population and ensure all territorians have access to safe, culturally appropriate infectious disease care.
