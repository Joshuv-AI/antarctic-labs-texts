Inhibitory deficits in Klinefelter syndrome are secondary to deficits in the auditory and motor domain
Authors: René J Huster, Christina Thunberg, Anne-Kristin Solbakk, Claus H Gravholt, Krister Fjermestad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The study does not support inherent inhibitory deficits in Klinefelter syndrome.•Brain and muscle activity point to deficits in auditory and motor domains.•Performance patterns indicate differences in task-component weighting.•Brain and muscle activity did not confirm inhibitory deficits.•Impaired inhibition in KS may stem from auditory and motor domain deficits.Keywords: Klinefelter, XXY, Response Inhibition, attention, Cognition, EEG

Introduction

Klinefelter syndrome (KS; 47,XXY), characterized by a double X aneuploidy in males, is one of the most common chromosomal disorders in humans. KS manifests in a variety of pathophysiological phenomena, including small testes, infertility, or hypogonadism. It is associated with manifold adverse consequences including social, emotional, and behavioral problems, as well as heightened levels of morbidity and mortality (Bojesen & Gravholt, 2011). Lower performance in several cognitive domains is also prevalent in KS males, alongside a heightened risk for psychological challenges (Gravholt et al., 2018).

While general cognitive abilities, reflected in intelligence scores, tend to be lower in KS than in controls, the attenuation in IQ may largely be driven by reduced capabilities in the verbal domain (Gravholt et al., 2018, Leggett et al., 2010). However, whereas verbal deficits are among the most characteristic cognitive features of KS (Gravholt et al., 2018), other cognitive domains exhibit deviations as well. Previous work focusing on executive functions (EF), i.e. those high-order processes that enable goal-directed and adaptive behavior, also indicates impairments. Studies show, for example, lowered working memory capacity and attenuated inhibitory control (Kompus et al., 2011a). Such deficits in EF were also corroborated in our own work. In a broad neuropsychological assessment, we found deficits in basically all tested domains, including processing speed, working memory, set shifting, and inhibition (Fjermestad et al., 2020). Set shifting and inhibition showed the largest decrements, and were further associated with health-relevant sleep patterns.

The reported deficits in inhibitory control are of substantial interest, as inhibitory capabilities are relevant for both mental health and general health-related behavior (Hannah & Aron, 2021). However, the tests used to probe inhibition in KS are not necessarily unambiguous in their interpretation. Most extant research, unfortunately, conflated tests of inhibitory control with language-related capabilities. The most common neuropsychological test of inhibition is the Stroop test, in which participants are asked to name the color a color-word is printed in. Since the semantic information (color-word meaning) and the task-relevant information (print color) can either be the same or incongruent, subtracting the response time of the latter from that of the former condition is usually considered to index inhibitory control. Thus, such language-related features as seen in the Stroop task may be a potential confound in previous studies showing attenuated inhibition in KS men (Fjermestad et al., 2020, Temple and Sanfilippo, 2003). In an alternative approach, Kompus et al. (2011) relied on the forced-left condition of the dichotic listening task. Here, participants are presented with two consonant–vowel syllables simultaneously, one to the left and one to the right ear. When instructed to report the syllable heard best, participants will most often report that presented to the right ear. This right ear advantage is driven by the anatomically stronger representation of the right ear input in the left language-dominant hemisphere (Kimura, 1967). Asking participants to report the stimulus presented to the left ear, the forced left condition, presumably causes a sensory conflict that needs to be resolved via inhibitory mechanisms. As predicted, KS participants exhibited lowered accuracy in this condition compared to male and female controls (Kompus et al., 2011a). However, whereas the dichotic listening task avoids the use of semantically meaningful material, the stimuli are clearly phonological and still related to the processing of language (Asbjørnsen & Helland, 2006).

As far as we know, only one study has probed inhibition in KS without any language-related confound. Van Rijn and Swaab (2015) implemented a Shifting Set Task in which a colored square moves along a horizontal axis and randomly shifts direction. Depending on the square’s color after a change in direction, participants are supposed to either press a button corresponding to the new direction of the square (e.g., left button with leftward movement), or the one opposite to it (right button with leftward movement). The accuracy in response to stimuli of the latter category is used as indicator of inhibition. However, this inhibition-accuracy measure is derived from a block that consists exclusively of “inhibition trials”. Given that “inhibition” is the only response required in those blocks, it is unclear to what degree the accuracy scores could be said to reflect the inhibition of an otherwise prepotent and conflicting response tendency. In sum, it cannot unambiguously be accepted that the performance deficits found in men with KS using these tasks are indeed inhibitory in nature.

Importantly, none of the previous studies utilized the best-accepted approach to study inhibition, namely the Stop Signal Task (SST; Verbruggen et al., 2019). In the SST, participants are asked to respond as fast as possible with a button press to a simple go stimulus (e.g., a green square or a low-frequency tone). Since this go stimulus is usually presented in the majority of trials, e.g. in about 75 %, a strong prepotency of responding is established. In the remainder of trials, the go stimulus is followed by a stop stimulus (e.g., a change in color, or tone frequency relative to the go stimulus), indicating that participants should cancel their initiated response. The time between the presentation of the go and stop stimulus in a stop trial is adapted, with a shortened stop signal delay (SSD) after unsuccessful stop trials, and a prolonged SSD after successful stop trials. This SSD adaptation aims at an overall stopping rate of 50 %. Using this procedure, it is possible to calculate the stop signal reaction time (SSRT), e.g., by subtracting the mean of the SSD from the mean of the go reaction time. The SSRT is interpreted as an estimate of the average time of the stopping process and is by many considered the gold-standard to quantify an individual’s inhibitory capabilities. The SSRT has indicated differences in inhibitory processing in a variety of mental disorders, such as attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder as well as schizophrenia (Lipszyc & Schachar, 2010).

The calculation of the SSRT is based on a formal theory (Logan et al., 1984) that assumes that the go process (representing response execution) and the stop process (representing response inhibition) race for execution. Whichever process wins the race determines whether a response will be successfully inhibited or not. Recent work established an improved SSRT estimate derived from Bayesian modeling (Matzke et al., 2013, 2017). It has been shown that the classic SSRT measure can be biased if participants exhibit a significant number of go or stop-trigger failures, i.e., the corresponding processes are not initiated. And of course, effective inhibitory control not only relies on the speed of the stopping process, but also on the reliability with which it is triggered in the first place. Comparing a classic approach of SSRT calculation to the Bayesian estimate of the SSRT, which accounts for such trigger failures, Weigard et al. (2019) replicated prior findings of prolonged classic SSRTs in children with ADHD. However, the Bayesian SSRT (SSRTB) could not corroborate such differences. Their modeling data rather indicated large group differences in the amount of go and stop-trigger failures. Given the broad difficulties in neuropsychological domains in KS (Fjermestad et al., 2020), controlling for general attentional problems and potential impairments in the ability to trigger the stopping process may be advised in KS as well.

The assumption of independence of the go and stop process, crucial for both the classic and Bayesian SSRT estimates, have been shown to not always hold though (Bissett et al., 2021). Much interest therefore lies in the identification of a direct biomarker of inhibitory control that could be extracted without additional assumptions. Whereas a direct marker for the central control of inhibition has yet to be established, a marker derived from electromyography (EMG), the so-called partial response EMG latency (prEMGlat), may act as an additional parameter to quantify the latency of motor inhibition (Raud et al., 2020, Raud et al., 2022, Raud and Huster, 2017). PrEMGlat is computed as the latency of the peak of muscle activity in the responding effector muscle in successful stop trials; i.e., it originates from the initial muscle energization in response to the go stimulus, which then is cut short by inhibition before proper response execution. Work using prEMGlat reliably indicates a stopping latency of about 150 ms. Not least, prEMGlat also provides further support for the notion that go and stop processes are not independent as required for SSRT estimates (Raud et al., 2022).

Event-related potentials (ERP), i.e. brain activity in response to specific experimental events measured via electroencephalography, can contribute further information on neurocognitive processes in the stop signal task that may be impaired. The N200 and P300, for example, are two ERPs that succeed the occurrence of the stop stimulus (reviewed in Huster et al., 2013). The N200 peaks at about 200 ms and usually exhibits a fronto-centrally pronounced negative deflection on the scalp. It has been associated with the detection and resolution of conflicts as for example seen in competing response tendencies in the stop signal task (to press the response or not; e.g., Botvinick, TICS, 2004). The stop-locked P300 follows the N200 as marked positive peak at the frontal-midline. Whereas its exact cognitive function is still a matter of debate, cross-task interpretations tie the P300 to stimulus evaluation as well as the allocation of attention and control (e.g., Polich 2007). However, studies assessing cognitive processes in KS through electrophysiological markers are rare. Even with respect to the P300, probably the most common EEG marker of attentional processing, only a single study was found applying it in context of KS. Ulas et al. (2006) had participants indicate the detection of an infrequent auditory target stimulus, which elicits a (parietal) P300, and found the P300 amplitude to be reduced in KS compared to controls.

Moreover, individuals diagnosed with KS exhibit differences in sensory processing or motor planning and execution. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging, Wallentin et al. (2016) found that KS patients exhibited heightened brain activity to auditory stimulation and response generation. Interestingly, no behavioral difference in EF was found when comparing KS participants to controls in a visual Stroop task, although the KS group showed relative hypoactivation in secondary visual areas in response to the written stimuli.

In sum, KS seems to be associated with impaired higher cognition as indicated in findings of neuropsychological tests. It is unclear though whether these tests unambiguously indicate inhibitory deficits, or whether such effects rather reflect deficits in supporting processes (e.g., perception and attention). This study therefore sets out to test for deficits of inhibitory control in individuals with KS, using the best-established task and measures for the assessment of inhibition while avoiding potential confounds with language-related processing. To this aim, we implemented a version of the SST with non-linguistic auditory stimuli and further supplemented the classic behavioral performance measures with indices of inhibition and attention derived from EMG, electroencephalography (EEG), and Bayesian modeling. If present, inhibitory deficits in KS would manifest in prolonged SSRTs and prEMG latencies or lowered stopping accuracy. Impairments in the processing of conflicts, stimulus evaluation, or performance monitoring would be associated with attenuated amplitudes of stop-locked ERPs. In addition, we assessed potential differences in auditory processing and response generation, and to what degree these may contribute to differences in EF.

Methods

Participants

Thirty men with KS and 21 controls (C) were recruited for the study through a resource center for rare disorders, the Klinefelter user association, and newspaper adverts (see Fjermestad et al., 2020 for further details on recruitment procedures). Due to insufficient EEG or EMG quality, 11 participants were excluded from the analysis. An additional 7 participants were excluded for having a go accuracy below 65 %, a stop accuracy below 20 %, or longer RTs in unsuccessful stop trials compared to go trials. This left 18 men with KS and 15 controls for further analysis. Table 1 provides an overview of basic demographic variables for KS and C participants. Bayesian tests comparing the two groups did not indicate evidence for differences in the demographic variables (age, years of education, educational status, vocational status).

The study was approved by the Regional committees for medical and health research ethics (#26163). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Travel and accommodation costs due to study participation were covered, and food and snacks were provided during test days. All participants took part in a draw for a universal gift card (1000 NOK).

Task and design

Study design. The study utilized a between-group design where behavioral and electrophysiological data were collected during the performance of an auditory stop-signal task. The data for this study were collected as a part of a larger study on KS. The complete study design included functional and structural magnetic resonance imaging, task- and resting-state EEG and EMG measurements, a neuropsychological assessment, as well as sleep measurements over a two-week period. For this study, only EEG, EMG and behavioral data from the stop-signal task will be reported.

Stop-signal task. The SST was an auditory two-choice SST consisting of 450 trials with a p(stop-signal) = 0.244. We used complex tones with fundamental frequencies of 500, 1000 and 1500 Hz (3 harmonics in total) as stimuli. The assignment of tone frequency to stimulus type (go left, go right, and stop) was counterbalanced across participants. All stimuli were presented at 65 dB.

Each trial started with a 100 ms go stimulus indicating the need for a manual response. In a random subset of trials, a stop stimulus followed the go stimulus after a short delay (stop-signal delay; SSD) indicating that the response should be cancelled. The SSD could vary between 100 and 600 ms and was adjusted based on a tracking algorithm that increased or decreased the SSD by 50 ms following a successful or unsuccessful stop, respectively. These SSD adjustments were collapsed across left- and right-handed responses. The intertrial interval was jittered between 700 and 1200 ms. A black fixation cross was centrally presented on a grey background throughout the experiment.

Prior to the experiment, all participants had to complete a set of training trials to familiarize themselves with the task. They could repeat this training several times and were encouraged to do so if training performance indicated that the task was not understood correctly. All participants were instructed to be both fast and accurate during the experiment, and they were told that they should not slow down to be able to stop every time the stop signal occurred. In addition, participants received brief performance-based feedback after each block. Specifically, they received the feedback ‘Be faster!’ if the mean go-RT of the previous block was above 600 ms, ‘Be more accurate!’ if the average stop accuracy of the previous block was below 40 %, ‘Be faster and be more accurate!’ if go-RTs were too high and the accuracy too low, and ‘Well done!’ if their performance was satisfactory. The feedback was followed by a self-paced pause.

The experiment was conducted using a x64 desktop PC running Windows and a BenQ monitor (model ID: XL2420-B). It was programmed using Psychtoolbox-3 (v. 3.0.14; Kleiner, Brainard, Pelli, 2007) and presented using Matlab (v. R2015a). Auditory stimuli were presented binaurally using in-ear earphones from Etymotic (Etymotic Research, Inc.) and participants responded via a response pad (The BlackBox Toolkit Ltd., UK).

EEG and EMG recordings

We recorded both EEG and EMG activity using the BrainVision Recorder software (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) with 250 Hz (EEG) and 1000 Hz (EMG) online high cutoff filters, a resolution of 0.1 µV (EEG) and 0.5 µV (EMG), and a sampling rate of 5000 Hz. EEG activity was measured using an EasyCap system with 32 active electrodes that covered the whole scalp and were positioned according to the international 10–20 system. The online reference and ground electrode were placed at FCz and AFz, respectively. Surface EMG activity was recorded with bipolar passive (Ag/AgCl) electrodes positioned parallel to the belly of the abductor pollicis brevis of both hands, with a ground electrode placed on each forearm.

Behavioral analyses

We extracted the following behavioral parameters for each subject: reaction times in correct go trials (goRT), reaction times in unsuccessful stop trials (usRT), the stop signal reaction time (SSRT), the stop signal delay (SSD), the percentage of go omissions (goOM), erroneous go responses (goERR), as well as the stopping accuracy (stopACC). Reaction times were computed as the time lapsed between onset of the go signal and the recording of the response. RTs more than 3 SDs away from each participant’s mean were removed as outliers. GoOM and goERR represent the percentage of go trials with no response, or the wrong response (e.g., the left button was pressed although the right one would have been correct), respectively. StopACC denotes the percentage of stop trials in which the response was successfully withheld. SSRTs were calculated using the integration method using full RT distributions with replacement of go omissions (Verbruggen et al., 2019). Here, the RT distribution is integrated with respect to the probability of responding (p(respond|signal); i.e., the probability of unsuccessful stopping) for a given SSD (here the mean SSD). The SSRT is then calculated by subtracting the SSD from the estimated finishing time.

We also used a hierarchical Bayesian parametric approach (“BEESTS”; (Matzke et al., 2013, 2017) to estimate stop-signal and go reaction times (SSRTB and goRTB), their variability (SSRT-SDB and goRT-SDB), as well as the probability of failing to trigger the stop (probTFB) and go (probGFB) processes. We used the BEESTS3-GF model accounting for choice errors, as well as failures to trigger the go and stop processes (Matzke et al., 2017), and ran the estimations using the Dynamic Models of Choice toolbox (v. 190819; Heathcote et al., 2019) in R Studio (v. 1.3.1093). First, we removed trials with improbable and/or outlier reaction times, here defined as RTs < 150 ms and/or more than 3 SDs away from each participant’s mean. We then used mildly informative priors when modelling both participant- and population-level parameters (e.g., (Heathcote et al., 2019, Skippen et al., 2019): all participant-level parameters as well as population-level means were defined as truncated normal distributions with a mean of 500 ms (all mu-parameters), 200 ms (sigma and tau for both go runners), 100 ms (sigma and tau for stop runner), and a standard deviation of 1000 ms. For go and trigger failures, the mean was set to −1.5 probability units, with a standard deviation of 1. For the population-level standard deviations, we used gamma distributions with scale and shape set to 1000 ms (or 1 probability unit for go and trigger failures) for all parameters. We first estimated participant-level parameters using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, running a total of 33 chains, and retaining every 10th sample. After checking that these chains converged, we used the model fits for individual participants as starting points for population-level MCMC sampling, again sampling 3 chains per parameter and retaining every 10th sample. We only allowed migration in the initial burn-in phase, with p(migrate) = 0.05 for both participant- and population-level estimates. Following this, we sampled until the chains were stable, followed by a final sampling of 200 points per chain to be used in the final analysis. Both visual inspection of trace plots and rhat-values < 1.1 indicated that the MCMC chains converged for both participant- and population-level parameters. Plots of posterior relative to prior distributions showed large updates of parameter values, supporting that our priors were largely non-informative. To assess how well the BEESTS-3GF model fit our data, we used the obtained posteriors to predict a total of 1000 new data sets, and then assessed the discrepancy between the predicted and the observed data. These checks indicated a good match between the predicted and the observed data, though the predicted inhibition function was a better match for KS compared to control participants.

EMG analyses

We processed EMG data using custom scripts in MATLAB (The Math Works, Inc., version 2017a). First, the data were low-pass filtered at 250 Hz and high-pass filtered at 20 Hz using a 2nd order Butterworth bandpass filter, and the data were down-sampled to 500 Hz. Then, we extracted EMG time courses for valid go trials, successful and unsuccessful stop trials. All trials were segmented from −200 to 2200 ms relative to the go stimulus, and baseline-corrected by subtracting the mean of the pre-stimulus baseline. Trials with an RT exceeding 3 SDs from the mean, with a mean baseline voltage exceeding 100 µV or where ExG amplifier saturation was detected, were rejected from further analysis. Following this, the average root mean square for each time point was calculated using a moving window with a width of 11 timepoints. The transformed activity was then divided by the mean of the trial-specific pre-stimulus baseline. All trials within a block were concatenated separately for each participant and each hand, and the activity was z-scored across all time points and trial types. The resulting time courses thus reflect a ratio of temporally smoothed EMG activity relative to baseline, z-scored across all trials in a block. EMG bursts were detected on the single-trial level and defined as post-stop-stimulus timepoints with a z-value of at least 1.2. PrEMG burst percentage was quantified as the percentage of successful stop trials in which an EMG burst could be detected. For prEMG peak latency detection, time courses in successful stop trials were re-epoched to segments spanning −200 to 800 ms relative to stop stimulus onset, baseline corrected and averaged for each participant. Peak latency estimates were extracted from these averaged time courses and defined as the latency of the peak amplitude prior to SSRT.

EEG analyses

EEG preprocessing was done in MATLAB using the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme & Makeig, 2004; version 14.1.2). EEG channels were re-referenced to the average reference, low-pass filtered at 200 Hz, down-sampled to 500 Hz, and high-pass filtered at 0.1 Hz. Data recorded during breaks, and pre- and post-experiment were excluded. We ran an independent component analysis (ICA) using the infomax algorithm, and components capturing eye or muscle artifacts were visually identified and rejected. New high-pass and low-pass filters were applied with filter edges of 1 and 35 Hz, respectively. All filtering was performed using the basic FIR-filter implemented in EEGLAB. Epochs ranging from −200 to 800 ms relative to the go and stop stimulus were extracted for valid go trials and successful stop trials, and epochs were baseline corrected to the mean of the pre-stimulus baseline. Go trials with an RT exceeding 3 SDs from each participant’s mean were excluded from the analysis. Trials with voltage values exceeding ±75 µV were considered artefacts and excluded from the data.

Event-related potentials were computed by separately averaging across correct go and successful stop trials. From stop trials, we extracted the peak latencies and amplitudes for the N200 and P300 by identifying the timepoint with lowest/highest amplitude (which provides the peak latency), and then averaging the amplitude values of 5 samples centered at the peak latency (which provides the peak amplitude). Temporal search windows for the local extrema were 100 to 200 ms and 180 to 420 ms for the N200 and P300, respectively.

From go trials, we extracted mean amplitudes for two time windows: 270 to 290 ms (go amp 1) and 330 to 360 ms (go amp 2). These variables aimed to quantify late cognitive processing and response preparation These go- and stop-related ERP-parameters were extracted from electrode FCz, which was expected to correspond to the location that would exhibit peak activity on the scalp for these ERPs.

Further, we extracted the amplitude of early sensory potentials from the go-ERPs of electrodes T7, Cz, and T8, as these electrodes best capture the activity generated in the auditory cortex. We rectified these potentials prior to the peak search to account for the inverted polarities of the central relative to the lateral electrodes. Then, the local maximum was identified in a search window from 80 to 160 ms, and the peak amplitude was computed as the mean of 5 samples centered at this maximum. The initial analysis of sensory ERPs was confined to go trials, because sensory ERPs exhibit a comparably small signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) that necessitates a high number of trials. However, we repeated the extraction of sensory peak latencies and amplitudes for exploratory analyses also on stop-ERPs.

Statistical approach

All variables were tested for deviations from the normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilks test. If significant deviations from normality were found, we tested for group differences in central tendency using Mann-Whitney U Tests, and correspondingly report median (M) and interquartile range (IQR). Variables that did not exhibit deviations from normality were tested using independent samples t-tests. Measures directly testing inhibitory capabilities were tested one-tailed (SSRT, prEMGlat, stopACC, SSRTB), in accordance with the hypothesized inhibitory impairment in KS. All other variables were tested two-tailed. Go-related ERP amplitudes were assessed using Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVAs. All analyses were performed within a Bayesian framework using the JASP software. JASP’s default settings were applied (Version 0.14.1; JASP Team 2020). Bayes factors (BF10) are reported for undirected or directed tests in accordance with the hypotheses and as specified in the results section. BFs are a measure of strength of evidence in favor of one hypothesis relative to another, and thus quantify how plausible each of the competing hypotheses are given the data collected. In the case of BF10, values below 1 support the null hypothesis, and values larger than 1 support the alternative hypothesis. It has further been suggested that the strength of evidence can be assessed as follows: values between 0.33 and 1 or 1–3 are considered inconclusive and only provide anecdotal evidence in either direction. Values between 0.10 and 0.33 or 3–10 are considered substantial, and values smaller than 0.10 or larger than 10 indicate strong evidence for H0 or H1, respectively.

Discussion

This study set out to test for deficits in inhibitory control as previously reported for KS (Fjermestad et al., 2020, Kompus et al., 2011b, Temple and Sanfilippo, 2003, van Rijn and Swaab, 2015). We did so using an auditory stop signal task with simple auditory stimuli devoid of any semantic information, thus relying on the best-established measure of inhibition while avoiding a major confound of previous studies: language-related processing. Notably, we did not rely on a single measure of inhibitory control but compared the classic SSRT to its Bayesian counterpart, and also extracted an EMG-based latency measure of the stopping process (the prEMG peak latency; Raud et al., 2020, Raud and Huster, 2017). Potential differences in early sensory processing or motor preparation, and attentional or performance-monitoring deficits were assessed via go- and stop-locked ERPs, respectively.

Prolonged SSRTs as well as a lowered stopping accuracy initially seemed to support the assumption that inhibitory control is impaired in KS. However, these indicators of inhibitory deficits were flanked by divergences in other behavioral parameters, including shorter goRTs and higher rates of go omissions and go errors. The latter finding is important as it potentially undermines SSRT differences, as the classic SSRT calculation is not independent of go behavior. This is especially true for the finding of a higher rate of go omissions, which may indicate impairments in the initialization of task-relevant processes rather than differences in their latency. The Bayesian modeling approach for the estimation of behavioral performance measures seems to confirm this assumption, as it found increased rates of failures to initiate both go- and stop-processes, while still largely confirming the regular behavioral results including that of prolonged SSRTs. However, the prEMG latency, i.e. the peak latency of residual EMG activity in successful stop trials that quantifies the timing of the deactivation of muscle engagement, did not show evidence for relevant group differences. This is important as the prEMG is the only stopping-latency measures that is fully “empirical”, i.e., it does not rely on model-assumptions and does not rely on a subtraction of manifest variables (e.g., the goRTs and SSDs) to quantify a latent variable (the stopping latency as SSRT).

Similarly, neither the stop-locked N200 nor the P300 exhibited statistically supported differences between groups. These ERPs therefore do not indicate prominent differences tied to cognitive processes such as the detection and resolution of conflicts or the evaluation of stimuli and subsequent allocation of attentional resources. Note that it is even proposed that the P300 links to response inhibition directly (e.g., Wessel and Aron (2015), but see Huster et al., 2022, and Huster et al., 2020, for counterarguments). If interpreting the P300 as an additional marker of inhibition, these results would mirror our prEMG findings and again indicate that the found deficits are not inhibitory in nature.

Go-related ERPs, on the other hand, differed broadly between groups. We analyzed three time frames over different electrode locations to extract indices associated with both early auditory processing (T7, Cz, T8, around 100 ms) and later cognitive processing and response preparation (FCz, 200–400 ms). Go-related activity corresponding to early sensory processing as well as activity preceding response generation between 200 and 300 ms exhibited markedly higher amplitudes in KS than in C participants. Furthermore, an exploratory analysis of associations between go-ERPs and go-related performance measures indicated that heightened sensory processing was associated with faster go response times across all participants as well as within the two sub-groups. In addition, larger go-ERP activity between 200–300 ms was associated with shorter response times in KS, and lower rates of go errors in C participants. Whereas we did not statistically assess the amplitudes of early sensory ERPs in stop trials, because of the lower SNR due to fewer trials, an exploratory inspection of the stop-locked ERPs suggests that the effect of heightened sensory processing in KS is in fact not specific to the go condition, and that it therefore may reflect a general biological or cognitive predisposition of KS men with apparent relevance for cognitive-behavioral performance. Our data thus mirror the findings of Wallentin et al. (2016) that indicated a heightened sensory response to auditory (but not visual) stimuli, as well as increased motor activity.

Both the auditory and the motor domain are known to be affected in men with KS, with impaired hearing, auditory hallucinations, or tremors being commonly reported symptoms. In accordance with this, heightened rates of go-omissions and errors may be caused by perceptual deficiencies, and compensatory strategies may also explain heightened activity in the auditory domain. However, one has to acknowledge that shorter go reaction times are not easily compatible with this notion. A compensatory behavioral strategy, when challenged with sensory impairments, would be to react with increased response caution and information sampling. However, over-compensation in favor of the primary task of the SST, i.e., stimulus discrimination and response generation in go-trials, may cause the observed pattern of behavioral and EEG responses. When challenged with the primary task associated with stimulus identification and response selection and in light of auditory and motor deficiencies, this aspect of the SST may become more salient than the stopping task as such, not least, because the SSD tracking of the latter by default enforces a stopping performance around 50 % across subjects. We therefore conclude that there is no convincing evidence for inhibitory deficits in KS. The prolonged classic and Bayesian SSRTs do not quantify the stopping latency directly but rely on other behavioral variables for their estimation. These variables, as well as the EEG parameters, clearly indicate deficits in KS tied to early sensory and motor processing, which better explain the overall pattern of results.

This study also highlights the importance of not merely relying on a single measure of inhibition, especially not when working with clinical samples. The classic SSRT has long been known to be biased in cases that indicate non-compliance with the independent horse race model (as, for example, evidenced by RTs that are longer for unsuccessful stop trials than go trials). More recent work further suggests that phenomena such as the inability to initiate the stopping or going process may strongly bias classic SSRT estimates. This case may be especially important in clinical samples, when we need to dissociate prolonged latencies of the stopping process from problems in its initiation or more general attentional impairments (e.g., Weigard et al., 2019). Not least, we must keep in mind that the SSRT, unlike the prEMG peak latency, is not a direct measure of inhibitory action, but rather is indirectly inferred by subtracting SSDs from goRTs. As such, the SSRT is prone to effects known to be associated with difference measures, such as lower reliability as well as the inability to easily dissociate whether variability is driven by the latent process (thought to be isolated by the subtraction), or variations in both or either of the originally observed variables.

As most studies, also this one is not without limitations. As is often the case when working with rare disorders, the sample size is relatively small. Further, our focus on cognitive control processes, and the findings made here, ultimately need to be viewed in context of the complexity of symptoms found in KS. Men with KS exhibit altered neuropsychological profiles and sleep patterns, among other physiological and psychological symptoms, which potentially complicate the straightforward interpretation of any isolated finding. This is not least the case also as we lack information regarding the use of medications for the treatment of mental health and sleep-related problems. Larger, internationally coordinated efforts will be necessary though to address these limitations.

In sum, when controlling for potential effects of language-related processes, we cannot unequivocally confirm the existence of deficits in inhibitory control. The overall pattern of results rather seems to suggest that indices of impaired high-level cognition result from low-level deficits in the auditory and motor domains, as well as a differential weighting of the different aspects of the task. Not least, this study highlights the difficulties associated with the interpretation of model-based indices of stopping when accompanied by more widespread basal sensory and motor deficits.
