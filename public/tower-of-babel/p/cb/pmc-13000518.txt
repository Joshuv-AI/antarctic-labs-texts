Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator utilization in coronary artery spasm: A case series of cardiac arrest survivors and a patient with typical angiography
Authors: Wen Gao, Huiyang Li, Yikai Zhao, Nanqing Xiong, Jian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery spasm (CAS) can trigger severe angina pectoris, acute coronary syndrome, or sudden cardiac arrest (SCA). Calcium channel blockers and nitrates are recommended treatments, but the role of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) remains controversial. This study explored ICD application in CAS via a case series of cardiac arrest survivors and a patient with typical angiography findings.Case presentationFour patients with recurrent angina (3 with prior cardiac arrest) were diagnosed with suspected or definite CAS based on predefined criteria and coronary angiography findings: transient ST-segment elevation, nitrate-responsive coronary narrowing, and no identifiable reversible triggers. Three patients with documented ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation (VT/VF) or SCA underwent ICD implantation, while the one without such history did not. During 3.6 ± 1.3 years of follow-up, all experienced recurrent angina (reduced by maximum-tolerated calcium antagonists plus long-acting nitrates). VT/VF events in two ICD recipients were successfully terminated by anti-tachycardia pacing or shocks.ConclusionsThis case series confirms recurrent angina is common in CAS and highlights the efficacy of calcium antagonists and nitrates. ICD implantation may be warranted for CAS patients who survive VT/VF, a strategy supported by the literature.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) remains a major global public health burden, with heterogeneous etiologies and distinct high-risk phenotypes requiring tailored prevention strategies [1]. While atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease accounts for the majority of SCA cases, coronary artery spasm (CAS) represents an underrecognized yet potentially lethal non-obstructive cause of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias and SCA. Within this broader SCA prevention landscape, patients with CAS-related cardiac arrest constitute a small but very high-risk subgroup in whom optimal risk stratification and therapeutic interventions—including the role of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs)—remain incompletely defined.

Although most patients with CAS present with rest-related angina episodes, occasional cases of exercise-induced angina have also been reported [2]. Current guidelines recommend several pharmacotherapies for CAS, including calcium channel blockers and nitrates, but the role of ICD implantation remains controversial [3]. In the present case series, we report four consecutive patients with CAS to illustrate the clinical profile and evaluate the role of ICD therapy, supported by a focused literature review.

Consistent with current guidelines [4], vasospastic angina is classified as definite when spontaneous angina is accompanied by confirmatory angiographic evidence of coronary spasm, and as suspected when nitrate-responsive spontaneous angina is present but diagnostic ECG or angiographic findings are absent or equivocal. In the present case series, Patients 1 and 4 fulfilled criteria for definite vasospastic angina based on angiographically confirmed coronary spasm. Conversely, Patients 2 and 3 were diagnosed as suspected vasospastic angina based on typical nitrate-responsive angina, despite the lack of definitive angiographic spasm.

Patient 1 was a 56-year-old male admitted in February 2023 due to recurrent palpitations and chest tightness for 1 year and one episode of syncope. He first experienced palpitations, chest tightness and profuse sweating without triggers in January 2022. CAG performed on March 31, 2022 revealed only mild stenosis in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and left circumflex artery (LCX), as well as mild-to-moderate stenosis in the right coronary artery (RCA). Despite antiplatelet therapy, his symptoms recurred 1–2 times monthly. On January 11, 2023, he had syncope with cold sweats at resting. Electrocardiogram (ECG) in emergency clinic showed sinus rhythm, third-degree AVB, junctional escape rhythm, intermittent complete left bundle branch block, and lateral lead coved ST-elevation, followed by ventricular fibrillation (VF). Spontaneous circulation was restored after cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Emergency CAG showed 90% luminal narrowing of the RCA, which was relieved by intracoronary nitrates. After stabilization, he was transferred to our hospital in February 2023. Repeat CAG showed no progression of coronary disease and an ICD was implanted on February 13th, 2023. Patient 2 was a 59-year-old male admitted to our center in January 2023. The patient first developed chest pain in May 2018, accompanied by tightness in the back, shoulders, and hands, with sweating that resolved spontaneously afterward. The chest pain disappeared for approximately 2.5 years after he took traditional Chinese medicine and long-acting nitrates in 2019, but recurred from April 2022. On December 19, he suffered sudden cardiac arrest with loss of consciousness during hospitalization for intestinal polyp resection. Local hospital ECG showed inferior lead ST-segment elevation, complete atrioventricular block, and slow junctional escape rhythm. Spontaneous sinus rhythm was restored after 2 min of external chest compression and endotracheal intubation, with ECG ST-segment resolution. Coronary CT angiography (CTA) on December 22 showed non-calcified plaques with mild stenosis in the mid-LAD and mid-distal LCX. Due to his cardiac arrest history and suspected coronary artery spasm, he was transferred to our center for further management and ICD assessment. An ICD was implanted on February 1, 2023 following contraindication exclusion. No recurrent ventricular tachycardia (VT)/VF events were detected during regular remote follow-up monitoring to date.

Patient 3 was a 60-year-old male admitted to the hospital by ambulance in July 2019 due to loss of consciousness during defecation. His family reported generalized cold sweats and limb convulsions. An ECG obtained during transportation demonstrated an escape rhythm and VF. CPR and endotracheal intubation were performed immediately. Spontaneous circulation and respiration were restored after 4 min, with gradual recovery of consciousness. Subsequent ECG showed 0.5–0.75 mm ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF. Similar episodes of chest pain and syncope were previously documented in 2011 and 2018. CAG performed in 2011 revealed no significant coronary stenosis, and repeat CAG during the current admission confirmed no new or progressive stenosis. A 24-hour Holter monitor, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and electroencephalography performed during hospitalization were unremarkable. Given his history of cardiac arrest, an ICD was implanted on July 15, 2019. He experienced an appropriate ICD shock in September 2020, and device interrogation confirmed a VT event (Fig. 1B).

Patient 4 was a 55-year-old female admitted to our hospital due to intermittent chest pain accompanied by cold sweats in March 24, 2021. During chest pain episodes, her ECG showed ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF, with complete resolution of these changes once the chest pain alleviated—findings consistent with myocardial ischemia related to CAS. She underwent emergency CAG, which revealed transient severe narrowing of the RCA lumen; notably, this luminal narrowing was promptly alleviated after intracoronary administration of nitrates (Fig. 2). Post-procedure, the patient was initiated on standard medical therapy for coronary artery disease, supplemented with diltiazem and isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN). During a mean follow up of 3.6 ± 1.3 years (range 2.5 to 5.1 years), all patients experienced recurrent angina episodes. However, the administration of calcium antagonists at the maximum tolerated dose combined with long-acting nitrates resulted in a reduction in angina recurrence across all patients.

Discussion

The present case series illustrates that recurrent angina episodes are common in patients with CAS and highlights the utility of non-dihydropyridine calcium antagonists in reducing such recurrence. ICD placement represents a reasonable consideration for CAS patients following resuscitated SCA or sustained VT/VF. The most typical manifestation of CAS is variant angina, characterized by recurrent angina episodes. Most episodes occur at rest, which is consistent with the clinical presentation of Patients 1, 2, and 4 in this series. However, approximately 25% of CAS patients may experience spasm triggered by exercise or exertion—such as Patient 3, who developed loss of consciousness (secondary to VF) during defecation. Transient ST-segment elevation is typically detectable on ECG during symptomatic episodes. Notably, some patients may present to the hospital with syncope or SCA [5], [6]. In these cases, VT/VF (with or without concurrent brady-arrhythmias) can be identified via ECG or continuous ECG monitoring, as observed in Patients 1 (VF), 2 (complete atrioventricular block with inferior ST-elevation), and 3 (VF during ambulance transport).

Universal diagnostic criteria for CAS have not yet been established. However, the Coronary Vasomotion Disorders International Study Group (COVADIS) has proposed suggested criteria for CAS-related angina, including three key components [4], [7]: (1) typical clinical manifestations, (2) transient ischemic ECG changes during spontaneous episodes and (3) transient coronary artery occlusion accompanied by angina and ischemic ECG changes. While intracoronary acetylcholine (Ach) provocation testing is recognized as the gold standard for CAS diagnosis, suspected CAS could be diagnosed when nitrate-responsive angina is present but diagnostic ECG or angiographic findings are equivocal or unavailable—consistent with the diagnostic approach applied in our case series. Given that CAS is associated with major adverse events, including acute myocardial infarction, ventricular tachyarrhythmias, and even SCA [8], prompt initiation of calcium channel blockers or nitrates is recommended in patients with confirmed or highly suspected CAS [9]. Furthermore, continuous long-term therapy is essential, as discontinuation may lead to catastrophic outcomes (e.g., recurrent VT/VF/SCA) [3], [10], given the recurrent nature of CAS.

As illustrated in the stepwise diagnostic and management flowchart (developed based on the 2022 ESC Guidelines [11]) (Fig. 3), ICD implantation is recommended for CAS patients complicated by SCA/VT/VF. While some scholars argue that recurrent cardiac arrest or syncope due to ventricular tachyarrhythmias is unlikely after a single episode of cardiac arrest [3], others have reported that even under optimal vasodilator therapy with maximally tolerated calcium channel blockers and long-acting nitrates, a small subset of high-risk CAS patients still experiences recurrent malignant ventricular arrhythmias [12], [13]. This observation further supports the need for individualized risk assessment and selective ICD implantation in this specific high-risk subgroup. In line with these reports, 2 out of 3 ICD recipients in our study (Patients 1 and 3) experienced recurrent VT/VF events, which were successfully terminated by ATP or appropriate ICD shocks. Notably, Patient 4—who presented with typical CAS-related angina and transient ECG changes but never experienced malignant arrhythmias (i.e., VT/VF or cardiac arrest)—did not undergo ICD implantation, which aligns with the guideline principle that ICD is not indicated for CAS patients without prior life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias or cardiac arrest.Fig. 3Stepwise diagnostic and management flowchart for patients with coronary artery spasm (CAS). To further contextualize our findings and place them within the existing evidence base, we reviewed relevant literature, summarized in Table 2. Among 13 CAS patients with ICD implantation included in the review, 8 received appropriate ICD shocks during follow-up (range: 1–120 months), confirming the long-term value of ICDs in preventing SCA in high-risk CAS patients. Notably, cases without appropriate ICD interventions were largely limited to those with very short follow-up durations of only 1–2 months, as reported by Serena et al. [14] and Adriana et al. [5]. In the case described by He et al. [15], coronary vasospasm was secondary to extrinsic compression from a myocardial bridge and occlusion of the left internal mammary artery, representing a distinct mechanistic subtype. Nakamura et al. [16] reported a large case series of 349 patients with vasospastic angina, 54% of whom had coronary stenosis ≥50%; however, ICD therapy was neither used nor discussed in that early investigation. Tateishi et al. [17] demonstrated that long-term outcomes of secondary prevention with ICD were comparable between patients with CAS and those with other cardiac causes of SCA, supporting the benefit of ICD in this high-risk population. Against this background of existing literature, our small case series, while limited in size, provides complementary, contemporary real-world evidence with several unique strengths: 1. Our cohort consists of severely affected, contemporary CAS patients treated with maximally tolerated modern vasodilator therapy, with long-term follow-up (3.6 ± 1.3 years), offering updated insights into arrhythmia recurrence and ICD effectiveness in the current era of optimized medical management. 2. We provide detailed ICD therapy data (anti-tachycardia pacing vs. shock delivery for recurrent VT/VF), which remains underreported in larger cohorts and offers practical guidance for electrophysiologists. 3. Our single-center, longitudinal design enables granular clinical characterization, including detailed angina recurrence, medication adherence, and serial angiographic features—details often diluted in large population-based studies. Given the substantial risk of recurrent arrhythmias, regular follow-up and remote monitoring are strongly recommended in this population. Building on these observations, we acknowledge the limitations of our study to ensure the conclusions are interpreted appropriately. Given the small sample size and retrospective single-center design, the present findings are hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, and further prospective studies with larger cohorts are therefore warranted to validate our observations. Notably, ICD implantation in our study cohort was driven by a history of prior life-threatening arrhythmic events; thus, no inferences can be drawn regarding the incremental clinical benefit of ICD therapy over optimal medical treatment alone in this patient population. Furthermore, our study included only patients with severe CAS phenotypes—characterized by recurrent refractory angina or prior SCA/VT/VF—so our findings cannot be generalized to the broader CAS population. As CAS represents a heterogeneous entity with variable triggers, recurrence rates, and therapeutic responses, individualized risk stratification is essential when formulating management strategies for CAS patients in clinical practice.

Conclusion

CAS can cause variant angina and concomitant SCA. Medications such as non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers or nitrates are effective in reducing angina recurrence and should be administered long-term. Our findings support consideration of ICD therapy in CAS patients with prior SCA or documented VT/VF.
