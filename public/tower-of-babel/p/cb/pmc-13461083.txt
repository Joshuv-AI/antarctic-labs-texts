Effects of prophylactic tranexamic acid on bleeding during transbronchial lung biopsy: a double-blind, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Marzieh Rostami, Ali Dabbagh, Alireza JolousJamshidi, Alireza Shakeri, Kamal Fani, Marzie Shahrabi, Hamidreza Samadpour, Seyed Mohammad Seyed-Alshohadaei, Maede Karimian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This randomized controlled trial evaluated the efficacy of tranexamic acid in preventing bleeding in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy.Materials and methods:This study was a double-blind, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial. Adult patients referred for flexible bronchoscopy with a planned transbronchial lung biopsy during 2024 were included in the current study. Participants in the intervention group received 10 mg/kg of intravenous tranexamic acid in normal saline, administered 20 minutes prior to the procedure. Those in the placebo group received an equal volume of normal saline. We assessed the need for bleeding control interventions, such as epinephrine or ice saline use, the incidence of tachycardia or hypotension, uncontrolled bleeding, and procedure length. We also measured the hemoglobin levels before and after the procedure.Results:In total, 60 patients – 32 (53.3%) in the tranexamic acid group and 28 (46.7%) in the control group – were enrolled in the study. The percentage of females (53.6% vs. 28.1%) and the mean age of participants (66.5 vs. 42.9 years) were significantly higher in the control group compared to the tranexamic acid group (P < 0.05). After adjusting for age and gender, epinephrine use (18.8% vs. 92.9%) and the procedure length (median of 8 vs. 13 minutes) were lower in the tranexamic acid group compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Additionally, tranexamic acid had a statistically significant effect on post-operative hemoglobin levels (F = 14.08, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.207).Conclusions:Tranexamic acid can be effective in preventing bleeding in individuals undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy and in enhancing procedural speed.

Introduction

Transbronchial lung biopsy, performed using a flexible fibreoptic bronchoscope, is a diagnostic procedure primarily used for the evaluation of lung lesions, particularly those located in the peripheral regions of the lung[1–3]. Although open lung biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing lung lesions and diseases, its invasive nature has led to the growing use of less invasive modalities, such as transbronchial lung biopsy, which have gained popularity in recent years for the diagnosis of lung diseases[4–6]. The accuracy of transbronchial lung biopsy has been well established in the literature, with an estimated sensitivity and specificity of about 90% for the diagnosis of lung diseases[7]. Although transbronchial lung biopsy is generally considered a safe procedure, with complications occurring in fewer than 2% of cases, it still carries certain risks, with approximately 1% of patients developing pneumothorax and about 0.6% experiencing hemorrhage[8,9]. However, the complication rate associated with transbronchial lung biopsy varies across studies and institutions, with reports indicating that 0.3% to 2.8% of patients undergoing this procedure may experience bleeding[10]. Although the incidence of hemorrhage following transbronchial lung biopsy is low, and most cases are mild with only a small fraction resulting in massive bleeding, the occurrence of bleeding can still complicate the procedure and present significant challenges[8,11]. This issue might be more prominent in patients with liver and kidney disease, who are at a higher risk of bleeding[9,12]. Bleeding following transbronchial lung biopsy may result in inadequate tissue specimens, prolongation of the procedure, or even abandonment of the procedure[13]. Although rare, in cases of severe bleeding, it can even lead to hemodynamic instability[14]. Therefore, there is a need for interventions for effective prevention and management of bleeding in people undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy to avoid its consequences.

Several approaches have been suggested for the management and prevention of bleeding in people undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy. Various methods, such as prophylactic intubation, balloon placement, and pre-procedural transfusion of blood products, have been employed to prevent bleeding in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy[10]. On the other hand, the management of bleeding during or following the procedure is performed according to its severity[11]. In cases of mild bleeding, suctioning, local epinephrine, and oxygen therapy can effectively manage the bleeding[11]. Conversely, in cases of severe bleeding, other approaches, such as the placement of a Fogarty balloon catheter and a rigid bronchoscope through a rigid bronchoscope, have been recommended[11]. Additional methods, including bronchoscopic tamponade, surgical management, blood transfusion, and ice saline, have also been utilized for the management of bleeding following transbronchial lung biopsy[14,15].

Tranexamic acid is one of the novel approaches that has been utilized for the prevention of bleeding in people undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy[13]. Tranexamic acid inhibits fibrinolysis by binding to the lysine sites on plasminogen and preventing its interaction with fibrin, and consequently prevents bleeding[16]. The effects of tranexamic acid on bleeding prevention in various respiratory interventions, such as pulmonary resection, as well as in the treatment of hemoptysis, have recently been studied and have shown promising results[17]. In a recent randomized controlled trial, it was shown that tranexamic acid was more effective than a placebo in reducing bleeding in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy[13]. However, no other studies have examined the effects of tranexamic acid in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy, and further research is needed to determine its impact on bleeding in this population. Therefore, given the scarcity of evidence on the efficacy of tranexamic acid in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy, we conducted a randomized controlled trial in Iran to evaluate its effectiveness in this setting.

Methods

This study was reported according to the CONSORT 2025 and Transparency in the Reporting of Artificial Intelligence (TITAN) guidelines[18,19]. It was registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) with the registration ID IRCT20230204057318N6. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of tranexamic acid on hemoglobin levels in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy. The secondary objective was to assess the effects of tranexamic acid on procedure complications and characteristics in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy.

Design & setting

This study was a double-blind, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial conducted in 2024. It was carried out at the Shahid Madani Educational Medical Center on patients who underwent elective or emergency flexible bronchoscopy with transbronchial lung biopsy. The Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences approved the study protocol (ethics code: IR.SBMU.RETECH.REC.1403.327).

Participants & eligibility criteria

Adult patients referred for flexible bronchoscopy with a planned transbronchial lung biopsy during 2024 were included in the current study. Those with bleeding disorders, those at risk of thromboembolism, those who were younger than 18 years, people with liver or renal failure, and those who didn’t provide consent to be included in the study were excluded. We described the study’s aims and objectives to the patients and obtained informed consent from the participants before enrolling them in the study.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7[20,21]. Considering p1 = 0.53, p2 = 0.87, α = 0.05, and power = 0.8, the required sample size was 32 participants per group[22]. However, due to dropouts, 28 participants were included in the analysis for the control group.

Intervention & procedure

Participants in the intervention group received 10 mg/kg of intravenous tranexamic acid in normal saline, administered 20 minutes prior to the procedure. Those in the placebo group received an equal volume of normal saline. Infusion protocols were identical in both groups with respect to volume and rate. The same team performed the transbronchial lung biopsy for both groups, and 4 or 5 biopsy samples were obtained for each patient according to the protocol.

Outcomes

At enrollment, participants’ age and gender were recorded. During the procedure, we documented the need for bleeding control interventions, including the use of epinephrine or ice saline. The administration of epinephrine and ice saline was decided through a multidisciplinary clinical judgment process involving an anesthesiologist and a pulmonary medicine specialist. The protocol at our center involves a stepwise approach to bleeding control. Initially, normal saline is used. If bleeding is not adequately controlled, normal saline is combined with epinephrine. In cases where bleeding persists despite these measures, ice saline is administered as a final step to achieve hemostasis.

Vital signs were continuously monitored during the procedure, and episodes of tachycardia or hypotension were recorded. Tachycardia was defined as a heart rate greater than 100 beats per minute, and hypotension as a systolic blood pressure below 80 mmHg[23,24]. We double-checked the post-procedure vital signs against the procedure records to ensure their accuracy. Furthermore, we recorded procedure length and the incidence of any bleeding that remained uncontrolled during the procedure. Uncontrolled bleeding was determined based on clinical presentation, field visibility, and suction findings. If there were no clinical signs of bleeding (e.g., hemoptysis), the visual field remained clear, and no gross blood was observed in the suction sample, the patient was considered not to have uncontrolled bleeding.

Blood samples were obtained from the participants to measure the hemoglobin levels before and after the intervention.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention or control group using a simple randomization method with a random number generator. The allocation sequence was generated independently and concealed in numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. A staff member allocated the envelopes to the participants in the order they were enrolled in the study, and after enrollment, the corresponding envelope was opened by a study nurse who was not involved in outcome assessment. Study solutions (tranexamic acid or normal saline) were prepared in identical, unlabeled containers to ensure allocation concealment.

Blinding

Participants, bronchoscopists, and outcome assessors were blinded to the intervention. The administration of the tranexamic acid or normal saline was performed by independent staff. The pharmacist, who was involved in the preparation of the medications, and the Data and Safety Monitoring Board were unblinded.

Statistical analysis

We reported the mean and standard deviation (SD) for the continuous variables, and frequency and percentage for the categorical ones. We used the chi-square test to compare the categorical variables between groups. We used the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. If they were normally distributed, we used the independent t-test to compare the groups; otherwise, we used the Mann–Whitney test. As there were differences between the groups with regard to gender distribution and age, we used binary logistic and linear regression models, adjusted for age and gender, to evaluate the association between the intervention and outcomes. In other words, age, gender, and intervention group were the independent variables, and the outcomes were the dependent variables, with a separate model fitted for each outcome. Due to the small sample size, assumptions of regression could not be evaluated. Furthermore, a one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to evaluate the effect of the intervention on post-operative hemoglobin levels, adjusting for baseline hemoglobin, age, and gender. We used SPSS version 26 for the analyses. We considered P < 0.05 statistically significant.

Results

In total, 60 patients, 32 (53.3%) in the tranexamic acid group and 28 (46.7%) in the control group, were enrolled in the study (Fig. 1). Nine participants in the tranexamic acid group (28.1%) and 15 in the control group (53.6%) were female, and there was a significant difference in the gender distribution between the groups (P = 0.045). The mean age of participants was 66.5 years (SD = 7.9) in the control group and 42.9 years (SD = 8.5) in the tranexamic acid group, and there was a significant difference between the groups in this regard (P < 0.001). Before the intervention, the hemoglobin level was significantly (P < 0.001) higher in the tranexamic acid group (mean = 14.0 g/dl, SD = 0.7) compared to the control group (mean = 12.3 g/dl, SD = 0.4). Similarly, after the intervention, the hemoglobin level remained significantly (P < 0.001) higher in the tranexamic acid group (mean = 13.6 g/dl, SD = 0.7) compared to the control group (mean = 11.5 g/dl, SD = 0.5). The hemoglobin levels decreased by 2.8% (SD = 1.4) in the tranexamic acid group and 6.2% (SD = 1.4) in the control group, and there was a significant difference between groups in this regard (P < 0.001). After adjusting for baseline hemoglobin, age, and gender, there was a statistically significant effect of the intervention on post-operative hemoglobin levels (F = 14.08, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.207).

Furthermore, procedure length was significantly shorter in the tranexamic acid group (median =8 minutes, IQR = 3) compared to the control group (median =13 minutes, IQR = 2) (P < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of tranexamic acid in preventing bleeding and related procedural complications in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy. Our findings showed that patients who received tranexamic acid experienced less reduction in hemoglobin levels, required fewer bleeding control interventions, and had a lower rate of related procedural complications. After adjusting for confounders, the need for epinephrine to control bleeding remained significantly lower in the intervention group compared with the placebo group. Moreover, the procedure time was significantly shorter in the intervention group, and this difference persisted even after adjusting for participants’ age and gender.

Although the frequency of bleeding control interventions, such as ice saline and epinephrine use, and related complications, including hypotension and tachycardia, were significantly lower in the tranexamic acid group compared with the control group, these differences may largely be attributable to differences in participants’ age and gender distribution across the two groups rather than the direct effects of tranexamic acid itself. Studies have shown that individuals over the age of 60 years have a higher risk of bleeding and hemorrhagic events compared with younger adults[25]. In addition, many older adults are prescribed medications, such as aspirin, which may further increase their bleeding risk[26,27]. Older adults are also at a higher risk of procedure-related complications, such as hypotension[28]. Therefore, such differences in the participants’ age and gender distribution might contribute to the differences observed between groups. The only exception was the epinephrine use for bleeding control, which was significantly lower in the tranexamic acid group compared with the control group. This finding adds to the existing literature, including a study by Kuint et al, which reported that the severity of bleeding in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy was significantly lower among those who received prophylactic tranexamic acid. However, in their study, no significant differences were observed between groups in terms of ice saline or epinephrine use, nor in complications such as hypotension and tachycardia[13]. Taken together, the findings from these two studies highlight the need for future well-designed controlled trials, as they both have limitations, such as small sample size, and our study groups were also different in terms of baseline characteristics. Nonetheless, the evidence suggests that prophylactic tranexamic acid may not only reduce the severity of bleeding in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy but also have clinical significance by lowering the need for bleeding control interventions, which may indicate a reduced rate of clinically significant bleeding in this population.

Another significant finding in our study was the lower procedure time in people who had received tranexamic acid compared to the control group, even after adjusting for age and gender. This factor is another point that adds to the clinical significance of tranexamic acid in preventing bleeding in this group. Bleeding during transbronchial lung biopsy can not only prolong the procedure but may also compromise sample adequacy, thereby affecting diagnostic accuracy[1,17]. Therefore, by preventing bleeding, as reflected in the smaller reduction in hemoglobin levels in our study, tranexamic acid may both shorten procedure time and improve its quality. However, it should be noted that in the Kuint et al study, there was no significant difference between groups regarding procedure time[13], and one might not confidently claim that tranexamic acid reduces procedure time. Nevertheless, considering the promising findings of our study, this is something that warrants further investigation in the future.

Despite the novel aspects of this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, despite our efforts to ensure allocation concealment and randomization, significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in baseline characteristics, including age, gender, and hemoglobin levels, which may have introduced bias. Although we attempted to minimize the impact of these confounders by adjusting the findings for age and gender, their potential influence cannot be overlooked. Therefore, further well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to assess the safety and efficacy of tranexamic acid in preventing bleeding in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy. Second, although we evaluated the effect of intravenous tranexamic acid in preventing bleeding among patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy, the findings may not be generalizable to other routes of administration, such as nebulized tranexamic acid. Future studies are needed to compare the efficacy of different administration methods in preventing bleeding in this patient population. Third, this study focused on the efficacy of tranexamic acid for the prevention of bleeding, and we did not evaluate its safety. Therefore, its safety in people undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy needs to be further assessed in future studies. Furthermore, our study focused solely on the effects of tranexamic acid on bleeding and procedural characteristics among participants, without evaluating other aspects such as the success or quality of the procedure. Therefore, future, more comprehensive studies would be helpful in further elucidating the effects of tranexamic acid in patients undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy. Fourth, our study had a relatively small sample size, was limited to a single center, and excluded high-risk patients, which might impact the findings of the current study and limit its generalizability. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes will be beneficial in this regard to determine the efficacy of tranexamic acid with more statistical power. Finally, some confounders, such as the existing comorbidities, medications used at the time of the study, and the reasons for undergoing transbronchial lung biopsy, were not evaluated in the current study, which might confound the findings.

Conclusions

Despite the differences in baseline characteristics between groups as the main limitation of our study, our findings suggest that tranexamic acid may decrease bleeding, reduce the need for epinephrine, and shorten procedure time, all of which could help improve sample quality and decrease patient-related complications. Future multicenter randomized trials with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the findings of our study.
