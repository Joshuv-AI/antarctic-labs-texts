Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling of Encorafenib in Healthy Participants and Patients with BRAF V600-Mutant Solid Tumors: A Semi-mechanistic Autoinduction Model
Authors: Derek Z Yang, Erik M Hahn, Joseph Piscitelli, Yazdi K Pithavala, Jennifer E Hibma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveEncorafenib is a potent and selective adenosine triphosphate-competitive BRAF V600-mutant kinase inhibitor. Encorafenib is approved for multiple indications in combination with binimetinib or cetuximab. This analysis aimed to develop a global population pharmacokinetics (popPK) model characterizing encorafenib disposition across tumor types.MethodsThe popPK analysis was based on nine phase 1 to 3 studies in participants with melanoma, colorectal cancer (CRC), non-small cell lung cancer, or other solid tumors, and healthy participants. A total of 1310 participants who received encorafenib as monotherapy or in combination were included. Nonlinear mixed effects modeling was performed using NONMEM v7.5.0. A semi-mechanistic enzyme turnover model was assessed to quantify the autoinduction effect. Stepwise covariate modeling using Perl-speaks-NONMEM (version 5.3.0) evaluated a broad range of covariates.ResultsA two-compartment model with first-order absorption and concentration-dependent autoinduction successfully characterized the concentration-time profile of encorafenib. Encorafenib apparent clearance (CL/F) was estimated to be 12.2 L/h after the first dose in a typical adult and increased by 186% to 35 L/h at steady state. This model indicates that maximum autoinduction is expected within 14 days of daily dosing (autoinduction half-life 64 h). Age and tumor type (melanoma, metastatic CRC, other [healthy, lung tumors, other solid tumors]) on CL/F and body weight on volume of distribution were significant covariates; these effects were not determined to be clinically significant.ConclusionsThis model successfully described the PK of encorafenib over time and across tumor types. No dose modifications are suggested on the basis of intrinsic or extrinsic factors evaluated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-025-01608-y.

Key Points

Encorafenib is a potent and selective adenosine triphosphate-competitive inhibitor of BRAF V600-mutant kinase that is approved for the treatment of melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer in combination with binimetinib and treatment of colorectal cancer in combination with cetuximab.The objectives of this analysis were to develop a global population pharmacokinetics (popPK) model for encorafenib using data from nine phase 1, 2, and 3 clinical studies across multiple tumor types (and including healthy participants) and to assess the impact of covariates on the disposition of encorafenib.The encorafenib popPK model, which incorporated a semi-mechanistic enzyme turnover model with concentration-dependent net-autoinduction through an increased enzyme production rate, successfully described the PK of encorafenib. No dose modifications are suggested on the basis of the intrinsic or extrinsic factors evaluated in this analysis.

Introduction

Encorafenib is a potent and selective adenosine triphosphate-competitive inhibitor of B-RAF proto-oncogene, serine/threonine-protein kinase (BRAF) V600-mutant kinase that suppresses the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway in tumor cells expressing several mutated forms of BRAF kinase, including V600E/D/K. Encorafenib has received marketing approvals for the treatment of unresectable or metastatic BRAF-mutant melanoma and metastatic BRAF-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in combination with binimetinib, and metastatic BRAF-mutant colorectal cancer (CRC) in combination with cetuximab and mFOLFOX6 in any line or in combination with cetuximab after prior therapy. The recommended dose of encorafenib is 450 mg once daily in combination with binimetinib for the treatment of melanoma and NSCLC and 300 mg daily for the treatment of CRC in combination with cetuximab with or without mFOLFOX6 [1, 2].

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of encorafenib was studied in healthy participants and patients with solid tumors during clinical development. Encorafenib demonstrated dose-proportional PK over the dose range of 50–700 mg after a single dose. After multiple doses, encorafenib exposure was less than dose proportional over the dose range of 50–800 mg. After oral administration, encorafenib is almost completely absorbed (86% of the dose), with peak concentrations achieved within 2 h. The primary metabolism pathway for encorafenib is via CYP3A4 (83%) and to a lesser extent via CYP2C19 (16%) and CYP2D6 (1%) [1, 3]. Encorafenib is a substrate and time-dependent inhibitor of CYP3A4, as well as a strong CYP3A4 inducer at steady state, hence exhibiting net CYP3A autoinduction after multiple dosing. After daily dosing, systematic plasma exposures of encorafenib were less than following single-dose administration, with steady state plasma exposure being 50% lower compared with exposure observed on day 1 [1, 4].

Prior encorafenib modeling was conducted to assess the sources of variability and predict exposures of encorafenib for subsequent analysis during the New Drug Application (NDA) submission [5] and the Supplemental NDA submissions. In the prior encorafenib model, the PK of encorafenib was described by a two-compartment model with first-order absorption, incorporating a time–Emax function to describe the observed time-dependent clearance. The objectives of this analysis were to develop a semi-mechanistic global popPK model for encorafenib using pooled data from nine phase 1, 2, and 3 clinical studies across tumor types (melanoma, CRC, NSCLC, other solid tumors, and healthy participants) and to assess the impact of covariates on the disposition of encorafenib.

Methods

Study Populations and Clinical Data

This analysis included data from nine phase 1, 2, and 3 studies: one study in healthy participants (ARRAY-162-105, part 1 only), four studies in participants with melanoma (COLUMBUS [NCT01909453], POLARIS [NCT03911869], C4221010 [(NCT01436656], LOGIC 2 [NCT02159066] studies], two studies in participants with metastatic CRC (C4221001 [NCT01719380] and BEACON [NCT02928224] studies), one study in participants with NSCLC (PHAROS study [NCT03915951]), and one study in participants with other solid tumors [C4221005 (NCT01543698)] [4, 6–13]. Supplementary Table S1 provides an overview of these nine clinical studies, including their dosing schedules and plasma samplings. The majority of the concentration data included in this popPK analysis were from patients who were dosed on the basis of the approved labeling [1]. In several phase 1/2 dose escalation studies, such as Studies C4221010 and C4221001, a wide range of encorafenib daily doses from 50–700 mg were evaluated (Supplementary Table S1). All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council of Harmonization Guideline for Good Clinical Practice and were approved by an institutional review board or ethics committee at each site. All participants provided written informed consent for study-related treatments and procedures.

Software

Analyses were performed using nonlinear mixed effects modeling methodology as implemented in Nonlinear Mixed-Effects Modeling (NONMEM) version 7.5.0 (ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD). Perl-speaks-NONMEM version 5.3.0 was used for conducting predictive checks and covariate testing. R software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for graphics, other statistical analyses, diagnostics, and data manipulation. The first-order conditional estimation method with interaction was used for the base model. However, the SAEM-IMP algorithm was used for the final model, which combines the stochastic approximation expectation-maximization (SAEM) framework with an importance sampling (IMP) step to improve precision in the likelihood approximation and enable reliable estimation of parameter uncertainty. For SAEM-IMP estimation with Mu-referencing, random effect variances estimated to be near zero were fixed to a small positive value (0.025) to maintain numeric stability of the stochastic approximation.

Base PopPK Model

Several PK models and structural approaches were evaluated to characterize the PK profile of encorafenib. These included a one-compartment model, a two-compartment model, and models incorporating transit absorption compartments. A base two-compartment model with linear elimination and first order-absorption was developed, which is in agreement with the prior encorafenib PK model [5]. Unlike the prior encorafenib popPK model (with time varying apparent clearance [CL/F]) developed to support the NDA, an encorafenib concentration-dependent autoinduction effect was incorporated in this global PK model instead of time–Emax. The autoinduction process in this model was described using an indirect-response, semi-mechanistic enzyme turnover model previously described in several publications [14–16]. Evaluation of Covariates and Full Model Development

Covariates evaluated in this analysis were based on clinically relevant parameters and known information from prior encorafenib modeling experiences [5]. These covariates included demographic characteristics (age, body weight, sex), baseline laboratory values (total bilirubin, total protein, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], lactate dehydrogenase [LDH]), baseline disease characteristics (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status [ECOG PS], tumor type), concomitant medication use (absence or weak, strong CYP3A inhibitors or inducers), and treatment type (monotherapy versus combination with binimetinib versus other combinations). Covariates were graphically plotted against individual ηs to identify any relationships. Covariates were screened for pairwise correlation and, if strong correlation existed, only one was selected for evaluation based on physiological or clinical relevance and graphical assessment. Demographic characteristics, baseline laboratory values, baseline disease characteristics, concomitant medication use, and treatment type were tested on the CL/F term. Demographic characteristics were tested on Vc/F and Ka.

The significance of covariates was tested using a stepwise covariate model building procedure (SCM). Categorical covariates were included by a linear model. Continuous covariates were included by a power model centered on the median value of the population in the analysis. The addition of an individual covariate parameter was performed at a prespecified significance level of α = 0.05 with the likelihood ratio test (LRT) to assess the significance of a covariate or covariates in the model in a stepwise fashion. The model was then subjected to a backward elimination algorithm, using LRT to assess the significance of a covariate or covariates in the model when eliminated from the full model. The test for elimination of an individual covariate parameter, given that others are kept in the model, was performed at a prespecified significance level of α = 0.001. Covariates were removed from the full model in a stepwise fashion, and the change in objective function value (OFV) was calculated.

Final popPK Model

The final model was obtained from the last stage of the elimination algorithm in the SCM, in which all of the remaining covariate parameters, when tested one at a time, resulted in a statistically significant result for the LRT (i.e., p < 0.001). To obtain the most parsimonious and stable final model, the candidate covariate model resulting from the backward elimination step in the SCM was subjected to a separate NONMEM run with $COV to examine any sign of model over parameterization (e.g. condition number) and poorly estimated parameters.

Assessment of Model Adequacy and Predictive Performance

At all stages of model development, assessment of model adequacy was conducted through multiple approaches. Goodness of fit (GOF) for different models was evaluated on the basis of the change in OFV, condition number, visual inspection of different diagnostic plots, precision of the parameter estimates, and decreases in inter-individual variability and residual variability. Standard diagnostic GOF plots included population predictions (PRED) and individual predictions (IPRED) versus dependent variable, individual weighted residuals (IWRES), conditional weighted residuals (CWRES); normalized prediction distribution error (NPDE) versus time; IWRES, CWRES, and NPDE versus PRED, ETAs versus continuous covariates, ETAs versus categorical covariates (box plots), density plots of ETAs, and ETA correlation plot. Confidence intervals (95% CI) around the parameter estimates were generated for standard errors generated on the basis of asymptotic standard errors from the NONMEM covariance step. Both η-shrinkage (1 − standard deviation [ηEBE]/ω) and ε-shrinkage (1 − standard deviation [IWRES]) were evaluated to assess the validity of using post hoc individual parameter estimates for model diagnosis [17]. The performance of the final model was evaluated by simulating data using the parameter estimates from the final model (fixed and random effects) and by conducting a prediction-corrected visual predictive check (VPC).

Missing Data and Imputations

All patients with at least one post-dose encorafenib plasma concentration value were included in the analysis. Plasma concentration values that are below the limit of quantification represented < 10% of the dataset and were excluded from the analysis using the M1 method [18, 19]. Baseline continuous covariate values that had < 10% of values (body weight, eGFR, total protein, albumin, bilirubin, AST, ALT, and LDH) were imputed with the population median value. Categorical covariates with < 10% missing values (ECOGPS) were assigned to the most common value. Race was not evaluated since 90% of participants were White (Table 1) and covariate assessment was not considered meaningful. Similarly, 99% of participants had either absence of use or concomitant administration of only a weak CYP3A inducer (Table 1); thus, concomitant CYP3A inducer category was not evaluated. Concomitant CYP3A inhibitor was evaluated as absence or weak CYP3A inhibitors versus use of concomitant moderate or strong CYP3A inhibitors. Tumor type was evaluated as melanoma versus CRC versus all other tumor types, which included healthy (1%), lung (7%), and other (5%).

Results

Data Summary

A total of 1310 participants from nine phase 1, 2, and 3 studies received encorafenib as monotherapy or in combination with binimetinib or other agents. A summary of all study information is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, 716 (55%) participants were male, 1178 (90%) participants were White, the median (range) baseline age was 58 years old (19–94 years), and the median (range) baseline body weight was 76 kg (34–168 kg) (Table 1). Excluding 263 values that were below the limit of quantification or missing PK values (from 11 participants), there were a total of 8651 observations from 1299 participants evaluated in this popPK analysis. The concentration-time profiles by dose levels for each study are shown in Fig. 2. Summary statistics for baseline demographic characteristics and covariates are presented in Table 1.

Model Development

A two-compartment base model with first-order absorption was found to be the most appropriate model to describe the popPK of encorafenib, in agreement with the prior modeling experience [5]. In addition, an encorafenib concentration-dependent Emax function was successfully incorporated to describe the time-varying autoinduction effect of encorafenib.

In the SCM analysis, demographic characteristics (age, body weight, sex), baseline laboratory values (total bilirubin, total protein, albumin, AST, ALT, LDH), baseline disease characteristics (ECOG PS, tumor type), concomitant medication use (absence or weak, strong CYP3A inhibitor or inducer), and treatment type (monotherapy versus combination with binimetinib versus other combinations) were tested on the CL/F term. Demographic characteristics (age, body weight, sex) were tested on Vc/F and Ka. The full model was reached in three forward addition steps, which included the effect of age and tumor type (metastatic CRC and other tumor vs melanoma [reference]) on CL/F, and baseline body weight on Vc/F. All three covariates met the significance criteria to be retained in the final model.

The final encorafenib model was a semi-mechanistic two-compartment model with first-order absorption, concentration-dependent autoinduction, effect of age and tumor type on CL/F, and body weight on Vc/F. GOF plots for the final model indicate a good overall model fit to the observed data (Fig. 3A–C). Three observations in the final model met the criteria for being considered outliers (CWRES > 6) and were therefore excluded. The final model parameter estimates with the 95% CIs of the parameter estimates are presented in Table 2. The median values of parameters and 95% CIs obtained from the converged bootstrap runs are also presented in Table 2. The induction half-life estimate was 64 h with NONMEM 95% CI of 56–71 h and the bootstrap distribution was right-skewed (median 56 h, 95% CI 53–61 h, 99% CI 52–71 h). The IIV for parameters other than CL/F and Vc/F were fixed to a small number (15.8%), which is common practice when using the stochastic approximation expectation-maximization estimation method. The IIV for Ka was fixed to 50% since our ability to characterize the absorption phase was limited (see Discussion). Both η-shrinkage and ε-shrinkage were ≤ 30% and the condition number was 56. The final model performed well in predicting the observed data (Fig. 3). Based on 1000 simulated trials using parameter estimates from the final model, the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles for the observed data were mostly within the 95% CI for simulated data in the generated VPCs on serial PK sampling days (Fig. 4) and overall profiles (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Summaries of Concentration-Dependent Autoinduction Effect

Encorafenib CL/F was estimated to be 12.2 L/h after the first dose in the typical adult participant and increased 186% (i.e., up to 35 L/h) at steady state assumed to be a result of CYP enzymes net autoinduction after multiple dosing. The estimated encorafenib EC50 for this induction effect was 9 ng/mL. The turnover half-life for net autoinduction was estimated to be 64 h, resulting in a rate constant for the first-order degradation of the enzyme pool (i.e., KENZ) of 0.0108 h−1. Thus, this model indicated that the maximum autoinduction effect is expected to be achieved within 14 days of daily dosing, assuming five half-lives to reach steady-state concentrations (Fig. 5).

The typical value of encorafenib CL/F in participants with melanoma is approximately 17.5% higher than in those with metastatic CRC and approximately 9% higher than in other participants (healthy participants and those with NSCLC or other solid tumors). To evaluate the magnitude of the effect of age on encorafenib CL/F, the values were calculated at extreme age values (10th and 90th percentile) in the analysis population and compared with the typical value at a median age of 58 years old. Relative to the typical value of 12.2 L/h at the median age of 58 years, CL/F was 15% higher (14.0 L/h) at an age of 38 years and 7% lower (11.3 L/h) at an age of 73 years. The magnitude of the effect of body weight on Vc/F was also evaluated using the same approach and compared with the typical value, assuming a median body weight of 76 kg. Relative to the typical value of 61.7 L at the median body weight of 76 kg, Vc/F was 16% (to 51.6 L) lower at baseline body weight of 56 kg and 18% (to 72.5 L) higher at baseline body weight of 100 kg. The magnitude of these changes for both CL/F and Vc/F were deemed not clinically meaningful.

Discussion

A two-compartment model with first-order absorption and concentration-dependent autoinduction successfully characterized the concentration-time profile of encorafenib PK in participants with the BRAF V600E-mutation and healthy participants. In total, PK data from 1299 patients who received encorafenib as monotherapy or as combination treatment (8648 observations) were included in this analysis. The typical value for encorafenib CL/F after a single dose was 12 L/h (56% CV) and the Vc/F was 62 L (50% CV). All model parameters were estimated with good precision (relative standard error mostly < 30%), within the NONMEM 95% CIs, and had shrinkage ≤ 30%. For most parameters, bootstrap- and NONMEM-based CIs were similar. For the induction turnover rate, the bootstrap distribution was right-skewed, resulting in a narrower CI and slightly lower median. Given this asymmetry, bootstrap intervals were considered primary and NONMEM CIs are included for comparison. The encorafenib CL/F estimate from this semi-mechanistic model (12.2 L/h) is in agreement with the previously published value (15.4 L/h) from the time-varying CL model included in the NDA [5]. GOF and VPC plots showed that the base and final model adequately characterized the concentration-time profiles observed.

A semi-mechanistic enzyme turnover model was used to quantify the net autoinduction of encorafenib, i.e., the impact of encorafenib concentration on encorafenib clearance. The maximum net-induction effect was a 186% higher encorafenib oral clearance (approximately 35 L/h for typical participants) following multiple dosing and was reached within 14 days. The day 1 and steady state day 14 CL/F estimates (12.2 and 35 L/h, respectively) from this model are in agreement with CL/F values previously reported in product labeling on day 1 (14 L/h [54% CV]) and steady state (32 L/h [59% CV]) [1, 2]. This concentration–Emax model improved a previous time–Emax model by quantifying the net autoinduction of encorafenib, which better represents the underlying mechanism for encorafenib time-dependent clearance [5]. This encorafenib concentration-dependent autoinduction model may potentially improve our understanding of the impact of encorafenib dose reductions or temporary drug holidays taken during treatment in clinical studies and may offer better predictability following such dose adjustments in future clinical studies of encorafenib.

Demographic factors, baseline disease characteristics, and clinical laboratory values were evaluated as potential covariates to further account for variabilities in CL/F and Vc/F unexplained by the autoinduction of encorafenib CL/F. In the SCM analysis, age and tumor type (metastatic CRC and other tumor) were statistically significant on CL/F and body weight was significant on Vc/F. When evaluating all identified covariate effects, i.e., tumor type and age on clearance and body weight on volume of distribution, the expected change in the PK parameters was estimated to be less than 20% and hence not considered clinically meaningful within the studied population (Fig. 6). This observation is consistent with the lack of strong trends observed in the graphical summaries of covariate effects observed during model development. None of the other covariates studied, including sex, total protein, albumin, LDH, ECOG PS, and combination treatment, were found to significantly affect the disposition of encorafenib. The known effect of CYP3A inhibitors (encorafenib AUCinf increased 180%) or inducers (> 50% decrease in encorafenib exposure predicted) on encorafenib PK were likely not identified in this analysis due to the limited number of patients taking these concomitant medications, particularly strong CYP3A inhibitors or inducers (Table 1) [5, 20].

One limitation of the current analysis is the lack of incorporation of the food effect on encorafenib absorption parameters, such as Ka. A food-effect study with encorafenib demonstrated that encorafenib overall exposure AUC did not change in the presence of food compared with the fasting state; however, Cmax was lowered and Tmax was extended [6]. In the nine clinical studies included in this analysis, data on fasted versus fed status during encorafenib dosing administration was not consistently collected across all studies; thus, the ability to fully characterize the absorption phase was limited. Nonetheless, the final model performed well in predicting the observed data (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S2), and the individual predictions closely aligned with the observed data in each study. This indicated that the developed global popPK model is sufficient for predicting post hoc encorafenib exposure metrics that can be used for subsequent exposure-response analyses. Race was also not evaluated in this analysis as most participants were White (90%), although no trends were observed in individual PK parameters (CL/F, Vc/F, and Ka) and race in previous analyses [5].

The effect of renal and hepatic impairment on encorafenib PK was also assessed in this pooled popPK analysis. Baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate was not found to have a statistically significant influence on encorafenib clearance. In addition, individual post hoc CL/F estimates for encorafenib in this model were summarized and stratified by organ impairment categories (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Fig. S4). This comparison showed that the CL/F of encorafenib was similar when comparing participants with normal renal function (n = 691) to those with mild (n = 524), moderate (n = 83), and severe (n = 1) renal impairment (Supplementary Fig. S3). Limited sample size in the severe renal impairment group warrants cautious interpretation. Overall, these observations are consistent with results from the human absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion study (LGX818A2101), in which encorafenib was shown to exhibit minimal renal clearance [3].

In the evaluation of hepatic impairment effect on encorafenib PK, none of the associated baseline liver function laboratory measurements (albumin, AST, or bilirubin), were found to be statistically significant covariates in the final model. Individual post hoc CL/F estimates for encorafenib stratified by hepatic impairment categories showed comparable clearance of encorafenib between participants with normal function (n = 1138) and those with mild (n = 153), moderate (n = 7), or severe (n = 1) hepatic impairment (Supplementary Fig. S4). The modeling results were consistent with findings from a dedicated hepatic impairment study, which showed only 25% higher encorafenib exposures in participants with mild hepatic impairment (Child–Pugh Class A) compared with participants with normal liver function. Although the post hoc CL/F values for patients with moderate (n = 7) and severe hepatic impairment (n = 1) are similar to participants with normal hepatic function (Supplementary Fig. S4), limited sample size in these special populations warrants cautious interpretation.

This is the first global popPK analysis of encorafenib, integrating data across multiple studies, dose levels, and indications. The model introduces a semi-mechanistic, concentration-driven autoinduction component to characterize the time-dependent changes in clearance observed clinically. This mechanistic framework provides a robust foundation for evaluating exposure–response relationships and future label expansions, thereby advancing the pharmacokinetic understanding of encorafenib and informing ongoing drug development.

Conclusions

The final encorafenib popPK model, which incorporated a semi-mechanism enzyme turnover model with concentration-dependent net-autoinduction through an increased enzyme production rate adequately described the PK of encorafenib over time and across tumor types. No encorafenib dose modifications are warranted based on the covariates evaluated in this analysis.
