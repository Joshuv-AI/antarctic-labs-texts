Herbal medicine (Kyungok-go) for fatigue in Long COVID patients: A prospective multicenter pilot study
Authors: Tae-Hun Kim, Jiwon Yoon, Byoung-Kab Kang, Jung Won Kang, Young-Hee Jin, Chan-Young Kwon, Sunoh Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong COVID, defined as symptoms persisting beyond 12 weeks after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, has emerged as a significant global health concern. Fatigue is one of its most common and disabling symptoms, yet robust clinical evidence for effective treatments remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, prospective, single-arm pilot study to evaluate the feasibility and potential effectiveness of Kyungok-go (Qiong-Yu-Gao), a traditional Korean herbal medicine, in individuals with Long COVID-related fatigue. A total of 100 participants experiencing fatigue for at least 12 weeks following a COVID-19 diagnosis received Kyungok-go (22.5 g twice daily) for 12 weeks. The primary outcome was the change in Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) scores. Secondary outcomes included fatigue severity assessed by a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and the Chalder Fatigue Scale (ChFS), along with sleep quality, depressive symptoms, cognitive and physical function, and quality of life. Feasibility was assessed through recruitment rate, treatment adherence, and dropout rates.ResultsNinety-four participants completed the study. FSS scores improved significantly (mean difference (MD) -2.1; 95% CI, -2.4, -1.9), accompanied by significant reductions in fatigue severity on the VAS (MD -35.6; 95% CI, -39.7, -31.4) and ChFS total score (MD -29.1; 95% CI, -32.3, -25.9). Sleep quality (MD -4.0; 95% CI, -4.8, -3.2), depressive symptoms (MD -8.1; 95% CI, -9.6, -6.7), and overall quality of life (EQ-5D-5L, MD 0.1; 95% CI, −0.1, 0.1) also showed improvements. Cognitive (MD 0.9; 95% CI, 0.2, 1.5) and physical function (MD 0.2; 95% CI, 0.0, 0.3) showed modest or limited changes. Medication adherence exceeded 97%, and no serious treatment-related adverse reactions were observed.ConclusionKyungok-go was well-tolerated and showed promising effects in alleviating fatigue among patients with Long COVID. These findings support its feasibility and potential as a therapeutic option, warranting further evaluation in randomized controlled trials.Trial registrationCRIS, KCT0009410 (https://cris.nih.go.kr/).Keywords: Long COVID, Post-acute COVID-19 syndrome, Fatigue, Kyungok-go, Qiong-Yu-Gao, Herbal medicine

Introduction

Long COVID, defined as symptoms persisting for at least 12 weeks after acute COVID-19 infection, has become a new healthcare challenge in the current endemic phase.1 Over 600 million people—estimated to be at least 10 % of those infected with COVID-19—are reported to experience Long COVID symptoms.2 Long COVID may decrease workforce productivity, accounting for at least USD141 billion in lost wages in Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries alone.3 Long COVID presents with a wide range of symptoms, such as neurological symptoms like cognitive dysfunction, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and tinnitus.4, 5 Fatigue is one of the most common symptoms experienced by patients with Long COVID. Recent evidence suggests that dual-task training may improve both fatigue and cognitive dysfunction in Long COVID, while structured resistance exercise programs are being investigated as feasible rehabilitation strategies.6, 7 However, evidence supporting these treatments remains limited due to a lack of clinical research.8

Kyungok-go (known as Qiong-Yu-Gao in Chinese) is an herbal medicine commonly used in Korea to manage fatigue. Kyungok-go may help fatigue recovery and improve cardiorespiratory endurance.9, 10 A previous study investigating the effects of Kyungok-go in patients with Long COVID found a 53 % success rate in alleviating fatigue symptoms and reported no drug-related adverse events (AEs), suggesting that Kyungok-go may be a practical option for addressing fatigue in this patient group.11 We conducted a larger study with 100 patients to assess the feasibility, possible effectiveness in fatigue and related cognitive (and physical) function, and safety of Kyungok-go. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the feasibility of conducting a clinical study with Kyungok-go for managing fatigue and related symptoms, and safety in patients with Long COVID.

Methods

Study protocol

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital (KOMCIRB 2023-11-003-001) and the IRB of Dong-eui University Korean Medicine Hospital (DH-2023-11). It was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS; KCT0009410, https://cris.nih.go.kr/).

Study design

This was a multicenter, prospective, single-group pilot study designed to assess the feasibility of the intervention and study design for future research on patients with Long COVID-related fatigue, using a 12-week course of Kyungok-go. Patients who had persisting fatigue 12 weeks after their initial COVID-19 diagnosis were enrolled, and Kyungok-go was administered for 12 weeks following the dosage approved by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. Various outcome measures, including fatigue and cognitive function, were evaluated before and after treatment, and medication adherence and safety were monitored. As this study was a single-group study, randomization and blinding were not applicable. No changes were made to the original study protocol during its implementation.

Participants

There was no public involvement in the design or conduct of this study. However, a future clinical trial planned based on these findings will consider incorporating patient involvement in the study design. This study was carried out at Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital in Seoul (60 patients) and Dong-eui University Korean Medicine Hospital in Busan (40 patients), Republic of Korea. Participants were recruited through public advertisements at the study sites.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were individuals aged over 19 diagnosed with COVID-19 at least 12 weeks prior who had experienced at least 4 weeks of new-onset continuous fatigue following COVID-19 infection, had a Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) score greater than 36, had no cognitive impairment, and provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they had a past or current history of diseases known to cause fatigue, including cancer, sleep disorders, chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, tuberculosis, asthma, or multiple sclerosis. Individuals with medical conditions that could affect drug intake or absorption, such as dysphagia, clinically significant digestive disorders, galactose intolerance, Lapp lactase deficiency, or glucose–galactose malabsorption were also excluded. Patients with uncontrolled diabetes, a known allergy to Kyungok-go, a history of liver or kidney disease, or abnormal liver and kidney function in blood chemistry tests (aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), or creatinine exceeding three times the upper limit of normal) were not eligible. Pregnant, potentially pregnant, or breastfeeding women, as well as those who had participated in another clinical study within 30 days prior to enrollment, were excluded. Finally, individuals with significant psychiatric symptoms, medical conditions, or laboratory findings that could interfere with participation, as determined by the investigator, were not enrolled.

Interventions

Participants received Kyungok-go at a dose of 22.5 g twice daily between meals. Kyungok-go is a dark brown soft extract packaged in aluminum stick packs. Each 22.5 g dose was prepared from 112.5 g of extract containing four ingredients: fresh root juice of Rehmannia glutinosa Liboschitz ex Steudel (39.9 g), root of Panax ginseng CA Meyer (6.2 g), sclerotium of Poria cocos Wolf (12.4 g), and honey (41.5 g). Kyungok-go was manufactured by Kyungjin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Icheon-si, Republic of Korea), a pharmaceutical company certified for Good Manufacturing Practice. This herbal product was manufactured according to the process approved by Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (Supplement 1). As there are no separate quality control (QC) standards for this product in Korea, it was produced according to the manufacturer’s internal standards, and results of liquid chromatography analysis are presented in Supplement 2.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

As this was a pilot study, its primary aim was to assess the feasibility of the intervention and study design. The primary clinical outcome was the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) score after 12 weeks of Kyungok-go administration.

To assess feasibility, the recruitment rate of participants (both overall and at each site), the dropout rate, and the reasons for dropout were recorded. Medication adherence was also used to evaluate feasibility. It was determined by calculating the total number of Kyungok-go doses actually taken during the 12 weeks, divided by the total number expected to be taken over that period (178 sachets in total).

Secondary outcomes

Fatigue was further assessed using the Fatigue Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Chalder Fatigue Scale (ChFS), both of which have been validated in research on chronic fatigue and post-infectious syndromes.12, 13 To assess quality of life, the EuroQol 5-Dimension, 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) was administered.14 Sleep quality was measured using the Korean version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI-K) .15 Depressive symptoms following COVID-19 recovery were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) .16

Cognitive function was measured using the Korean version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (K-MoCA), Digit span Forward (DF) and Backward (DB) tests, and a Computerized Neurocognitive Test (CNT) .17

To assess the participants’ physical function, the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) was used. This battery consists of three tests: a static balance test, a gait speed test, and a repeated chair stand test. The test was carried out using the AndanteFit system (Dyphi Inc., Daejeon, Korea) .18

Sample size calculation

Because this study was designed as a prospective, multicenter pilot trial evaluating a single intervention, no formal hypothesis-driven power calculation was conducted. Pilot studies aim to assess feasibility, refine study procedures, evaluate safety, and obtain preliminary estimates of effect size rather than determine definitive efficacy. Therefore, methodological guidelines recommend adopting a pragmatic sample size that is sufficiently large to characterize feasibility outcomes but smaller than that required for a fully powered randomized controlled trial. Prior literature suggests that pilot studies typically include 30–50 participants per group or approximately 70–100 participants in total to achieve stable estimates for planning future confirmatory trials.19, 20 In accordance with these recommendations, we selected a total sample size of 100 participants, which was considered appropriate for evaluating feasibility, safety, and preliminary effectiveness across two clinical trial centers.

Adverse events

The known potential adverse events (AEs) of Kyungok-go are as follows:

•Skin: rash, urticaria, eczema, and worsening of dermatitis

Skin: rash, urticaria, eczema, and worsening of dermatitis

•Gastrointestinal: loss of appetite, stomach discomfort, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea

Gastrointestinal: loss of appetite, stomach discomfort, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea

In this study, any AEs—defined as harmful signs (including abnormal lab results), symptoms, or conditions occurring during participation regardless of whether they were related to the study drug—as well as any adverse drug reactions, defined as harmful events where a link to the intervention could not be excluded, were monitored throughout the study. All AE-related symptoms and signs, their duration, severity, and possible causal relationship were recorded. In addition to symptoms and signs, vital signs, laboratory tests, and electrocardiograms were also checked for safety monitoring.

Statistical analysis

Effectiveness outcomes were analyzed using the full analysis set, while the safety set was used to assess safety outcomes. Missing data were handled using the last observation carried forward method. Feasibility outcomes were summarized by calculating the recruitment rate (number of enrolled participants divided by the number screened, %), dropout rates ( %), and the frequency of reasons for dropout. Data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and appropriate statistical tests were chosen based on data type, including the paired t-test, McNemar test, repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), chi-squared test, or Fisher’s exact test. The significance level was set at 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the latest version of R statistical software.

For the safety assessment, the number of participants experiencing AEs, the incidence rate, and the total number of AEs, adverse drug reactions, serious adverse events, and serious adverse drug reactions were recorded.

Results

Participants

A total of 100 participants were enrolled at two clinical trial centers from March to August 2024 (Fig. 1). The mean age of participants was 44.1 years (SD 14.2), and 76 were female. Few participants reported engagement in physical labor. The average time since COVID-19 infection was 693.1 days (SD 196.7), and the average duration of fatigue symptoms was 672.5 days (SD 216.5). Before treatment, the mean fatigue VAS score was 73.4 (SD 13.0), and the FSS score was 5.4 (SD 0.8), indicating that most participants experienced moderate to severe fatigue at baseline (Table 1).

Primary outcome

The primary outcome, the FSS score, showed a significant improvement following administration of the study medication (mean difference, -2.1; 95% CI,-2.4, -1.9). Regarding feasibility, all 100 participants were successfully enrolled within 5 months. Six participants dropped out due to unexpected illnesses (n = 3) or voluntary withdrawal (n = 3). Medication adherence during the 12-week treatment period ranged from 97.5 % to 99.7 % across study visits. These findings indicate that the study design and administration of the intervention were feasible (Supplement 3).

Secondary outcomes

Most secondary measures related to fatigue symptoms showed significant improvement after the medication was administered. The fatigue VAS score showed a significant reduction. ChFS scores improved significantly in the physical domain, mental health domain, and total score. For sleep quality, the PSQI showed a slight improvement. Improvements were also noted in the EQ-5D-5L, and the BDI indicated modest improvement. However, although the K-MOCA score improved slightly, no significant changes were found in the DF or DB tests. For the CNT, no significant improvement was observed, except in the trail-making test. Physical performance did not change significantly (Table 2).

To track changes in fatigue symptoms over time, VAS for fatigue, FSS, and ChFS total scores were measured monthly from baseline. All three measures showed significant improvement over time (Supplement 4).

Adverse events

Most AEs were mild, with two moderate AEs (shoulder pain and tendinitis) and one severe AE (osteoporosis). All three moderate to severe AEs were determined to be unrelated to the herbal medicine. Five cases of abnormal BUN levels were observed after administration. However, apart from one case where the level rose from 25 to 31, the other cases showed only slight deviations from the normal range (8–20), with values of 21 (n = 3) or 7 (n = 1). Regarding creatinine, two participants had values below the normal range (0.7–1.3), recorded as 0.45 and 0.4, but there were no clinically significant increases. For AST, there was one case outside the normal range (43), and three cases showed elevated ALT levels after administration (44, 48, and 84). Of these, two participants already had elevated AST before joining the study (40–48 and 61–84), suggesting that the increases were unlikely caused by the medication. Other cases with elevated AST or ALT showed only minor increases, suggesting the medication likely does not have a hepatotoxic effect. As for ECG results, 14 cases showed abnormal findings after administration, but no serious abnormalities such as QT prolongation, ventricular fibrillation, or atrial fibrillation were detected. No clinically significant ECG changes were noted. No adverse drug reactions were judged to be related to Kyungok-go by the investigators. Overall, these results suggest that Kyungok-go does not pose serious safety concerns (Supplement 5).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This multicenter pilot study demonstrated that Kyungok-go administered over 12 weeks was feasible and well tolerated in patients with Long COVID–related fatigue. The primary outcome, the FSS, showed significant improvement, along with notable improvements in VAS and ChFS scores. Most AEs were mild, with no events determined to be drug-related, and no clinically significant laboratory or ECG abnormalities were found, indicating that Kyungok-go is a feasible and potentially effective treatment option for this condition.

Comparison with previous studies

These results extend findings from a prior study involving only 15 patients with Long COVID, in which Kyungok-go was associated with a 53 % response rate for fatigue relief and no major safety concerns.11 By enrolling 100 participants across two centers, the present study provides more robust feasibility evidence than previous work. In contrast to current medical treatments, Kyungok-go represents a holistic, multitargeted therapeutic approach based on traditional medicine and increasingly supported by pharmacological and mechanistic research. Unlike monotherapies that focus on a single disease mechanism, Kyungok-go influences multiple pathways related to Long COVID fatigue.

Clinical and scientific implications

The biological mechanisms underlying fatigue in Long COVID overlap with those seen in conditions like myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome and involve factors such as immune dysregulation, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, autonomic imbalance, and vascular insufficiency.21 Kyungok-go, a traditional tonic used in East Asia, has historically been prescribed to help manage fatigue, age-related weakness, and recovery from illness.22 Made from Panax ginseng, Poria cocos, Rehmannia glutinosa, and honey, its composition indicates multiple actions that align with current biomedical perspectives on post-viral fatigue. However, because the mechanisms by which post-COVID-19 fatigue develops remain unclear, even if clinical improvement is observed, it is difficult to pharmacologically explain how such improvement occurs. Nevertheless, based on the potential biological mechanisms of Kyungok-go described above, we acknowledge the possibility that it may alleviate fatigue symptoms and recognize that further research is needed to explore this area.

A longitudinal cohort study of patients infected with COVID-19 reported that the risk of Long COVID decreases approximately two years after infection.23 However, according to a recent systematic review on the disease trajectory among post-COVID patients, fatigue was the most frequently reported symptom across all follow-up time points (52 % at 3 months, 46 % at 1 year, and 54 % at 2 years), suggesting that fatigue in the Long COVID population may persist over time rather than showing substantial improvement as part of the natural course of the condition.24 In the present study, participants had experienced symptoms for more than two years on average, and their mean fatigue VAS score was 73 at baseline, indicating a considerable severity of fatigue at the time of study enrollment. In this context, the improvement observed in this study is unlikely to be explained solely by the natural course of the condition and may instead reflect the effect of the intervention. Nonetheless, confirmatory randomized controlled trials are required to establish efficacy and safety before routine clinical use can be recommended. Future studies in Long COVID should therefore aim to establish condition-specific responder definitions and clinically meaningful change thresholds for fatigue outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, the intervention was given over a relatively extended period (3 months), and data were collected for multiple outcomes, including fatigue, sleep, depression, cognitive function, quality of life, and physical function. Because fatigue in Long COVID differs from fatigue in the general population, longer treatment durations and comprehensive assessments are needed.25 In this regard, the study provides valuable preliminary data by thoroughly examining patient symptoms over an extended treatment period. Second, this study expanded the range of tools used to measure cognitive and physical function, adding instruments such as the CNT for cognitive assessment and the Short Physical Performance Battery for evaluating physical function. In previous work, only the K-MOCA and DF/DB tests were used for cognitive assessment, but these mainly detect significant cognitive impairment and were not entirely suitable for this population.11 Moreover, earlier research did not include appropriate measures for physical function. In this study, the CNT was used to identify mild cognitive decline,26 and validated tools for physical function were employed to examine how Long COVID affects cognition and physical function.

This study has several limitations. The main drawback is the absence of a control group. Because of this, the results should be considered preliminary and cannot be used as definitive clinical evidence for the effectiveness and safety of Kyungok-go for this condition. The current findings will serve only as preliminary data to guide future research. In addition, study results should be interpreted cautiously due to the limitation of the study design (a single-arm, pre-post study without blinding). The study results are highly susceptible to non-specific effects such as the placebo effect, regression to the mean, or the Hawthorne effect. In addition to this, the generalizability issues of our study findings need to be cautious when interpreting results. A randomized controlled trial with a placebo group is planned for the future.

Conclusions and suggestions for future research

Administering Kyungok-go for 12 weeks to patients with Long COVID and fatigue symptoms was shown to be feasible, as it led to improvements in fatigue scores, had no serious drug-related AEs, and was associated with high medication adherence. However, to generate solid clinical evidence for this intervention, a randomized controlled trial with a placebo group should be conducted in the future. Given the severity of this condition and its significant impact on public health, it remains important to continue investigating clinical evidence for different interventions and to make continued efforts to develop effective treatment strategies.
