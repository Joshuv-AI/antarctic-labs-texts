Comparative Evaluation of Aqueous Toluidine Blue Stain and Conventional Papanicolaou Stain in Cervicovaginal Smear
Authors: Moutusi Roy, Sanchita Hazra, Ayandip Nandi, Ranu R Biswas, Anuradha De(Pati)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cervical cancer is the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer. In India, cervical cancer is the second most diagnosed cancer among women following breast cancer. Early detection of precursor lesions of cervical cancer on cervical cytology by a screening program remarkably reduces the death rate.Objective:The objective of this study is to see the efficacy of aqueous toluidine blue stain in cervicovaginal smears in comparison with conventional Papanicolaou stain, compare staining properties on nucleus as well as cytoplasm, reporting time, cost-effectiveness, and clinicopathological profile of the patient.Materials and Methods:An institution-based, prospective cross-sectional study of 100 sexually active females aged 21–60 years. Two to three cervical smears were collected from each patient over a period of 18 months. Two percent aqueous toluidine blue stain and conventional Papanicolaou stain were performed on the smears. Cytomorphology of the smears was studied and compared in reference to staining in the nucleus and cytoplasm, timing, and efficacy of stain.Results:Efficacy and nuclear staining properties by 2% aqueous toluidine blue stain are as good as in conventional Papanicolaou stain in cervicovaginal smears. Reporting time and cost are less in aqueous toluidine blue stain.Conclusion:Both stains show identical results; however, reduced staining time and cost-effectiveness are critical in a lower-middle-income country like India.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical cancer is the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of death among women, with an estimated 6,04,000 new cases and 3,52,000 deaths in the year 2020.[1,2] The global burden of cervical cancer is mainly distributed in Sub-Saharan Africa, Melanesia, South America, and South-Eastern Asia. Early detection of cervical cancer on cervical cytology by screening programs, more than 90% human papilloma virus vaccination coverage, and a socioeconomically better lifestyle helped decrease the incidence of cervical carcinoma in the developed countries.[1] In India, cervical cancer is the second most diagnosed cancer among women, following breast cancer. It contributes to 1,23,907 cases annually and 77,348 deaths every year.[3]

In 1943, Papanicolaou first described the Papanicolaou stain (Pap), and since then, it has been commonly used as a screening test despite being time-consuming and necessitating the use of a generous amount of alcohol.[4] Pap stain yields a polychromatic, transparent staining reaction with crisp nuclear and cytological features, which can be easily interpreted by a trained pathologist.[5] The Pap stain has been most frequently used for screening of cervical cancer. The conventional Pap stain is a 30 min procedure and uses a significant amount of absolute alcohol as a dehydrating agent, which is highly expensive and difficult to obtain in a developing country like India.[6]

Toluidine blue is a basic metachromatic dye that selectively stains acidic tissue components.[7] Toluidine blue has an affinity for nucleic acids and, therefore, binds to nuclear material of tissues with a high DNA and RNA content.[6] Toluidine blue stain (TBS) is partially soluble in both water and alcohol and is a member of the thiazine group.[8]

Various researchers have extensively used TBS as a vital stain for oral mucosal lesions and aspiration cytology reporting in recent years.[9,10] The literature shows TBS is a practical, rapid, inexpensive, and effective adjunct diagnostic tool.[11,12] Toluidine blue is a supravital stain that accentuates good cytological and nuclear details. It is easily available, very cheap, cost-effective, and used for quick reporting.[11]. Hence, we undertook this study on cervicovaginal cytology to see and compare the efficacy of the toluidine blue staining and the conventional Pap staining method.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This institution-based observational cross-sectional study was conducted in a tertiary care institute in collaboration with the Departments of Pathology and Obstetrics & Gynaecology over a period of 18 months. Cervicovaginal smear was obtained from sexually active women in the age group of 21–60 years presenting with complaints of persistent vaginal discharge, intermenstrual bleeding, postcoital bleeding, and an unhealthy cervix on examination or for screening purposes. Women <20 years and >60 years of age, pregnant women, and women with frank cervical growth were excluded from this study. Permission was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from patients. Patient particulars, such as history and clinical examination details, were taken.

Cervicovaginal smear preparation was done with the help of Cusco’s speculum, Ayer’s spatula, cytobrush, and slides. Two sets of cervicovaginal smears of the same patient were prepared and labeled independently, fixed into 95% ethyl alcohol separately in a Coplin jar for 10 min. One set of smears was stained by 2% aqueous toluidine blue, and another set of smears was stained by conventional Papanicolaou stain. For the Papanicolaou stain, standard textbook protocol was followed.[13] The Standard Bethesda Reporting Format was followed in reporting.[14] Results were documented and analyzed using a Microsoft Excel sheet and Medcalc software (Medcalc software Ltd., Acacialaan, Ostend, Belgium).

RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

Table 1 provides a detailed distribution of sociodemographic variables among the study subjects (n = 100). The participants had a mean age of 40.69 years (standard deviation [SD] = 11.49 years), ranging from 21 to 60 years. The majority fell into the 30- to 39-year age group (31%), and there was a balanced distribution across parity, menstrual status, contraception methods, and socioeconomic classes. Notably, 91% had no history of chemoradiation, 91% were free from cervical carcinoma, and 96% had no other tumors. Additionally, 88% had no history of surgery, 21% used tobacco either in the form of gutkha or bidi smoking. On microscopy, 88 cases were identified as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM). Of these, 2 cases of bacterial vaginosis and 12 cases of atrophic vaginitis were identified. Two cases had inadequate cellularity for reporting. Two cases of atypical squamous cell of undetermined significance (ASCUS), two cases of low grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), five cases of high grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), and one case of atypical glandular cell of undetermined significance (AGUS) were seen. No cases of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and atypical squamous cell-cannot exclude HSIL (ASC-H) were found.

DISCUSSION

We had prepared two to three slides of each patient and stained them separately with both TBS and conventional Papanicolaou stain in our laboratory. The toluidine blue staining procedure was easy and required less staining time and less reporting turn over time compared with conventional Papanicolaou staining. Toluidine blue staining required hardly 5 min, whereas Papanicolaou staining required around 30 min. Patil et al.[15] also reported overall reporting time for 2%TBS was 5 min after fixation, and conventional Pap stain requires 30 min.

Papanicolaou staining had more costly reagents, such as absolute alcohol, hematoxylin, Orange G, and eosin azure 50, so the cost of staining per slide was slightly higher in Pap stain than in TBS. TBS was cheaper than the conventional Papanicolaou stain. Ammanagi et al.[10] showed that on-site toluidine blue staining and screening improved the efficiency of fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) reporting. Our study showed a significant difference between the staining methods regarding costing and timing. The cost-effectiveness can be a critical factor in resource-constrained settings. In India, where financial limitations often hinder widespread health care initiatives, the lower cost of toluidine blue makes it a more accessible and feasible option for large-scale cervical screen like in health camps/vaccination drives/high-risk population areas.

Kumari et al.[16] found the sensitivity of acetic acid, Lugol’s iodine, and toluidine blue were 81.8%, 84.09%, and 90.9%, respectively, while the specificity of the three stains were 62.5%, 65.17%, and 75.8%, respectively. Epstein et al.[7] showed sensitivity and specificity of toluidine blue in premalignant oral squamous cancer as 92.5% and 63.2%, respectively. Singh et al.[17] found that toluidine blue was more sensitive 70.83% and specific 88.46% in the detection of abnormal cervical lesions when compared with acetic acid (58.3% and 87.5%) and Lugol’s iodine (62.2% and 85.1%), with higher positive and negative predictive value.

In our study, in a fair no of cases, the inflammatory background of polymorphs and lactobacilli was better appreciated in TBS. In bacterial vaginosis, characteristic clue cells were strikingly noted in TBS. Nuclear details like nuclear size, shape, chromatin pattern, nuclear membrane, and cytoplasm were prominently visualized in TBS as well as conventional Papanicolaou stain.

Saba et al.[11] used the TBS staining in fine needle aspiration cytology and concluded that TBS in FNAC improves the diagnostic accuracy by decreasing the smearing and drying artifact, loss of cell sample during fixation, and staining.

In our study, we have got as follows: NILM was in 88% of cases, ASCUS was in 2% of cases, LSIL was in 2% of cases, HSIL was in 5% of cases, ASC-H was in 0% of cases, and AGUS was in 1% of cases. Nikumbh et al.[18] reported in their study as follows: NILM 94.7% cases, ASCUS was in 21% of cases, LSIL was in 1.2% of cases, HSIL was in 0.8% of cases, ASC-H was in 0.8% of cases, and SCC was in 0.4% of cases [Figures 1,2,3,4,5].

CONCLUSION

The cost-effectiveness can be a critical factor in India, where financial limitations often hinder widespread health care initiatives. Lower cost of toluidine blue staining makes it a more accessible and feasible option for large-scale cervical screening purposes for early detection of cervical pathology. The integration of TBS for cervical screening aligns with the imperative of providing affordable and efficient healthcare solutions, especially in regions marked by economic challenges. The study results emphasize the potential of TBS as a valuable tool for advancing public health initiatives and mitigating the burden of cervical cancer.
