An observational study of the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute’s switch from in-person to virtual research application review panels during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Robin Bloodworth, Rachel Hemphill, Laura P Forsythe, Carolyn Mohan, Esther C Nolton, Ponta Abadi, Lisa J Stewart, Krista P Woodward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) reviews research applications using a process that includes preliminary review by primary reviewers, followed by panel discussion where primary reviewers and other reviewers on the panel (panelists) discuss and score the most competitive applications. PCORI switched from in-person to virtual panel discussions during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study compared reviewers’ scores and perceptions of panel interactions for virtual and in-person review panels and assessed reviewers’ perspectives about virtual panels. PCORI uniquely includes patients and other stakeholders as reviewers alongside scientists; thus, differences between reviewer types were also explored.MethodsThis observational study utilized repeated cross-sectional data from PCORI funding cycles before and after switching to virtual review, including reviewer score data and closed- and open-ended responses from reviewer surveys. The study team used linear and logistic regression to examine reviewer scores and quantitative survey responses and conducted thematic analysis of open-ended survey responses.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences between in-person and virtual panels in primary reviewers’ post-discussion scores or the magnitude of score change from pre- to post-discussion. In contrast, panelists gave stronger post-discussion scores in virtual panels, and this difference between formats was greater for stakeholder reviewers compared to scientists. Reviewers’ ratings of panel interactions appeared to be similar between formats. While reviewers noted challenges of virtual panels, including challenges related to discussion quality and social interactions, they also noted benefits, such as lack of travel.ConclusionsOverall, this study suggests that virtual and in-person review panels were largely comparable on reviewer scores and some key aspects of reviewer experiences in a multi-stakeholder review process. Despite having some challenges, virtual review processes may be a viable approach for funders during times of necessity and beyond. Practical considerations and recommendations for virtual panels are discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41073-026-00198-y.

Background

Advances in health care rely on research evidence and thus are dependent on the quality and speed of funders’ processes to select research studies for funding. Research applications are typically selected for funding based on merit, determined through rigorous review processes that include convenings of panels of experts to discuss applications’ strengths, weaknesses, and suitability for funding. At many funding organizations, review panel discussions have typically been held in-person. However, the global response to the COVID-19 pandemic starting in early 2020 necessitated a rapid switch to virtual meeting formats (e.g. teleconference, video conference), while also reinforcing the need for funders to award and disseminate healthcare research expediently. Beyond critical situations like the COVID-19 pandemic, funders may have additional compelling reasons to opt for virtual panel meetings. It is thus important to assess virtual processes for reviewing and discussing research applications to ensure they enable funders to continue selecting high-quality applications for funding and to identify ways to enhance virtual review processes.

Panel discussions are a common component of research application review processes that offer a setting for reviewers to share their expert opinions and discuss the merits of applications prior to determining their final assessments [1]. The few prior studies examining virtual review processes for biomedical and healthcare research are mostly small-scale and provide somewhat mixed results about the process and outcomes of virtual versus in-person review panel discussions. Studies have observed shorter application discussion times in virtual settings [2–5], and there is some evidence that reviewer scores change less from before to after the panel discussion in virtual settings [3, 6]; however, differences in reviewers’ scores between in-person and virtual panels tend to be small. Moreover, some studies have shown that virtual review panels produce similar average reviewer scores and levels of agreement between reviewers [2–4], compared to in-person panels. While one study found that most reviewers perceived teleconference discussion as similarly fair and thorough to in-person discussion [2], others found that some reviewers perceived video discussions as less thorough or nuanced and more prone to distraction than in-person discussion [4, 5, 7]. Virtual formats limit opportunities for reviewers to develop rapport and network outside of panel discussions, yet they may also promote fairer participation because discussion dynamics (e.g. turn-taking) can be more strictly controlled [7]. Despite potential drawbacks, virtual review panels can offer significant advantages to both funders and reviewers, such as reduced costs and time burden and greater support for work-life balance [5, 7, 8]. A more in-depth assessment of reviewers’ experiences in virtual panel meetings is needed to better understand tradeoffs of this approach.

Regardless of format, it is important to ensure that research application review processes align with funder values. The Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) is committed to involving patients and other healthcare stakeholders (e.g. clinicians, health system representatives, payers, policy makers) [9] as reviewers alongside scientists to improve representation of research end users in the funding selection process and to fund research that is more patient-centered, relevant, and feasible [10]. While it is not the norm in clinical research, funders have increasingly begun to include patients, consumers, or other stakeholders in research proposal review [11–14]. Patients, other stakeholders, and scientists often bring different lived experiences, expertise, and perspectives to the review process and may have different experiences during panel discussions. It is thus necessary to consider how aspects of review processes, such as meeting format, may differentially affect the experiences and contributions of different types of reviewers. Prior studies examining virtual review panels have not considered potential differences across reviewer types.

PCORI offers a unique opportunity to address gaps in the literature on virtual review processes. PCORI is a nonprofit organization authorized by Congress to fund patient-centered comparative clinical effectiveness research [15]. PCORI’s mission is to help people make informed healthcare decisions and improve healthcare delivery and outcomes by funding research guided by patients and other stakeholders that produce high-integrity, evidence-based information. In addition to scientific experts, PCORI involves patients and other stakeholders in all phases of the research process, from research application review (referred to as PCORI Merit Review), to study design and conduct, to dissemination and implementation of results. Like many funders, PCORI’s merit review process has traditionally involved in-person review panel meetings. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, PCORI began conducting review panels by video conference in April 2020, which required training and adaptation of in-person processes to the virtual environment.

This observational study used repeated cross-sectional data routinely collected during the merit review process and recurring surveys of merit reviewers to examine how PCORI’s virtual review panels compare to prior in-person panels in terms of reviewers’ scores and experiences, as well as to better understand reviewers’ perspectives on virtual review processes. More specifically, this study sought to assess whether there were differences between virtual and in-person multi-stakeholder panels in reviewer scores (Aim 1) or reviewer perceptions of interactions during the panel discussion (Aim 2). It also sought to understand reviewer perspectives on discussion quality, challenges, and benefits of virtual review panels (Aim 3). For each of these aims, potential differences by reviewer type (i.e. scientists, patients, stakeholders) were explored where possible. The goal of this exploratory work was to produce information that is useful to funders in facilitating virtual review processes that are rigorous, collegial, and acceptable to a range of reviewers.

Overview of PCORI’s merit review process

PCORI funds research through PCORI Funding Announcements (PFAs), which are typically offered in three funding cycles per year (e.g. Cycle 1 2019, abbreviated as 19c1). Research applications are evaluated through a process called PCORI Merit Review. Two key phases of PCORI’s merit review process are preliminary online review and the panel meeting. The preliminary online review begins with assigning research applications to primary reviewers based on their expertise and experience. Primary reviewers include a mix of scientists, patients, and other stakeholders (e.g. clinicians, health system representatives, payers, policy makers); for the current study, each application was assigned three scientists, one patient, and one stakeholder. Primary reviewers evaluate their assigned applications based on established review criteria and provide a written critique with scores for each review criterion and an overall preliminary score. Next, a subset of the most competitive applications moves forward to the panel meeting, where the entire panel of reviewers and the panel chair discuss the merit of each application. Primary reviewers and all other reviewers on the panel (hereafter referred to as panelists) provide a final overall score for each discussed application. Panel meetings have traditionally been held in person but moved to a fully virtual format in April 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Methods

This observational study used existing reviewer score data and other administrative data (e.g. application number, funding cycle, PFA) collected during PCORI’s merit review process, as well as survey data (both closed- and open-ended responses) collected from PCORI merit reviewers for research applications submitted to three primary types of PCORI funding opportunities that were offered both before and after the switch to virtual panel discussions: Broads, Methods, and Targeted PFAs (See Additional file 1B for additional descriptions of PFAs). Applications were excluded if they were from PFAs with significantly different review processes (non-research awards) and/or that were not offered during both in-person and virtual review cycles. For a complete list of PFAs included in this study, see Additional file 1C. This article follows applicable reporting guidelines for observational studies [16].

PCORI merit reviewer scores

For each application considered by the review panel, primary reviewers submitted a preliminary overall score before the panel discussion (hereafter referred to as “pre-discussion score”), and both primary reviewers and panelists submitted a final overall score after the discussion (hereafter referred to as “post-discussion score”). Reviewers used the online platform Salesforce (SalesForce, Inc., California, USA) to enter their scores. These score data were exported along with relevant administrative data (e.g. application number, funding cycle, PFA) for analytic purposes. The score data (hereafter referred to as “score sample”) included all 12 review cycles (eight with in-person panel meeting and four with virtual panel meeting) that took place beginning with Cycle 1 2017 (17c1), which is when PCORI implemented a new platform for collecting reviewer scores.

Response variables for the score sample included primary reviewers’ pre-discussion score (1 “Exceptional” to 9 “Poor”) and primary reviewers’ and panelists’ post-discussion score (1 “Exceptional” to 9 “Poor”). Magnitude of change in primary reviewers’ score from pre-discussion to post-discussion was also calculated (absolute value of pre-discussion score minus post-discussion score; possible range of 0–8) to reflect the extent to which primary reviewers’ overall assessment of an application’s merit changed after the panel discussion.

PCORI merit reviewer survey responses

PCORI routinely administers voluntary online surveys to assess reviewers’ experiences with PCORI Merit Review and inform continuous process improvement [10]. Surveys are conducted for at least one funding cycle per year and include closed- and open-ended survey questions developed by PCORI regarding various aspects of PCORI’s merit review process. Reviewers typically receive email invitations within a few days after the review panel meeting and are given approximately two weeks to complete the survey. Surveys were administered using DatStat Illume software (Datstat, Utah, USA) or Qualtrics XM software (Qualtrics, Utah, USA).

The survey data (hereafter referred to as “survey sample”) included responses from three review cycles: one with in-person panel meetings (16c3) and two with virtual panel meetings (19c3 and 20c3). These three cycles were selected because the reviewer survey was fielded in these cycles and included survey questions relevant to the current study’s aims (more details on survey questions below). The reviewer survey was not fielded for every review cycle, and in some cycles, the survey was fielded but did not include survey questions relevant to the current study. Additional file 1D shows the cycles of data included for both the reviewer score and reviewer survey samples. Survey data was collected from reviewers during the following time frames: 28 March 2017–16 April 2017 (16c3), 3 April 2020–19 April 2020 (19c3), and 12 April 2021–26 April 2021 (20c3). Survey response rates for these cycles ranged from 75%−82%.

The current study utilized responses to closed- and open-ended survey questions. Closed-ended survey questions asked after all panel meetings focused on reviewer perceptions of interactions during the panel meeting: whether patient and stakeholder reviewers were receptive to input from scientific reviewers, whether scientific reviewers were receptive to input from patient and stakeholder reviewers, and whether the panel chair ensured different viewpoints were heard. Closed-ended survey questions asked only after virtual panel meetings focused on reviewer perceptions of their ability to participate in the virtual discussion and quality of the discussion. All closed-ended survey questions used a 5-point Likert-scale ranging from 1 “Strongly Disagree” to 5 “Strongly Agree” with a neutral mid-point. Responses were dichotomized to reflect agreement ("Strongly Agree” and “Somewhat Agree”) versus all other response options. Open-ended survey questions focused on reviewers’ experience with the virtual panels (e.g. challenges, benefits). See Additional file 1E for all survey questions included in this study and number of responses per question.

Data analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using Stata 17 software (StataCorp, Texas, USA). Results were considered significant with alpha < 0.05. Appropriate pre- and post-diagnostic testing of assumptions and model fit were conducted. To describe the sample, the study team examined distributions of all variables of interest overall and stratified by panel format (virtual vs. in-person), conducted bivariate regression models of each variable with panel format, and conducted regression models with primary reviewers’ pre-discussion scores as the response variable to verify that application scores did not significantly differ between virtual and in-person panels prior to panel discussions.

To assess differences between virtual and in-person panels (Aims 1–2), the study team conducted a series of regression models using listwise deletion. For reviewer scores, linear regression models that included interactions between panel format (in-person or virtual) and reviewer type (scientist, patient, or stakeholder) were conducted to assess whether differences in scores and score changes between virtual and in-person panels varied by reviewer type. For survey responses, logistic regression models examining main effects of panel format and reviewer type were conducted; interactions between panel format and reviewer type were not included due to smaller sample sizes.

The study team conducted unadjusted models, which included only the main variables of interest, and adjusted models, which included additional variables that might have an association with the response variables examined (i.e. control variables). All score sample models adjusted for PFA type (Broads, Methods, or Targeted), returning reviewer status (returning reviewer vs. first-time PCORI reviewer), and resubmission status (resubmission vs. first-time submission). Primary reviewer post-discussion score and magnitude of score change models adjusted for pre-discussion score, and score change models adjusted for score change direction (change toward poorer score, no change in score, or change towards stronger score). All survey sample models adjusted for PFA type and returning reviewer status, except one model for which PFA type could not be included due to insufficient variance in the response variable. Survey sample models examining questions asked in only virtual panels (Aim 3) also adjusted for cycle (19c3 vs. 20c3) to account for potential differences between the two virtual panels. This paper presents results from adjusted models as the main results, but unadjusted models are included in results tables for context. Additional file 1F describes notable results from unadjusted models, as well as multilevel models and sensitivity analyses that were conducted to assess the robustness of results.

Thematic analysis [17] was conducted to identify trends in open-ended reviewer survey responses about the virtual panel discussions (Aim 3). For each survey question, a single analyst reviewed responses and used an iterative approach to develop and apply a set of inductive codes for that question. For questions that had been asked in prior PCORI reviewer surveys (prior to the current study), the analyst also applied existing codes where applicable. Although each survey question was coded separately, the same codes were applied across questions when similar concepts appeared in the responses to multiple survey questions. A second analyst reviewed the coding for each survey question to confirm accuracy and refine where needed. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. The two analysts (PA, RH) then reviewed the final coding to identify and describe themes and examine whether themes varied by reviewer type. This thematic analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Washington, USA).

Results

Sample characteristics and preliminary analyses

The score sample consisted of 2,897 primary reviewer scores and 8,599 panelist scores across 12 review cycles (Table 1). Primary reviewers gave a mean pre-discussion score of 4.1 (range 1–9, with lower numbers indicating stronger score), mean post-discussion score of 4.6 (range 1–9), and mean magnitude of pre- to post-discussion score change of 0.8 (range 0–7). From pre- to post-discussion, scores did not change for 49% of primary reviewers, changed to a poorer score for 42%, and changed to a stronger score for 9%. Panelists gave a mean post-discussion score of 5.0 (range 1–9). Overall, distributions of variables were similar between in-person and virtual panels. Among primary reviewers, mean pre-discussion score and mean post-discussion score were statistically significantly stronger in virtual panels versus in-person panels (3.9 v. 4.1, p = 0.018; and 4.5 v. 4.7, p = 0.012, respectively). Panelists’ mean post-discussion score was also statistically significantly stronger in virtual panels (4.8 v. 5.0, p < 0.001). Notably, however, these differences are minimal in practical terms.

Descriptive results for reviewer score samples

The survey sample consisted of 388 survey responses across three review cycles (191 responses from one cycle with in-person panels and 197 responses from two cycles with virtual panels) (Table 2). Nearly all reviewers (~ 95%) across panel formats agreed (either somewhat or strongly) that patient and stakeholder reviewers were receptive to input from scientific reviewers, that scientist reviewers were receptive to input from patient and stakeholder reviewers, and that the panel chair ensured different viewpoints were heard. Among survey responses from virtual panels only, most reviewers (> 90%) agreed that they were able to provide input when they were not the primary reviewer and that the virtual panel meeting allowed for thorough discussion. More than two-thirds (72%) of reviewers who had previously reviewed for PCORI agreed that the quality of virtual discussions was about the same as previous in-person meetings; notably, agreement on this item was higher among reviewers in the second virtual cycle (20c3) compared with those in the first virtual cycle (19c3) (76% v. 66%). Comparing reviewer scores and score changes between virtual and in-person panels (aim 1)

In adjusted regression models, primary reviewer post-discussion score and magnitude of score change were not statistically significantly different between virtual and in-person panels overall, nor were the interactions of panel format and reviewer type (Table 3). There were two statistically significant findings for panelists. Virtual panelists gave post-discussion scores on average 0.144 points stronger than in-person panelists (95% CI = [−0.241, −0.047], p = 0.004), and further, the interaction term for stakeholders in virtual panels was statistically significant, meaning that the difference between scores for virtual and in-person panelists was larger for stakeholder panelists compared to the difference between virtual and in-person scores for scientist panelists (b = −0.236, 95% CI = [−0.443, −0.028], p = 0.026).

Open-ended survey questions solicited reviewers’ feedback about their experience with the virtual review format. Questions focused primarily on challenges and ways to improve virtual review, with fewer questions about positive aspects of virtual review. The themes that emerged from open-ended feedback were expressed by all types of reviewers (scientists, patients, and stakeholders) and, unless otherwise noted below, were observed for both 19c3 and 20c3 review cycles.

Challenges of virtual review (open-ended survey feedback)

Reviewers’ open-ended survey feedback about challenges of virtual review panels focused on four key areas: discussion quality and flow, social aspects, technical issues, and reviewer well-being and environment.

Discussion quality and flow

Reviewers noted some challenges related to the quality and flow of virtual discussions, including needing more time for robust discussion or feeling that the discussion was rushed. Other challenges included difficulties following the discussion, knowing when to chime in, or knowing when others wanted to speak. Relatedly, some reviewers expressed a desire to see more of the reviewers on camera at once, noting that lack of visual cues hindered the discussion. Additionally, in the first cycle using virtual panel meetings (19c3), which occurred early in the COVID-19 pandemic, some reviewers noted less participation or engagement from the review panel in the virtual setting, compared to past in-person review meetings; however, this theme did not emerge for 20c3 virtual discussions, which occurred about a year later.

Social aspects

Reviewers reported missing the social aspects of in-person meetings, such as making connections, having the chance for informal conversations with other reviewers, and building rapport. A few reviewers noted that getting to know each other in person facilitates discussions of research applications; that is, developing rapport and respect for fellow reviewers outside of panel discussions makes differences of opinion easier to navigate during the discussion.

Technical issues

Reviewers reported some technical issues common to virtual meetings, including audio issues (e.g. background noise, difficulty hearing others) and internet connectivity. A few reviewers reported difficulty accessing or navigating systems and documents used for the review process; this theme was more common in 19C3 and may have been improved by PCORI switching to a different online meeting platform for subsequent virtual panels. Although some reviewers experienced technical difficulties in both virtual cycles, they also noted that technical issues were minimal or quickly resolved.

Reviewer well-being and environment

Reviewers noted issues related to the timing or schedule of the virtual panel meeting, including fatigue from the length of the meeting and a need for more and/or longer breaks throughout the day. A few reviewers suggested holding future meetings over two days instead of one to allow for shorter days. Additionally, reviewers mentioned challenges related to working from home, including trouble focusing or being more easily distracted, caregiving responsibilities, and lack of adequate technology. These themes emerged in both cycles but were more prominent in the first virtual cycle (19c3) that occurred early in the COVID-19 pandemic.

Positive aspects of virtual review (open-ended survey feedback)

Although reviewers collectively noted challenges of virtual review meetings, they also reported that the virtual meetings went well. Reviewers praised PCORI and panel chairs for their preparation, support, and meeting organization and facilitation. Several reviewers reported good participation in discussions or noted that discussions were respectful and allowed everyone’s voice to be heard. Several reviewers, particularly in 20C3, felt the technology worked well. Helpful aspects of the virtual format included seeing other reviewers on camera and having a chat feature to signal when you wanted to speak. Although many reviewers expressed a preference for in-person meetings, several reviewers noted benefits of virtual meetings, including convenience and lack of travel, or expressed enthusiasm for the use of virtual review meetings in future.

Discussion

This study examined differences in virtual versus in-person review panels in the context of PCORI’s multi-stakeholder research application review process. The study team sought to understand whether reviewer scores and experiences differed between panel formats and to assess reviewer perspectives about virtual review processes, including their challenges and benefits. Across study aims, we explored whether differences between panel formats and perspectives about virtual panels varied across scientist, patient, and stakeholder reviewers. Quantitative results had few statistically significant differences, suggesting that virtual and in-person review panels were largely similar on most aspects examined. Furthermore, we found little evidence that differences by panel format vary between reviewer types, although there were some differences between reviewer types independent of panel format. Open-ended survey responses provided insight into both challenges and positive aspects experienced by reviewers serving on virtual panels.

This study found no statistically significant difference between virtual and in-person review panel formats in primary reviewers’ post-discussion scores or magnitude of score change from pre- to post-discussion. In contrast, panelists in this study gave statistically significantly stronger post-discussion scores in virtual panels compared to in-person panels; notably, however, this difference was small and unlikely to be practically significant. Although the literature is somewhat mixed, prior studies of review panels consisting mainly of scientist reviewers have tended to conclude that differences in reviewers’ scoring behavior in virtual versus in-person review meetings, though sometimes statistically significant, are minimal [2–4, 6]. The current study, which assessed review panels comprising scientist, patient, and stakeholder reviewers, fits with this overall trend.

This study found that reviewers in both panel formats had similarly high levels of agreement that reviewers were receptive to input from other reviewer types and that the panel chair ensured that different viewpoints were heard. These findings suggest that virtual panels were comparable to in-person panels in terms of important dynamics of the discussion. Moreover, survey responses showed that a strong majority of reviewers in virtual panels perceived the virtual discussion to be thorough and felt able to participate, and over two-thirds of returning reviewers agreed that discussion quality was similar to prior in-person panels. These findings expand on prior research showing that reviewers gave similar ratings of discussion thoroughness and fairness in virtual and in-person review meetings [2].

Open-ended survey responses provided further insights into the challenges and benefits of virtual review. Reviewers described challenges related to the quality and flow of the panel discussion, social aspects of the meeting, technical difficulties, and their well-being and work environment during the meeting. These findings align with and expand on prior studies of virtual review processes [5, 7, 18]. Many of the challenges raised by reviewers, such as reduced social interaction, disruptions in communication flow, technical difficulties, and distractions while working at home, were reported as challenges of virtual work in general during the COVID-19 pandemic [19]. Notably, some of the challenges raised by reviewers were more prominent in the first virtual review cycle, which occurred early in the pandemic, suggesting that time and experience may have reduced some challenges of the virtual setting. PCORI also switched to a different online meeting platform after the first virtual cycle, which may have contributed to a smoother experience. Overall, although more reviewers expressed a preference for in-person meetings than virtual, many reviewers noted benefits of virtual review, namely convenience and lack of travel, and generally expressed that the virtual panels went well despite some disadvantages. Challenges noted by reviewers suggest some practical steps funders can take to facilitate successful virtual review processes, which we describe below.

Considering findings from this study’s analysis of reviewer scores and surveys of reviewer experiences together, virtual panels appeared to be largely similar to in-person panels in terms of reviewers’ scores and perceptions of panel interactions. Furthermore, across aims, this study found little evidence that the virtual format was experienced differently by different types of reviewers (scientists, patients, and other stakeholders). The one exception was a finding indicating that panelists gave slightly stronger post-discussion scores, on average, in the virtual format compared to in-person, and this difference between panel formats was larger for stakeholder reviewers compared to scientist reviewers. This study’s survey sample was not large enough to examine interactions between panel format and reviewer type for survey questions asked in both in-person and virtual panels. However, we saw no statistically significant differences by reviewer type in reviewers’ ratings of the quality of virtual discussions, and open-ended themes about challenges and positive aspects of virtual review were raised by reviewers of all types, suggesting that scientists, patients, and stakeholders did not have substantially different experiences in the virtual format. Most differences observed between reviewer types were unrelated to panel format and outside the scope of current study aims; see Additional file 1E for a discussion of these results.

Limitations and strengths

This study has some notable limitations. While the review process adaptations necessitated by the COVID-19 pandemic provide a unique opportunity for learning, this was an observational study, so we cannot definitively conclude that the few statically significant differences observed between in-person and virtual review panels were due to panel format. It is possible that other, unmeasured factors related to reviewers or the research applications submitted during these cycles differed in ways that may have affected reviewers’ scoring behavior or perceptions. For example, effects of the pandemic on professional or personal circumstances (e.g., stress, personal or family illness, changes in workload, changes in childcare, increased distractions while working from home) could have affected who was available and willing to serve as a reviewer during the early pandemic or may have impacted aspects of applicants’ proposed research, such as the topic focus, staffing, or methods. We also cannot definitively conclude that a lack of statistically significant differences between in-person and virtual review panels in this study means there are no differences in reality; the current analyses may not have detected subtler differences. Additionally, although reviewer scores and survey responses are important measures of review processes and outcomes, they do not fully capture the complexity of the review process. Analysis of additional measures, such as assessment of the quality of primary reviewers’ written critiques, systematic observations of panel discussions’ dynamics and topical focus, or in-depth interviews with reviewers would provide a more complete understanding of potential effects of switching to virtual review and may reveal more differences between panel formats. This study’s analyses were also limited in some ways by sample sizes and skewness of the data, particularly for the survey sample. For the three survey questions asked in both in-person and virtual panels, there was insufficient data to examine the interaction between panel format and reviewer type (though we did examine main effects of both panel format and reviewer type), so that remains a question for future research.

This study also has important strengths. Although some other funders involve patients or consumers in reviewing research applications [11–14], PCORI is unique in the extent to which patients and other healthcare stakeholders are included as key players in the review process. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to explore how changes in panel format may affect or be experienced differently by reviewers representing different communities (scientists, patients, and other healthcare stakeholders). Other strengths include the combination of reviewer score data and closed- and open-ended survey data, relatively large sample sizes compared to prior research, and sensitivity analyses that bolster confidence in the results.

Implications and recommendations

Overall, this study’s findings suggest that virtual review meetings, despite some challenging aspects, are a reasonable alternative to in-person review meetings that still enable funders to facilitate the essential processes and functions of research application review. Although PCORI’s switch to virtual review meetings was necessitated by the COVID-19 pandemic, this study’s findings have implications beyond this context. Funders may have multiple reasons for considering virtual review processes. For one, virtual review processes can dramatically reduce costs for funders and save reviewers time and money since travel is not required [5, 8]. Importantly, virtual review meetings may also be one way for funders to foster a review process that enables greater participation of a range of reviewers. Individuals with chronic illnesses, disabilities, financial barriers, or work or caregiving responsibilities that limit their ability to travel may be more likely to serve as a reviewer if the process is fully virtual [20]. It is important to acknowledge, however, that virtual formats may also present barriers to current or potential reviewers, such as requirements for specific equipment and sufficient computer literacy, that may affect who is willing and able to serve as a reviewer. More research is needed to understand potential differences in individuals’ interest and ability to participate in virtual vs. in-person review processes. Future research could also examine similar questions for hybrid review meetings, where some reviewers attend in-person and some participate virtually.

PCORI’s experience and this study’s findings suggest some practical considerations and recommendations for funders facilitating or considering virtual review processes. To prepare for the switch to virtual panel meetings, PCORI made significant efforts to anticipate and address difficulties that might arise, including training and practice sessions prior to the panel meetings and additional support for troubleshooting during the meetings. PCORI’s periodic survey of reviewers was also adapted to collect feedback about several aspects of the virtual process to inform improvements to subsequent virtual panels. Thoughtful preparation and commitment to evaluation and process improvement may help funders facilitate virtual review meetings that maintain the integrity of the review process and are generally acceptable to reviewers. Feedback from reviewers in this study and another recent study [7] indicated that they missed the social aspects of in-person review meetings, such as opportunities to make connections and chat informally with other reviewers. Thus, funders should consider ways to enable reviewers to build rapport in the virtual setting. This may be especially important for funders with multi-stakeholder review panels, as rapport building can help reviewers representing different communities understand each other’s perspectives. Reviewers may also experience more fatigue and trouble focusing in a virtual setting, so funders may need to adjust meeting schedules to allow for more breaks or shorter sessions over multiple days. Additionally, it is essential for funders with multi-stakeholder review panels to consider and evaluate how process changes may impact different types of reviewers to ensure the process works well for all participants.

Conclusions

This observational study adds to the literature on research application review processes by examining differences between virtual and in-person review meetings and how differences in panel format might vary across reviewers representing different communities. Overall, the study found few differences in reviewer scores or experiences between in-person and virtual panel formats and found little evidence that scientist, patient, and other stakeholder reviewers experienced the virtual setting differently. Findings suggest that despite some challenges, the virtual review format was acceptable to multiple types of reviewers and enabled PCORI to effectively facilitate its rigorous review process during the COVID-19 pandemic. This article provides useful information for other funders who are considering starting, enhancing, or continuing virtual review processes.
