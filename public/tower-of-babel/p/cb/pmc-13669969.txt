Sentio Bone‐Conduction Implant: Early Outcomes in Patients With Conductive or Mixed Hearing Loss
Authors: Katarzyna Beata Cywka, Piotr Henryk Skarzynski, Monika Baczyk, Magda Balwicka‐Kowalczyk, Henryk Skarzynski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesSentio (Oticon Medical) is an active bone‐conduction implant approved in 2024. This study evaluates initial clinical outcomes, safety, and audiological efficacy of the Sentio in patients with conductive or mixed hearing loss.MethodsA total of 28 patients (10 conductive, 18 mixed hearing loss) were implanted with Sentio. Preoperative assessments included pure‐tone audiometry and speech recognition testing in unaided and aided conditions. Postoperative evaluations of hearing thresholds and speech recognition were performed in free field. Subjective benefit was assessed using the APHAB questionnaire before and after implantation. The first follow‐up occurred 1 month after activation.ResultsAll surgeries were completed without complications. One patient required revision surgery, which was successful. No other adverse events occurred. Mean hearing threshold improved from 56.3 dB HL (unaided) and 31.7 dB HL with Ponto on a softband to 25.3 dB HL post‐activation and remained stable at 1‐month follow‐up (p < 0.001). Speech discrimination was 11.9% preoperatively (unaided) and 85.5% with Ponto. It reached 87.3% at activation and 93.3% at the follow‐up (p < 0.001). Speech perception in noise was +1.97 dB SNR before implantation (unaided) and 2.22 dB SNR (aided), −2.04 dB SNR at implant activation and −2.67 dB SNR at the follow‐up (p < 0.001 for implant conditions vs. unaided). APHAB global score decreased from 53.2 points preoperatively to 32.6 points postoperatively (p < 0.001).ConclusionSentio implantation appears effective and safe for the rehabilitation of conductive or mixed hearing loss. The first clinical results demonstrate audiological improvement and subjective benefit, supporting the potential of the system as a reliable bone‐conduction hearing solution.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: bone conduction implants, conductive hearing loss, hearing loss, mixed hearing loss, Sentio implantation

Introduction

Hearing loss is a significant public health concern and sometimes described as a disease of modern civilization. According to the World Health Organization, the condition currently affects over 1.5 billion people worldwide [1]. The number is expected to rise due to an aging population and ubiquitous exposure to noise [2]. Hearing loss results in numerous difficulties in everyday functioning, including the understanding of speech and identifying or locating sounds [3]. This often leads to a feeling of isolation and significantly reduces quality of life [4].

The choice of treatment and rehabilitation depends on the etiology and type of hearing loss. In many cases, traditional hearing aids may not be able to provide effective auditory stimulation [5], such as in people with conductive or mixed hearing loss or single‐sided deafness. In this group of patients, significantly better results can be achieved with devices using bone conduction [6, 7, 8]. This approach allows mechanical vibrations to be transmitted directly to the inner ear, bypassing the outer and middle ear [9, 10, 11].

Bypassing the outer and middle ear, which are not always intact, is the key function of implantable bone‐conduction devices. Early designs were based on a titanium screw embedded in the skull, which was connected to a processor via a percutaneous connector [12]. These devices provided satisfactory results in terms of hearing levels but caused numerous complications due to skin penetration. These included soft tissue damage, infections, skin hypertrophy, and even necrosis [13, 14]. To avoid these complications, a system featuring magnetic attachment of the processor was developed. This approach preserves skin integrity, thereby reducing the risk of complications and improving both cosmetic outcomes and user comfort. Moreover, the system can be quickly activated, allowing the patient to benefit shortly after surgery [14, 15, 16].

The first active bone‐conduction implant, Bonebridge (MED‐EL), was introduced in 2012. In 2019, another device of this type, Osia (Cochlear), was approved for clinical use. The Sentio system (Oticon Medical) is a third modern active implant that uses bone conduction. It obtained regulatory approval for sale in Europe and the United States in 2024 [17, 18]. The device consists of a Sentio 1 processor and a Sentio Ti subcutaneous implant. The system includes a coil, magnet, transducer, and screws with mounting strap. Sentio uses an electromagnetic transducer to convert electric signals into mechanical vibrations, which directly stimulate the cochlea through the skull bone. These signals are transmitted transcutaneously via inductive coupling from the external processor to the implanted component. A similar electromagnetic mechanism is employed in Bonebridge, whereas Osia utilizes a piezoelectric transducer [19, 20]. A comparison of implant dimensions shows that Osia has the largest overall profile (approximately 72 mm in length), followed by Bonebridge (approximately 64 mm), while Sentio features a more compact design (approximately 60 mm). This makes Sentio the smallest system currently available on the market [21, 22]. The small size of the Sentio Ti means that implantation can be minimally invasive. The design of the implant allows for optimal positioning during surgery with a retaining magnet, eliminating the need for skin penetration [23, 24, 25]. Nevertheless, although the magnet does reduce the risk of postoperative infection, the implant itself still poses a risk of skin complications [21].

Qualification of a patient for implantation requires an evaluation by a multidisciplinary team of specialists. The Sentio system is designed for individuals 12 years or above with conductive or mixed hearing loss provided their mean bone conduction hearing threshold does not exceed 45 dB HL. The device is also suitable for patients with single‐sided deafness whose mean air conduction hearing threshold in the contralateral ear does not exceed 20 dB HL [24]. In contrast, Osia and Bonebridge can be used in patients from the age of 5 years. For conductive and mixed hearing loss, Bonebridge has a similar indication limit of up to 45 dB HL, whereas Osia can be used in patients with bone conduction thresholds up to 55 dB HL. However, for single‐sided deafness, the indication criteria are the same across these systems [16, 20].

The aim of this study was to assess the early clinical outcomes, safety, and audiological effectiveness of the Sentio system in patients with conductive or mixed hearing loss.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards and norms of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Institute of Physiology and Pathology of Hearing (IFPS/KB/7/2020). Patients who had been implanted with a Sentio and meeting the following criteria were considered eligible for the study:

Age at time of implantation ≥ 12 years;

Age at time of implantation ≥ 12 years;

Mean bone conduction thresholds (for 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz) ≤ 45 dB HL;

Mean bone conduction thresholds (for 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz) ≤ 45 dB HL;

There were 28 participants who met these criteria.

Audiological Assessment

Audiological assessment was conducted preoperatively, on the day of implant activation and at a 1‐month follow‐up. All tests were performed in a soundproof booth using an Interacoustics AC40 audiometer, calibrated H‐39P headphones, and an Indiana Line Nano 2 loudspeaker. Each patient underwent pure‐tone audiometry testing to determine air conduction thresholds (125–8000 Hz) and bone conduction thresholds (250–4000 Hz). Speech audiometry was performed using the Demenko and Pruszewicz word lists at a sound level of 65 dB SPL. Preoperative testing in free‐field conditions was also conducted using a fitted Ponto bone‐conduction hearing aid. Hearing thresholds were assessed under aided conditions from 500 to 4000 Hz. During speech audiometry in a free field, a sound level of 65 dB SPL was used. Measurements were performed in silence and in noise using the Polish Matrix Sentence Test. This test enabled the determination of the speech recognition threshold (SRT), with SRT ≤ −2.9 dB SNR considered the normative value [26, 27]. At the first follow‐up (1 month after activation), free‐field measurements were repeated. Comparison of the results allowed an assessment to be made of the implant's effectiveness in improving hearing thresholds and speech perception.

Questionnaire Assessment

Before surgery and 1 month after activation, patients completed the APHAB (Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit) questionnaire. This tool assesses the degree of subjective hearing impairment and the impact of hearing loss on the patient's functioning. The questionnaire consists of 24 questions evaluating four areas: ease of communication in favorable conditions (EC), in background noise (BN), in reverberant environment (RV), and the acceptability of unpleasant sounds (AV). The global APHAB score is obtained by calculating the average across all subscales. Lower scores reflect fewer hearing difficulties [28, 29]. Comparing results obtained before and after implantation enabled evaluation of patient satisfaction with the implant.

Surgical Procedure

Surgical preparation for implantation began with analysis of CT scans. Bone thickness was assessed and areas less than 3–4 mm thick were considered unsuitable for implant placement. The implant needed to be placed in a bed up to 3 mm deep to maintain at least three contact points with the bone tissue. A shallower bed is possible in exceptional circumstances, although this may elevate the risk of fistula formation due to increased subcutaneous tissue tension. A skin assessment was then performed to confirm the absence of birthmarks or other skin lesions at the intended implant site. Other dermatological abnormalities that may increase the risk of implant exposure after implantation were also carefully evaluated.

A C‐shaped incision, approximately 40 mm long, was made with a scalpel. Hemostasis was then performed using monopolar coagulation using power of 20–50 W. For patients with contraindications to monopolar coagulation (e.g., a hearing implant on the other side, other metal elements in the body), bipolar coagulation was used. Subcutaneous tissue and periosteum were then dissected. The periosteum was lifted upward and slightly forward at the transducer placement site, so that the processor could be placed in the optimal position behind and above the patient's auricle. Bone beds were created using cutting and diamond drills (diameter 1.8–5.0 mm) at a maximum speed of 80,000 rpm, to a depth of approximately 3 mm. Then, after checking hemostasis and ruling out bleeding from the subcutaneous tissues and bone, the implant was placed. The implant was attached to the bone with the special 39 mm long tape supplied. Although the kit also included a 45 mm tape, the surgeon considered it too long and it was not used. Use of a longer tape is possible if the implant is recessed by less than 1–1.5 mm. After checking the fixation, the wound was closed with three layers of sutures: two subcutaneous layers (2.0 suture) and one skin layer (3.0). To prevent hematoma, a pressure dressing was applied in all cases. Figure 1 shows intraoperative photographs.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using Jamovi open‐source software. A Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. The results of pure‐tone audiometry and the APHAB questionnaire were analyzed using Student's t‐test for paired samples. Comparisons of the three time‐point measurements were carried out using ANOVA for repeated measurements, with an accepted significance level of p < 0.05. Bonferroni corrections were applied to post hoc comparisons.

Results

Research Group

A total of 28 participants were enrolled in the study, comprising 17 women (61%) and 11 men (39%) aged 13–66 years (M = 42.1; SD = 17.7), all of whom received a Sentio implant. Of the participants, 10 had conductive hearing loss (36%) and 18 had mixed hearing loss (64%). In 19 patients (68%) the procedure was performed on the right ear and in 9 patients (32%) on the left. Etiologies of hearing loss were diverse and included microtia with atresia, chronic otitis media, cholesteatomatous chronic otitis media, and congenital malformation of the middle ear. Surgical Intervention

The procedure was conducted in line with established surgical protocols. Surgery lasted on average 32.6 min (SD = 8.59). Subcutaneous tissue thickness varied between 4 and 8 mm (M = 5.9 mm; SD = 1.14). During positioning of the implant, the internal coil was bent 10° in 5 patients (18%), 15° in one patient (4%), and not at all in the remaining cases. No contact with the sigmoid sinus was observed in any participant. In 3 patients (11%) there was slight contact between the implant (1/5 of the surface) and the dura mater. Figure 2 illustrates contact between the dura mater and the bone bed prepared for the implant. In one person (4%) revision surgery was necessary due to a skin fistula over the internal part of the implant.

Hearing Thresholds

Preoperatively, the mean air conduction hearing threshold was 57.5 dB HL (SD = 12.2), and the mean bone conduction hearing threshold was 16.8 dB HL (SD = 6.5). Results are presented in Figure 3. After the procedure, no statistically significant change was observed in the mean bone conduction hearing threshold (t(25) = −1.88; p = 0.072; d = −0.37; 95% CI [−0.76, 0.03]), which remained at 18.7 dB HL (SD = 8.51).

Mean air conduction and bone conduction hearing thresholds before surgery. Bars represent standard deviations.

Before implantation, the mean unaided air conduction hearing threshold for frequencies from 500 to 4000 Hz was 56.3 dB HL (SD = 13.4). With the Ponto hearing aid on a softband, the mean threshold was 31.7 dB HL (SD = 6.81). On the day of implant activation, the mean threshold was 25.3 dB HL (SD = 8.1), and at the first follow‐up after 1 month, it was 25.2 dB HL (SD = 6.39). Repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed significant differences across measurements (F (3,81) = 96.5; p < 0.001; η 2 p = 0.71; 95% CI [0.59, 0.80]). Hearing thresholds measured with the Ponto on a softband and those obtained after implant activation differed significantly from preoperative unaided threshold (p < 0.001). A statistically significant improvement was also observed between the aided preoperative threshold and the thresholds obtained after implant activation (p < 0.001). Figure 4 presents a comparison of hearing thresholds.

Speech Discrimination

The mean unaided speech discrimination score before surgery at a presentation level of 65 dB SPL was 11.9% (SD = 19). Preoperatively, with Ponto hearing aid on a softband, the mean score was 85.5% (SD = 11.2). On the day of implant activation, the score was 87.3% (SD = 13) and at the first follow‐up it reached 93.3% (SD = 6.79). A repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of measurement condition on speech discrimination scores (F (3,72) = 252; p < 0.001; η 2 p = 0.86; 95% CI [0.79, 0.91]). Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction showed that unaided preoperative scores were significantly lower compared with all other scores (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between score obtained preoperatively with Ponto on a softband and those obtained with the Sentio system, either on the day of implant activation or at one‐month follow up (p > 0.05). The speech discrimination scores are presented in Figure 5.

Mean percentage of speech discrimination (at a presentation level of 65 dB SPL) before implantation (unaided and with Ponto on a softband), on the day of implant activation and at the first follow‐up. Bars represent standard deviations.

Before surgery, the mean score of the Polish Matrix Sentence Test was +1.97 dB SNR (SD = 4.99). The mean score achieved using the Ponto hearing aid on a softband was −2.22 dB SNR (SD = 2.65), while on the day of implant activation the score was −2.04 dB SNR (SD = 2.01). One month later, at the first follow‐up, the score improved to −2.67 dB SNR (SD = 1.71). Statistical analysis indicated that the scores obtained with the Ponto, on the day of implant activation, and at the first follow‐up were significantly better than preoperative scores (F (2,38) = 16.9; p < 0.001; η 2 p = 0.47; 95% CI [0.27, 0.63]). No statistically significant differences were observed between the aided preoperative score (Ponto on softband), implant activation, and the first follow‐up (p > 0.05). APHAB Questionnaire

Before surgery, the mean global score (GS) of the APHAB questionnaire was 53.2 (SD = 15.6). A statistically significant improvement was noted at the first follow‐up (t(18) = 5.44; p < 0.001; d = 1.25; 95% CI [0.63, 1.84]), as the mean score decreased by 32.6 (SD = 17.8). Numerical improvements were observed across all questionnaire subscales, reflecting a meaningful reduction in hearing difficulties following implantation. The changes in the EC, BN, and RV subscales were statistically significant: EC (t(18) = 5.47; p < 0.001; d = 1.26; 95% CI [0.64, 1.85]), BN (t(18) = 5.58; p < 0.001; d = 1.28; 95% CI [0.66, 1.88]), RV (t(18) = 4.00; p < 0.001; d = 0.92; 95% CI [0.37, 1.45]). The difference in the acceptance of unpleasant sounds (AV) did not reach statistical significance (t(18) = 0.65; p = 0.522; d = 0.15; 95% CI [−0.30, 0.60]). Details are presented in Figure 7.

Discussion

The Sentio implant was commercially introduced in 2024. To date, only a limited number of studies on this system are available, all of which were conducted prior to its official approval for clinical use. For this reason, data on its long‐term efficacy and safety remain limited. However, Sentio belongs to the group of active bone‐conduction implants, and so results obtained for other devices in this category may provide important insights into expected clinical benefits. Studies on implants such as Bonebridge and Osia indicate the effectiveness and safety of such solutions in the treatment of mixed hearing loss, conductive hearing loss, and single‐sided deafness [30, 31]. Nevertheless, additional studies specifically evaluating the Sentio system are warranted.

A 2023 study by Aukema and colleagues [25] presented the results of using the Sentio implant in 50 adults with conductive or mixed hearing loss and single‐sided deafness. The duration of the surgery ranged from 45 to 85 min and no adverse events occurred during any of the procedures. The authors emphasize that the implant's design facilitates a straightforward surgical procedure and that the patients' initial hearing experiences following device activation were positive [25].

Hol and colleagues [32] conducted an analysis of data collected across six European centers between 2022 and 2023. The study included 51 adults with conductive or mixed hearing loss and single‐sided deafness who were implanted with the Sentio device. The mean duration of surgery was 58 min (SD = 14) and the mean subcutaneous tissue thickness was 6 mm. Skin thickness is relevant in this active transcutaneous bone‐conduction implant, as signal and power transmission rely on electromagnetic inductive coupling across soft tissues. Increased distance between external and internal coils leads to reduced energy transfer efficiency and may attenuate device output. Conversely, insufficient tissue thickness may result in increased implant prominence or protrusion [33]. In the referenced study, contact between the implant and the dura mater was not found in any patient. A comparison of preoperative test results with those obtained 3 months after implantation showed a statistically significant improvement in hearing thresholds, with a mean functional gain of +32.8 dB. After surgery, patients achieved a mean speech discrimination threshold of 97.9%, an improvement of 51.6 percentage points compared to the unaided preoperative score. In addition, 96.1% of respondents indicated a positive change in their quality of life [32].

Active bone‐conduction implants, including the Sentio system, are indicated for the treatment of various conditions, including congenital ear malformations. However, as clinical evidence on Sentio remains limited, published data on comparable active bone‐conduction systems, such as Bonebridge, are referenced to provide context for expected outcomes in this group of patients. The article in 2025 by Piecuch and colleagues [34] describes the case of a 14‐year‐old child with a double internal auditory canal. On the day of Bonebridge implant activation, the patient achieved a Matrix test score of −10.3 dB SNR, compared with +3.5 dB SNR preoperatively and −0.4 dB SNR with a fitted Ponto bone‐conduction hearing aid on a softband. Improvements were also noted in the APHAB questionnaire scores on the EC, BN, and RV subscales [34]. Volgger and colleagues [35] conducted a comparable assessment of the effectiveness of bone‐conduction implants in patients with congenital ear malformations. The study group consisted of 18 patients with congenital microtia and atresia. After implantation, the mean hearing thresholds decreased from unaided 69.2 to 33.4 dB HL, while speech discrimination increased from unaided 22.9% before surgery to 87.1% after surgery. Of the 18 patients, 15 reported a subjective improvement in their overall hearing experience [35].

As an active bone‐conduction implant, Sentio is also suitable for patients who have undergone cholesteatoma surgery, including radical mastoid cavity [24]. The effectiveness of an active bone‐conduction implant in a patient with cholesteatoma post‐radical surgery was evaluated in 2025 by Cywka and colleagues [36]. The preoperative Matrix test score in a 10‐year‐old female patient prior to Bonebridge implantation was +4.5 dB SNR, while after the procedure it improved to −2.3 dB SNR. The speech discrimination score increased from 0% preoperatively to 80% on the day of activation. The mean hearing threshold also improved significantly [36]. Another study conducted in 2018 by Rader and colleagues [37] also confirmed the effectiveness of Bonebridge in patients with hearing loss caused by cholesteatoma. Over 80% of the participants had previously undergone various ear surgeries. Analysis of data from 61 patients, including 17 with cholesteatoma, revealed a mean functional gain of 26.2 dB in warble‐tone audiometry and a mean increase of 53.9% in speech discrimination [37]. Study by Carnevale et al. also presents audiological outcomes of Bonebridge implantation in 52 patients with conductive or mixed hearing loss, all of whom had previously undergone ear surgery. No intraoperative or early postoperative complications were observed. One case of implant exposure was noted in a patient with a prior face lift. The mean gain in free‐field audiometry was 31.83 dB, and the world recognition score at a presentation level of 50 dB improved from 11% unaided to 85% after implantation [38].

In our study group, minor intraoperative complications were noted, including contact with the dura mater in 3 cases (11%). One patient (4%) with a history of multiple previous ear surgeries required revision surgery due to postoperative skin fistula over the internal part of the implant, which developed several weeks after device activation. This revision rate is consistent with a previous report by Skarżyński et al. who observed a revision rate of 4.8% in a cohort of 355 patients implanted with a different active bone‐conduction implant—Bonebridge [6]. During the revision surgery of our patient, the edges of the perforation were debrided. A mobile flap was dissected, mobilized, and positioned over the perforation edges. The appendix showed no signs of inflammation. The postoperative course was uneventful.

The findings of previously cited studies align with the observations reported here. We found that implantation of the active bone‐conduction Sentio device led to significant improvements in both hearing thresholds (functional gain of 31 dB) and speech discrimination (increase of 75.4 percentage points compared with preoperative unaided condition) in patients with diverse etiologies of hearing loss. Speech reception thresholds in noise also improved, from +1.97 dB SNR preoperatively unaided to −2.04 dB SNR after implant activation, with postoperative results being comparable to the preoperative aided condition (−2.22 dB SNR with Ponto on a softband). Data from patient‐completed questionnaires further indicated enhanced quality of life, high satisfaction with using the device, and a reduction in self‐reported hearing difficulties, with a mean reduction in the APHAB global score of 20.6 points. These findings reflect a meaningful improvement in everyday listening function, particularly in communication ease, background noise and reverberation. The lack of significant change in the aversiveness subscale suggests that the intervention primarily improved auditor function without substantially altering perceived loudness discomfort. This pattern has been observed in some previous APHAB‐based evaluations of bone‐conduction devices [38, 39].

Certain limitations of the Sentio implant should also be considered. The system is intended for patients with bone‐conduction thresholds up to 45 dB HL, which may limit its applicability in cases of more severe hearing loss. Implantation may require soft tissue reduction when tissue thickness over the receiver coil exceeds 9 mm, as it may negatively affect system performance [40]. Sentio has several safety‐related limitations, including restrictions regarding electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). Moreover, MRI examinations in patients implanted with Sentio are generally possible only at 1.5 T, provided that all specified safety conditions are strictly followed. In addition, MRI image quality is compromised in the region surrounding the implant, with artifacts up to 18 cm from the implant [40, 41].

Conclusion

Implantation of the Sentio active bone‐conduction implant is associated with marked improvements in hearing thresholds, speech discrimination, and overall quality of life. Our results suggest it is a safe and effective treatment option for conductive or mixed hearing loss in patients with diverse etiologies. Nevertheless, additional research with larger study populations and extended follow‐up is necessary to comprehensively assess the long‐term efficacy and safety of the device.
