Frailty and social support in older orthopedic surgical patients: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Kezban Koraş Sözen, Songül Güngör, Berk Karter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is a common and clinically significant condition among older adults undergoing surgical procedures and is associated with adverse postoperative outcomes. In addition to biological vulnerability, psychosocial factors such as social support may influence recovery processes. However, studies examining the relationship between frailty and perceived social support in older orthopedic surgical patients remain limited.AimThis study aimed to examine the relationship between frailty and perceived social support in older orthopedic surgical patients and to determine how these variables differ according to sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted with 241 older orthopedic surgical patients aged 65 years and older who underwent orthopedic surgical procedures. Data were collected using the Descriptive Characteristics Form, the Edmonton Frail Scale, and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support. Descriptive statistics, independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and Spearman correlation analysis were used. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsParticipants showed clinically relevant levels of frailty, with many classified as mildly, moderately, or severely frail, and reported moderate to high levels of perceived social support. Social support was highest from family and lowest from a significant other. A substantial proportion of patients were identified as having mild, moderate, or severe frailty. No significant relationship was found between total perceived social support and frailty; however, family support was significantly and inversely associated with frailty (p < 0.05). Frailty levels were significantly higher among female participants and those with lower educational levels (p < 0.05).ConclusionFrailty remains a prevalent condition among older orthopedic surgical patients, even in the presence of relatively high levels of perceived social support. Family-based support was significantly and inversely associated with frailty; however, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of a protective or causal effect. Integrating frailty screening with the assessment of social support in perioperative care may support the development of individualized and patient-centered nursing interventions. Future studies should explore causal relationships using longitudinal designs and include detailed clinical variables such as surgical type.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07952-3.

Introduction

With advances in modern medicine, average life expectancy has increased, leading to a growing proportion of older adults worldwide [1, 2]. As population demographics shift toward older age groups, the mean age of individuals undergoing surgical procedures has also risen [3]. It is estimated that by 2030, one in five individuals aged 70 years and older will undergo at least one surgical intervention [3, 4]. Compared with younger adults, older individuals are more vulnerable to surgery and anesthesia, and the rate of orthopedic surgical procedures among this population continues to increase steadily [1, 5, 6]. These changes highlight the need for geriatric-sensitive perioperative approaches that consider not only clinical risks but also functional and psychosocial vulnerabilities.

Recovery following orthopedic surgery is a multidimensional process involving physical, psychological, and social restoration [4, 7, 8]. In older adults, postoperative recovery may be further complicated by age-related physiological decline, comorbidities, and reduced adaptive capacity. Quality of recovery is a critical health outcome that reflects the patient’s ability to regain control during the postoperative transition and return to preoperative levels of independence or dependence in daily life [9]. Therefore, identifying factors that may influence recovery quality is important in older orthopedic surgical patients.

Frailty is one of the key factors affecting recovery in older adults. It is characterized by a decline in multiple physiological systems and increased vulnerability to stressors, leading to adverse outcomes such as functional dependence or death [10]. Frailty has also been associated with impaired mobility, disability, accidental falls, hospitalization, morbidity, mortality, and postoperative outcomes across various surgical specialties. Approximately 10% of surgical patients, particularly older adults, exhibit features of frailty [10, 11]. Importantly, frailty is a dynamic and potentially modifiable condition, which emphasizes the importance of early identification in surgical populations.

Physical health, psychological status, and social context are closely interrelated. Physical illness may negatively affect emotions, thoughts, social relationships, and overall quality of life [12, 13]. Conversely, psychosocial factors may influence physiological resilience and recovery capacity. Accordingly, older surgical patients should be evaluated from a holistic perspective that includes physical, social, emotional, economic, cultural, and spiritual dimensions [13].

Social support refers to the material and emotional assistance provided by close social networks such as family members, spouses, and friends during difficult or stressful periods [14]. Adequate social support has positive effects on health protection and promotion, strengthens coping abilities, supports recovery from illness, improves quality of life, and reduces social isolation [14–16]. It may also help patients cope with symptoms, reduce hospitalizations and disease-related mortality, and decrease the risk of anxiety and depression [17–20]. These findings suggest that social support may be an important psychosocial factor in the care and recovery of older orthopedic surgical patients.

Although frailty and social support are both relevant to postoperative recovery, studies simultaneously examining the relationship between frailty and perceived social support among older orthopedic surgical patients remain limited [20, 21]. Existing research has predominantly focused on frailty in relation to postoperative mortality, complications, or other clinical outcomes, whereas studies that jointly evaluate frailty with psychosocial factors such as social support in this patient population are scarce [20, 22]. Understanding this relationship may provide valuable insight for comprehensive, multidisciplinary, and patient-centered perioperative assessment and care.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the relationship between perceived social support and frailty in older orthopedic surgical patients and to determine how these variables differ according to sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. It was hypothesized that higher levels of perceived social support would be associated with lower levels of frailty. The findings are expected to contribute to a more holistic understanding of older orthopedic surgical patients and to inform clinical decision-making in geriatric orthopedic surgical care.

Methods

Study type, place, and time

This cross-sectional study was conducted among patients who underwent orthopedic surgical procedures at the Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic of XXX University Training and Research Hospital between January 1 and November 30, 2025. The study sample consisted of 241 patients who met the inclusion criteria, volunteered to participate during the specified period, were aged 65 years and older, were literate, and had intact orientation to time and place. Patients with cognitive impairments that could hinder communication were excluded from the study.

Participants were assessed during the inpatient postoperative period, after orthopedic surgery, when they were clinically stable and able to communicate effectively.

The study focused on older adults undergoing orthopedic surgical procedures as a broad clinical category, without further classification based on surgical type. Because detailed procedure types were not recorded as analyzable categories in the dataset, a descriptive overview of the main procedure types could not be provided. Therefore, the sample should be interpreted as a clinically typical heterogeneous group of older orthopedic surgical patients in this setting, rather than as a statistically representative sample by procedure type.

The study sample was determined using a non-probability convenience sampling method, including all eligible patients who were admitted during the data collection period.

Accordingly, the sample should not be considered statistically representative of all older orthopedic surgical patients, and the findings should be interpreted within the context of the study setting and eligibility criteria.

Prior to data collection, a sample size estimation was performed based on an expected moderate correlation (r ≈ 0.30), a power of 80%, and a significance level of 0.05, indicating that a minimum of approximately 84 participants would be sufficient. The final sample size of 241 participants was therefore considered adequate to support the statistical power and precision of the planned correlational analyses within this sample. However, this sample size calculation and the achieved sample size do not by themselves ensure statistical representativeness or external validity, particularly given the single-center design and non-probability convenience sampling strategy.

The study was initiated after obtaining approval from the Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024/18-40; Date: 02.10.2024) and receiving institutional permission from the hospital where the study was conducted. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) being aged 65 years or older, (ii) having undergone an orthopedic surgical procedure, (iii) being able to communicate verbally, (iv) being literate, and (v) having intact orientation to time and place. Patients who agreed to participate voluntarily were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria included: (i) presence of diagnosed cognitive impairment, such as dementia or delirium, that prevented the participant from understanding the study questions or completing the self-report questionnaires reliably; (ii) severe hearing or speech problems that prevented effective communication and reliable completion of the questionnaires; (iii) critical clinical condition requiring intensive care; and (iv) refusal to participate in the study.

Measures

Descriptive characteristics form

The Descriptive Characteristics Form was developed by the researchers specifically for this study, based on a review of the relevant literature [1–3]. The form includes items designed to collect information on patients’ sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, sex, marital status, educational level, and economic status.

In addition, the form comprises clinical and health-related variables, including previous surgical history, type of anesthesia, presence of chronic diseases, and living arrangements, to enable a comprehensive assessment of factors potentially associated with frailty and perceived social support.

An English version of the Descriptive Characteristics Form is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1).

Edmonton frail scale

The Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS) was developed by Rolfson et al. in 2006 to assess frailty in older adults. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Aygör et al. in 2013 [23, 24]. The scale consists of nine domains that are included in comprehensive geriatric assessment and are considered key determinants of frailty. These domains include cognitive status, general health status, functional independence, social support, medication use, nutrition, mood, continence, and functional performance. The scale comprises a total of 11 items. Cognitive status is assessed using the “clock drawing test,” and functional performance is evaluated using the “Timed Up and Go test” [23, 24].

The total score of the scale is calculated based on the sum of the 11 items covering the nine frailty domains. Possible scores range from 0 to 20, with higher total scores indicating greater severity of frailty [23, 24].

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) was developed by Zimet et al. in 1990, and its Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Eker et al. in 2001 [25, 26]. The scale is a self-report questionnaire that evaluates individuals’ subjective perceptions of social support received from their family, friends, and a significant other. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

Scores obtained from the three subscales are summed separately to yield subscale scores, and the total scale score is calculated by summing all subscale scores. Higher scores on the total scale and subscales indicate higher levels of perceived social support [25, 26].

Data collection

The data of the study were collected using the Descriptive Characteristics Form to determine sociodemographic characteristics, the Edmonton Frail Scale, and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support. The data collection process was carried out by the researchers through face-to-face interviews with patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria.

Data were collected at a single postoperative time point during the inpatient postoperative period, when patients were clinically stable and able to communicate effectively.

During data collection, the purpose of the study was explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained based on voluntary participation. The questionnaires were administered by the researchers using language that the patients could easily understand, and the forms were completed in accordance with the participants’ responses. Throughout the data collection process, participants’ privacy and confidentiality were strictly maintained, and they were informed that the collected data would be used solely for scientific purposes.

All data were anonymized and stored securely, and no identifying personal information was included in the dataset.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0. Descriptive statistics, including number, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were used to summarize the participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics as well as scale scores. Prior to inferential analyses, the distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Age and the MSPSS total score were approximately normally distributed, whereas the EFS total score and the MSPSS subscale scores (family, friends, and significant other) showed deviations from normality. Considering the sample size and the absence of severe distributional violations in variables used for group comparisons, parametric tests were applied for group comparisons of mean scale scores.

For comparisons between two independent groups (gender, marital status, presence of chronic disease, having children, and previous surgical history), the independent samples t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups (educational level and type of anesthesia), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. The relationship between frailty and perceived social support was assessed using Spearman rank correlation analysis rather than Pearson correlation because the EFS total score and MSPSS subscale scores deviated from normality and the analysis aimed to evaluate monotonic relationships between scale-derived variables.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. No separate alpha level was applied for correlation analyses. Formal correction for multiple testing (e.g., Bonferroni correction) was not applied because the subgroup comparisons and correlation analyses were exploratory; therefore, the findings were interpreted cautiously, with attention to the direction and magnitude of associations as well as statistical significance.

Results

The majority of the participants were women, accounting for 61.0%, and 82.2% were married. With regard to educational level, 44.0% of the participants had completed primary school, while 70.1% perceived their economic status as moderate. It was determined that 94.6% of the participants had children and 60.6% were living with their spouse. Chronic disease was present in 73.4% of the participants, and 82.6% had a history of previous surgery. In terms of anesthesia type, spinal anesthesia was administered to 79.7% of the participants. The mean age of the participants was 70.47 ± 5.72 years, with ages ranging from 65 to 88 years (Table 1).

The mean total Edmonton Frail Scale score of female participants (7.87 ± 3.03) was significantly higher than that of male participants (5.77 ± 2.71) (t = 5.48; p < 0.001). In contrast, female participants had a significantly higher mean total score on the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (68.43 ± 9.14) compared with males (65.54 ± 7.64) (t = 2.55; p = 0.012).

Participants with a history of previous surgery had a significantly higher mean Edmonton Frail Scale score (7.56 ± 2.91) than those without prior surgical experience (4.62 ± 2.71) (t = 6.03; p < 0.001). However, no significant difference was observed between these groups in terms of total perceived social support scores (t = − 0.87; p = 0.388).

There was no statistically significant difference in mean Edmonton Frail Scale scores between married participants (7.01 ± 2.96) and single participants (7.26 ± 3.63) (t = − 0.42; p = 0.673). Similarly, total perceived social support scores did not differ significantly between married (66.92 ± 8.08) and single participants (69.07 ± 11.01) (t = − 1.48; p = 0.141).

Participants with children had a significantly higher mean frailty score (7.21 ± 3.02) than those without children (4.31 ± 2.93) (t = 3.37; p = 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was found between these groups with respect to total perceived social support scores (t = − 1.68; p = 0.094).

The mean Edmonton Frail Scale score was significantly higher among participants with chronic disease (7.94 ± 2.76) compared with those without chronic disease (4.59 ± 2.56) (t = 8.46; p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed in perceived social support scores between the two groups (t = 1.30; p = 0.194).

A statistically significant difference in mean Edmonton Frail Scale scores was observed according to type of anesthesia (F = 3.62; p = 0.028). The highest frailty scores were found among patients who received general anesthesia (7.73 ± 3.69), while the lowest scores were observed among those who underwent nerve block anesthesia (5.52 ± 2.50). In contrast, no significant differences were found in total perceived social support scores across anesthesia types (F = 0.51; p = 0.602).

Finally, a statistically significant difference was identified in mean Edmonton Frail Scale scores according to educational level (F = 32.22; p < 0.001), with participants who were literate without formal education exhibiting the highest frailty scores (9.58 ± 2.42), and frailty scores generally decreasing as educational level increased. Total perceived social support scores did not differ significantly by educational level (F = 0.67; p = 0.650) (Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined perceived social support and frailty among older orthopedic surgical patients, with particular attention to the source of perceived support. The findings showed that participants had moderate to high levels of perceived social support and clinically relevant levels of frailty. Among the subdimensions of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, support was perceived at the highest level from family and at the lowest level from a significant other. These findings suggest that social support in older orthopedic surgical patients should not be interpreted only through the total MSPSS score, but also through the specific sources from which support is perceived.

The main contribution of this study is the use of perceived social support as a psychosocial construct in the context of frailty among older orthopedic surgical patients. Previous research in orthopedic populations has mainly emphasized clinical outcomes, functional status, complications, and mortality, whereas psychosocial resources such as perceived social support have received relatively less attention. Tseng et al. (2025) reported that higher perceived social support among postoperative patients with hip fractures was associated with better recovery-related indicators, including range of motion, muscle strength, nutritional status, depressive symptoms, and quality of life [27]. Although the present study did not measure postoperative recovery outcomes, the findings support the relevance of considering perceived social support as part of the broader psychosocial context of older orthopedic surgical patients.

In the orthopedic context, social support may be particularly important because older patients often require assistance with mobility, daily activities, treatment adherence, rehabilitation attendance, and adaptation to temporary or permanent functional limitations. However, social support should not be understood only as the presence of someone available to help. The MSPSS measures the patient’s subjective perception of support from family, friends, and a significant other, rather than the objective amount, frequency, or quality of actual assistance received. This distinction is important because perceived support may differ from what is commonly documented in healthcare records, such as marital status, living arrangement, or the presence of a caregiver. A patient may live with family members but still perceive inadequate emotional support, whereas another may live alone but perceive strong support from relatives or friends. Therefore, perceived social support provides a patient-centered perspective that may not be fully captured by routine clinical or demographic measures.

One notable finding of this study was that perceived support from a significant other was lower than perceived support from family and friends. In the MSPSS, the “significant other” dimension may include a spouse, partner, or another emotionally important person; therefore, this finding requires careful interpretation. In older adults, lower support from a significant other may be related to widowhood, spousal illness, advanced age of the spouse, dependency within the couple, or changes in family roles. In the Turkish cultural context, caregiving responsibilities for older adults are often shared within the extended family, especially by adult children and close relatives, rather than being limited to the spouse or partner. This may partly explain why family support was perceived more strongly than support from a significant other. Thus, the findings may reflect not only individual relationship patterns but also cultural expectations regarding family-based care for older adults.

In the present study, the total MSPSS score was not significantly associated with frailty, whereas family support was significantly and inversely associated with frailty. This suggests that the relationship between social support and frailty may depend more on the source and perceived relevance of support than on the overall level of perceived support. Similarly, Mao et al. (2025) emphasized that the effect of social support on health outcomes may depend on whether the support matches individuals’ specific needs [28]. Yu et al. (2024) also suggested that the influence of social support on frailty may be related not only to its quantity but also to its source, continuity, and functional characteristics [29]. Accordingly, the present findings indicate that family support may be particularly relevant for older orthopedic surgical patients, but the cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions about whether family support reduces frailty or whether less frail patients perceive greater family support.

The observed association between family support and lower frailty is also consistent with broader literature suggesting that social relationships may play a protective role in later life. Levasseur et al. (2025) reported that perceived social support buffered the negative association between frailty and life satisfaction [30]. Chu et al. (2023) found that emotional and instrumental social support from both within and outside the family reduced the risk of developing physical frailty in later life [31]. Although these studies support the importance of social support, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than causation. They also highlight the need to distinguish between different dimensions of support, including emotional reassurance, practical help, companionship, and perceived availability of assistance.

Frailty remained common in this sample despite moderate to high levels of perceived social support. This finding indicates that perceived support alone may not be sufficient to explain frailty status in older orthopedic surgical patients. Frailty is a multidimensional condition shaped by biological, functional, psychological, and socioeconomic factors. In the present study, frailty scores were higher among women and among patients with lower educational levels. Similar findings have been reported by Zeidan et al. (2023) and Corbi et al. (2019), who found higher frailty levels among women [32, 33]. Previous studies have also shown an inverse relationship between educational level and frailty [34, 35]. These findings suggest that frailty should be interpreted within a broader social and clinical context rather than as an outcome of social support alone.

From a clinical perspective, the findings support the value of assessing perceived social support, particularly family support, alongside frailty in older orthopedic surgical patients. Such assessment may help multidisciplinary teams identify patients who may require additional psychosocial or discharge-related support. However, because this study was cross-sectional and did not measure postoperative recovery outcomes, the findings should not be interpreted as showing that social support improves recovery or reduces frailty. Instead, they suggest that perceived social support, especially family-based support, is associated with frailty status and may be an important component of holistic assessment in this population.

Future studies should examine perceived social support and frailty longitudinally in orthopedic surgical patients and should include postoperative outcomes such as functional recovery, complications, readmission, rehabilitation participation, and quality of life. Qualitative studies may also help clarify how older patients define support, how they distinguish support from family, friends, and significant others, and how cultural expectations influence their perceptions of being supported. Such research would provide a more detailed understanding of the role of perceived social support in the perioperative experience of older orthopedic surgical patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Although all participants underwent orthopedic surgical procedures, detailed procedure type and surgical complexity (e.g., hip fracture surgery, elective arthroplasty, or minor orthopedic procedures) were not categorized or adjusted for in the analyses. Including a heterogeneous orthopedic surgical population may reflect clinical realism and support ecological validity; however, this heterogeneity may also introduce unmeasured confounding, because differences in surgery type and complexity may influence both frailty levels and postoperative recovery-related outcomes. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted cautiously in relation to specific orthopedic procedure groups, and the results cannot determine whether the observed patterns differ according to surgical type or procedure complexity.

The study was conducted in a single center using a non-probability convenience sampling method, which may have introduced selection bias and may limit the external validity of the findings. Although the achieved sample size supported statistical power and precision for the planned analyses, it should not be interpreted as evidence of representativeness or external validity. Because the sample included only literate, oriented, communicative, and clinically stable older patients who were willing to participate postoperatively, patients with cognitive impairment, severe communication problems, or critical illness were not represented. In particular, excluding patients with cognitive impairment or severe communication difficulties that prevented reliable completion of the questionnaires is an important limitation, because cognitive impairment is a key component of frailty. Although this exclusion was necessary to ensure the validity of self-reported data, especially for the assessment of perceived social support, it may have led to an underestimation of frailty levels and limits the generalizability of the findings to older orthopedic surgical patients with cognitive impairment or substantial communication difficulties. Therefore, the findings should be generalized cautiously beyond similar older orthopedic surgical patients and comparable clinical settings. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between frailty and perceived social support.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the interaction between frailty and social support in older orthopedic surgical patients and highlights the need for future research incorporating detailed surgical procedure variables, procedure complexity, and longitudinal designs. Future studies should also consider alternative data collection strategies, such as caregiver-assisted assessments or clinically validated cognitive screening approaches, to include older orthopedic surgical patients with cognitive impairment where ethically and methodologically appropriate.

Conclusion

This study highlights that frailty remains a prevalent and clinically significant condition among older orthopedic surgical patients, despite the presence of moderate to high levels of perceived social support. The findings indicate that overall perceived social support was not significantly associated with frailty, whereas family-based support showed a significant inverse association with frailty. This association should not be interpreted as evidence that family support reduces or prevents frailty. The results underscore the importance of adopting a holistic and patient-centered approach in perioperative care, integrating both biological and psychosocial dimensions. In particular, routine frailty screening combined with the assessment of social support resources may facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals and support the development of individualized care plans.

From a clinical perspective, strengthening family involvement in the care process and implementing frailty-focused, multidisciplinary interventions may support more individualized perioperative care. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence emphasizing the need for comprehensive geriatric care models in surgical settings.

Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and include detailed clinical variables, such as surgical type and complexity, to better understand the causal pathways between frailty and social support. Such efforts may support the development of more targeted and effective interventions for older surgical patients.
