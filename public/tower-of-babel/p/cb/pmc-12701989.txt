Efficacy and safety of BRAF-MEK inhibitors in treatment of Chinese patients with papillary craniopharyngioma: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rui Han, Xiaorong Yan, Boyuan Yao, Boyu Cai, Nidan Qiao, Zengyi Ma, Zhen Ye, Xufang Gu, Zhao Ye, Buqing Liang, Zhengyuan Chen, Wenqiang He, Yifei Yu, Xiaoyun Cao, Haixia Cheng, Hong Chen, Yongfei Wang, Hongying Ye, Zhaoyun Zhang, Yao Zhao, Ruofan Huang, Qilin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious research supports the efficacy of BRAF-MEK inhibitor therapy for papillary craniopharyngiomas (PCPs). This retrospective study of 30 Chinese PCP patients evaluated tumor response and safety outcomes during treatment. All patients maintained 100% progression-free and overall survival throughout the follow-up. Tumor measurements revealed consistent reductions over time, with maximum diameter decreasing by 45.81% (31.01%–66.67%) at 1 month, 56.26% (46.67%–66.67%) at 3 months, and 59.50% (46.84%–69.94%) at the final follow-up. Similar patterns emerged for both solid and cystic components. Notably, neoadjuvant therapy demonstrated superior efficacy (60.35% reduction) compared to adjuvant approaches (42.90% reduction) at 1 month, particularly for solid tumor portions. Adverse events occurred more frequently during the initial 3-month period. These findings highlight the clinical value of BRAF-MEK inhibitor combinations for PCP treatment while suggesting potential limitations of extended targeted therapy duration.Keywords: MT: Regular Issue, papillary craniopharyngiomas, PCPs, BRAF-MEK inhibitors, nature history, efficacy, safety

Introduction

Craniopharyngioma is a rare and difficult-to-treat epithelial brain tumor, with an incidence of 0.5–2.0 per 1,000,000 persons.1,2 Craniopharyngiomas can be classified into two histological subtypes: adamantinomatous and papillary, with BRAF V600E mutations found in papillary types and activating β-catenin (CTNNB1) mutations in adamantinomatous types.3,4,5,6 This locally aggressive tumor typically displaces critical structures such as the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, third ventricle, and brainstem, leading to significant neurological dysfunction.7,8,9

Primary treatment for craniopharyngioma usually involves surgical resection. However, total resection is difficult to achieve due to the location of the tumors.10 Postoperative radiation therapy is commonly employed to assist with local control, but it carries risks of long-term adverse effects on surrounding tissues, particularly vasculopathy and hypopituitarism, which contribute to post-treatment complications in most patients.7,11,12

Due to the limitations of the aforementioned treatments, targeted therapy researches for craniopharyngioma are widely conducted.13,14,15,16,17,18,19,20 Among them, the therapy of papillary craniopharyngiomas (PCPs) made breakthrough progress due to its BRAF mutation.21 BRAF inhibitors, such as vemurafenib and dabrafenib, specifically block the activity of mutated BRAF-related proteins, thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation. However, tumor cells tend to develop resistance to BRAF inhibition by reactivating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/ERK pathway through alternative mechanisms. To overcome this, MEK inhibitors, such as trametinib or cobimetinib, are used in combination with BRAF inhibitors. MEK is a downstream effector in MAPK/ERK pathway, and dual inhibition provides a more comprehensive blockade, delaying resistance and improving treatment efficacy.22

Inspired by the clinical success of BRAF and MEK inhibitors in cancers with BRAF V600E mutations,23,24 BRAF and MEK inhibitor therapy has shown promise in treating recurrent BRAF V600E-mutated PCPs.13 A recent single-group, phase 2 clinical trial conducted on 16 newly diagnosed PCPs confirmed the efficacy of this treatment, with 15 of 16 patients having a partial response (PR) to the BRAF and MEK inhibitors.15 The adverse events (AEs) were also observed and led to the termination of treatment for three patients. Another retrospective French multicenter study further validated its therapeutic effect in PCPs with BRAF V600E mutations, with 94% of patients showing a PR or better to targeted therapy.13 There are also several case reports demonstrating the benefits of BRAF and MEK inhibitor therapy in isolated PCPs.25,26,27

Recent studies have shown encouraging results in initial trials of BRAF and MEK inhibitor therapy for newly diagnosed BRAF-mutated PCP without prior radiation treatment, highlighting the potential of targeted therapies in this challenging disease. However, these studies have primarily focused on European populations, and there is currently no clear research on the efficacy of BRAF and MEK inhibitor therapy in Chinese patients with PCPs.28 Besides, the relationship between the duration of targeted therapy and the occurrence of drug resistance or AEs is not yet clear.13 Therefore, this study aims to retrospectively analyze the effectiveness and safety of BRAF and MEK inhibitor therapy in Chinese patients with PCPs, providing insights to guide precision treatment strategies for this population.

Results

Patients and total efficacy

From January 2023 to November 2024, we retrospectively collected information from 30 patients in our hospital (Table 1). This project received the approval of the Huashan Institutional Review Board (KY2022-567), and all participants have signed informed consent forms. Among them, 4 patients achieved a subtotal response (SR, a diameter reduction ≥80%) at the last follow-up, with 26 achieving a PR (a decrease ranging from 20% to 80% in the diameter, Figure 1A). None of the patients showed tumor progression. Twenty one patients had finished the BRAF-MEK inhibitions medication at the time of the last follow-up, and the remaining patients continued to receive the medication (Figure S1). All 30 patients achieved an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance-status score of either 0 or 1. Thus, the cohort for this study consisted of 30 patients who were followed up over the course of 23 months. The median age was 48.7 years (range, 7–72), with one pediatric patient. Seventeen patients had undergone more than 3 months of targeted therapy, and the remaining patients received 3 months of medication administration (Figure 1B).

The primary endpoint was evaluated with the use of the diameter measurement data from the baseline, first 1 month, first 3 months, and the last follow-up of BRAF-MEK inhibitor combination therapy, separately. This retrospective research collected tumor imaging data from 30 patients (Table 2; Figure S2). The estimated progression-free survival and overall survival was 100% throughout the follow-up. Compared with the baseline data (Figure S3), the mean reduction rate of maximum diameters at the first month was 45.81% (31.01%–66.67%), which was not significantly different compared with the 56.26% (46.67%–66.67%) of reduction at the first 3 months. All of the 17 patients who received more than 3 months of targeted therapy showed at least a PR (Figure S2). Symptoms after administration of medication remained unchanged in three patients (hypopituitarism), while no patients displayed deterioration of symptoms. No patient had tumor progression while they were receiving medication treatment.

Based on the existence of both solid and cystic components in PCPs, we also evaluated each component separately (Figures 1D and 1E). The median reduction in solid tumor part from baseline was 56.72% (46.67%–73.68%) at 1 month, 52.33% (45%–65%) at 3 months, and 63.68% (48.5%–78.13%) at the last follow-up (Figure 1D). The median reduction in cystic part from baseline was 35.53% (25.85%–40.13%) at 1 month, 54.12% (40%–66.67%) at 3 months, and 57.36% (45%–70.59%) at the last follow-up (Figure 1E). Since solid and cystic components were evaluated separately, the trend and direction of tumor shrinkage was further explored. Based on the imaging data from 30 patients, we found that the solid part of tumors tended to reduce faster than the cystic part, leading to the tumors displaying cystic residue in most cases after the medical treatment. In addition, the significant increase of the reduction rates in cystic parts at the last follow-up compared with 3 months suggests that PCP patients with more cystic parts tend to receive longer targeted therapy. In addition, among 13 patients, the tumors showed downward shrinkage, creating a separation from the hypothalamus in 8 patients, while no such gap was observed in 5 patients. In 5 cases, shrinkage occurred in an upward direction, likely due to inflammatory adhesions around the tumor. The remaining 12 patients exhibited a mixed shrinkage pattern (Table 1).

Efficacy in NEO and ADJ groups

Among the 30 patients, 9 did not receive surgical resection before targeted therapy and were classified as the neoadjuvant (NEO) group (patients with newly diagnosed, non-resectable, large tumors). The other 21 patients, who were classified as the adjuvant (ADJ) group (patients who had already undergone single or multiple surgical procedures with or without receiving radiations), had undergone operations and were confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) of the tumor pathological section (Figure 2A). The reduction rates of total tumor, solid parts, and cystic parts at the first 1 month and 3 months were explored separately to find the differences in targeted therapy between the two groups (Figures 2B–2D). For the results of the first month, in total tumor, the reduction rates of the NEO group (60.35% [53.86%–66.84%]) were significantly higher than the ADJ group (42.90% [29.38%–61.67%]), which was the same in only solid parts (71.67% [65.83%–77.5%] for the NEO group and 52.45% [43.33%–61.84%] for the ADJ group). However, there was no significant difference in only cystic parts (32.06% [29.67%%–34.45] for the NEO group and 36.69% [23.32%–45.83%] for the ADJ group) at the first month. Besides, none of the reduction rates of total tumor, solid parts, or cystic parts at the first 3 months showed difference between NEO and ADJ groups.

Following treatments after targeted therapy

Twenty one patients who ended the medication treatment went on to receive the following: radiation treatment alone as standard-of-care treatment (1 patient), radiation followed by surgery (1 patient), and surgery (1 patient). Eighteen patients received no treatment after discontinuing therapy.

One of the two patients who received operation after the medication was a 7-year-old girl and received operation according to the predesigned medical plan. Until the last follow-up, she had no evidence of tumor progression. Both of them demonstrated significant reduction of tumors after medication and received operations to further excise residual tumors (Figure S1). Three patients received radiotherapy after the medication (patient 20 and 25 in Table 2). One of the patients (patient 25 in Table 2) also received the operation after radiotherapy. The tumors of all the three patients displayed reduction during the medication treatment. However, during continued medication, the rate of tumor shrinkage slowed (Figure S4). Therefore, these patients chose to receive further treatment (operation or radiotherapy). Although patient 20 (blue line in Figure S4) and patient 25 (purple line in Figure S4) did not undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after further treatment, the tumor of patient 19 (red line in Figure S4) further decreased in size after radiotherapy.

Safety

A total of 6 of 30 patients had grade 3 AEs, and 1 had a grade 4 AE that was determined by the treating physician to be at least possibly related to treatment (Table 3). No patients discontinued treatment owing to AEs. Grade 3 AEs that were observed in two or more patients were fever and rash. One patient had an asymptomatic grade 4 lymph node enlargement that was determined to be possibly related to treatment. Besides, the frequency of AEs was higher in the first 3 months than the first 1 month, while the frequency did not tend to increase after 3 months during medication administration (Figure 3).

Discussion

Although craniopharyngioma is a histologically benign brain tumor, its deep-seated location often leads to severe clinical symptoms and long-term complications due to compression of surrounding nerves and tissues.8,29 Consequently, the diagnosis and treatment of craniopharyngioma have remained key focal points in neurosurgery. Previous sequencing studies have revealed that the two subtypes of craniopharyngioma are associated with distinct gene mutations, suggesting differing origins.4,21 However, in practice, treatment for both subtypes has historically been similar, primarily involving surgical resection followed by postoperative radiotherapy. Due to challenges in achieving complete tumor resection and the high recurrence rates of residual tumors, patients frequently face recurrence when total resection is not achieved. Studies indicate that over 90% of PCPs carry the BRAF V600E mutation, making them suitable candidates for targeted therapies with BRAF-MEK inhibitors.30,31 In our study, the rate of headache in the NEO group tended to be significantly higher than in the ADJ group (p = 0.017). However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of visual impairment (p = 0.141) or neurological dysfunction (p = 0.637). These baseline data highlight the need to further investigate headache as a key research factor in PCP studies, particularly in exploring the potential of targeted therapies to alleviate headache symptoms in this patient population. Besides, all Chinese PCP patients who received at least 3 months of BRAF-MEK inhibitor therapy showed tumor responses. Both the solid and cystic components of the tumors exhibited shrinkage, with the solid portions showing a more significant reduction. No patients experienced tumor recurrence from the start of drug therapy through their last follow-up. This research focuses on the targeted therapy and following treatment of PCPs from efficacy, the onset time and maintenance duration, drug resistance, and AEs.

In prior studies, BRAF inhibitors have shown therapeutic benefits for recurrent PCPs, with notable results reported in multiple case studies.25,26,27 Recently, an international multicenter study demonstrated that BRAF-MEK inhibitors produce significant effects in patients who have undergone at least 1 month of treatment.15 While these studies primarily focused on populations in Europe and US, our research addresses a gap in the data for Chinese patients with PCP. Compared to the approximately 93% tumor shrinkage reported in European and American patients after 12 months of treatment, Chinese patients demonstrated approximately 60% tumor shrinkage, yet none experienced recurrence. This difference may be due to ethnic variations and the inclusion of patients who had undergone prior surgery or radiation therapy in our study.

Regarding the onset timing, both previous and current research indicate that BRAF-MEK inhibitors induce a volumetric response within 3 months of therapy.13,15 Our findings further reveal that the most significant tumor shrinkage occurs within the first month of therapy, accounting for about 80% of the overall response, primarily affecting the solid tumor components. As treatment continued, the response rate diminished and ultimately stabilized, mainly with residual cystic components. Prior investigations demonstrated a pronounced disparity in targeted therapy response at 3 months, with solid tumor components exhibiting superior regression compared to cystic regions.13 However, this difference diminished by the 12-month follow-up, indicating delayed but ultimately comparable efficacy in cystic lesions. This pattern aligns with our preliminary data and underscores the value of extended monitoring to fully capture treatment effects.

Among the patients who did not receive further treatment after drug therapy, 8 out of 13 showed no tumor recurrence, while 7 of 30 patients achieved stable residue in our study. Currently, the risk of recurrence associated with these residual tumors has not been systematically investigated, and corresponding follow-up assessments remain necessary even after patients discontinue drug therapy. Furthermore, for such residual lesions, combining radiotherapy or surgical intervention may contribute to improved tumor control.

Research has shown that BRAF monotherapy can lead to drug resistance, reactivating ERK through RAF dimer formation.32 This ERK reactivation can occur due to the relief of negative feedback regulation upstream or downstream of BRAF.33,34 Therefore, BRAF inhibitor monotherapy, such as vemurafenib, is prone to resistance in PCPs, with combination BRAF-MEK inhibitor therapy now more commonly recommended for PCP-targeted treatment. While BRAF-MEK combinations are generally less toxic and more effective than monotherapy, drug resistance has been observed in melanomas.35 The potential for resistance in PCPs remains uncertain but may impact long-term efficacy, warranting further post-treatment exploration.

Combination therapy with BRAF-MEK inhibitors has shown promising tumor control effects with fewer adverse reactions in other BRAF-mutant cancers, such as melanoma and papillary thyroid carcinoma.36,37,38 Our study differs in that fever was more common as an AE. Consistent with studies on BRAF-MEK inhibitors across various tumor types, fever is indeed one of the most critical AEs requiring close attention during treatment.39 Combination therapy (BRAF + MEK inhibitors) induces fever more frequently than monotherapy with vemurafenib or dabrafenib. Fever typically occurs shortly after treatment initiation, primarily within the first 4 weeks.40 The exact underlying cause of fever remains incompletely understood.22 Baseline clinical characteristics cannot predict fever occurrence, and no clear correlation has been established between fever and treatment efficacy or prognosis.41 Fever can be managed symptomatically. Previous studies recommend interrupting drugs if body temperature exceeds 38.5°C. Once fever subsides for at least 24 h, BRAF + MEK inhibitors can be resumed.42 Most patients experience recurrent episodes of fever.41 For persistent recurrence, dose reduction or intermittent dosing is recommended.

Besides, lymph node enlargement was observed in one patient, indicating the need for individualized prevention and management of side effects in Chinese patients. Additionally, AE frequency increased at 3 months compared to the first month, and though frequency may not rise in long-term treatment, the accumulation of side effects could affect the patient’s quality of life. As treatment progresses, the potential for serious AEs increases, underscoring the importance of optimizing drug therapy duration based on tumor response dynamics. Determining the appropriate endpoint for drug therapy is crucial for future research.

The four factors discussed earlier underscore the role of BRAF-MEK inhibitors in PCP treatment, while also highlighting that prolonged therapy may not be the optimal strategy. Future studies should focus on defining the appropriate treatment duration and subsequent therapy options for refined PCP management.30 Moreover, our research and prior findings suggest that NEO may be particularly beneficial for PCPs.25,43 Given the efficacy of BRAF-MEK inhibitors in this subtype, these patients may be eligible for direct drug therapy in the future. However, as PCPs comprise approximately 30% of craniopharyngioma cases, early identification of tumor subtype is essential to initiate drug therapy promptly. Surgical resection is still preferred for acquiring pathological tissue for subtype confirmation. Although biopsy may suffice, surgery for skull base tumors remains high risk. Our findings indicate that while drug therapy is effective, residual tumors tend to stabilize, necessitating further consolidation therapies including surgery or radiotherapy. Advancements in non-surgical subtype identification methods, followed by timely drug therapy for PCPs and subsequent surgery after treatment stabilization, could reduce resection risks and improve the total tumor removal rate. Previous studies have identified four MRI-based models that effectively predict craniopharyngioma subtypes by comparing MRI characteristics of different subtypes.44 Furthermore, the presence of tumor cell shedding in cerebrospinal fluid may also assist in preoperative prediction of tumor type. Such research requires further exploration, and the timing of intervention with drug therapy for skull base tumors also needs further clarification.

One limitation of this study is its retrospective design and the initial variability in treatment regimens, which led to inconsistencies in efficacy assessment criteria for BRAF-MEK inhibitors. Additionally, imaging data were not available for all patients, highlighting the need for expanding the study population. Therefore, prospective clinical trials among Chinese patients with PCPs are urgently warranted.

Materials and methods

Patients

This was a retrospective cohort study involving patients diagnosed with PCPs, treated using anti-BRAF/-anti-MEK therapy in our hospital from January 2023 to October 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board Huashan Hospital (KY2022-567). Histological diagnosis was confirmed via local pathology analysis, performed on samples obtained during surgery or from neuro-endoscopic biopsy. Data on medical history, MRI, and signs and symptoms at the time of diagnosis (including endocrine evaluation, visual field and acuity assessment, anthropometric measurements, and neurological evaluation) were collected through a review of medical records. Measurement of maximum tumor diameter using MRI was conducted centrally by two radiologists. The cystic and solid portions of the lesions were analyzed together and separately on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images.

In 23 of 30 cases, medical treatment consisted of combined, orally administered anti-BRAF and anti-MEK agents, the BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib (150 mg, twice daily) and the MEK inhibitor trametinib (1–2 mg, once daily). In five cases, only one patient was administered the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib (960 mg, twice daily), and one patient received only the MEK inhibitor trametinib (1–2 mg, once daily). One patient firstly took vemurafenib (960 mg, twice daily) for the first 10 months and then took dabrafenib/trametinib for the 12th to the 24th month. According to the treatment process, 30 cases were divided into two groups including (1) neoadjuvant therapy (NEO), in patients with newly diagnosed, non-resectable, large tumors; and (2) adjuvant therapy (ADJ), in patients who had already undergone single or multiple surgical procedures with or without receiving radiations. In all adjuvant therapy (ADJ) cases, the presence of a BRAF V600E mutation was confirmed via IHC. All patients provided written informed consent to participate in the study after a thorough explanation of the purpose and nature of all study procedures.

Treatment response assessment and AEs

Radiological changes in diameter were classified using recently published prespecified criteria. Both the solid and cystic tumor maximum diameters were included in the response measurement. A complete response was defined as the disappearance of all measurable lesions, while a diameter reduction of ≥80% characterized as an SR. A PR was reflected by a decrease ranging from 20% to 80% in the diameter of measurable lesions. Progressive disease was considered in the case of a >20% increase in the diameter of all measurable lesions, the appearance of a new lesion, or the occurrence of clinical worsening of neuro-ophthalmologic signs or symptoms. Stable disease was defined as either a ≥20% shrinkage or a <20% increase in the diameter of all measurable lesions, both at the 3-month early assessment and at the last available follow-up during medical treatment.

The AEs were recorded according to the follow-up information and corresponding test results during targeted therapy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as median and interquartile range according to the distribution of the data as assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Dichotomous and ordinal variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Based on the distribution of variables, t test for paired samples or Wilcoxon’s signed rank test for paired data was performed to compare tumor responses between two time points within the same patient cohort. Differences between groups regarding dichotomous or ordinal variables were assessed by χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Bivariate correlations between continuous variables were analyzed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation test, depending on the distribution of the data. One-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted to compare radiological changes in diameter across multiple groups of subjects after stratification based on treatment type, tumor consistency, or anatomical location. Post hoc analyses were performed via Tukey’s or Dunn’s test, as appropriate, and the result was reported only in case of significant statistical differences. Bonferroni’s correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Statistical significance was accepted for p <0 .05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc.) and GraphPad Prism 9.0.
