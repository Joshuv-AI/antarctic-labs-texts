Early vs delayed ablation for new-onset atrial fibrillation: 5-Year real-world data outcomes
Authors: Jacob R. Heath, Sangwoo Han, Ashraf Alzahrani, David Hamon, E. Michael Powers, Sergio Conti, Peter D. Farjo, Paari Dominic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUpdated guidelines recognize catheter ablation as a first-line therapy for symptomatic atrial fibrillation (AF). The optimal timing of catheter ablation following AF diagnosis remains uncertain.ObjectiveThis study assessed the impact of diagnosis-to-ablation time (DAT) <1 year vs DAT ≥1 year on AF recurrence and adverse clinical outcomes.MethodsWe queried the TriNetX Research Network for patients ≥18 years of age with a diagnosis of AF who underwent ablation between January 1, 2010, and June 30, 2019. Patients were stratified into cohorts based on DAT <1 year vs ≥1 year and matched using 1:1 propensity scores, resulting in 8403 patients in each cohort. The primary outcome was AF recurrence, defined as a composite of cardioversion, antiarrhythmic use, or re-ablation at 3 and 5 years, after a 3-month blanking period. Secondary outcomes included a composite of heart failure exacerbation, ischemic stroke, all-cause hospitalization, and mortality, along with individual components.ResultsDAT <1 year was associated with significantly lower AF recurrence both at 3 years (adjusted odds ratio 0.68 [95% confidence interval: 0.64–0.72]; P < .001) and 5 years (adjusted odds ratio 0.68 [95% confidence interval: 0.64–0.72]; P < .001). At 3 years, all secondary outcomes were significantly reduced in the DAT <1 year group, except for incident cerebrovascular accident and mortality. At 5 years, all secondary outcomes were significantly reduced in the DAT <1 year group.ConclusionCatheter ablation within 1 year of AF diagnosis is associated with reduced AF recurrence and major adverse clinical outcomes. These findings support early referral for catheter ablation.

Key Findings ▪Early ablation therapy for atrial fibrillation (AF), ideally within the first year from diagnosis onset, leads to improved normal rhythm at 3- and 5-year follow-up.▪Diagnosis to ablation within 1 year of diagnosis leads to decreased incidence of heart failure exacerbation, ischemic stroke, hospitalization, and mortality.▪The overall benefits of early ablation therapy are seen independent of the type of AF a patient develops.▪Ablation therapy warrants early discussion for patients and should be considered in conjunction with or even before other medical treatments of AF.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is characterized by progressive structural and electrical remodeling that promotes atrial fibrosis and creates a self-perpetuating cycle of arrhythmia progression.1, 2, 3 As AF advances, the risks of adverse clinical outcomes such as stroke, systemic embolism, and hospitalization for heart failure (HF) increase.4 Thus, there is a strong rationale to treat AF early to prevent progression and adverse outcomes. Catheter ablation as first-line therapy is an effective strategy for maintaining sinus rhythm and has demonstrated superiority over antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs).5,6 Catheter ablation has also been shown to delay AF progression, reduce recurrence, and improve quality of life compared with AAD.3,7,8

Historically, guidelines have recommended the use of AAD before ablation, potentially delaying more definitive rhythm control to prevent AF progression.9,10 However, as of 2023, updated guidelines recognize catheter ablation as a reasonable first-line therapy for symptomatic AF.11 Although early intervention with ablation appears promising, the optimal timing for maximum clinical benefit remains unclear. In particular, the effect of diagnosis-to-ablation time (DAT) on long-term outcomes is still under investigation. Emerging evidence suggests that a shorter DAT may be associated with improved outcomes, including lower rates of AF recurrence, HF, ischemic stroke, and mortality.8,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 However, few studies have evaluated this question in large, real-world populations. We examined the impact of early (<1 year) vs late (≥1 year) DAT on AF recurrence and major adverse clinical outcomes, including hospitalization, mortality, HF exacerbation, and stroke, in a large population using the TriNetX database.

Methods

Data source

This study used data from the TriNetX Research network (TriNetX, Inc), a global, federated health research network providing access to statistics in electronic medical records (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, and genomic information) from approximately 140 million patients across 106 large health care organizations.18 The research reported in this study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. TriNetX received a waiver from Western Institutional Review Board as only de-identified, aggregate-level data is received; therefore, Institutional Review Board approval was waived for this study and patient informed consent was not required.

Patient selection

Patients were identified based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes (Supplemental Table 1). Patients aged ≥18 years with a diagnosis of AF (ICD-10: I48) who had an ablation procedure between January 1, 2010, and June 30, 2019, were included. AF ablation was determined by Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 93656 (intracardiac catheter ablation of AF by pulmonary vein isolation) and 93657 (linear or focal intracardiac catheter ablation of the left or right atrium for treatment of AF remaining after completion of pulmonary vein isolation). Exclusion criteria were a history of general AAD use or an abnormal electrocardiogram (ICD-10: R94.31) prior to diagnosis of AF. AAD use was defined within the TriNetX database using RxNorm codes for common class I and III antiarrhythmics. RxNorm is used to normalize clinical drug names used by differing health care organizations within the TriNetX database. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Supplemental Figure 1. The exclusion criteria ensured that the diagnosis of AF reported by ICD-10 coding was as accurate as possible. Patient groups were subdivided into DAT <1 year and DAT ≥1 year cohorts. Sensitivity analyses cohorts were created: (1) Outcomes of DAT <1 year vs DAT >1 year, in patients with persistent AF only, excluding those with paroxysmal AF; (2) Outcomes of DAT <1 year vs DAT >1 year in patients with paroxysmal AF only, excluding those with persistent AF. An additional sensitivity analysis cohort was created for the ablation CPT code 93656, excluding all other ablation CPT codes from analysis.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was AF recurrence, defined as a composite of cardioversion, re-ablation, or AAD use after a 3-month blanking period from the indexed AF ablation event. A secondary composite outcome included congestive HF (CHF) exacerbation, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), all-cause hospitalization, and mortality. Separate components of the primary and secondary composite outcomes were also used as secondary outcomes. Outcomes were analyzed at 3 and 5 years after the indexed AF ablation event, with a 3-month post-ablation blanking period. All secondary outcomes were determined by appropriate ICD coding (Supplemental Table 1).

Statistical analysis

To control for baseline characteristics, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed between the 2 DAT cohorts. One-to-one PSM was used to balance demographics, comorbidities, and medications (Supplemental Figure 2). Propensity scores were generated using greedy nearest-neighbor algorithms with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between cohorts with independent-sample t tests. Categorical variables were described as n (%) and compared between cohorts with χ2 testing. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for primary and secondary outcomes. Survival analysis was performed by plotting log-rank test Kaplan-Meier curves between the 2 DAT cohorts. Sensitivity analyses were performed after a 1:1 PSM using the same statistical analytics and significance markers as described for the primary analysis. All statistical analysis was completed within the TriNetX database.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary composite outcomes—comprising CHF exacerbation, CVA, all-cause hospitalization, and death—were also significantly lower in the early ablation group. At 3 years, 2790 patients (34.5%) with DAT <1 year and 3139 patients (37.1%) with DAT ≥1 year met the composite end point (aOR 0.89 [95% CI: 0.84–0.95]; P < .001). At 5 years, rates were 41.5% vs 45.5% (aOR 0.85 [95% CI: 0.80–0.90]; P < .001).

Discussion

In this large, real-world cohort study, we found that shorter DAT in patients with AF was associated with significantly lower rates of AF recurrence, cardioversion, AAD use, and adverse clinical outcomes. Specifically, patients who underwent catheter ablation within 1 year of diagnosis experienced improved outcomes at both 3- and 5-year follow-up compared with those who underwent delayed ablation (DAT ≥1 year). These findings support the growing body of literature emphasizing the importance of early rhythm control and reinforce recent guideline updates advocating for ablation as a first-line therapy in selected patients with symptomatic AF.

Our results align with prior studies, including the EARLY-AF trial,19 the CABANA trial,20 a review of the National Danish Registry,7 and the recent meta-analysis by Kris and Kühne,17 all of which suggest superior outcomes with earlier ablation. Importantly, our study adds to this evidence by examining DAT as a modifiable, time-dependent factor and evaluating its effect, not only on arrhythmia recurrence, but also on clinically meaningful end points such as stroke, HF exacerbation, hospitalization, and mortality.

Patients with DAT <1 year had significantly lower rates of AF recurrences at both time points, with reductions in cardioversion, re-ablation, and AAD use. The benefits of rhythm control on AF recurrence were observed in sensitivity analyses of both paroxysmal and persistent AF subgroups, suggesting that early ablation offers clinical benefit regardless of AF type. Moreover, this benefit of early rhythm control was maintained, even on sensitivity analysis of ablation procedure type, suggesting early ablation offers clinical benefit regardless of the procedural type chosen.

Early DAT was also associated with lower risk of CHF exacerbation and all-cause hospitalization at both 3 and 5 years, whereas CVA and mortality were reduced at 5 years in the primary analysis. These findings are likely driven by improved rhythm control and a reduction in AF burden, which is known to correlate with thromboembolic events and HF events.21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27

These findings support the mechanistic rationale that delayed ablation allows time for adverse atrial remodeling to progress, such as collagen deposition, extracellular matrix disruption, gap junction remodeling, and impaired calcium handling.28,29 As such, patients with longer DAT may experience higher ablation failure rates, as prolonged remodeling secondary to AF is difficult to reverse, and comorbidities further compound the likelihood of procedural failure.30, 31, 32

Paroxysmal AF is associated with better arrhythmic outcomes compared with persistent AF.33,34 However, subgroup analyses by AF type demonstrated consistent benefit with early ablation for both sensitivity analysis with paroxysmal and persistent AF, suggesting that the timing of intervention may be an important factor in addition to AF subtype in predicting long-term outcomes.

Our evaluation of the impact of DAT on a broad range of outcomes is a notable strength of our study that few prior studies have examined. For example, Tønnesen et al7 and Ding et al35 reported decreased mortality with early ablation and Chew et al14 found decreased all-cause hospitalization with early ablation treatment. However, these studies did not provide a detailed analysis of additional clinically relevant outcomes. Another key strength of this study is the large, diverse, and nationally representative population derived from the TriNetX network. Furthermore, the extended follow-up period of 5 years and robust PSM further enhance the reliability of our findings.

Limitations

Nonetheless, our study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be fully excluded. DAT was treated as a binary variable and derived from ICD-coded diagnosis dates, which may not precisely capture the true clinical onset of AF. We lacked access to granular data such as AF burden, echocardiographic or imaging variables, and ablation technique, which may influence outcomes. Additionally, although we observed reductions in all-cause hospitalization, we could not determine whether these were directly related to AF or other comorbidities. Finally, details regarding symptom burden and how this affected the timing of AF ablation and resultant outcomes are unknown.

Conclusion

In this large retrospective cohort study, early catheter ablation within 1 year of AF diagnosis (DAT <1 year) was associated with significant reductions in AF recurrence, cardioversion, AAD use, stroke, hospitalization, HF exacerbation, and mortality, compared with delayed ablation. These benefits were consistent across paroxysmal and persistent AF subtypes and were maintained over long-term follow-up. Our findings support the adoption of early ablation as part of an aggressive rhythm control strategy to improve patient outcomes. Further research is warranted to refine the timing of intervention and validate these observations in prospective studies.
