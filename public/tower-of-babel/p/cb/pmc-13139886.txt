Clinical, Genetic, and Pathologic Variability in Myelodysplastic Syndromes and Precursor Conditions Across Race, Ethnicity, and Sex
Authors: Nancy Gillis, Christelle Colin‐Leitzinger, Yi‐Han Tang, Michael Otterstatter, Seth Sherman, Ling Zhang, Lynn C Moscinski, Mary Ellen Walker, Jeffrey S Painter, Gregory A Abel, Tareq Al Baghdadi, H Joachim Deeg, James M Foran, Steven D Gore, Alexandra M Harrington, Steven H Kroft, Jane Jijun Liu, Wael Saber, Shamsuddin Virani, Rafael Bejar, R Coleman Lindsley, Eric Padron, Matthew J Walter, Rami Komrokji, Amy E DeZern, Mikkael A Sekeres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe epidemiology of myelodysplastic syndromes/neoplasms (MDS) is challenging to define due to inconsistent reporting, complex diagnostic procedures, and evolving diagnostic criteria. Using the National MDS Natural History Study—a prospective cohort with centrally adjudicated histopathology and genetic variant review—we characterized the landscape of MDS across the United States and identified differences across demographics. Among 2115 participants, 64% (1346) had an MDS spectrum condition, including MDS (24%), MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasm (5%) and precursor conditions—clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (22%) and idiopathic cytopenia/dysplasia of undetermined significance (13%). The median age was 74 years, and participants were predominantly male (66%), White (91%), and Non‐Hispanic (92%). Myeloid‐associated variants were detected in 68% of participants, most commonly in TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, SF3B1, and SRSF2. Black, compared to White, participants were younger at diagnosis (69 vs. 74 years, p = 0.01), had equal or increased prevalence of higher‐risk MDS, lower hemoglobin, and higher peripheral blood blasts, yet were less likely to receive MDS‐directed therapy (14% vs. 42%, p = 0.008). Black and Hispanic participants had fewer detectable gene mutations than White participants. Females had lower variant allele frequencies and fewer RNA splicing gene mutations than males. After multivariable adjustment, TP53 mutations, MDS diagnosis, and higher‐risk disease were associated with worse progression‐free and overall survival; age was also associated with overall survival. Black race trended toward improved progression‐free survival. These findings highlight the need for enhanced understanding of MDS pathogenesis across patient groups and refined prognostic tools to improve personalized management of MDS spectrum conditions. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02775383

Introduction

Defining the epidemiology of myelodysplastic syndromes/neoplasms (MDS) and precursor conditions, such as clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS), is challenging due to inconsistent reporting, complex diagnostic procedures, and evolving diagnostic criteria [1, 2]. Available data largely come from retrospective registries, which may be limited by variable levels of source data, interobserver variability, misclassification, and incomplete clinical, cytogenetic, prognostic, and molecular annotation—factors essential for accurate MDS diagnoses [1, 3, 4]. For example, the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program does not include all US states and has an oversampling of underrepresented minority populations, which can bias epidemiology measures [5]. Additionally, registries have not historically required bone marrow biopsy confirmation for an MDS diagnosis, despite its necessity for accurate classification. This lack of standardization is particularly concerning given the significant diagnostic inconsistencies between community and tertiary care centers [6, 7]. Our research, for instance, found that nearly one‐third of MDS‐related diagnoses from community centers were reclassified after central, adjudicated pathology review—20% due to errors in diagnosis and 13% due to data entry errors [7]. These discrepancies undermine the reliability of retrospective registry databases in capturing the true epidemiology and public health impact of MDS.

The National MDS Natural History Study (NHS) provides a unique opportunity to establish a precise and accurate epidemiology landscape of MDS and precursor conditions [8]. Unparalleled features of the MDS NHS include adjudicated central pathology review, a U.S. nationally representative population, and prospective observational follow‐up from diagnosis or time of workup for suspected MDS. The unique enrollment beginning at evaluation for patients with cytopenia suspected to have MDS resulted in capture of patients with MDS precursor conditions, such as CCUS. Here, we used the MDS NHS to precisely define the epidemiology of MDS and its precursor conditions, with a focus on clinical, genetic, and pathologic variability across a representative U.S. sample.

Methods

Patient Population

The National MDS NHS (NCT02775383) is a prospective cohort that enrolled patients with suspected MDS from June 2016 to July 2024 (enrollment was paused March 27, 2020—May 18, 2020 due to the COVID‐19 pandemic) [8]. The protocol was approved through the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Central Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all patients before participation. Participants were enrolled at 102 centers across the U.S. and 1 center in Israel, including both NCI Community Oncology Research Program (NCORP) and academic sites (non‐NCORP). To be eligible, individuals were either undergoing diagnostic workup for suspected MDS or were diagnosed with de novo or therapy‐related MDS within 12 months and had not received hematopoietic growth factors in the past 6 months or any other therapy for MDS. At enrollment, participants underwent bone marrow assessment with centralized histopathology review and third‐party adjudication, when needed, for diagnosis. Information on other potential causes of cytopenia (e.g., vitamin or iron deficiencies, endocrine disorders, and systemic intercurrent illnesses) was used to help exclude non‐clonal causes. Participants were assigned to longitudinal follow‐up on the MDS NHS if central diagnosis according to World Health Organization 2016 criteria [9] was MDS, MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasms (MDS/MPN), CCUS (beginning January 2019), acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with < 30% blasts not including core binding factor mutations or acute promyelocytic leukemia (i.e., oligoblastic AML), or idiopathic cytopenia or dysplasia of undetermined significance (ICUS or IDUS). Participants with other diagnoses (e.g., AML ≥ 30% blasts, MPNs, non‐myeloid malignancies, or other cytopenias) were not included in follow‐up and classified as cross‐sectional. Here, we present data from the full participant population enrolled June 2016—July 2024, with a primary focus on those with MDS‐related conditions (excluding oligoblastic AML), based on the MDS NHS database from August 1, 2025.

Data Collection

Demographic information was collected at study enrollment using standardized data collection forms. Race, ethnicity, and sex were self‐reported. Additional data collected include comorbidities, family history, and lifestyle and environmental exposures. Participant diagnostic classifications were based on centrally‐adjudicated histopathology review, using the WHO 2016 classifications for MDS [7, 10]. A subset of participants underwent baseline cytogenetic re‐review in October 2025 for quality assurance, which is included in these analyses. For patients with MDS or MDS/MPN overlap with white blood cell count < 12 000 cells/μL, prognostic risk was calculated using the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS‐R) for MDS [11]. Participants with MDS‐related conditions (i.e., the longitudinal cohort) were followed at 6‐month intervals and when medically indicated. Serial collections at follow‐up visits included laboratory values, progression, treatment, quality of life, survival data, and biospecimens.

DNA Sequencing and Mutation Annotation

DNA was extracted at the MDS NHS central laboratory and biorepository (Moffitt Cancer Center and Research Institute) from baseline bone marrow samples for targeted exon sequencing of 96 myeloid malignancy‐related genes (Table S1). Deep next‐generation exonic sequencing was performed at the McDonnell Genome Institute (St. Louis, MO) using a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with a targeted mean depth of coverage > 1000×. Sequencing data were processed at the MDS NHS Data Coordinating Center (Emmes, Rockville, MD) using previously described methods [12]. Briefly, sequencing reads were aligned with a patched version of human reference GRCh38 using BWA‐MEM (version 0.7.15) [13]. VarScan2 was used to detect single nucleotide variants and insertions and deletions with a minimum VAF of 2% and 5%, respectively [14]. FLT3 internal tandem duplication variants were excluded from the model because of the inaccuracy in VAF estimates. Additionally, VAFs were not adjusted for chromosome X or autosome ploidy. Resulting variants from 53 of the 96 genes were manually curated (by MJW, RCL, and RB) and were classified as likely disease‐associated variants (somatic and germline; Table S1). Presented analyses focus on variants classified as likely disease‐associated variants by manual review [12].

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of patient and clinical characteristics was examined overall using Kruskal–Wallis rank sum tests, Pearson's χ 2 tests, and Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Self‐reported race, ethnicity, and sex were used for analyses. Stage was reported for participants with MDS using the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS‐R) [11] and classified as lower‐risk MDS if IPSS‐R was ≤ 3.5 and higher‐risk MDS if IPSS‐R was > 3.5. Prognostic risk for patients with CCUS was classified based on the clonal cytopenia risk score (CCRS) [15] and categorized into two groups: low/intermediate risk or high risk. Pairwise comparisons between patients focused on differences between Black Non‐Hispanic and White Non‐Hispanic, between Non‐Hispanic and Hispanic (regardless of race), and by sex. Exploratory analysis included z‐score correction of hemoglobin, platelet, and white blood cell counts using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III data for adjustment by race, ethnicity, sex [16]. Overall survival (OS) was calculated using the time between MDS NHS enrollment and death or last contact. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was calculated as the time between MDS NHS enrollment and progression, defined based on worsening disease (following the International Working Group 2018 criteria [17]), change in disease‐directed therapy consistent with worsening of disease, increase in red blood cell transfusion dependency, or all‐cause mortality [18]. Unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves, log‐rank tests, and multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to examine the association of demographic and MDS‐related factors with OS and PFS. All models adjusted for age at enrollment (years), sex, race, ethnicity, study classification/diagnosis, IPSS‐R or CCRS risk group, and TP53 mutation status. Comparisons between tumor mutation frequencies between groups were conducted using Pearson's χ 2 and Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. The associations between demographic (e.g., age, race, ethnicity, sex) and clinical (e.g., diagnosis) factors with risk for specific likely disease‐associated variants by gene was assessed using multiple logistic regression. Due to the absence of detectable driver mutations and uncertain disease trajectories, participants with ICUS/IDUS were excluded from genetic analyses and multivariable models of OS and PFS. A p‐value < 0.05, without multiple comparisons correction, was considered statistically significant. Exploratory analyses included false‐discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons, but power was limited by small samples. Statistical analysis was performed with R version 4.4.1 (2024‐06‐14).

Results

Clinical and Genetic Features of the Patient Population

A total of 2115 participants were suspected to have MDS and enrolled in the MDS NHS, of which 64% (1346) were found to have MDS‐related conditions. Of participants without MDS‐related conditions, other malignancies were diagnosed in 22% (172/769) and AML in 12% (93/769). Factors associated with increased likelihood of a myeloid‐spectrum diagnosis (i.e., MDS‐related condition or AML) included age (OR 1.05 95% CI 1.04–1.06, p < 0.001), Hispanic ethnicity (OR 2.24, 95% CI 1.22–4.40, p = 0.01), and male sex (OR 1.29 95% CI 1.04–1.59, p = 0.02), while Black race trended toward a decreased likelihood of a myeloid‐spectrum diagnosis (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.43–1.04, p = 0.07) after adjustment for the factors mentioned. These findings were consistent when assessing risk for MDS‐related conditions alone (i.e., excluding AML).

Primary analyses focused on participants with MDS‐related conditions (Table 1). The median age was 74 years (interquartile range or IQR, 67–80) and a majority were male (884/1346, 66%). Consistent with the known epidemiology of MDS, most participants reported White race (1225/1346, 91%), and fewer reported Black (56/1346, 4.2%) and Asian (17/1346, 1.3%) races; Hispanic ethnicity was reported by 4.2% (56/1346) of participants. Upon centrally adjudicated pathology review, most patients were diagnosed with MDS (510/1346, 38%) and CCUS (469, 35%), followed by ICUS/IDUS (264/1346, 20%), and MDS/MPN overlap (103, 7.7%). The majority of participants with MDS had lower‐risk disease (63%). Median follow‐up time as of August 1, 2025 was 1.98 years (IQR 1.03–3.63), with 23% followed for ≥ 3.5 years, the median historical survival of people with MDS. Only 1.8% of participants (n = 21) were lost to follow‐up and the rate was similar across races and ethnicities (range 1.7%–2.1%, p = 0.7). During follow‐up, withdrawal rate was low (17%, 231/1346), 24% (318/1346) of participants died, 5.6% (75/1346) progressed to AML, and 3.3% (44/1346) received hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT).

Pathologic Differences by Patient Demographics

Pairwise comparisons between patients focused on differences between Black Non‐Hispanic and White Non‐Hispanic (referred to as Black and White moving forward), between Non‐Hispanic and Hispanic (regardless of race), and by sex. After central pathology diagnosis using WHO criteria [10], Black, compared to White participants trended toward more MDS (42% vs. 38%) than CCUS (25% vs. 36%) or ICUS/IDUS (28% vs. 18%) diagnoses (p = 0.1), while Hispanic, compared to Non‐Hispanic, participants had more ICUS/IDUS (38% vs. 18%, p = 0.005) (Table 1). Females, compared to males, had a higher prevalence of precursor condition diagnoses (e.g., ICUS/IDUS 27% vs. 16%) and lower prevalence of MDS/MPN overlap (4.3% vs. 9.4%, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

The median bone marrow blast count overall was 1.0% (IQR 0.7–2.0) and was consistent across race, ethnicity, and sex (Tables S3 and S4). In patients with confirmed MDS, the median bone marrow blast count was 2.0 (IQR 1.0–5.5), which was similar between Black and White race, but trended toward lower in Hispanic than Non‐Hispanic individuals (1.0% vs. 2.0%, respectively, p = 0.057) (Table S3). Of evaluable participants, dysplasia was observed in 31% (389/1261), a rate that was consistent across race and ethnicity, but lower in females than males (26% vs. 35%, respectively, p = 0.002). Complex karyotype tended to be more common in Black compared to White individuals (14% vs. 6%, respectively) and females compared to males (7% vs. 6%, respectively), but these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.08 and p = 0.5). Black, compared to White, participants with MDS trended toward worse cytogenetic risk group according to IPSS‐R (very poor 15% vs. 9%; poor 10% vs. 7%; intermediate risk 15% vs. 9%), although the overall difference was not statistically significant. While the distribution of cytogenetic risk groups was different between males and females with MDS, the overall groupings of cytogenetic risk were similar (e.g., very good + good risk approximately equal and poor + very poor approximately equal) (Table S4).

Clinical Differences by Patient Demographics

There were differences in clinical presentation by race, ethnicity, and sex. Black, compared to White, participants were younger at diagnosis (69 vs. 74 years, respectively, p = 0.01) although there was no difference between Hispanic and Non‐Hispanic participants (74 years each, p = 0.8) (Table 1). There were no differences in follow‐up time or withdrawal rates across racial or ethnic groups. Black participants with MDS were less likely than White participants to receive MDS‐directed drug therapy (14% vs. 42%, p = 0.008) and none underwent HCT (0 vs. 8% White, p = 0.4) during the follow‐up period. Overall, baseline blood counts were largely similar across racial and ethnic groups (Table S5). Statistically, but perhaps not clinically, significant differences in Black compared to White participants included lower hemoglobin (9.8 vs. 10.7 g/dL, p = 0.04), lower thyrotropin (TSH, 1.4 vs. 2.0 mIU/L, p = 0.02), lower mean corpuscular volume (MCV, 90 vs. 97 fL, p < 0.001), and higher peripheral blood blasts (1.9% vs. 0%, p = 0.002) (Figure S1). The statistical difference in hemoglobin was abrogated after z‐score adjustment for race‐based differences in baseline hemoglobin using NHANES III as a reference population (p = 0.4).

Differences in disease presentation by sex included younger age at diagnosis in females (72 vs. 75 years, p < 0.001). Prognostic risk at diagnosis was similar in both sexes, but death was less common in females than males (21% vs. 30%, p = 0.001), with no difference in median follow‐up time (2.2 vs. 1.9 years, respectively, p = 0.3). There were no differences in receipt of MDS‐related treatment, HCT, or progression to AML by sex. There were no notable clinically significant differences in blood counts by sex, but females had statistically significantly higher platelets, lymphocytes, and vitamin B12, and lower MCV compared to males (Figure S1, Table S6). The statistically higher hemoglobin and platelets in females compared to males persisted after sex‐based adjustment using NHANES III as a reference (p < 0.001).

Genetic Differences by Patient Demographics

Disease‐associated variants in the 53 manually‐reviewed genes were less common in Black than White participants (57% vs. 70%, respectively, p = 0.03) and in Hispanic compared to Non‐Hispanic participants (50% vs. 70%, respectively, p = 0.002) (Figure 1B). This trend for mutations is consistent, but no longer statistically significant, when considering only patients with MDS (Black vs. White prevalence 77% vs. 83%, p = 0.4; Hispanic vs. Non‐Hispanic participants 74% vs. 83%, p = 0.3). Black participants, compared to White participants, had a higher median VAF for mutations (0.39 vs. 0.30, p = 0.04), lower rates of TET2 mutations (13% vs. 25%, p = 0.047) and a trend for more in TP53 (13% vs. 6.7%, p = 0.09) (Table S7, Figure S2). There were no statistically significant differences in mutations by ethnicity, but Hispanic, compared to Non‐Hispanic participants trended toward more U2AF1 mutations (13% vs. 6%, p = 0.08). In MDS, Black compared to White participants had more RUNX1 (23% vs. 8%, p = 0.04) and TP53 (27% vs. 12%, p = 0.048) mutations. Classifying mutations into functional pathways, there were no statistically significant differences by race; however, mutations in genes involved in DNA methylation (i.e., DNMT3A, IDH1, IDH2, TET2) were less common in Hispanic compared to Non‐Hispanic individuals (27% vs. 42%, respectively, p = 0.03) (Figure 1E, Figure S3). None of the racial or ethnic differences in mutations met FDR‐corrected statistical significance. Putative germline variants in DDX41 were identified in 1.8% (23/1261) of White participants and not in Black or Hispanic participants; the prevalence was consistent across sexes (p > 0.9).

Compared to males, females had lower rates of mutations (63% vs. 71%, p = 0.003) and lower VAFs (0.28 vs. 0.33, p < 0.001) (Figure 1). This result persists when excluding X chromosome variants (0.29 vs. 0.32, p = 0.001). In females, there were no mutations observed in ZRSR2 (vs. 4.5% of males), PRPF8 (vs. 1.4% in males), or NF1 (vs. 1% in males). Significant differences in mutations between females and males after FDR correction included fewer variants in ASXL1 (9.7% vs. 17%, q = 0.004), SRSF2 (7.4% vs. 14%, q = 0.004), U2AF1 (2.2% vs. 8.6%, q < 0.001), and ZRSR2 (0 vs. 4.5%, q < 0.001) (Table S8, Figure S2). Females had more mutations in DNMT3A (19% vs. 14%, p = 0.01), although the statistical difference was abrogated after multiple comparisons correction (q = 0.1). In pathway analysis, RNA splicing (SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2) and chromatin modifying (AXSL1 and EZH2) mutations were less common in females than males (q < 0.001) (Figure 1E, Figure S3). In multivariable regression analyses that included mutations, and adjusted for diagnosis, genes associated with increased age were TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, SF3B1, U2AF1, EZH2, GNB1, and PRPF8 (Figure 2, Table S9). Black, compared to White, race was associated with mutations in DNMT3A (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.1–4.8, p = 0.03) and a trend toward more mutations in TP53 (OR 2.19, 95% CI 0.8–5.0, p = 0.08). Hispanic, compared to Non‐Hispanic, ethnicity was associated with risk for mutations in U2AF1 (3.5, 95% CI 1.3–8.4, p = 0.007) and trends for more mutations in GNB1 (OR 3.97, 95% CI 0.6–16.0, p = 0.09) and PTPN11 (OR 4.55, 95% CI 0.7–19.7, p = 0.7). Compared to females, males had greater risk for mutations in SRSF2 (OR 1.7, 95% CI 1.1–2.6, p = 0.01), U2AF1 (OR 3.8, 95% CI 2.0–8.1, p < 0.001), and ASXL1 (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.0–2.2, p = 0.05). Males had lower risk for mutations in DNMT3A (OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.4–0.7, p < 0.001) compared to females. Of note, genes not observed in a subgroup of the population could not be included in multivariable regression for that variable (e.g., ZRSR2 and PRPF8 for sex).

Overall and Progression‐Free Survival

Median OS for the cohort was 7.3 years (95% CI, 5.8—not reached). By diagnosis, median OS for patients with MDS was 4.2 years (95% CI 3.5–5.4) and not reached at the time of analysis for patients with CCUS or ICUS/IDUS. Comparing White, Black, and Hispanic participants using a Kaplan–Meier curve and unadjusted log rank test, there was a non‐significant difference in OS (p = 0.16; Figure 3A), and males had improved OS compared to females (p = 0.002, Figure 3B). In univariable analysis, factors associated with worse OS included age (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.08, p < 0.001), male sex (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.14–1.90, p = 0.003), diagnosis (MDS vs. CCUS: HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.42–2.55, p < 0.001), higher‐risk compared to lower‐risk disease (HR 1.97, 95% CI 1.49–2.60, p < 0.001), and TP53 mutations (HR 2.94, 95% CI 2.02–4.28, p < 0.001) (Figure 3C). In multivariable analyses, significant factors associated with worse OS were age (adjHR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.08, p < 0.001), diagnosis (MDS vs. CCUS: HR 2.01, 95% CI 1.49–2.71, p < 0.001), higher‐risk compared to lower‐risk disease (adjHR 1.90, 95% CI 1.41–2.57, p < 0.001), and presence of a TP53 mutation (adjHR 2.09, 95% CI 1.36–3.21, p < 0.001) (Figure 3C). Black participants had a non‐significant improvement in OS compared to White participants in univariable and multivariable analyses (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.30–1.22, p = 0.2).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the clinical, genetic, and pathologic features of MDS spectrum conditions, offering valuable insights into disease epidemiology, genetic landscapes, and clinical outcomes across sex, race, and ethnic groups. Unlike prior retrospective studies with limited diagnostic consistency, the prospective MDS NHS used standardized and adjudicated pathology review to ensure diagnostic accuracy. For the first time, we show that among those evaluated for suspected MDS at academic and community sites across the U.S., 64% were diagnosed with an MDS spectrum condition, including precursor states. Our findings reveal important differences in disease presentation and genetics by patient demographics, which have meaningful implications for improving screening strategies, guiding individualized precision treatment approaches, and informing drug development for MDS.

To better characterize sex‐based differences in MDS spectrum conditions, we examined clinical presentation and outcomes across males and females. Enrolled based on suspected MDS, participants were predominately male (66%), consistent with existing literature indicating a higher incidence of myeloid malignancies in men [1, 2, 19]. Compared to males, females were younger at diagnosis, had similar rates of CCUS and MDS, but more often presented with ICUS/IDUS and less often with MDS/MPN overlap syndrome. Additionally, female sex trended toward being more common among Black and Hispanic participants than White participants, supporting evidence that MDS spectrum conditions may be more prevalent in females who are Black or Hispanic [20]. While prognostic risk classifications did not differ by sex, females trended toward improved overall survival. This survival advantage may reflect underlying sex‐specific biology, such as males having more mutations in RNA splicing and chromatin modification genes (e.g., U2AF1, SRSF2, ASXL1, ZRSR2) [21, 22, 23, 24] and females having more DNMT3A mutations [25]. These findings underscore the relevance of sex as a biological variable in MDS and highlight the potential impact on prognosis and personalized treatment approaches.

There is a need to understand the landscape of MDS spectrum conditions across racial and ethnic groups, since existing data largely focus on White Non‐Hispanic populations. The racial and ethnic diversity of the MDS NHS cohort mirrors that expected based on retrospective nationwide population‐based cohorts (e.g., North American Association of Central Cancer Registries), enabling a representative analysis [26]. In the MDS NHS, Black, compared to White, participants were younger at diagnosis and trended toward more MDS than precursor conditions and higher risk MDS, and more often presented with advanced disease features, including lower hemoglobin, higher peripheral blood blast counts, and higher VAF of mutations. These differences suggest that Black individuals may be further along in the MDS disease course at the time of initial suspicion or workup. Whether this disparity stems from more aggressive disease biology, screening bias driven by the perception of low MDS risk, or barriers to care for Black individuals warrants further investigation. Evidence of access‐to‐care differences is supported by a retrospective analysis of Medicare recipients, which found that Black, compared to White, patients with MDS were less likely to receive a pre‐diagnostic bone marrow biopsy [27]. In the MDS NHS, indicators of potential access‐to‐care differences for Black individuals include farther travel distances, higher enrollment at community than academic centers, and lower use of MDS‐directed treatment and HCT, despite their higher‐risk disease features.

Despite presenting with more advanced disease features and receiving less MDS‐directed treatment, Black participants showed a trend toward improved survival compared to White participants. Retrospective analysis of SEER data suggests that the improved OS for Black patients with MDS, despite more risk factors (e.g., comorbidities), is driven by the higher prevalence in females, who typically have better prognosis [27]. Similarly, in the MDS NHS, the trend for improved OS of Black participants is abrogated in our multivariable model that accounts for sex and age. Contrarily, the trend for improved PFS in Black participants persists, and strengthens, after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that there are other unaccounted for factors impacting this difference. Taken together, these findings point to a complex interplay of disease biology, sex, healthcare access, and treatment differences that may influence survival outcomes among Black individuals with MDS, underscoring the need for deeper investigation into the biological, clinical, and systemic factors shaping racial differences in disease trajectory and prognosis.

Genetic analyses revealed significant differences in the mutational landscape of MDS spectrum conditions across racial and ethnic groups. White participants had a higher overall burden of mutations, measured by number and VAF, compared to Black and Hispanic participants. We posit that this finding is likely multifactorial, reflecting population‐specific mutations as well as potential differences in disease biology, such as dysplasia or epigenetic drivers [28]. After multivariable adjustment, Black race was associated with DNMT3A mutations and a trend toward enrichment of TP53 mutations. Hispanic participants also exhibited unique genetic patterns, including over twice the rate of U2AF1 mutations and three times the prevalence of GNB1 mutations—a gene rarely mutated in MDS—and PTPN11 mutations. While these observations offer valuable early insight into racial and ethnic differences in MDS biology, studies employing broad genetic testing across diverse populations are needed to fully understand the spectrum of MDS‐driving mutations.

Survival analyses further confirmed the prognostic impact of key clinical and genetic features of MDS spectrum conditions. Older age, MDS versus precursor conditions, higher‐risk CCRS and IPSS‐R scores, and TP53 mutations were independently associated with worse OS. Risk factors for inferior PFS were the same, although the effect of age was abrogated after multivariable adjustment. The worse survival for males than females in univariable models, which did not persist after multivariable adjustment, offers evidence of differences in MDS pathology driving differences in outcomes. Although racial differences did not reach statistical significance, the trend toward improved PFS and OS among Black participants is notable, particularly considering their higher‐risk features and lower treatment. These patterns suggest that biological mechanisms, such as mutations, may play a more prominent role in race‐related differences in MDS outcomes than socioeconomic or access‐to‐care differences.

The MDS NHS cohort offers notable strengths not available in any other study, but limitations remain. For example, residual selection bias may exist due to enrollment through NCORP and academic centers, which may exclude patients from private community practices that do not participate in clinical research; however, targeted strategies to enhance community accrual resulted in over 140 sites across the U.S., providing a broad representation of patients evaluated for unexplained cytopenias. In fact, the racial and ethnic distribution of MDS NHS participants closely mirrors those with MDS in the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR) [26], further supporting broad inclusion. Interpretation of differences by race, ethnicity, and sex may be limited by the small number of Black and Hispanic participants with MDS‐spectrum disorders, and these findings should therefore be viewed as hypothesis‐generating. Observed differences may also reflect variation in health care engagement or access, though formal assessment is beyond the scope of this study. Lastly, the study design emphasizing comprehensive, adjudicated diagnostic workup led to inclusion of participants ultimately found not to have MDS or myeloid precursor states. Although this reduced diagnostic specificity at enrollment, it enabled rigorous confirmation of diagnoses and characterization of the heterogenous cytopenic conditions encountered in real‐world practice, while still providing reasonable sample sizes for key analyses.

In summary, our findings highlight the clinical and molecular heterogeneity of MDS spectrum conditions and emphasize the critical influence of demographic variables on disease course and prognosis. The observed differences by sex, race, and ethnicity support the need for more personalized approaches to diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment in MDS and call for inclusive research to ensure equitable care for all patient populations.
