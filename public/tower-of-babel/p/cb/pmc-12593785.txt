Identification of a VHL germline deletion in a family with Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome using MLPA-NGS
Authors: Yongchen Yang, Xiaolan Ren, Chaoran Xia, Ying Zhang, Xiaozhen Song, Xiaojun Tang, Chengkan Du, Wuhen Xu, Wenhao Weng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVon Hippel-Lindau syndrome (VHL) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by the development of tumors and cysts in multiple organs. Pathogenic variants in VHL are known to be associated with the development of this syndrome. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate VHL rearrangements in a family with VHL syndrome. In the proband, who presented with retinal hemangioma, whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed significant genetic variation. However, WES did not detect germline deletion in VHL in the proband’s son. Furthermore, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA)-next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis showed that the genomes of the proband and her son encompassed a deletion in VHL, extending from exon 2 to exon 3. Via increasing the probe density to gradually approach the breakpoint and following Sanger sequencing analysis, it was verified that an intermediate fragment of 6,662 bp was lost, with a reconnection occurring between chromosome 3:10145434 and 10,152,097 (GRCh38/hg38). Overall, the present study demonstrated that the application of MLPA-NGS technology combined with Sanger sequencing could be employed to precisely locate deleted DNA ‌fragments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02252-y.Keywords: Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome, Genomic rearrangement, Germline deletion, Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, Next-generation sequencing, Breakpoints

Background

Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome (VHL; OMIM 193300) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by the development of tumors and cysts in multiple organs, such as the retina, the central nervous system, the kidneys and adrenal glands [1, 2]. These tumors, such as hemangioblastomas and renal cell carcinomas, can manifest at various stages of life, with significant variability in clinical presentation. It has been reported that ~ 80% of cases result from familial transmission, while the remaining 20% arise from de novo pathogenic variants [3]. The aforementioned de novo mutations can occur either in the affected individual or as postzygotic events in a mosaic parent.

VHL syndrome is caused by mutations in the VHL gene, which is located on chromosome 3. A comprehensive analysis of VHL mutations, encompassing >700 unique variants, using the Universal Mutation Database (UMD) and the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), identified a wide spectrum of mutation types, including missense (52%), frameshift (13%), nonsense (11%), in-frame deletions/insertions (6%), large/complete deletions (11%), and splice site (7%) mutations [4]. Notably, direct and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) sequencing in diagnostic laboratories in the Netherlands detected 47 novel mutations. In a large cohort of patients with VHL in China, 126 distinct VHL mutations were identified, with missense mutations being the most prevalent (70%), followed by nonsense (20%) and splicing mutations (10%) [5]. Among these, 32.6% of families carried insertions or deletions, while the remaining 67.4% carried point mutations. Germline mutations were predominantly located in exon 1, with fewer in exons 2 and 3, and intron 2.

While single-gene detection has historically been used to identify VHL mutations, whole-exome sequencing (WES) is considered as a powerful tool for unbiased detection of pathogenic variants across multiple genes. However, the accuracy of WES in detecting copy number variations (CNVs) of < 100 kb is questionable [6–8]. MLPA remains the gold standard for detecting gene CNVs [9]. Recent advancements in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have led to the development of MLPA-NGS [10, 11], also known as the digital MLPA approach, as shown in Fig. 1. This technique combines the specificity of MLPA with the high-throughput capabilities of NGS. It offers significantly higher throughput compared with traditional MLPA and can fully replace MLPA in detecting CNVs, including deletions and duplications.

The workflow of the MLPA-NGS technology. The process begins with probe design, where left and right probes are synthesized (a). These probes include a region for binding to the target DNA and a universal primer-binding region derived from Illumina adapter sequences. Following target DNA denaturation, the probes hybridize to their complementary sequences (b). Under the catalysis of ligase, the left and right probes are then ligated together (c). Subsequently, PCR amplification is performed using the Illumina adapter sequences as primers to amplify the ligated probes (d). This step generates amplified products containing diverse target segments (e). The products are then sequenced (f), and the resulting data is analyzed to determine copy number variations (CNV) across the target regions (g). p5 and p7 sequences：They are sequences that allow the library to bind and generate clusters on the flow cell; Rd1 SP and Rd2 SP：They are sequencing primer binding sites to initiate sequencing; Index 1 and Index 2：They are index sequences. Index 1 and Index 2 are sample identifiers that allow multiplexing/pooling of multiple samples in a single sequencing run or flow cell lane.

Herein, the case of a family with a deletion in VHL was reported to elucidate the etiology of the disease in the affected individual. The current study focused on pedigree investigation and on conducting genetic tests to detect variations in VHL in both the proband and other family members. Therefore, the MLPA-NGS method was used to detect small deletions in VHL in the proband and their children. Additionally, Sanger sequencing was performed to precisely identify the recombination site responsible for the possible deletions.

Methods

Case presentation/subjects

The female patient, suspicious of VHL disease, was referred to the Department of Neurosurgery, Shanghai Children’s Hospital. She was diagnosed with retinal hemangioma at the age of 14 and she was later confirmed as the proband (III2). The proband’s mother (II3) was also diagnosed with VHL syndrome. The family pedigree is illustrated in Fig. 2. Despite surgical intervention and treatment, the proband continued to exhibit cysts in multiple organs. In 2023, at the age of 29, the proband underwent WES capture using the OmniSeek probe on the Illumina sequencing platform. Flash Analysis online software was used for medical exome analysis, with copy number determination achieved via comparing the number of reads within the sample interval, focusing on genes associated with hereditary diseases and clinical symptoms. Based on the sequencing coverage and other parameters, a heterozygous deletion was identified in VHL (Gene ID, 7428), and more particularly in exons 2 and 3. Although her son was strongly suspected of inheriting the affected VHL, he has not manifested any symptoms so far. Using the same method, a total of two rounds of WES was conducted. Therefore, the first round failed to detect VHL, while the second round verified the presence of the same deletion in VHL as that found in the proband. In December 2023, the proband couple came for genetic counseling at the Genetic Counseling Clinic, Shanghai Children’s Hospital. Healthy control samples were collected from male pediatric participants during routine physical examinations at Shanghai Children’s Hospital between October and December 2023. The current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children’s Hospital (approval no. 2024R049E01) and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Venous blood samples were obtained from the patient and her relatives (III1, III2, IV1). Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (cat no. DP318-02; Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd.). The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using the Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Subsequently, the DNA samples were stored at −20˚C for further analysis.

MLPA-NGS analysis

To identify CNVs in VHL (NG_008212.3), probe sets were designed using a three-step process (Table SI and SII). This is a method that incrementally approaches the breakpoint via increasing probe density. Each pair of probes consisted of a left (L) and a right (R) probe. The universal adapter sequences incorporated into the probes were as follows: the left probe contained the F1 sequence (a subset of the Rd1 SP adapter, 5’-CCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-3’), and the right probe contained the R1 sequence (a subset of the Rd2 SP adapter, 5’-TCCAACCCTTAGGGAACCCCGATC-3’). The 5’ end of the right probe encompassed a phosphate group. Probes and primers were synthesized using the DNA phosphoramidite approach at Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. The upstream and downstream primer sequences used for DNA amplification were as follows: 5’-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACACNNNNNNNNACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-3’ and 5’-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGATNNNNNNNNGTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTCGGGGTTCCCTAAGGGTTGGA-3’, where N in the primer sequences indicating the location of the feature sequences [11]. The 3’ sequences of the upstream and downstream primers are in a same-directional sequence and reverse complementary sequence relationship with the 5’-CCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-3’ and 5’-TCCAACCCTTAGGGAACCCCGATC-3’ sequences on the probe, respectively. This design conforms to PCR principles, enabling these two primers to initiate the PCR amplification process using the connected left and right probes as templates.

For example, for the VHL-1a probe pair, after the final amplification, a sequence segment can be generated as follows: 5‘-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACACNNNNNNNNACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTGAACTGGGACGAGGCCGAGGTAGGCGCGGAGGAGGCAGGCGTCGAAGAGTTCCAACCCTTAGGGAACCCCGAGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCACNNNNNNNNATCTCGTATGCCGTCTTCTGCTTG-3’. In this sequence, the segment 5‘-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACAC-3’ corresponds to the p5, the NNNNNNNN corresponds to two index, the segment 5‘-ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT − 3’ corresponds to the Rd1 SP, the segment 5‘-TCCAACCCTTAGGGAACCCCGAGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC − 3’ corresponds to the Rd2 SP, and the segment 5‘-ATCTCGTATGCCGTCTTCTGCTTG-3’ corresponds to the p7 adapter. The middle segment, 5‘-GAACTGGGACGAGGCCGAGGTAGGCGCGGAGGAGGCAGGCGTCGAAGAGT-3’, is the region where the VHL-1a probe binds to the DNA and is the target sequence for sequencing. These designs strictly adhere to Illumina’s guidelines for adapter sequence design.

The pre-PCR procedure for the MLPA-NGS assay was performed using the VHL probe sets. Initially, 20–500 ng of isolated human genomic DNA dissolved in TE to 5 µL was added into a 0.2-mL PCR tube and then denatured. Then, equal volumes of working solution of the MLPA buffer (MRC Holland) and probe mix were mixed, and 3 µL was added to each of the PCR tubes. The next day, the ligation solution was prepared for each reaction tube, including 3 µL ligation buffer A, 3 µL ligation buffer B, 1 µL ligase-65 (MRC Holland), and 25 µL dH2O. After mixing, the temperature of the PCR instrument was reduced to 54 °C. The lid of the PCR tube on the PCR instrument was opened, and 32 µL of ligase mixture was added, mixed with pipettes adequately, incubated at 54 °C for 15 min, heated at 98 °C for 5 min, and removed from the PCR instrument after incubation at 20 °C. The probe ligation product was then obtained. Finally, the PCR samples were prepared in new PCR tubes. Each PCR tube included 2 µL 10×Taq enzyme buffer, 0.25 µL TaKaRa Taq (R001B, Takara-Bio, Dalian, China), 4 µL dNTP (Takara, Japan), 1 µL forward primer, 1 µL reverse primer, and 31.75 µL dH2O. Ten microliters of the ligation product was added at room temperature, and then the PCR procedure was performed as follows: 35 cycles of 30 s at 95℃, 30 s at 60℃, and 60 s at 72℃, and finished with a 20 min extension period at 72℃, incubated at 15 °C. Upstream and downstream primers with barcodes were used for sample identification. Ten microliters of PCR amplification product was taken from each tube and mixed well. The library concentration was initially quantified by a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), and the integrity and inserted fragment size of the library were detected by an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA). If the sample was qualified, forward 40 bp + 8 bp sequencing was performed by a NextSeq 550AR gene sequencer (ANOROAD Gene Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd.); that is, forward sequencing of 40 bp was performed in the probe binding region with gDNA, and the index of the forward primer was sequenced 8 bp. A fastQ file with index information was obtained. To ensure data quality, no less than 0.02 G of raw bases were obtained for each sample.

After quality assessment of the exported FASTQ files, total reads were counted in fastQ files, and assignment of the reads to VHL probe sets and data analysis were performed by a program written in Python with the recommended GRCh38/hg38 reference version for mapping. First, three segments of 8 bp each used as the detective sequence of reads for the 40 bp region were intercepted from the forward to the backward probe in the binding region, as shown in Supplementary Tables 1and 2.

To determine the count of reads containing each feature sequence, the findall function from the Python Regular Expressions module can be utilized. Each set of sequences was specific for each pair of probes and the corresponding binding sites. The read number generated by each pair of probes for each sample was defined as follows: if a read contained the detective sequence of a pair of probes, the read was considered an analyzable read that was attributed to the probe. Second, the relative amplification of each pair of probes in the VHL probe sets was obtained by comparing the reads of each pair of probes with the sum of read numbers from the internal reference probes located on autosomes and the target probes. In each specimen, normalization of the target genes was achieved by calculating the ratio of the total reads within the target region (VHL, BRK1, IRAK2) to internal reference genes (PKHD1, EYA1, ATP7B, TCF4). The sex of the sample was determined by the ratio of relative amplification from two pairs of probes located on the Y chromosome to two pairs of probes located on the X chromosome. A ratio less than 0.1 was considered female, while a ratio greater than 0.5 was considered male. Finally, the normalized values of each fragment in the sample were compared to the average normalized values of multiple male control samples in order to ascertain the relative copy number of each probe pair within the sample. In general, quality control was considered acceptable if the value of the relative copy number fluctuated within approximately 30% of the theoretical value, which is the same criteria as a commercial kit (MRC-Holland). The test accuracy was assessed by whether each positive or negative sample passed quality control. For reference reads of autosomes in each sample, the average ± SD was used for statistics to analyze amplification uniformity.

Gene chip analysis

Gene chip analysis was performed using the Infinium OmniZhongHua-8 v1.3 BeadChip (Ilumina, Inc.). This method is used to detect ~ 240 CNV-affected genetic diseases/regions, including uniparental disomy and polyploidy. The detection range included deletions and duplications of >1 megabase (Mb) and >2 Mb, respectively, on autosomes [1–22] and sex chromosomes (X/Y), and heterozygosity loss of >5 Mb (cn-LOH). The genotypes of the study samples were determined using Gene chip analysis. IMPUTE2 [12] was utilized to impute supplementary autosomal single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Download and usage instructions for the IMPUTE2 software can be referred to at the following website: http://mathgen.stats.ox.ac.uk/impute/impute.html. The genotype assignments were grounded on the phase 3000 Genomes cosmopolitan reference panel.

Sanger sequencing

To target the breakpoint site the probe was strategically positioned to allow a theoretical maximum amplification area of ~ 1,500 bp. The forward and reverse primers were subsequently designed for amplification at the probe closest to the fractures, while maintaining a copy number of two. The primer sequences were as follows: 5’-AAACAGAATCTTAGGGCTGGAGGATTGCT-3’ and 5’-GGAAAACTGGAGTGTCTCATTCACTTCA-3’. The amplification mix (cat. no. R001B; Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) consisted of 2 µl DNA template, 36.75 µl dH2O, 5 µl 10x Taq Buffer, 4 µl dNTP, 0.25 µl Taq enzyme and 1 µl of each forward and reverse primers. The thermocycling conditions for PCR were as follows: 35 cycles of denaturation at 95˚C for 30 s; annealing at 60˚C for 30 s; and extension at 72˚C for 60 s. The mix was further incubated at 72˚C for 20 min and then cooled to 15˚C. The amplification products were subjected to 15 g/l agarose-gel electrophoresis and were subsequently verified by Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.) using Sequencher 5.0.1 (Genecodes). The sequence structure of the breakpoint site was analyzed using the UCSC Genome Browser. The “Repeats” option was selected to analyze the distribution and characteristics of repetitive elements, including Alu elements, within the breakpoint site.

Results

Gene chip analysis

As shown in Fig. 3, no copy number and CNV variations were identified on chromosome 3 and in the vicinity of VHL, respectively. The analysis was carried out as previously described [13]. Consequently, if a copy number variation existed in VHL, its magnitude should be smaller than the chip’s detection range.

MLPA-NGS analysis

A total of five normal male samples served as controls. The first batch of probes identified a heterozygous deletion of exon 2 and 3 in VHL in both the proband and her son. No deletions were detected in exon 1 or genes around. This finding was consistent with the WES results (gray probes; Fig. 4). The second batch of probe sets was added for further validation (green probes; Fig. 4), thus enabling the approximation of the two breakpoints. The maximum theoretical distance between the two pairs of probes with a copy number of 2 still exceeded the limit of conventional PCR amplification. The above distance included the sum of the length between VHL-a4 and VHL-a3 (~ 1,374 bp) and that between VHL-b3 and VHL-b1 (~ 5,070 bp). To address this issue, the results from the third batch of probe sets not only verified the previous findings (red probe; Fig. 4), but also determined the maximum theoretical distance between the two pairs of probes with a copy number of 2. This distance was the sum of the length between VHL-a35 and VHL-a4 (~ 479 bp) and that between VHL-b13 and VHL-b14 (~ 989 bp), resulting in a fragment of 1,468 bp in length. Consequently, primer design sites were selected to allow efficient amplification using common Taq enzymes. The gradual approach of the probe toward the breakpoint on the chromosome is depicted in Fig. 5. The original data for the eight samples are presented in Table SII.

Sanger analysis

During the amplification process, upstream primers targeting the VHL-a35 probe position and downstream primers targeting the VHL-b14 position were employed. Subsequent electrophoresis revealed a distinct band < 500 bp in both the proband and her son, while no similar band was observed in the proband’s husband (Fig. 6A). Subsequently the PCR product was subjected to Sanger sequencing (Fig. 6B). A total of two breakpoints (positions, 10145434–10145435 and 10152096–10152097) were identified in chromosome 3 compared with the wild type VHL sequence in GRCh38/hg38 (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/refseq/vertebrate_mammalian/Homo_sapiens/annotation_releases/109.20200228/GCF_000001405.39_GRCh38.p13/). Therefore, an intermediate fragment of 6,662 bp was lost, while a reconnection occurred between locations 10,145,434 and 10,152,097 in chromosome 3.

Sanger analysis of the proband family. A Agarose gel electrophoresis displaying the PCR products of the proband family. B The red arrow indicates the reconnected site after the fracture

Alu elements represent the most abundant class of repetitive sequences in the human genome, with approximately one million copies distributed throughout. These sequences, typically ~ 300 bp in size, are classified as short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) [14]. The breakpoints identified in this pedigree were located within Alu repeats, and more particularly within AluY and AluSx, as determined by analyzing the ‘Repeats’ track of the UCSC Genome Browser (GRCh38/hg38 assembly) at the respective genomic coordinates. The AluY element spans chr3:10145424–10,145,735 (GRCh38/hg38) in the positive orientation and has a length of 312 bp in the genome. The breakpoint was located between the 11th and 12th bases of this element. Similarly, the AluSx element spans chr3:10152086–10,152,384 (GRCh38/hg38), also in the positive orientation, and has a length of 299 bp. In this element, the breakpoint was located between the 10th and 11th bases. These findings were similar with those reported by Franke et al. [15], where ~ 90% of the breakpoints were associated with Alu repeats. The association between the deletion regions and the Alu repeats is illustrated in Fig. 7.

Conclusion

Exome sequencing is a powerful tool for identifying CNVs in genetic disorders [16]. However, while large CNVs are readily detectable, smaller CNVs commonly require additional validation methods, such as qPCR, MLPA, gene chip analysis, or third-generation sequencing. Although qPCR is widely used due to its simplicity, its accuracy can be inconsistent, particularly for smaller CNVs [17, 18]. Gene chip analysis is effective for detecting large CNVs. However, its reliability depends on the use of specific probes targeting the region of interest. Without such probes, CNV detection can become unreliable. Similarly, medical exome sequencing can fail to identify CNVs in the absence of a well-defined disease phenotype. While third-generation sequencing offers superior resolution, its high cost and technical complexity have hindered its widespread adoption in clinical settings. By contrast, MLPA remains the gold standard for detecting small CNVs due to its cost-effectiveness and reliability [19]. However, traditional MLPA relies on capillary electrophoresis for band differentiation based on the length of the amplified fragments. Additionally, the synthesis of long fragments requires incorporating redundant sequences using bacteriophage T7 DNA packaging, thus making MLPA complex and limiting its usage to a few specialized companies.

MLPA-NGS integrates the advantages of both MLPA and NGS, thus enabling the sequence-based differentiation of amplified products. This approach can reduce synthesis costs and enhance bioinformatics analysis, thus making it particularly valuable for researchers with expertise in bioinformatics analysis. MLPA-NGS not only retains the advantages of traditional MLPA, but also significantly increases throughput. As NGS becomes more accessible and cost-effective, MLPA-NGS is poised to become a viable alternative to conventional MLPA [20]. Herein, MLPA-NGS was utilized to analyze CNVs in VHL within a family pedigree. Through iterative probe design, the breakpoint region was narrowed to a 1,500-bp span, which was subsequently confirmed by PCR amplification to be < 500 bp in length. Sequencing and comparative analysis successfully identified the breakpoint, thus confirming the deletion in both the proband and her son. This finding underscored the reliability of MLPA-NGS for precise CNV detection. MLPA-NGS is highly effective for detecting medium to large CNVs. By designing multiple probe pairs that progressively approach the breakpoint, it allows for direct PCR amplification. By contrast, gene chip probes are fixed, and their high replacement costs limit their utility. Similarly, while qPCR is widely used, its throughput and sensitivity are limited. These factors position MLPA-NGS as a superior method for the precise detection of genomic deletions.

VHL encodes the VHL protein, which regulates critical cellular processes, including cell growth, differentiation and angiogenesis. Structurally, it consists of two main regions: an N-terminal domain involved in protein-protein interactions and a C-terminal domain responsible for binding to hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF). Functionally, VHL acts as the substrate recognition component of an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, targeting HIF-α subunits for proteasomal degradation under normal oxygen conditions. Mutations in the VHL gene disrupt its ability to regulate HIF, leading to excessive HIF-α accumulation, uncontrolled angiogenesis, and tumor development, which characterize VHL syndrome, a disorder characterized by tumors, such as renal cell carcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors [5]. Precise identification of breakpoints is essential for accurate diagnosis and can serve as a biomarker for preimplantation genetic screening in families considering in vitro fertilization [6]. Furthermore, it provides critical information for genetic counseling, family member verification, and the development of future therapeutic strategies, such as gene-targeted therapies.

The identification of this exon 2–3 deletion has immediate clinical importance for the family. Based on the ACMG/ClinGen guidelines for the interpretation of copy number variants [21], this deletion can be definitively classified as Pathogenic (PVS1, PS1, PM2). This confirms the molecular diagnosis for the proband and her affected mother, provides a clear result for genetic counseling regarding the son’s asymptomatic carrier status, and offers a precise biomarker for future family planning.

Our finding also expertly contextualizes the novelty of our work within existing knowledge. Deletions encompassing exons 2 and 3 of the VHL gene represent a recurrent pathogenic mechanism in VHL syndrome, frequently recorded in ClinVar and the literature [22–25], often mediated by homologous recombination between Alu repeats. The primary novelty of our work, therefore, lies not in the discovery of an exon 2–3 deletion per se, but in the precise determination of its breakpoints at chr3:10145434 and chr3:10152097 (GRCh38/hg38), which we mapped to within AluY and AluSx repetitive elements, respectively. This precise mapping adds a new data point to the spectrum of known Alu-mediated rearrangements in the VHL gene and provides this family with a unique molecular signature.

This study, for the first time, described the use of MLPA-NGS combined with Sanger sequencing technology to precisely identify a 6,662 bp deletion in the VHL gene, with breakpoints located within Alu repeat sequences (AluY and AluSx). The high precision and efficiency of this approach provide a novel method for molecular diagnosis of VHL gene deletions. Although Alu-mediated recombination is a common mechanism in VHL gene deletions, the specific breakpoint combination identified in this study (reconnection between chromosome 3:10145434 and 10152097) has not been recorded in the ClinVar database, indicating that this represents a novel variant. This finding was consistent with previous studies, such as that reported by Casarin et al. [7], which demonstrated that Alu repeats were highly prevalent in VHL, occurring approximately every 1 kb and accounting for 20–25% of the gene sequence. This frequency was significantly higher than the genome-wide average of one Alu repeat per 4 kb.

Alu-mediated recombination is a well-documented mechanism underlying genomic deletions in several human diseases, including VHL syndrome [15, 26]. Franke et al. [15] analyzed 54 germline deletions in VHL, thus identifying Alu repeats in 90% of cases. Notably, seven of these deletions were associated with the AluYa5 repeat, a relatively recent evolutionary element. Similarly, Vocke et al. [26] observed a high frequency of breakpoints associated with AluYa5 (26/58, 45%). Herein, the identified fracture region was queried in the ClinVar database, yet no corresponding mutation was recorded.

In conclusion, MLPA-NGS combined with Sanger sequencing successfully identified CNVs in VHL within this pedigree, thus overcoming the limitations of medical exome sequencing and gene chip analysis. The iterative probe design approach enabled precise breakpoint localization, thus supporting the superior accuracy of MLPA-NGS compared with qPCR and gene chip methods. Furthermore, the identification of a novel Alu repeat-associated breakpoint could provide valuable insights for future research into VHL-related genomic rearrangements.
