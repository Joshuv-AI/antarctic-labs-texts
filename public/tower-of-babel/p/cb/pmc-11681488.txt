Prevalence, Characteristics, and Treatment Strategy of Different Types of Heart Failure in Patients With Nonvalvular Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Yulong Xia, Jie Jiang, Fangfang Fan, Yannan Pan, Jing Zhou, Yan Zhang, Jianping Li, Jun Liu, Na Yang, Yongchen Hao, Dong Zhao, Jing Liu, Changsheng Ma, Yong Huo, CCC‐AF investigators
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia in patients with heart failure (HF). Epidemiological data regarding HF in patients with AF are lacking. We describe the epidemiology, clinical features, treatment strategies, and in‐hospital outcomes in patients with AF and HF.Methods and ResultsPatients with HF and nonvalvular AF in the Improving Care for Cardiovascular Disease in China‐AF cohort from February 2015 to December 2019 were included. Patients were stratified by left ventricular ejection fraction into HF with reduced EF, HF with mildly reduced EF, and HF with preserved EF groups. The primary outcome was the occurrence of hospitalization for major adverse cardiovascular events, including death, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, and stroke. Overall, 16 562 patients with AF and HF were included (mean age: 72.35±11.07 years; 46.1% female). HF with preserved EF (63.1%) accounted for the largest proportion, followed by HF with mildly reduced EF (19.0%) and HF with reduced EF (17.9%). Different HF subtypes in patients with AF had unique baseline demographic and clinical characteristics after multinomial logistic regression analysis. Compared with the HF with preserved EF group, hospitalization for major adverse cardiovascular events was increased in the HF with mildly reduced EF group (odds ratio=1.55 [95% CI, 1.18–2.03]) and HF with reduced EF group (odds ratio 1.60 [95% CI, 1.21–2.13]) after adjusting for confounders.ConclusionsIn this large Chinese AF registry, the distribution of HF differed from the non‐AF population. Patients with AF with different types of HF have unique demographic and clinical characteristics. Occurrence rates of in‐hospital outcomes were higher in patients with HF with mildly reduced EF and patients with HF with reduced EF compared with the HF with preserved EF group.RegistrationURL: http://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT02309398.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, epidemiology, heart failure, outcomes, quality measuresSubject Categories: Heart Failure, Atrial Fibrillation, Epidemiology

Heart failure (HF) remains a serious public health challenge worldwide because of its high prevalence, mortality, morbidity, and costs of care. The global number of HF cases increased by 91.9% between 1990 and 2017, with China experiencing the largest increase. 1 Although HF prevalence in China was 1.3%, there was a 44.0% increase after 2000, with >8 million patients with HF in China currently. 2

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia in patients with HF, with a prevalence rate of 25%. 3 The AF prevalence was reportedly 1.6% in the Chinese adult population. 4 Anticoagulation therapies reduce thromboembolism‐related mortality in patients with AF, while cardiac complications, with a significant HF burden, remain the leading cause of adverse events. 5 , 6 The interrelationship between these frequently co‐existing conditions significantly impacts future health care economics. However, epidemiological data regarding HF in patients with AF are lacking.

HF can be classified by the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) into 3 subtypes: HF with reduced ejection fraction (EF) (HFrEF), HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF), and HF with preserved EF (HFpEF). 7 Heterogeneity was found regarding clinical features and prognostic significance of AF in different types of HF, 8 , 9 with contrasting findings in meta‐analyses. 10 , 11

This study aimed to describe the epidemiological features, treatment status, and in‐hospital outcomes of patients with AF with different subtypes of HF from the Improving Care for Cardiovascular Disease in China‐AF (CCC‐AF) project, 12 as well as the differences between clinical practice and the recommendations of current treatment guidelines.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

The CCC‐AF program was a collaboration between the American Heart Association and the Chinese Society of Cardiology. This program was designed to promote adherence to guideline recommendations and outcomes in hospitalized patients with AF. Overall, 151 tertiary and 85 secondary hospitals were recruited from across 30 Chinese provinces after launching in February 2015. A web‐based data collection platform was used, with the initiative of quality improvements. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital (No. 2014018), with a waiver of informed consent. Detailed organizational frameworks, quality control of data collection, and performance measures have been described elsewhere. 12 The investigation conforms with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

This analysis focused on patients diagnosed with AF and HF. Participants were included if 1 of the following criteria was met: (1) history of HF or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), (2) newly diagnosed HF or cardiogenic shock during hospitalization, (3) CRT during hospitalization, and (4) diagnosis of HF or New York Heart Association functional classification class III or IV. Participants were excluded if they had missing LVEF documentation during hospitalization or a history of mitral valve stenosis or valve replacement. Participants with missing study variables were also excluded. Patients were stratified by LVEF into HFrEF with LVEF <40%, HFmrEF with LVEF between 40% and 49%, and HFpEF with LVEF of ≥50%, according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines. 7

Study Variables

The study variables included demographics (age, sex category), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure, medical history (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI], CRT, ischemic stroke, peripheral vascular disease, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal disease, liver disease, thyroid disease, cancer, cognitive disorders, bleeding events or bleeding tendency, smoking, and drinking), clinical characteristics of AF (AF type, comorbid atrial flutter, CHA2DS2‐VASc score, European Heart Rhythm Association score, resting heart rate), and in‐hospital treatments (rhythm control [if any of the following during hospitalization: (1) catheter ablation, (2) electrical cardioversion, (3) Maze procedure, and (4) Type Ic or III antiarrhythmic drug]) or non–rhythm control therapies [if none of the above therapies were used], CRT, implantable cardiac defibrillator, pacemakers, PCI, and in‐hospital medical therapies. Anticoagulation therapies were further detailed according to CHA2DS2‐VASc scores, with a score ≥2 representing a high risk of thromboembolic events.

In‐Hospital Outcomes

This analysis defined a composite of hospitalization for major adverse cardiovascular events, including death, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, and stroke.

Statistical Analysis

HF prevalence was calculated based on sex category, age, region, and year of admission. Baseline characteristics, treatments, and in‐hospital outcomes were compared between the LVEF groups. Smooth curves of the odds of prevalence of HFrEF and HFmrEF compared with HFpEF across ages were drawn using the generalized additive models by R Foundation. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages and compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables were reported as means±SD or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate. The groups were compared using ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis rank test, as appropriate. Multinomial logistic regression was used to analyze differences in clinical characteristics according to HF subtypes. Treatments for different HF subtypes were compared according to the year of admission. Adjusted logistic regression models were used to determine associations between HF subtypes and in‐hospital outcomes. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI were calculated. All analyses were performed using R Foundation (version 4.1.0). A 2‐tailed P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Mean LVEFs were 31.81%±5.59%, 44.25%±2.87%, and 60.53%±6.58% in the HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF groups, respectively. A greater percentage of patients with persistent AF were found in the HFrEF group (29.4%) compared with HFmrEF (28.7%) or HFpEF (27.1%) groups. With increases in LVEF, SBP and CHA2S2‐VASc scores increased (P<0.001), while symptom severities (European Heart Rhythm Association grade III and IV) decreased (P<0.001). The resting heart rate was highest in the HFrEF group.

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics Across LVEF Groups

Patients with AF with different HF subtypes had unique baseline demographic and clinical characteristics after multinomial logistic regression, as shown in Table S1. Compared with the HFpEF group, those in the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups were younger and less likely to be female. A lower proportion of hypertension and a higher proportion of previous myocardial infarction, renal disease, and severe symptoms (European Heart Rhythm Association grade IV) were found in the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups, compared with the HFpEF group. Patients in the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups also tended to have a lower SBP, higher diastolic blood pressure, and faster resting heart rate.

A higher proportion of diabetes, previous CRT, and a lower proportion of stroke, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, thyroid disease, and cancer were found in the HFrEF group compared with the HFpEF group. Also, patients with persistent, permanent, or long‐standing AF were more prominent in the HFrEF group than in the HFpEF group. The proportion of patients with a history of PCI, bleeding, or bleeding tendency and paroxysmal AF in the HFmrEF group was lower than in the HFpEF group.

In‐Hospital Treatments of HF Subtypes

Treatments are summarized according to HF subtypes in Table 1. Few patients in our study cohort adopted rhythm control strategies (15.4%). No differences in rhythm control therapy were found among the 3 groups (P=0.206). More patients in the HFpEF group received radiofrequency AF ablation and pacemakers than in the HFmrEF and HFrEF groups (P<0.001). The majority of implantable cardiac defibrillators were implanted in the HFrEF group. Regarding in‐hospital medical therapy, β‐blockers and digoxin were more frequently used in the HFrEF group, whereas propafenone was more frequently used in the HFpEF group. No differences were observed in amiodarone, sotalol, aspirin, and oral anticoagulation therapy use among the groups. Specifically, warfarin was more frequently used in the HFrEF group, whereas dabigatran and rivaroxaban were more frequently used in the HFpEF group. Overall, 9285 (56.1%) patients with CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥2 were not anticoagulated. Reasons included patient preference (13.7%), inability to adhere (9.0%), and physician preference (6.3%), as shown in Table S2.

For discharge medication, oral anticoagulation therapy and aspirin use were similar among the groups. β‐blockers, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), aldosterone antagonists, digoxin, diuretics, and concomitant use of β‐blockers and ACEIs/ARBs were less prescribed with increase in LVEF. Statins were prescribed more frequently in patients with HFpEF.

Increasing trends in anticoagulation use in patients with CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥ 2 and statin prescriptions in all HF subtypes were found. Increasing trends in β‐blockers in HFrEF and HFpEF groups were found. However, the use of ACEIs/ARBs had a decreasing annual trend in patients with HFrEF and HFmrEF, even after excluding those with renal disease. In addition, the concomitant use of β‐blockers with ACEIs/ARBs did not change significantly among all HF subgroups, as shown in Tables S3 through S5.

In‐Hospital Outcomes

Occurrence rates of in‐hospital outcomes, particularly cardiac arrest, cardiogenic shock, and death, were higher in the HFmrEF and HFrEF groups, as shown in Table S6. After multivariable adjustment, the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events was higher in the HFmrEF group (OR=1.55 [95% CI, 1.18–2.03]; P=0.001) and HFrEF group (OR=1.60 [95% CI, 1.21–2.13]; P=0.001) compared with the HFpEF group, as shown in Table 2. Regarding specific outcomes, after adjusting for confounders, increased incidence rates of death (HFmrEF: OR 2.15 [95% CI, 1.27–3.63]; HFrEF: OR 2.57 [95% CI, 1.51–4.36]); and cardiac arrest (HFmrEF: OR 3.04 [95% CI, 1.66–5.56]; HFrEF: OR 3.60 [95% CI, 1.97–6.59]) were found in the HFmrEF and HFrEF groups compared with the HFpEF group, while increased incidence rates of cardiogenic shock (OR 1.84 [95% CI, 1.18–2.85]) were found in the HFmrEF group compared with the HFpEF group, as shown in Tables S7 through S10.

DISCUSSION

AF is common in patients with HF, and they have recently emerged as a dual epidemic. Recent HF studies have shown that the proportion of AF comorbidity was 36.8% in the PARADIGM‐HF (Prospective Comparison of ARNI (angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor) with ACEI (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors) to Determine Impact on Global Mortality and morbidity in Heart Failure trial) study of patients with HFrEF, 13 whereas it was 32.4% in the PARAGON‐HF (Prospective Comparison of ARNI (angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor) with ARB (angiotensin‐receptor blockers) Global Outcomes in HF with Preserved Ejection Fraction) study of patients with HFpEF. 14 Notably, patients with HFpEF in the PARAGON‐HF study were defined as LVEF ≥45%, which differs from this study. Trials focusing on patients with AF, such as the CABANA (Catheter Ablation vs Antiarrhythmic Drug Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation) study, 15 reported 15.7% and 14.9% of patients with HF were in the radiofrequency ablation and medication groups, respectively. The EORP‐AF (EURObservational Research Programme Pilot survey on Atrial Fibrillation) Pilot study 16 showed that 47.5% of patients had HF in a European AF cohort. Patients with HF accounted for 42.1% of the CCC‐AF registry participants, between the EORP‐AF pilot study 16 and the CABANA study. 15 There are differences in HF proportions in these AF registries for the following reasons. First, our study population was different, with a considerable proportion of patients having paroxysmal and first‐diagnosed AF. Additionally, this was a cohort study involving East Asian populations. Furthermore, differences were found in the definitions of HF. In this study, the largest proportion of HF subtypes according to LVEF in patients with AF was HFpEF (63.1%), followed by HFmrEF (19.0%) and HFrEF (17.9%), which differs from European HF registries, where patients with HFrEF accounted for the largest proportion in both inpatient 17 and outpatient 18 HF cohorts. However, in an Asian hospitalized HF patient cohort, 19 the proportion of HFpEF (43%) was the highest, followed by the HFrEF (36%) and HFmrEF (21%) groups. Similar to this study, a large HF registry in China 20 reported that HFpEF accounted for the largest proportion of hospitalized patients with HF. Previous studies also estimated a high prevalence of AF in patients with HFpEF and vice versa. 21

Our study included >60 000 patients with AF in 30 provinces in China, representing the actual situation of hospitalized patients with HF and AF in China. HFpEF accounted for the largest proportion of patients with AF, and women were more pronounced in the HFpEF group than in the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups. The prevalences of HFrEF and HFmrEF decreased with aging compared with HFpEF, and the trend was more prominent in men than women. This is similar to the characteristics of patients with HFpEF and HFrEF in HF cohort studies, 18 , 19 in which patients with HFrEF were younger and less likely to be male. This large registry revealed the epidemiological characteristics of the different types of HF in patients with AF in China.

The term HFmrEF is defined as an LVEF in the range of 40% to 49% in patients with HF, a gray area for the phenotype and outcomes between HFrEF and HFpEF, the characteristics, pathophysiology, and treatment of which still lack clinical evidence, let alone HFmrEF in patients with AF. The European Society of Cardiology HF Long‐Term Registry 18 reported that the HFmrEF and HFrEF groups had some similar features, including age, sex category, and ischemic pathogenesis. However, the HFmrEF group had less left ventricular and atrial dilation. In a study of inpatients with HF in Japan, 19 scholars observed that the age and percentage of women in the HFmrEF group was between those in the HFrEF and HFpEF groups; however, the proportion of hypertension and coronary heart disease was the highest among the 3 groups. The China Heart Failure Center Registry Study 20 reported that the HFmrEF group was in a midposition between the HFrEF and HFpEF groups in terms of age, sex category, heart rate, SBP, and hypertension and had the highest proportion of coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction. In our analysis, patients with HFmrEF and HFrEF had unique clinical features compared with those with HFpEF. Additionally, patients with HFmrEF were young and less likely to be female compared with those in the HFpEF group. There was also a lower proportion of patients with hypertension, a history of PCI, bleeding or bleeding tendency, and paroxysmal AF, but a higher proportion of patients with a history of myocardial infarction, renal disease, and severe symptoms (European Heart Rhythm Association grade IV). Patients in the HFmrEF group also tended to have a lower SBP, higher diastolic blood pressure, and a faster resting heart rate than those in the HFpEF group. The CCC‐AF is a real‐world registry for inpatients with AF across China that adds new evidence for the clinical features of patients with HFmrEF and AF. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity in these different studies also suggests that more studies are needed to reveal the actual clinical characteristics of patients with HFmrEF to provide better support for their reasonable treatment.

Various studies have reported contradictory conclusions regarding the prognosis of different types of HF. The European Society of Cardiology HF Long‐Term Registry 18 discovered that the HFrEF group experienced higher mortality rates at 1 year for outpatients with HF than those of the HFmrEF and HFpEF groups. The Acute Heart Failure Global Registry of Standard Treatment Hospitalized 22 reported that patients with HFmrEF have a lower risk of short‐term mortality than those with HFrEF but a similar risk to patients with HFpEF. A study on hospitalized patients with chronic HF in Japan 19 reported that the mortality rates were similar in the HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF groups during hospitalization and long‐term follow‐up, despite the different clinical characteristics among the 3 groups. However, another study of patients with acute decompensated HF in Japan 23 reported that the in‐hospital prognosis of patients with HFrEF was the worst. Additionally, these were all registries for patients with HF and varied in patient characteristics and study end points. For patients with AF and HF, a meta‐analysis of 10 cardiovascular prognostic studies involving 45 100 patients with AF 11 found that all‐cause mortality was higher in the HFrEF group than in the HFpEF group, with a mean follow‐up of 30 days to 3.4 years and that the readmission rates of HF and stroke were similar between the groups. In our analysis, the in‐hospital prognosis of the patients with HFrEF and HFmrEF was significantly worse than those with AF in the HFpEF group. These differences were primarily driven by cardiac arrest and death. One possible explanation is that previous studies have found that patients with low LVEF may have a higher risk of sudden cardiac death. 24 , 25 However, the mechanism leading to this conclusion is still unclear; therefore, further research is required. Meanwhile, the follow‐up time should be extended to better understand the long‐term prognosis of patients with HF and AF.

The CCC‐AF program was designed to promote adherence to AF guideline recommendations and outcomes for inpatients with AF. We performed an analysis to show the yearly changes in quality improvement measures for both AF and HF. In our study, 15 091 (91.1%) patients had a CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥2, and only 35.1% of the patients received oral anticoagulation therapy. Despite the low proportion of anticoagulation in our study population, anticoagulation in patients with a higher risk of thromboembolic events had an increasing annual trend, regardless of HF subtypes. There was a low rate of rhythm control and catheter ablation therapy use, with an annual increasing trend of rhythm control therapy only seen in the HFpEF group. The 2020 European Society of Cardiology guidelines 26 recommend that catheter ablation should be considered in selected patients with AF and HF with a reduced LVEF to improve survival and reduce hospitalization for HF, which suggests that there is still room for improvement. No annual change was found for concomitant use of β‐blockers with ACEIs/ARBs in all 3 HF subgroups. However, an annual decreasing trend was found in the use of ACEIs/ARBs in the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups, even after excluding those with chronic renal disease. Findings of the Get With The Guidelines–Heart Failure score showed that there was an improvement in adherence with performance measures, including the use of ACEIs/ARBs and β‐blockers in patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction over time, regardless of the hospital type. 27 The relatively low rates of β‐blocker and ACEIs/ARB use in patients with AF and HF, particularly in patients with HFmrEF and HFrEF, suggest that the quality improvement program still needs to be continuously promoted to improve the prognosis of patients with AF and HF in China.

The study's strengths were as follows: The CCC‐AF program included a sufficient number of hospitalized patients with AF nationwide, with standardized recruitment criteria for hospitals and patients and strict quality control, making this registry representative of hospitalized patients with AF in China. To our knowledge, this is the first study to reveal differences in the epidemiology, in‐hospital treatments, and prognosis of the latest guideline‐defined HF types in a large AF registry. Moreover, the clinical characteristics, in‐hospital treatments, and prognosis of patients with HFmrEF with AF were compared with those of the HFrEF and HFpEF groups, providing more evidence for treating this specific group. Lastly, we discussed the trends of guideline‐directed treatment regimens for AF complicated by HF, providing more evidence for future quality improvements in this population in China.

However, this study had some limitations. First, the LVEF of all patients was recorded only once, and LVEF data were missing in ≈20% of the patients in the registry. It was known that LVEF deteriorates with disease progression or improves with treatment. However, the change in LVEF was unknown, which may have resulted in errors in the classification of HF according to LVEF, since the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of HF proposes the concept of HF with improved EF. 28 Though Simpson (Biplane) methods were mostly used in the measurements of LVEF calculation for patients with HF and AF, the methods of measurement were not standardized or recorded. Additionally, for patients diagnosed with HFpEF in this AF registry, we lacked objective evidence of cardiac structural and/or functional abnormalities that were consistent with the presence of LV diastolic dysfunction or raised LV filling pressures. To prevent the overdiagnosis of HFpEF in AF, in addition to the traditional definition of HF, we only included patients with New York Heart Association classes III and IV, which may have caused selection bias. Moreover, only hospitalized patients with AF were included in our study. Therefore, our conclusions cannot be extended to outpatients with AF. Meanwhile, we could not differentiate acute from chronic HF in the CCC‐AF registry, which may have resulted in a bias in our analysis. Only occurrence rates of in‐hospital outcomes were available for our study patients. Without long‐term follow‐ups, we may not have observed the actual prognostic impact of in‐hospital treatments. Therefore, future studies with prolonged follow‐up periods in patients with AF and HF are warranted. Finally, we noticed that sacubitril‐valsartan, sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors, and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists had been proven to improve the prognosis of patients with HF in recent clinical trials. 13 , 14 , 29 , 30 However, during the period of CCC‐AF, the above drugs may not be available in China or have not been recommended by HF guidelines. The lack of use of those drugs may influence outcomes in patients with HF.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this large Chinese registry of patients with AF comorbid with HF and stratified by LVEF, the distribution of HF differed from the non‐AF population. Patients with different subtypes of HF in AF have unique demographic and clinical characteristics. Additionally, occurrence rates of in‐hospital outcomes were higher in patients in the HFmrEF (OR=1.55 [95% CI, 1.18–2.03]; P=0.001) and HFrEF (OR=1.60 [95% CI, 1.21–2.13]; P=0.001) groups compared with the HFpEF group after adjusting for confounders.
