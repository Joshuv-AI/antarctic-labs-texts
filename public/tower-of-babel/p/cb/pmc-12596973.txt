Integrative Transcriptomic and Genomic Analysis Reveals the Regulatory Mechanisms Underlying Semen Quality Stability in Ganders
Authors: Shenqiang Hu, Jiasen Chen, Xiaoyong He, Guibi Li, Zhujun Wang, Yujie Zhang, Yang Song, Xiaopeng Li, Tanze Liu, Jiwei Hu, Hehe Liu, Liang Li, Jiwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Semen quality significantly affects the reproductive performance of ganders. As seasonal breeders, ganders typically experience a decline in semen quality over the breeding season; however, the mechanisms regulating semen quality stability during this period remain poorly understood. Maintaining high semen quality throughout the entire breeding season is crucial for improving production efficiency. In this study, we systematically investigated the semen quality parameters of Tianfu Meat Goose II ganders at three distinct stages of the female geese’s annual laying cycle: early (36-week-old), peak (47-week-old), and late (66-week-old) laying stages. The results showed that the ganders in stable (S) semen quality group exhibited superior semen quality parameters compared to those in unstable (U) semen quality group. Notably, the gander semen quality factor (SQF) and sperm density were significantly higher in the S group than in the U group (P < 0.05). Histomorphological analysis revealed that the testicular weight and size of ganders from the S group were significantly greater than those from the U group (P < 0.05), and so were the numbers of testicular spermatids and spermatozoa (P < 0.05). Through a combination of transcriptomic and selective sweep analysis, protein-protein interaction network construction, and RT-qPCR validation, four hub genes (EP300, RAB31, PRKG1, and PIK3R1) and their significantly enriched KEGG pathways including adherens junction, gap junction, endocytosis, TGF-beta signaling, and Toll-like receptor signaling were identified to have important roles in regulating gander semen quality stability. Specifically, much higher levels of testicular EP300, RAB31, and PRKG1 expression might be associated with impaired semen quality in ganders from the U group, whereas the upregulated expression of PIK3R1 in testes of ganders from the S group appeared to play protective roles in maintaining the gander semen quality stability. These findings would provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying semen quality stability in ganders during the breeding season and offer valuable references for genetic selection and reproductive management in waterfowl breeding programs.

Introduction

Geese, when compared to other domesticated poultry species such as chickens and ducks, are generally characterized by much lower reproductive efficiency (fertility and hatchability)—a limitation that constrains the global goose production. This problem arises from both the lower annual egg production in females and inferior semen quality in males, posing a significant constraint on commercial scalability. Addressing this issue is of paramount importance for China, as the world’s largest producer and consumer of meat geese, where the goose industry is vital to achieving high-quality development in animal husbandry and rural revitalization (Shi et al., 2015; Lukaszewicz, et al., 2003; Ding, et al., 2014). Moreover, under natural conditions, almost all domestic goose breeds generally exhibit the reproductive seasonality, which means that they actively breed during certain periods of the year depending on both the genetic background and environmental conditions (Shi, et al., 2008; Salamon, 2020). Of particular note, except the widely recognized photoperiod-driven seasonality of egg laying in female geese, the reproductive activity of ganders has also been observed to display seasonal variations during the annual reproductive cycle, as reflected by the much poorer semen quality and fertility during the non-breeding season (Zhu, et al., 2017; Akhtar, et al., 2021). It has also been reported that the semen quality of ganders fluctuates significantly during the breeding season (Gumułka and Rozenboim, 2013, 2015). Even during the latter period in the breeding season, the ganders exhibit much lower sperm density and motile sperm counts, which could be due to decreased circulating testosterone levels (Zeman, et al., 1990). Besides, there is evidence that other environmental factors such as light source and duration can significantly influence the gander seasonal fertility variations by regulating plasma testosterone levels (Shi, et al., 2007; Chang, et al., 2016; Akhtar, et al., 2021)

Recently, with the rapid expansion of meat goose consumption as well as the increasing demand for efficient goose production, food security, and environmental protection, the Chinese goose rearing systems have been upgrading from the traditional free-range rearing ones to the modern intensive large-scale dryland rearing ones. In this context, the artificial insemination (AI) technology has been increasingly explored and adopted in commercial goose breeding and production (Hu, et al., 2025), which can readily reduce the number of commercial goose breeders, expand the male to female mating ratio and the breeding efficiency of excellent ganders, minimize disease transmission, ensure accurate pedigree records, and avoid the reproductive disorders frequently occurred under natural mating (Łukaszewicz, 2010). Nevertheless, the large fluctuations in gander semen quality throughout the annual female egg-laying period hinder the further improvement of reproductive efficiency by the application of AI in commercial goose breeder flocks. Apparently, the gander semen quality stability, which is hereafter defined as remaining relatively high and stable throughout the annual female laying cycle in the first one to two years of life, can be considerably improved through both selective breeding and optimized animal husbandry practice.

To ensure the availability of high quality semen for both natural mating and AI in practical goose production, it is necessary to assess the stability of semen quality in ganders throughout the annual egg-laying cycle of female geese and to reveal the histological and molecular differences that distinguish the ganders with stable semen quality from those with variable quality throughout the breeding season. Therefore, in the present study, given the observed variations in gander semen quality during the female laying period (Łukaszewicz, 2010; Gumułka and Rozenboim, 2015; Akhtar et al., 2021), we first systematically evaluated semen quality parameters in Tianfu Meat Goose Ⅱ ganders at early (36-week-old), peak (47-week-old), and late (66-week-old) laying stages. Then, the testicular histomorphological characteristics were compared between the ganders showing relatively stable and unstable semen quality throughout the annual egg-laying period of female geese. Lastly, the key genes and interaction networks regulating the gander semen quality stability were revealed by integrating the genome-wide transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses. These findings would provide new insights into the regulatory mechanisms responsible for the robustness and stability of the gander spermatogenesis and be useful for the genetic improvement of the breeding goose reproductive efficiency through selective breeding.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving the manipulation of geese in this study were conducted in concordance with the “Guidelines for Experimental Animals” of the Ministry of Science and Technology (Beijing, China). The animal use protocol has been reviewed and approved by the Sichuan Agricultural University Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee (Approval No.: 20240411).

Experimental Animals, Semen Quality Analysis, and Sample Collection

The geese used in this study were provided by the Waterfowl Breeding Experimental Farm of Sichuan Agricultural University (Ya’an, Sichuan, China). A total of 150 healthy 0-day-old ganders were hatched in the same batch and raised under the same indoor environmental and feeding conditions. Specifically, all ganders were first reared in brooders during the first three weeks post-hatch, then shifted to the net-floor mixed rearing system until week 10, consisting of a 60 m2 plastic net at a height of 1 m above the ground level and an 18 m2 fermentation bed, and finally kept into a single cage, with a size of 550 mm length × 350 mm width × 700 mm height. When reaching sexual maturation, the ganders showing normal external genital morphology were trained for artificial insemination through abdominal massage by a skilled technician, as described in a previous study (Li, et al., 2025). By the end of abdominal massage training, the successfully trained and healthy ganders showing similar body weights, were subjected to semen collection for at least three times at an interval of three days during three different stages of the egg-laying period of female geese hatched at the same batch, including early (36-week-old), peak (47-week-old), and late (66-week-old) egg-laying stages. After fresh semen collection, several semen quality parameters, including ejaculate volume, sperm motility, sperm viability, sperm deformity rate, sperm density, acrosomal integrity, and semen quality factor (SQF) were measured and calculated as previously described (Hu, et al., 2025; Li, et al., 2025). Based on the above results of semen quality analysis, six ganders showing relative stable (S) and unstable (U) semen quality throughout the annual egg-laying period of female geese were selected, respectively. The selection criterion for the S group was that the gander consistently showed an SQF value of more than 10 at all three examined times points throughout the egg-laying period, while the criteria for the U group were that the gander showed an average SQF value of less than 11 and a coefficient of variation of more than 85% among these three time points, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. At the end of semen quality measurement, after recording the live body weights after a 12-hour fasting, the blood samples were collected from these selected 12 ganders and stored at -80°C after a rapid frozen in liquid nitrogen until DNA extraction. Thereafter, the ganders were euthanized by inhaling carbon dioxide, followed by cervical dislocation. After slaughter, the appearance, weight, long, short, and dorsoventral diameter of the left and right testis were photographed and measured to reveal their morphological characteristics. The testicular organ index was calculated using the formula: Organ index (%) = (organ weight (g) / body weight (g)) × 100%. Subsequently, the right testes of 5 ganders from each experimental group were used for histological examination, while the left testes of 3 ganders from each experimental group were sampled and stored at -80°C after a rapid frozen in liquid nitrogen until RNA extraction.

Histological observation

The freshly collected gander testes were fixed in 4 % formaldehyde at room temperature for 72 h. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions of increasing concentrations, transferred to xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Three sections of approximately 5 µm thickness, including the largest cross-sectional one and the two adjacent ones, were cut from each testis and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The H&E-stained sections were observed and photographed under a BA410-Digital microscope equipped with a digital camera (Motic China Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China), and at least three fields of view per section were randomly selected for histological analysis. The testicular histological parameters, including the number of seminiferous tubules, the number of Sertoli cells, the number of Leydig cell, the number of spermatogonium, the number of spermatids, the number of spermatozoa, seminiferous epithelium thickness seminiferous tubule diameter, testicular interstitium, and testicular parenchyma, were measured manually by a skilled technician using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Each testicular histological parameter was calculated as the mean of the observations of all examined individuals from each experimental group.

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the left testes of 6 ganders from the two experimental groups using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and was then treated with DNase I (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using both a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq mRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing data for this study have been deposited into the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) at the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) web server, with the BioProject ID: PRJCA043632 and Accession Number: CRA028223.

DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples of 12 ganders from the two experimental groups using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). The quality and quantity of DNA were assessed using an Agilent 2200 Tape Station (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), and agarose gel electrophoresis. According to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), the paired-end libraries with an insert size of 350 bp were constructed using the TruSeq Library Prep Kit, and the genome resequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (Novogene Co. Ltd., Tianjin, China). The sequencing data for this study have been deposited into the GSA at the CNCB web server, with the BioProject ID: PRJCA043652 and Accession Number: CRA028229.

Bioinformatics analysis of transcriptomic data

The raw reads of RNA-Seq were quality-controlled using Fastp v0.23.4 software (Chen, et al., 2018). After removing the adapter reads, reads with > 5 % ambiguous bases, and low-quality reads, the clean reads were obtained and mapped to our recently assembled reference-grade genome of Sichuan White goose using HISAT2 v2.2.1 software (Kim, et al., 2019). The output sequencing alignment/mapping (SAM) files were then converted to binary alignment/mapping (BAM) files and sorted using SAMtools v1.6.0 software (Li, et al., 2009). Subsequently, The FeatureCounts v1.6.0 software (Liao, et al., 2014) was used to calculate the relative expression of each transcript. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the DESeq2 R package v1.40.2 (Love, et al., 2014), with a criterion of |log2Foldchange| > 1.5 and an adjusted P value < 0.05. Both Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed using KOBAS v3.0 (Bu, et al., 2021).

Genomic scan of selective sweeps and protein-protein interaction network construction

The raw reads of genome resequencing data were quality-controlled using Fastp v0.23.4 software (Chen, et al., 2018). After removing the adaptor reads, reads with > 5 % ambiguous bases, and low-quality reads, the clean reads were obtained. The high-quality reads were then mapped to our recently assembled reference-grade genome of Sichuan White goose, using the "mem" algorithm of Burrows–Wheeler Alignment (BWA) v0.7.18 software (Li and Durbin, 2009) with default parameters. After sequence alignment, both SNPs and InDels were called using the VariantFiltration tool in GATK v4.6.0.0 software (DePristo, et al., 2011). The filtering criteria for SNPs were as follows: QD < 2.0, MQ < 40.0, FS > 60.0, SOR > 3.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. The filtering criteria for InDels were: QD < 2.0, FS > 200.0, SOR > 10.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. The filtered SNPs and InDels were subsequently merged into a single GVCF file using VCFtools v0.1.16 software (Danecek, et al., 2011). To detect genomic regions with selective scanning significant features, both fixation index (FST) and nucleotide diversity difference (log2 (θπ,U/θπ,S)) values were calculated within a 40 kb sliding window and a step size of 20 kb across the entire genome using VCFtools v0.1.16 software (Danecek, et al., 2011). The genomic regions showing both the top 5 % of allele frequency differentiation and the top 5 % of nucleotide diversity differences were considered as candidate regions for selective sweeps. Genes located within or near these sweeps were considered as candidate genes. Both KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of these candidate genes were performed using KOBAS v3.0 (Bu, et al., 2021). The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of the selected genes was analyzed using the STRING v10 database (Szklarczyk, et al., 2023) and visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.1 software.

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR validation

Equal amounts of total RNA from each sample were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript® RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The RT-qPCR reaction system contained 10 μL of 2 × ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 0.4 μL of each primer, 2 μL of cDNA, and 7.2 μL of double-distilled water. The amplification protocol was listed as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at the appropriate primer-specific temperature for 30 s. The specificity of each primer set was confirmed by melt curve analysis, and the accuracy of all amplification products was verified by sequencing. Each RT-qPCR run included no-template controls and negative controls, and each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. The relative expression levels of target genes were normalized to the two reference genes, GAPDH and β-ACTIN, using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the obtained semen quality parameters was performed using the two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) procedure, while that of the testicular histomorphological parameters was performed using the independent sample t-test procedure in SPSS v24.0 software. The P values below 0.05 and 0.01 were considered statistically significantly and extremely significantly different, respectively. The GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and R v4.2.1 software was used to visualize the results.

Results

As shown in Fig. 1, except for the sperm viability and sperm motility at 36 weeks of age, all semen quality parameters except sperm deformity rate were higher in ganders from the S group than those from the U group. In particular, the SQF value was consistently significantly higher in the S group than in the U group at all three examined time points (P < 0.05). Also, the sperm density was significantly higher in the S group than in U group at both 47 and 66 weeks of age (P < 0.05). Identification of Testicular DEGs

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms regulating the gander semen quality stability, the left testes of ganders from the two groups were collected for transcriptomic analysis. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, a total of 226,448,626 raw reads were obtained from these 6 samples, with an average of 37,299,715 clean reads per sample. The Q20 value ranged from 96.97% to 98.37%, and the Q30 value ranged from 91.87% to 95.18%. The average mapping rate of all samples was 93.06%. Additionally, our results of principal component analysis (PCA) showed that these 6 samples clustered together according to the experimental group (Supplementary Figure 1). These data collectively supported the high quality and good biological reproducibility of our sequencing data, which could be used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis. By comparing the gander testicular transcriptomes between the S and U groups, a total of 1,966 DEGs were identified, including 1,119 upregulated genes and 847 downregulated genes (Supplementary Figure 2A). Also, hierarchical clustering analysis of these DEGs further confirmed their differential expression patterns in the testes between the two groups (Supplementary Figure 2B).

Functional enrichment analysis of testicular DEGs

We then performed both KEGG and GO enrichment analysis on these identified testicular DEGs. As shown in Fig. 4A, a total of 30 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched, among which the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, Wnt signaling pathway, melanogenesis, MAPK signaling pathway, TGF-beta signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), gap junction, GnRH signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, endocytosis, adherens junction, apelin signaling pathway are potentially involved in regulating semen quality stability. As shown in Fig. 4B, these testicular DEGs were mainly significantly enriched into the GO biological process (BP) terms associated with either gene transcription (such as positive regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, negative regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, and regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II) or cellular proliferation and differentiation (such as negative regulation of cell population proliferation, cell differentiation, neuron differentiation, positive regulation of cell population proliferation, ureteric bud development, fibroblast growth factor receptor signaling pathway, and cilium movement involved in cell motility).Fig. 4The top 30 KEGG pathways (A) and top 10 GO biological process terms (B) significantly enriched by the testicular DEGs between the stable (S) and unstable (U) semen quality groups during the annual egg laying period of female goose.Fig 4

Detection of Selective Sweeps Associated with the Gander Semen Quality Stability

At the genomic level, the DNA extracted from blood samples of 12 ganders from the two groups were resequenced for selective sweep analysis. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, a total of 919,427,013 clean reads were obtained from these 12 samples, with an average sequencing depth of 8.86. The average mapping rate for all samples was 99.72%, and the genome coverage (4 ×) ranged from 82.94% to 91.43%. These data altogether supported the high quality of our resequencing data, which could be used for subsequently bioinformatics analysis. Functional enrichment analysis of the genes annotated in selective sweeps

We further performed KEGG and GO enrichment analysis on the 221 candidate genes identified using selective sweep analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, among the 10 significantly enriched KEGG pathways, the adherens junction, MAPK signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, gap junction, TGF-beta signaling pathway, and endocytosis are potentially crucial for regulating semen quality stability. As shown in Fig. 6B, these 10 significantly enriched GO BP terms were primarily associated with four functional categories: gene transcription (including positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription elongation), cellular transport and signal transduction (encompassing phagosome maturation, Golgi to plasma membrane protein transport, intracellular signal transduction, and regulation of protein kinase B signaling), cell motility (specifically flagellated sperm motility), and cell differentiation and protein regulation (covering B cell differentiation, protein phosphorylation, negative regulation of protein localization to plasma membrane, and endocytosis).Fig. 6The top 10 KEGG pathways (A) and top 10 GO biological process terms (B) significantly enriched by the candidate genes related to the gander semen quality stability identified through selective sweep analysis between the stable (S) and unstable (U) semen quality groups during the annual egg laying period of female goose.Fig 6

PPI network analysis of the genes enriched in common KEGG pathways identified through transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses

A total of 6 KEGG pathways were commonly enriched in both transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses, including adherens junction, gap junction, endocytosis, MAPK signaling Toll-like receptor signaling, and TGF-beta signaling pathways. These overlapping pathways are likely to play important roles in the regulation of semen quality stability in ganders. To further explore the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms, all genes involved in these common pathways were used to construct a PPI network. As shown in Fig. 7, the resulting network revealed complex interactions among these genes. Genes located in the darker-colored nodes may play more critical roles in the regulatory network. Notably, EP300 (EP300 Lysine Acetyltransferase), PRKG1 (Protein Kinase CGMP-Dependent 1), PIK3R1 (Phosphoinositide-3-Kinase Regulatory Subunit 1), and RAB31 (RAB31, member RAS oncogene family) were identified as the common genes through both transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses. Discussion

Semen quality determines the gander reproduction performance, and the maintenance of relatively high semen quality throughout the annual reproductive cycle is of significant importance to improve the economic values of goose breeder flocks. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying the semen quality stability remain largely unknown in the goose. In this study, a comprehensive comparative analysis of semen quality parameters, testicular developmental changes, as well as transcriptomic and genomic characteristics were performed between the ganders showing relatively stable (consistently high and robust SQF value) and unstable (low or large fluctuations in SQF value) semen quality throughout the annual egg-laying period of female geese. Our results from semen quality analysis showed that most semen quality indicators (especially sperm density) were much higher in ganders from the S group than in those from the U group, but the opposite was seen in the sperm deformity rate, implying that both sperm density and sperm deformity rate could be considered as critical indicators for assessing semen quality stability during the annual reproductive cycle. At the histomorphological level, we found that the ganders from the S group showed much higher testicular weight and size than those from the U group, and so were the number of testicular spermatids and spermatozoa, indicating that the testicular developmental differences could be responsible for the divergent semen quality stability between the two groups. The testis, as a pivotal endocrine and reproductive organ in ganders, not only reflects the normal developmental status of the reproductive system but also serves as an indirect indicator of spermatogenic capacity (Sarabia Fragoso, et al., 2013). Compared to other domestic poultry species, the testicular development of adult ganders remains relatively limited, consequently resulting in a much lower spermatogenic capacity (Łukaszewicz, 2010). Similar to our findings, recent studies reported that the sperm motility in male chickens is significantly associated with testicular weight, and the heavier testicular weight generally exhibited higher sperm motility (Sun, et al., 2019). In seasonally breeding avian species, testicular regression progresses gradually toward complete quiescence at the terminal phase of the reproductive cycle, characterized by histological involution and cessation of spermatogenesis (Jamieson, 2011). During the non-breeding season, the seminiferous tubules of drakes’ testes exclusively contain spermatogonia and primary spermatocytes, with complete absence of spermatids and spermatozoa (Khatun, et al., 2021). Similarly, when the goose testes exhibit significant structural regression, the seminiferous tubules are predominantly composed of Sertoli cells and spermatogonia (Akhtar, et al., 2021). Taken together, these results supported the notion that the gander semen quality stability is closely related to the spermatogenic process during the annual reproductive cycle, which depends on the testicular developmental status. A previous study in mallards have reported a positive correlation between testicular size and circulating testosterone levels (Denk and Kempenaers, 2006), and several lines of evidence in ganders have also demonstrated that seasonal alterations in testicular weight and functions are finely tuned by the varied levels of circulating testosterone and expression of genes involved in spermatogenesis pathways in the testes (Zhu, et al., 2017; Zhuang, et al., 2019; Gumułka, et al., 2023). Photoperiod has a significant effect on testosterone secretion in ganders. During the normal breeding season under short-day conditions, testosterone concentrations in ganders are relatively high, whereas during the non-breeding season with long photoperiods, testosterone levels decrease markedly (Shi, et al., 2007; Leska, et al., 2012; Gumułka and Rozenboim, 2015). Hence, it was further proposed that the stability of a gander’s semen quality could be orchestrated by circulating levels of testosterone, which is mainly produced by the testes.

To reveal the molecular mechanisms regulating the gander sperm quality stability, a combination of transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses was used to identify key genes, pathways, and interaction networks. At the transcriptomic level, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs associated with semen quality stability were predominantly enriched in the pathways regulating endocrine function, cellular proliferation, and differentiation. Among them, both neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction and apelin signaling pathways may play important roles in spermatogenesis (Das, et al., 2022). The Wnt, MAPK, and TGF-beta signaling pathways are well known to be crucial for both testicular development and spermatogenesis (Itman, et al., 2006; Dong, et al., 2015; Luo, et al., 2022). The CAMs pathway has also been reported to play essential roles in regulating spermatogenesis (Xiao, et al., 2013). The Toll-like receptor signaling pathway has been shown to reduce the negative effects of inflammation on the spermatogenic cell development and functions in the avian testes (Anastasiadou, et al., 2011; Hedger, 2011). There is also evidence that the endocytosis has been implicated in the process of sperm release (Kumar, et al., 2015). Thus, these above-mentioned pathways may influence the gander semen quality stability by directly regulating testicular development and spermatogenesis, as manifested by the observed differences in testicular histomorphology and semen quality parameters between the two groups. In addition, a previous study indicated that melanin production may be associated with testosterone synthesis in avian species. (Fargallo, et al., 2007). Although the exact role of the melanogenesis pathway in regulating spermatogenesis awaits further investigations in birds, it was conceivable that this pathway might indirectly influence the gander semen quality stability by regulating circulating levels of testosterone. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in BP terms related to gene expression regulation, cellular proliferation, and cellular differentiation. Notably, the GO BP terms associated with cell differentiation and RNA polymerase II transcription may play pivotal roles in regulating avian testicular development and spermatogenesis (Wilkerson and Sarge, 2009; Estermann, et al., 2021).

At the genomic level, KEGG enrichment analysis of genes annotated in selective sweep regions revealed that they were predominantly enriched in pathways closely related to testicular structure and functions, including adherens junction, gap junction, endocytosis, MAPK signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, and TGF-beta signaling pathways. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis demonstrated that these genes were predominantly enriched in BP terms related to gene transcription, cell differentiation, regulation of sperm motility, endocytosis, as well as protein transport and phosphorylation, and some of them have been extensively documented to play pivotal roles in the avian testicular development and spermatogenesis (Ashizawa, et al., 2009; Liu, et al., 2024). It should be noted that a limited number of ganders used for our genomic sequencing might impact the accuracy in identifying candidate regions under selection due to random genetic drift and bottleneck effect. To improve the accuracy, by overlapping the significantly enriched pathways by both transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses, and 6 commonly enriched pathways, including adherens junction, gap junction, endocytosis, MAPK signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, and TGF-beta signaling pathways were obtained, and the genes enriched in these pathways were further used to construct a PPI network. As a result, four hub DEGs (EP300, RAB31, PRKG1, and PIK3R1) were identified to be overlapped in our transcriptomic and selective sweep analyses, so the adherens junction and TGF-beta signaling pathways enriched by EP300, the endocytosis enriched by RAB31, the gap junction enriched by PRKG1, and the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway enriched by PIK3R1 may play critical roles in maintaining the stability of gander semen quality during the annual reproductive cycle.

The expression of EP300 increases at the onset of meiosis and decreases in post-meiotic cells, suggesting that it may play a regulatory role during meiotic progression (Boussouar et al., 2014). Although the exact roles of RAB31, a member of the RAS family, remain unclear during the process of spermatogenesis, a previous study has reported that overexpression of RAB -associated autophagy gene significantly reduced the sperm survival rate during cryopreservation (Fraser, et al., 2020). So, the observed higher expression of EP300 and RAB31 in testes of ganders from the U group might reflect their negative roles in regulation of spermatogenesis. The upregulation of PRKG1 has been shown to result in an increased proliferation and accumulation of senescent Leydig cells in rat testes (Sokanovic, et al., 2018). Our results showed that both the mRNA levels of PRKG1 and the number of Leydig cells in testes of ganders from the S group were much lower than in those from the U group, implying a potential role for PRKG1 in regulating gander testicular cell development and thereby semen quality stability. It has also been reported that expression of PIK3R1 was significantly upregulated during gonadal differentiation and early testicular development in Muscovy ducks (Bai et al., 2020), which was similar to our observations that testicular organ index, germ cell number, and PIK3R1 mRNA levels were much higher in ganders from the S group than in those from the U group, suggesting positive actions for PIK3R1 in regulating testicular development and spermatogenesis in waterfowl. Therefore, these signaling pathways, interaction networks, and key genes identified through a combination of transcriptomic, selective sweep, and PPI analysis, as well as RT-qPCR validation were proposed to play important roles in maintaining gander semen quality stability during the annual reproductive cycle.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the ganders from the S group showed more superior and smaller fluctuations in semen quality throughout the annual reproductive cycle compared to those from the U group, which were due to their better testicular development, as reflected by larger testicular weight and size as well as significantly higher numbers of spermatids and spermatozoa. Through a combination of transcriptomic, selective sweep, and PPI analysis, as well as RT-qPCR validation, four hub genes (EP300, RAB31, PRKG1, and PIK3R1) were proposed to play important roles in regulation of gander semen quality stability. Specifically, much higher levels of testicular EP300, RAB31, and PRKG1 expression might be associated with impaired semen quality in ganders from the U group. In contrast, the upregulated expression of PIK3R1 in testes of ganders from the S group appeared to play protective roles in maintaining the gander semen quality stability. Consequently, these genes involved pathways, including adherens junction, gap junction, endocytosis, TGF-beta signaling, and Toll-like receptor signaling, were also predicted to be implicated in regulating the gander testicular development and semen quality stability during the annual reproductive cycle.
