Romosozumab‐Associated Recurrent Erythema Nodosum in Mixed Connective Tissue Disease
Authors: Jose Luis Diaz Macip, Izabela Turcu, Jyothsna Goranti, Sreeja Sivaguru, Sai Priyanka Kodam, Zeeshan Afzal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to describe recurrent erythema nodosum (EN) temporally associated with romosozumab in a patient with mixed connective tissue disease and to examine the biologic plausibility of this association in the context of Wnt/β‐catenin–mediated immune regulation.MethodsWe evaluated a patient with mixed connective tissue disease and osteoporosis who developed EN following romosozumab exposure. Clinical assessment included detailed medication reconciliation, infectious evaluation, markers of inflammation, response to glucocorticoids, and recurrence upon re‐exposure. A focused literature review was conducted to assess prior reports and mechanistic relevance.ResultsA 71‐year‐old woman developed EN approximately one month after her first dose of romosozumab, with complete remission following prednisone therapy. Within days of the second dose, she experienced a more extensive recurrence involving the hands, thighs, and right calf, which resolved after a glucocorticoid taper. Infectious evaluation was negative, markers of inflammation were normal, and medication reconciliation did not identify alternative pharmacologic triggers. Romosozumab was discontinued, and no recurrence occurred during follow‐up before transition to denosumab.ConclusionThis case supports a probable association between romosozumab and recurrent EN. Rechallenge, exclusion of alternative etiologies, and emerging evidence on Wnt‐mediated immune regulation suggest a mechanistic link between sclerostin inhibition and panniculitic hypersensitivity reactions.

INTRODUCTION

Romosozumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting sclerostin that increases bone formation and reduces bone resorption through modulation of Wnt signaling. 1 It is approved for the treatment of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women at high fracture risk and has demonstrated significant reductions in fracture risk in clinical trials. 2 Although commonly reported adverse effects include arthralgia, headache, and nasopharyngitis, postmarketing data and case reports have identified immune‐mediated cutaneous reactions, including erythema multiforme, angioedema, and other hypersensitivity phenomena. 3 Erythema nodosum (EN) has not previously been reported in association with romosozumab.

EN is the most common form of panniculitis and is a reactive condition typically associated with infections, systemic inflammatory diseases, and medications. 4 Reports of EN in association with zoledronic acid and denosumab suggest that bone‐active therapies may, in rare instances, precipitate immune‐mediated panniculitic reactions. 5 Given the emerging role of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in immune regulation, including effects on T cell differentiation, dendritic cell activity, and cytokine production, 1 a mechanistic link between sclerostin inhibition and immune‐mediated cutaneous inflammation is biologically plausible.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

We describe a patient with mixed connective tissue disease who developed recurrent EN in temporal association with romosozumab exposure. Clinical evaluation included detailed medication reconciliation, infectious workup, markers of inflammation, and assessment for autoimmune disease activity to exclude alternative etiologies such as infection or disease flare. Temporal association with drug exposure, response to glucocorticoids, and recurrence upon re‐exposure were assessed to evaluate causality. A focused literature review was conducted to identify prior reports of EN associated with romosozumab and related osteoporosis therapies, as well as relevant data on Wnt signaling and immune‐mediated cutaneous reactions. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this report and accompanying images.

RESULTS

A 71‐year‐old woman with long‐standing mixed connective tissue disease, manifesting predominantly as scleroderma and Sjögren disease, and comorbid pulmonary arterial hypertension, mild interstitial lung disease, and osteoporosis was evaluated in the rheumatology clinic. She had recently been hospitalized for a left knee effusion; synovial fluid analysis was noninfectious with negative Gram stain and cultures. She was discharged on a seven‐day course of amoxicillin.

Two weeks later, she developed multiple erythematous, mildly tender, nonpruritic subcutaneous nodules involving the hands and bilateral lower extremities, clinically consistent with EN (Figure 1A and B). A skin biopsy was not performed because the diagnosis was established clinically based on characteristic features of the lesions. Infectious workup was negative, and markers of inflammation were normal. Medication reconciliation did not identify additional new or temporally associated medications. She had received her first romosozumab injection approximately one month before symptom onset. A short course of prednisone 20 mg daily for one week resulted in complete remission.

Clinical presentation of romosozumab‐associated erythema nodosum. (A and B) Initial presentation showing tender, erythematous subcutaneous nodules on bilateral knees. (C) Recurrent episode with similar lesions involving the right calf following re‐exposure to romosozumab.

Because the initial episode followed both amoxicillin exposure and romosozumab therapy, the etiology was initially uncertain. However, within days of receiving a second dose of romosozumab, she developed a recurrent eruption involving the hands, thighs, and right calf, with representative lesions shown on the right calf (Figure 1C). A five‐week prednisone taper, initiated at 40 mg daily with weekly decrements of 10 mg, resulted in complete resolution. Romosozumab was permanently discontinued, and she remained free of recurrent EN during the subsequent two months before transitioning to denosumab.

DISCUSSION

This case supports a probable association between romosozumab and recurrent EN on both clinical and mechanistic grounds. The temporal relationship between exposure and symptom onset, recurrence upon re‐exposure, and resolution after discontinuation are characteristic of medication‐induced hypersensitivity reactions. 4 A thorough medication reconciliation was performed to exclude alternative pharmacologic triggers, and no other new or temporally associated medications were identified. The absence of systemic markers of inflammation and lack of autoimmune disease flare further argue against EN as a manifestation of mixed connective tissue disease. Although the initial episode followed exposure to amoxicillin, only romosozumab reproduced the reaction upon rechallenge, strengthening the causal inference.

The biologic plausibility of this association is supported by current understanding of EN pathogenesis and romosozumab pharmacology. EN is a hypersensitivity‐mediated panniculitis associated with T cell activation, immune complex deposition, and cytokine signaling, including tumor necrosis factor α and interleukin‐6. 4 Romosozumab inhibits sclerostin and modulates Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, a pathway increasingly recognized to influence immune regulation, including dendritic cell function, T cell differentiation, and cytokine production. 1 In patients with underlying autoimmune disease, such immune modulation may lower the threshold for hypersensitivity reactions such as EN. Case‐based reports have identified immune‐mediated cutaneous events associated with romosozumab, including erythema multiforme–like eruptions and other severe cutaneous hypersensitivity reactions requiring drug discontinuation, supporting a plausible immune‐mediated mechanism. 3 Additionally, EN has been reported with other osteoporosis therapies, including zoledronic acid and denosumab, supporting the concept that pharmacologic modulation of bone and immune pathways may precipitate inflammatory cutaneous reactions in susceptible individuals. 5

The limitations of this report include its single‐patient design, the absence of histopathologic confirmation, and the observational nature of the causal inference. Although the reproducible exposure‐response pattern strongly supports a drug‐related reaction, mechanistic biomarkers were not assessed, and causality cannot be definitively established. In summary, romosozumab may rarely trigger recurrent EN in susceptible individuals, and clinicians should be aware of this potential adverse effect, particularly in patients with underlying autoimmune disease.
