Linking temporal dynamics of children’s natural behavior to brain development
Authors: Andrew E. Koepp, Monami Nishio, Allyson P. Mackey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Across the school day, children must continually modulate their behavior to be appropriate to the classroom. Such behavioral regulation is effortful and children vary in their ability to sustain behavioral regulation over time. We have previously shown that daily increases in activity levels (DIA) across the school day relate to questionnaire measures of self-control, and that children who show shallower DIA in third grade are more likely to graduate from college. Here, we ask how individual differences in brain development relate to children’s ability to sustain behavioral regulation as measured by DIA. We take advantage of neuroimaging and actigraphy data collected in the ABCD study (N = 4338, Mage = 11.95, 15.65 days of actigraphy data per child). We preregistered hypotheses that steeper DIA (lower sustained control) would be associated with thinner cortex, lower surface area, and weaker correlations between the executive control network and somatomotor network. We first replicated typical patterns of DIA, as well as associations between steeper DIA and lower inhibitory control. We then showed that greater DIA is associated with thinner cortex in areas related to executive control (βs = -0.04 to −0.06) and motor action (βs = -0.04 to −0.05). Greater DIA was also associated with lower connectivity between the executive control and somatomotor networks (β = −0.07), and greater connectivity within the somatomotor network (β = 0.12). Our results suggest that DIA is a promising measure of naturalistic self-control that can be used to study between- and within-person variability at scale.

Introduction

Formal schooling requires children to regulate their behavior on an ongoing basis. From the start of first period to the end of the school day, they must manage the conflict between internal goals (say, a desire to pass a note in class or run through the halls) and the external expectations for their behavior (to instead focus on algebra or walk inside the school building; Duckworth et al., 2019). Some children find it easier to control their behavior than others (e.g. (Rothbart et al., 2006) and these children typically learn more in school (Duckworth et al., 2019, Robson et al., 2020, Zelazo et al., 2016) and complete more years of formal education (Koepp et al., 2023, Moffitt et al., 2011). Though self-regulation has largely been investigated in terms of trait differences (Robson et al., 2020, Rothbart et al., 2001), emerging work indicates that children’s self-regulatory behaviors can also be state-like—children find it more difficult to regulate their behavior on some days compared to others (Blume et al., 2022, Koepp and Gershoff, 2024, Leonard et al., 2022).

Despite theoretical models describing self-regulation as an effortful and ongoing process (Duckworth et al., 2019, Nigg, 2017), relatively little work has examined whether and how children’s control of their behavior might erode over time (Eberhart et al., 2023). Some researchers have observed the temporal dynamics of self-regulation through intensive video coding of brief laboratory tasks designed, for example, to elicit frustration (Cole et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2022). Investigating such temporal information can provide information beyond a static average and instead reveal how children’s behavior changes across a given task, including, for example, their behavior early on in a task (intercept) and the direction and rate of change (slope). Yet, these laboratory tasks are often brief, lasting less than ten minutes, and observing natural behavior for several hours across the school day has been prohibitive.

Recently, scholars have developed solutions to collect intensive repeated observations, using wearable devices to record natural behavior (de Barbaro, 2019, Elbaum et al., 2024, Horn et al., 2024, Messinger et al., 2022, Wass, 2021). For example, a recent study used wearable devices to conduct extended observations of children’s behavior in a natural task that schools frequently impose—to refrain from vigorous motor activity except during specific periods (Koepp et al., in press). In a large birth cohort, they found that school-age children’s gross motor activity increases across the school day, suggesting decreasing regulation of behavior. Children who showed slower increases in activity throughout the day in third grade (slope) were rated by teachers and parents as having greater inhibitory control, even after accounting for their overall activity level (intercept). These children also learned more over the course of the third-grade year and completed more education by age 26 (Koepp et al., in press). Such intensive, repeated observations can thus reveal fluctuations in self-regulation and temporal dynamics that index meaningful individual differences (Pérez-Edgar et al., 2025), advancing basic science and creating valuable knowledge for educators and clinicians (Eberhart et al., 2023). However, we know little about whether and how temporal dynamics of self-regulation relate to children’s brain development. Identifying links would provide further evidence that such measures capture processes of behavioral control. Further, linking natural behavior to neuroimaging could reveal how neural systems support self-control in everyday environments for extended periods, grounding neuroscience in real-world behavior and the kinds of tasks that children are asked to accomplish on a daily basis.

Research on the neural correlates of behavioral control has mostly focused on linking neuroimaging to children’s performance on brief tasks with limited ecological validity (Janssen et al., 2021) or to subjective reports assessing average behavior, precluding investigations of within-person change within the same day. This work has consistently implicated lateral prefrontal cortex, and the executive control network more broadly (Crone and Steinbeis, 2017, Diamond, 2013, Hoogman et al., 2019, Niendam et al., 2012). These regions exert control over other brain regions by sending “bias signals,” which serve to amplify or dampen neural activity in other regions (Friedman and Robbins, 2022). To control motor action, either through initiation or suppression, executive control regions send signals to primary motor cortex, which is implicated in both automatic, unplanned movement as well as the volitional control of deliberate action (Ebbesen & Brecht, 2017). Motor cortex was once thought to be organized as a homunculus, with different “effector regions” to initiate and control movement in specific areas (e.g., hands, mouth, feet; Penfield & Rasmussen; 1950). However, in addition to these effector regions, motor cortex has recently been shown to contain hubs of a “somato-cognitive action network” essential for the planning, coordination, and initiation of goal-directed action (Gordon et al., 2023). Motor cortex may thus play a key role in deliberate action and goal pursuit, as all behavior occurs through motor action (Adolph and Hoch, 2019).

Brain structure is frequently measured by cortical thickness and surface area. As the brain matures, cortex thins, reflecting biological process such as myelination and synaptic pruning, from about age 2 through adulthood (Bethlehem et al., 2022). Cortical surface area increases until around age 11. Because executive function also improves throughout childhood (Baum et al., 2017, Blair and Raver, 2015), one might think that better control of behavior would be associated with more adult-like brain structure. For surface area, this seems to be the case: greater cortical surface area is associated with greater behavioral control (Bernanke et al., 2022, Hoogman et al., 2019) and lower impulsivity (Gilman et al., 2024). However, cortical thickness tends to show the reverse association in adolescence: thinner cortex is associated with greater impulsivity (Pehlivanova et al., 2018), more externalizing behaviors (Tanzer et al., 2021), and lower executive functions (Laurent et al., 2020). Thinner cortex is also associated with exposure to environmental risk factors including adversity (Zhi et al., 2026), fewer positive social experiences (Bates et al., 2025) and greater air pollution exposure (Jara et al., 2026). Consistent with these associations across typically-developing children, children diagnosed with ADHD, and those with more severe symptoms, have lower total surface areas and reduced thickness (Mous et al., 2017) with particularly strong effects in rostral middle frontal gyrus and caudal middle frontal gyrus (Gilman et al., 2024, Owens et al., 2020, Owens et al., 2023). Behavioral regulation has also been linked to functional connectivity of cortical networks. As children grow up, functional brain networks show stronger within-network associations and weaker associations with other networks (Baum et al., 2017, Tooley et al., 2022). Greater segregation, especially between the internally focused default mode network and externally focused attention networks (ventral attention and dorsal attention) has been associated with stronger executive functions (Baum et al., 2017) and fewer hyperactive, impulsive, and inattentive behaviors (Norman et al., 2023). Children with ADHD show less segregation between the default mode network and other task-positive networks, and this lack of segregation is associated with impaired response control within this group on error detection and continuous attention tasks (Duffy et al., 2021, Mills et al., 2018). Similarly, a study of broad psychopathology in adults revealed that people who showed greater impulsivity showed lower connectivity between executive control and somatomotor networks (Baker et al., 2019, Kebets et al., 2019). Weaker connectivity between the executive control network and the somatomotor network might indicate lower tendency to dampen, suppress, or inhibit automatic motor responses. Thus, it is reasonable to predict that connectivity of the executive control and somatomotor networks could enable sustained control of behavior in the classroom.

The current study

In the current study, we linked the temporal dynamics of youth’s natural self-regulatory behavior at school to their brain development. To assess changes in children’s behavioral regulation across the school day, we used their motor activity as a proxy (Koepp et al., 2022, Koepp and Gershoff, 2024) because schools often require children to refrain from vigorous movement except during specific periods. We used several days of micro-level actigraphy data from wearable Fitbit devices in the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. We first conducted a conceptual replication of Koepp et al., in press to test the hypothesis that children show daily increases in activity (DIA), on average, across the school day (Research Question [RQ] 1). To understand what dimensions of self-regulation were most associated with our actigraphy measures of behavior, we explored associations among behavioral ratings of regulatory constructs and two child-specific parameters characterizing behavior—their baseline activity, reflecting behavior at the start of the school day, and DIA, reflecting their rate of change across the school day. Next, we examined whether baseline activity and DIA showed unique associations with brain structure (cortical thickness and surface area) in regions for executive control (rostral and caudal middle frontal gyri) and motor control (precentral gyrus) (RQ 2). We chose these regions because they overlap well with the hubs of executive control and motor control, respectively, when mapping to a structural atlas of sulci and gyri (Desikan et al., 2006, Yeo et al., 2011). Finally, we examined associations with resting-state functional connectivity in executive control and somatomotor networks, as well as external attention networks (dorsal attention, cingulo-opercular) and default mode network (RQ 3). Analytical plans for all research questions were preregistered.

Methods

Data were drawn from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Data Release 5.1). The ABCD is a national, multi-site study with publicly available data. Recruitment occurred through schools across 21 sites and study design is detailed in (Bagot et al., 2018, Hagler et al., 2019).

Participants

Data were drawn from the two-year follow-up wave when participants were aged 11–12 years. Our pre-registered inclusion criteria were that participants have valid Fitbit data and that these data were collected during times of the year when school was likely to be in session. Our analytic sample (N = 4338) was 48% female, 62% White, 14% Hispanic, 11% Black, 2% Asian, and 11% Other race/ethnicity. Mean age of participants was 11.95 (SD = 0.64).

Measures

Activity at school

Children’s physical activity was objectively assessed using Fitbit Charge HR 2 devices (Bagot et al., 2018). Participants were offered the devices to wear on the nondominant wrist and were instructed to wear them devices for up to three weeks continuously. We focused on weekday observations (Fig. 1A). The devices contained triaxial accelerometers. The accelerometers record raw activity counts that index acceleration on the three axes. The raw data undergo band-pass filtering and an algorithm converts the activity to an estimate of energy expenditure, measured in units called Metabolic Equivalent Tasks (METs). The scale begins at 1, which equals energy expenditure at rest and higher values indicate greater exertion and therefore greater gross motor activity (Fig. 1B). We used micro-level data files, which contain a record of activity for each minute of the day (see example in Fig. 1C). Each participant was observed for approximately 15 weekdays. For each child, we estimated a slope and intercept that would characterize their average baseline activity starting at 9 am and their average daily increase in activity (DIA) across school hours (to 3 pm; Fig. 1D; see analysis plan for estimation details). Following Koepp et al., in press, we pre-registered a focus on changes from 9 am to 3 pm, hours when youth are typically in school.Fig. 1Summary of data, feature extraction, and pre-registered regions of interest.

We also pre-registered data exclusions for actigraphy data collected during periods when school is typically not in session in the US: summer months (June, July, and August), federal holidays, and the final week of the calendar year (December 24- January 1). Actigraphy data were collected starting in November 2018 and we used data collected through the end of February 2020. We excluded any data collected in March 2020 or later, when the COVID-19 pandemic began to disrupt schooling. After making these pre-registered exclusions, the largest predictor of missing actigraphy data was, by far, study site, with percentage missing ranging from 23% missing to 100% missing across sites (see Table S1; chi-square(20) = 1129.20, p < . 001). Black children were more likely than White children to have missing actigraphy data (OR = 1.30, p < .001), but rates of missingness for other races/ethnicities were similar to that of White children (ORs = 0.93–1.01, ps = ns). Missingness was not related to parent education (OR = 1.00, p = .256) or child sex (male OR = 0.94, p = .196).

Behavioral ratings

We identified several behavioral ratings, provided by youth, parents, and teachers, that assessed constructs related to self-regulation. We used these measures in exploratory analyses to identify which dimensions of behavior were most associated with actigraphy measure of behavior. To assess dimensions of impulsivity, youth completed the Urgency, Premeditation, Perseverance, Sensation Seeking Impulsive Behavior Scale (UPPS; alpha =.81; Whiteside and Lynam, 2001), which contains five subscales: positive urgency (alpha =.84), negative urgency (alpha =.69), sensation seeking (alpha =.59), lack of planning (alpha =.77), and lack of perseverance (alpha =.78). We also examined the approach scales of the BIS/BAS (Carver and White, 1994), which has subscales for fun seeking (4 items, alpha =.75), reward responsiveness (5 items, alpha =.82), and drive (4 items, alpha =.81). Finally, youth reported on ADHD symptoms using the Brief Problem Monitor (BPM; 6 items, alpha =.78; Achenbach et al., 2017). Parents also completed the Early Adolescent Temperament Questionnaire (Ellis and Rothbart, 2001), and we examined the following subscales: inhibitory control (5 items, alpha =.54), surgency (9 items, alpha =.72), and attention control (6 items, alpha =.78). Parents also reported on youth’s ADHD symptoms using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach and Ruffle, 2000). We used the subscale targeting symptoms of ADHD; 13 items, alpha = .82). Finally, teachers reported completed the BPM to report on children’s behavioral difficulties. We again used the subscale for ADHD symptoms (6 items, alpha =.89).

Neuroimaging

Youth underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on a 3 T scanner manufactured by Siemens, Philips, or GE (Casey et al., 2018). We used pre-processed neuroimaging data available from the ABCD Data Analysis and Informatics Core (Casey et al., 2018, Hagler et al., 2019, Hoogman et al., 2019). T1 images were corrected for gradient nonlinearity distortions and cross-modality registrations were performed. Cortical surfaces were reconstructed with FreeSurfer v7.1.1(Fischl and Dale, 2000) and cortical thickness and surface area were calculated for regions in the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006). We examined cortical thickness and surface area in bilateral rostral middle frontal gyrus, caudal middle frontal gyrus, and precentral gyrus (Fig. 1E).

Functional connectivity during resting-state was assessed via three to four five-minute resting-state scans. Resting-state images were corrected for head motion, B0 and gradient nonlinearity distortions, and registered to the structural images using mutual information. Initial scan volumes were removed, and each voxel was normalized and demeaned. The signal from estimated motion time courses (including six motion parameters, their derivatives, and their squares), quadratic trends, and time courses of white matter, gray matter, and whole brain, plus first derivatives, were regressed out, and frames with greater than 0.2 mm displacement were excluded. Standard region-of-interest time courses were extracted for default mode, executive control, dorsal attention, cingulo-opercular, and somatomotor networks (Gordon et al., 2016) Pairwise correlations were calculated within and across these networks. We examined within- and between network connectivity for the default mode and somatomotor networks with the executive control, dorsal attention, and cingulo-opercular networks (Fig. 1F).

Analysis

We pre-registered an analysis plan, which can be found at: aspredicted.org/h4bx-x6f5.pdf. Our first research aim was to investigate youths’ typical within-person trajectories of activity across the school day. We used multi-level models (Raudenbush and Bryk, 2002) to nest hourly observations within day and within person. We estimated both a piecewise model to explore the functional form of activity and a linear model to characterize the average rate of increase in activity across the day while controlling for day of the week. Both models included random intercepts at the person level and the linear model included a random slope term. After estimating the linear model, we estimated person-specific features (Fig. 1D): the intercept (baseline activity) and slope (DIA) using the postestimation command “predict” in Stata version 18 StataCorp, 2024). We also explored how individual differences in baseline activity and DIA were associated with behavioral ratings capturing constructs related to self-regulation including impulsivity, the hyperactive, impulsive, and inattentive symptoms of ADHD, inhibitory control, and attention. We examined standardized associations in linear regression models that included baseline activity, DIA, and control variables of age, sex, and site.

Our second research aim was to understand how baseline activity and DIA were associated with brain structure. We examined cortical thickness and surface area in three pre-registered areas. Two were related to executive control (rostral middle frontal gyrus and caudal middle frontal gyrus) and one associated with motor behavior (precentral gryus). We chose these regions because they represent the primary hubs for executive control and motor control. Though executive control draws on distributed brain regions, the primary hub, when mapped to the structural atlas of sulci and gyri, corresponds to the frontal gyrus and motor control draws primarily on the precentral gyrus.

We ran a series of regression models with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) for testing six hypotheses (three areas of interest in both hemispheres) and controlled for age, sex, and site. When preregistering analyses, we chose not to control for intracranial volume in our main models because there is debate about whether absolute or relative volume is more tightly linked to cognition (Wang et al., 2024). However, we ran a post-hoc sensitivity analysis that include intracranial volume as a control variable. We excluded structural MRI data that did not pass quality control procedures of the ABCD study organizers (2.8%). Thickness and surface area were standardized (z-scored) for each area of interest, as were baseline activity and DIA so that regression estimates could be interpreted in standard deviation units. We ran multi-level regression models with observations nested within sibling pairs.

Our third aim was to understand how baseline activity and DIA were associated with functional connectivity within and between brain networks. We again used a series of regression models, using FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg) correction for 12 hypotheses and controlling for age, sex, site, and mean head motion during resting-state scans, and accounting for participant nesting within families. We excluded fMRI data that did not pass quality control procedures of the ABCD study organizers (12.7%) or had mean head motion greater than .5 mm during resting-state scans (3.6%). For both RQ2 and RQ3, we examined whether baseline activity and DIA were better predictors of neural structure and function than children’s average physical activity during the school day. We also ran sensitivity analyses to examine whether associations held after controlling for race/ethnicity and parent education and after excluding children who take ADHD medications. To determine ADHD medication use, we followed Shoval et al. (2021) and used parent report of common ADHD medications (e.g., Ritalin, Adderall, methylphenidate, etc.).

Temporal dynamics of behavior across the school day

Our first goal was to conduct a conceptual replication of the analysis reported in Koepp et al., in press that reported that children showed daily increases in activity (DIA) across the school day (Fig. 2A). Our multi-level models revealed strikingly similar findings in the ABCD dataset (Fig. 2B). In the piecewise model, which allowed the functional form to vary, children showed overall increases across the school day period (9 am to 3 pm) and, similar to Koepp et al., in press, showed a marked reversal following the 12-o’clock hour. When estimating DIA with a linear model, children showed significant hourly increases in their activity, b = 0.06, p < .001, 95% [0.057, 0.063]. Output for the linear and piecewise regression models is shown in Table S2. After estimating the linear model with a random-intercept only, we added a random slope term to the linear model and a likelihood ratio test revealed that the random-intercept model was a worse fit to the data compared to the one that added a random slope, chi-square(1) = 7545, p < .001. We thus concluded there was substantial variability in slopes across youth. Linking baseline activity and dia to ratings of behavioral regulation

We conducted an exploratory analysis to understand which dimensions of behavioral ratings from parents, teachers, and youth were most associated with baseline activity and DIA after accounting for age, sex, and site (Table 2). In general, baseline activity showed stronger associations and a greater number of significant associations with behavioral ratings than did DIA. Higher baseline activity was associated with higher teacher ratings of ADHD symptoms (B = 0.14, pFDR <.001), youth reports of fun-seeking (B = 0.11, pFDR <.001), positive urgency (B = 0.07, pFDR <.001) sensation seeking (B = 0.06, pFDR =.001), and parent reports of lower inhibitory control (B = −0.08, pFDR <.001), greater surgency (B = 0.06, pFDR <.001), and lower attention (B = −0.04, pFDR =.036). Greater DIA was also associated with behavioral ratings, namely youth reports of positive urgency (B = 0.05, pFDR =.028) and parent reports of lower inhibitory control (B = −0.06, pFDR =.014). Thus, baseline activity and DIA were most related to behavioral ratings of impulsivity, excitability, and lack of inhibitory control. By contrast, associations with attention-related constructs were weaker and less consistent, as were scales targeting lack of planning and lack of perseverance. Overall, baseline activity showed stronger and more consistent associations with behavioral ratings than did DIA. Results were similarly null in the sensitivity tests for average activity during the school day, race and parent education, and ADHD medication (Tables S6 to S8).

We again used parent education as an aid for interpreting the direction and magnitude of associations. Greater parent education was associated with resting-state connectivity in the opposite direction of DIA and baseline activity. That is, greater parent education was associated with greater between-network connectivity for the frontoparietal and somatomotor network (B = 0.12) and weaker within-network connectivity for the somatomotor network (B = −0.16) but showed null associations for connectivity between dorsal attention and somatomotor network (B = 0.01). The associations for baseline activity and DIA with functional connectivity were therefore over half the size of the association for parent education and were stronger than most associations with traditional ratings of behavior, which showed associations with functional connectivity ranging in magnitude from null to B = 0.08 (Table S9).

The two parameters summarizing changes across the day (baseline activity and DIA) were again better predictors of neural measures than the simple average of children’s activity across the school day (Table S10). The pattern of results was similar across the sensitivity analyses when excluding children taking ADHD medication (Table S11) and when controlling for participants’ race ethnicity and parent education (Table S12).

Discussion

We investigated how temporal dynamics of children’s natural behavior relate to brain structure and function. We focused on children’s behavioral regulation at school, using the imperative that children face to modulate motor activity at school to be appropriate to the setting. Consistent with our pre-registered hypotheses, actigraphy measures of behavioral regulation (DIA and baseline activity) showed unique associations with brain structure in areas for executive control and motor action. Similarly, DIA and baseline activity also showed unique associations with functional connectivity implicated in executive control of motor action. Our use of intensive behavioral data from wearable devices revealed the temporal dynamics of children’s natural behavior and replicated findings from a prior national study (Koepp et al., in press), showing that youth’s physical activity, objectively assessed using actigraphy, increased across the school day and that individual differences showed modest but statistically significant associations with behavioral ratings of self-regulation.

Structural brain development and natural behavior

Our structural analyses revealed that the temporal dynamics of youth’s natural behavior, captured in DIA and baseline activity, were associated with thinner cortex in preregistered regions of interest important for motor action (precentral gyrus) and control of behavior (rostral middle frontal gyrus). Though small in absolute terms, the magnitude of associations were similar to previous studies of the ABCD dataset (e.g., Gilman et al., 2024; Owens et al., 2023) and were about half the size of associations between cortical thickness and parent education in the same areas. Our findings converge with prior work linking thinner cortex to impulsivity (Schilling et al., 2012). For example, Owens et al. (2023) found that behavioral ratings of positive urgency in the ABCD dataset were associated with thinner cortex in the rostral middle frontal gyrus. Somewhat surprisingly, however, other behavioral ratings we examined showed null associations with cortical thickness in the middle frontal regions associated with executive control (i.e., rostral and caudal middle frontal regions). By contrast, the behavioral ratings were consistently related to thinner cortex in the precentral gyrus, also known as motor cortex. Notably, DIA also predicted thinner cortex in the precentral gyrus. Together, these convergent findings illustrate how the regulation of reactivity in motor systems is a primary domain of self-regulation (Blair and Ku, 2022, Rothbart et al., 2001) and how motor activity and the control of behavior are tightly linked—controlling one’s behavior means controlling actions, and all actions occur through motor systems (Adolph and Hoch, 2019).

In contrast to cortical thickness, cortical surface area in the regions of interest showed no significant associations with actigraphy measures. The null associations with surface area were somewhat surprising because prior work using the ABCD linked children’s impulsivity, measured using behavioral ratings, to lower cortical surface area in the ABCD dataset (Gilman et al., 2024). Similarly, studies have linked ADHD traits to lower cortical surface area, both in the ABCD (Bernanke et al., 2022) and in meta-analyses (Grasby & Jahanshad, 2020). One explanation for the divergent findings may be that cortical surface area stabilizes earlier and is more “set in place” by age 11 compared to cortical thickness. In the current sample, age was related to cortical thickness but not surface area, suggesting that age-related increases in surface area expected across development had already taken place. Thickness, on the other hand, shows ongoing changes, namely the thinning of cortex across development (Bethlehem et al., 2022). Although one might expect thinner cortex to be adaptive because it represents a more mature trajectory, thinner cortex in adolescence is associated with greater impulsivity (Pehlivanova et al., 2018, Tanzer et al., 2021). Similarly, thinner cortex was associated with greater impulsivity and lower socioeconomic advantage in our analyses with the current sample.

Functional brain development and natural behavior

Consistent with our structural results, our functional analyses revealed that DIA and baseline activity were associated with functional connectivity in networks related to motor and executive control. Our findings converged with prior work on psychopathology linking greater somatomotor connectivity to greater impulsivity, and lower behavioral control to less connectivity, between frontoparietal and somatomotor networks (Baker et al., 2019, Kebets et al., 2019). The current study extends the literature to a typically developing sample: we uncovered correlates of frontoparietal-somatomotor connectivity with variation in normative behavior rather than psychopathology. Stronger within-network connectivity of the somatomotor network may reflect being primed for movement, whereas greater between-network connectivity for the frontoparietal and somatomotor networks may reflect stronger executive capacities for the inhibition of motor actions. After all, the executive control network is thought to influence control over other brain regions by sending bias signals that amplify or dampen neural activity in other regions (Friedman and Robbins, 2022). Stronger between-network connectivity for dorsal attention and somatomotor might similarly reflect greater top-down control of attention and action.

Notably, capturing the temporal dynamics of children’s natural behavior improved prediction of functional connectivity. The intercept and slope of activity across the day were better predictors of functional connectivity compared to children’s average activity level, which showed a weaker association with somatomotor within-network and null associations with between-network connectivity for somatomotor to frontoparietal networks. Perhaps the temporal dynamics, which appear to capture the wearing out of behavioral regulation (Koepp et al., in press), reflect individual differences in how long the frontoparietal network can exert influence on control of motor behavior and action throughout the school day. However, our analyses are cross-sectional, so we cannot establish directionality. The reverse is also possible—the experience of needing to sustain behavioral control could build connectivity between executive and motor networks.

The magnitude of associations uncovered in our functional analyses were relatively large compared to previous studies using the ABCD (e.g., Norman et al., 2023; Rakesh et al., 2021). Resting-state functional imaging is thought to reflect patterns of functional connectivity in everyday life (Gordon et al., 2016). It makes sense that having an intensive measure of natural behavior collected objectively over several hours and days might account for more variance in functional connectivity compared to standard behavioral ratings that are subjective and offer less gradation in measurement. At the same time, however, evidence in the current study suggests that the actigraphy measures, focused on motor behavior, have a narrower scope of measurement than is possible to achieve with a broad range of behavioral ratings.

We originally hypothesized that we might find associations with functional connectivity for the default mode and cingulo-opercular networks, but these hypotheses were not borne out. This was somewhat surprising because the cingulo-opercular or “action mode” network contains hubs of the motor cortex that coordinate and plan goal-directed action (Dosenbach et al., 2025, Gordon et al., 2023). One possibility is that we did not capture the right kind of motor behavior or examined the wrong time scale. The current study investigated changes in behavior at an hourly time scale, but previous work suggests that changes in behavior on more micro timescales (i.e., seconds or minutes; Koepp et al., 2022; Koepp and Gershoff, 2024) might better index parameters of reactivity like the suddenness or intensity of a response, features that might capture more about motivation, salience, and drive that animate goal-directed behavior (Rothbart and Bates, 2006). High-volume actigraphy data like those used here are ripe for data-driven explorations to identify temporal patterns of behavior that are associated with network connectivity. We also expected that we might uncover a weaker anticorrelation between default mode and somatomotor networks, as has been observed for ADHD and ADHD symptoms (Duffy et al., 2021, Norman et al., 2023). The null association uncovered here may suggest that DIA and baseline activity may capture more about being primed for movement rather than internally directed thought interrupting action and vice versa. Indeed, the null fundings for default mode connectivity are consistent with the null associations we encountered for actigraphy and ratings of attention.

Temporal dynamics of natural behavior

Children demonstrated substantial increases in their activity across the school day. These results bear a striking similarity to the results of a previous study (Koepp et al., in press), with nearly identical estimates for the average hourly rate of change, despite the two studies collecting data nearly two decades apart and with different devices (Bagot et al., 2018; Freedson et al., 1997). Here, youth exhibited a drop in activity following the midday hour, which prior work has linked to the timing of children’s lunch periods (Koepp et al., in press). Though mechanisms for the midday drop are not yet identified (there may be social or physiological explanations), the consistency across datasets is notable. Finally, we note that children’s activity is lowest in the morning, suggesting a circadian pattern of behavior with a nightly reset. Overall, the consistency of the findings across two large, national studies lends confidence that the daily increase in activity (DIA) is a reliable behavioral phenomenon that can be detected using wearable accelerometers. The findings show that children’s behavioral control may be highest in the morning, suggesting that the morning may be the ideal time for the most challenging classroom instruction. The data also suggest that youth may benefit from periods that give them a break from the demands to control their behavior. Future work should test these possibilities.

Both daily increases in activity and children’s baseline activity showed associations with established measures of behavioral control. Notably, the actigraphy measures showed associations across multiple informants—parents, teachers, and youth themselves, capturing distinct dimensions of functioning in everyday life (De Los Reyes et al., 2015). Overall, the pattern of associations revealed that baseline activity and DIA capture processes of controlling behavior (inhibitory control, lack of impulsivity), especially when excited about something (positive urgency, surgency). The associations with behavioral ratings were modest in size, but weak associations among measures of even the same self-regulatory construct are common in the literature (Toplak et al., 2013), especially when comparing across measurement modality like tasks versus questionnaires (Eisenberg et al., 2019, Li et al., 2023, Malanchini et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the findings imply that our actigraphy-based measures may be most closely related to impulsive behavior and action rather than to other aspects of emotional regulation or cognitive control. Interestingly, baseline activity showed stronger and more consistent associations with behavioral ratings than did DIA. This pattern may reflect the fact that behavioral ratings capture mean differences in behavior rather than variability across short time scales and may therefore be more conceptually aligned with baseline activity rather than DIA. By contrast, DIA showed strongest associations with functional connectivity, which may reflect the control of behavior over time.

Limitations, strengths, and future directions

The current study leveraged data from wearable devices to conduct intensive observations of children’s behavior at school (de Barbaro, 2019). Using a large, national dataset meant that youth attended many different schools, which likely varied in their structure, schedule, and, to some extent, their expectations for student behavior at school. Future work should investigate how school factors may shape patterns of activity across the school day as well as how behavior is interpreted in those contexts. One limitation of our study is that we did not have access to coding of children’s behavior through direct observation. However, the use of wearable devices enabled intensive, continuous measurement across several school days for thousands of participants. This allowed us to assess the temporal dynamics of natural behavior at scale. The use of a large national dataset enhances external validity, though we note that Black children were underrepresented in the actigraphy analyses due to missing data. Another limitation is that our investigation focused on a particular domain of self-regulation (motor behavior). Although the motor domain is a primary domain of self-regulation (Blair and Ku, 2022, Rothbart et al., 2001) and important for adaptive behavior at school, we did not capture all relevant dimensions and it is not yet clear how these findings would generalize to other domains of self-regulation. Future studies might augment data collection with additional wearable devices to detect speech patterns that could indicate impulsivity (like interrupting others) or eye gaze that could index attention (Yu et al., 2019). Future work might also consider how patterns of activity outside of school hours might be associated with behavioral profiles, as youth with ADHD demonstrate elevated activity levels in the evening hours relative to children without ADHD (Zerón-Rugerio, 2021).

Conclusion

Across structural and functional analyses, the temporal dynamics of children’s activity in natural settings was associated with brain regions known to subserve motor action and the top-down control of behavior. These findings help to validate DIA as a meaningful marker of behavioral regulation and illustrate how neural structure and function are associated with the unfolding of natural behavior over time. Findings highlight the promise of wearable devices to study behavior naturalistically and objectively (de Barbaro, 2019, Messinger et al., 2022) and understand temporal patterns of behavior that index between-child differences in behavioral regulation (Koepp et al., in press).
