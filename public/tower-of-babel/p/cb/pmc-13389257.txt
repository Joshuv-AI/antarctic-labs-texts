What does color on an echocardiogram tell you? I'm sure you have been asked that many times by now. In my opinion, that is a very good question and one that warrants a nuanced discussion.

Nowadays, the term color requires additional clarification since colorization of the conventionally displayed gray scale (black and white display) may be applied to either two- or three-dimensional (2D or 3D) images. These B-mode images can be displayed in a variety of options (e.g., sepia). This colored 2D display does not alter the information shown in black and white and is entirely a reader preference mode. However, the colorization of 3D volume-rendered displays allows the reader to better appreciate and visualize the perception of depth (e.g., lighter shades are more superficial than darker shades). Color is also used with 3D displays to demonstrate segmental details (e.g., regional motion). Lastly, parametric maps (bull’s-eye displays) use color to emphasize segmental velocity, strain, timing of contraction, and more.

But for this editorial, we will be thinking about the most common use of the term color to mean "color-flow Doppler" (CFD).

The ability to combine anatomic and physiologic information together in a straightforward and easily recognizable display is one of the most underrated and clinically valuable cardiac imaging tools we have. It was more than 50 years ago that investigators created ultrasound systems that allowed the operator to assign blood flow sampling at any anatomic location of their choosing. Combining pulsed-wave spectral Doppler displays with B-mode grayscale imaging remains the most important approach to the assessment of the vasculature (e.g., "duplex" scanning). Displaying this pulsed-wave Doppler information as a color template (or map) directly on top of the 2D (or 3D) anatomic display allows ultrasound systems to further modify this combined anatomic/physiologic display to now reflect the direction, the velocity, and even the presence or absence of a mixture of blood flows (variance mode).

Color-flow Doppler is a pulsed-wave Doppler technique that uses multiple gates (regions of interest) within the ultrasound beam (Figure 1A). At each region of interest, a flow velocity estimate is superimposed directly over the 2D image using a color display scale that is based on flow direction, mean (not peak) velocity, and potentially, velocity variance. There are multiple CFD maps available to select, but most commonly, these maps will display laminar flow as a pure color (with red toward and blue away from the transducer) and disturbed flow with a random amalgamation of many velocities as a multicolor mosaic. The relative velocities are displayed indirectly. Dark reds and blues are slow, whereas bright reds and blues are fast. Black is zero velocity. Since lower velocity is darker, it makes sense that no flow is displayed as black. Interestingly, our CFD scale is displayed as a vertical scale, but in fact, it is a circular scale that repeats itself as velocities may exceed the maximal scale. For example, in the figure shown, the Nyquist limit is set at 69 cm/s. Velocities greater than this will flip over to blue. Therefore, velocities at >69 cm/s will not be bright red/yellow but will instead become blue. At the point that they double this maximal velocity (1.38 M/s in this example), they will once again return to the middle of this scale and will be black. We take advantage of this fact when we modify the scale to optimally display the radius of the proximal isovelocity surface area (PISA; Figure 1C). I am convinced that Neil Young was describing this when he sang “Out of the blue and into the black." Hopefully, now you will be too.

As you would correctly suspect, combining all of this anatomic 2D and physiologic CFD data together into a single image display is complex. There is a time commitment necessary to process this information. Anything that takes more time will result in lower temporal resolution (lower frame rates; lower Hertz). It is important to maintain high-quality standards, so always strive for a smaller area of interest, lowest depth possible, and CFD scale (Nyquist limit) set just below the threshold for noise.

In this month's issue of CASE, the authors of ”Atrial Fibrillation in an Unroofed Coronary Sinus” (CS) provide readers with one of the best examples of a clinically significant unroofed CS in an adult. They include transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) images demonstrating the dilated right heart and the deficient left atrial wall that abuts the CS, cardiac computed tomography (CCT) images that are reconstructed to clearly show the left atrium–CS communication, and right heart catheterization findings to emphasize the shunt physiology. They provide readers with a unique understanding of this congenital defect and its relationship with atrial fibrillation.

In “Complete Right Ventricular Thrombus in Pediatric Ebstein Anomaly,” the authors present readers with the devastating consequences that may occur after complex congenital surgery including a Fontan palliation. By comparing the serial echocardiograms, readers are able to visualize what complete intracavitary thrombosis looks like and how this can alter physiology such that the patient experiences cardiac tamponade. They include an excellent discussion regarding variations in surgical approaches, atypical clinical presentations, and strategies for anticoagulation.

The authors of “Giant Right Atrial Appendage Aneurysm Misdiagnosed as Pericardial Effusion” offer readers another striking example of congenital heart disease that is first diagnosed in adulthood. Their 62-year-old patient had a large right atrial appendage aneurysm that was initially misinterpreted as a pericardial effusion. They include outstanding 2D and 3D TTE, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and CCT images for readers to compare and hopefully be able to recognize this rare finding.

In “Severe Mitral Regurgitation and Constrictive Pericarditis in a Patient with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus” (SLE), the authors illustrate the difficulty of diagnosing complex autoimmune disease that impacts the heart. Their young patient with acute SLE presented with decompensated heart failure and was found on echo to have severe mitral regurgitation as well as constrictive physiology. I think you will agree with me that the TEE images are "must see TV." To complete their report, they included the gross pathologic specimen of the mitral valve as well as the histopathology confirming their diagnosis.

The authors of “Embolic Stroke of Unknown Source in a Young Patient due to Pulmonary Arteriovenous Malformation Masquerading as a Patent Foramen Ovale” provide readers with an image smorgasbord that includes TTE, TEE, 2D and 3D CCT, and invasive pulmonary angiography. Their young patient with an embolic stroke was found to have a complex pulmonary arteriovenous malformation as the most likely source. There are numerous important teaching points throughout their article.

Lastly, in “Transesophageal Echocardiographic Guidance of Transapical Aortic Cannulation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass,” the authors outline a step-by-step approach to using TEE guidance for transapical aortic cannulation procedures in patients with a type A thoracic aortic dissection. Since this cannulation approach is not common, this interventional TEE guide is valuable for readers who participate in surgical procedures or are regularly involved in TEE in the operating room.
