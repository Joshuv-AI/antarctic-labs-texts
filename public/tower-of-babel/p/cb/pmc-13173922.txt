Genotype × harvest time effects on yield, root size distribution, and sensory quality in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.)
Authors: Moon Modak, Mst Masuma Akter, Sanzida Akter Trisha, Mahmuda Akhter Hasi, Ebna Habib Md Shofiur Rahaman, Arif Hasan Khan Robin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.) is a vital root crop valued for its nutritional quality, yield potential, and adaptability; however, the effects of early harvesting on yield, root size distribution, and marketable value are not well understood. This study evaluated nine genotypes to compare genotype performance at two harvest times, classify roots by economic value, and assess consumer-preferred traits.MethodsThe experiment was conducted at 90 and 120 days after transplanting (DAT). Morphological, yield, and nutritional traits were measured, including SPAD value, vine and leaf characteristics, shoot weight, root number and mass, economic root number and mass, soluble sugars, and total yield. Roots were classified into six size classes based on mass and number.ResultsMore than 40% of root mass per plot consisted of roots weighing 30–100 g, with the remainder distributed among five other size classes. Genotypes G-89 and BARI Sweetpotato-15 produced 150% and 65% higher yields at 120 DAT compared with 90 DAT. At 120 DAT, BARI Sweetpotato-17, G-193, G-89, and BAU Sweetpotato-5 produced roots across all six size classes. Sensory evaluation at 90 DAT indicated that G-54 and G-193 were preferred for texture, boiling, and baking, while G-89 and BAU Sweetpotato-5 were favored for aroma, indicating potential traits associated with earlier maturity under the study conditions.ConclusionsGenotypes G-89, G-184, BARI Sweetpotato-15, and G-138 exhibited comparatively higher yields and larger storage roots at 120 days after planting (DAT) under char land conditions, while several other genotypes including G-54 produced satisfactory yields and acceptable root quality at 90 DAT. This indicates their comparatively earlier performance within the evaluated harvest times under the specific char land agro-ecological conditions of the study. Broader applicability and definitive classification would require further validation across additional harvest stages and agro-ecological zones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08693-6.Keywords: Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.), Root class, Root yield, Harvest time, Sensory evaluation, Short duration, Genotype selection

Introduction

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.) is a hexaploid (2n = 6x = 90) root crop species of the family Convolvulaceae with significant agricultural and economic importance among > 1,000 species across 55 genera of this family [1]. Globally, it ranks as the sixth most important food crop after rice, wheat, maize, potato, and cassava, due to its contribution to food and nutritional security [2, 3]. It is rich in energy, carbohydrates, dietary fiber, and micronutrients such as potassium, calcium, and iron [4, 5].

According to FAOSTAT [3], global sweet potato production reached 88.9 million metric tons in 2021, with Asia contributing over 63%—led by China with nearly 52 million tons. In Bangladesh, sweet potato is primarily cultivated on riverbanks, char lands, and marginal highlands during the rabi season (November–March), which is characterized by relatively cool temperatures, low rainfall, and dry climatic conditions, producing 280 thousand metric tons from 26 thousand hectares in 2020–2021 [6]. The crop’s adaptability to marginal soils, drought-prone areas, and low-input systems makes it highly valuable for smallholder and climate-vulnerable agricultural systems [4, 5, 7, 8]. Its ability to maintain high photosynthetic efficiency, complete its growth cycle quickly, and deliver high yields even under resource-constrained environments makes it especially attractive for sustainable cultivation [7–10].

Sweet potato exhibits extensive phenotypic diversity due to its high heterozygosity and outcrossing nature, offering broad potential for genetic improvement [7, 11, 12]. Agronomic traits such as yield, dry matter content, root shape and size, and flesh color show continuous variation [11, 13]. Notable genotype-specific differences in biomass production, maturity duration, and nutritional composition further highlight the importance of selecting appropriate genotypes for diverse agro-ecological conditions [13–15].

Sweet potato plays a vital role in household food security, particularly in tropical regions, due to its ability to remain in the ground for “piecemeal harvesting”—a traditional storage practice that supports staggered consumption [16, 17]. Although harvest age effects have been investigated across various harvest timings, relatively limited evidence is available under char land conditions, potentially restricting the identification of early-bulking genotypes suitable for short-season production in marginal environments [5, 18].

Root size distribution across harvest ages has been previously reported and is generally classified as small (< 100 g), medium (100–300 g), and large (> 300 g) [19, 20]; the present study focuses on reclassification into six root size classes and provides an economic interpretation specific to char land conditions in Bangladesh. Tuberous root class affects marketability, storability, and processing suitability [21]. Early harvesting (e.g., 90 DAT) tends to produce more small and medium tubers suited for fresh markets, while late harvesting (e.g., 120 DAT) increases total yield and the proportion of large tubers [20, 22, 23]. However, oversized tubers (> 500 g) may reduce market value due to poor uniformity and storability [19, 21], whereas medium-sized tubers are widely preferred for their cooking quality, ease of handling, and consumer acceptance [19, 20]. Genotype and harvest timing both significantly affect tuber class distribution, with some genotypes being early-bulking and others late-maturing [5, 23].

In addition to agronomic performance, sensory attributes—such as sweetness, aroma, hardness, crumbliness, and overall palatability—play a crucial role in consumer preference and market demand [22, 24]. Orange-fleshed sweet potato (OFSP) varieties, in particular, are often preferred when boiled due to their enhanced flavor and nutritional benefits [25, 26]. Yet, despite its relevance, sensory evaluation is rarely integrated into sweet potato breeding programs in Bangladesh [23].

However, limited research has simultaneously examined agronomic performance and sensory attributes across genotypes and harvest stages under char land conditions, constraining the identification of cultivars suited to early or staggered harvesting in marginal environments [18, 27]. Moreover, the performance of OFSP across varying harvesting times remains poorly understood, limiting its full utilization in nutrition-sensitive and market-oriented production systems [25, 26].

Therefore, this study evaluates the effects of genotype and harvest timing (90 and 120 days after planting) on yield performance and root size distribution of sweet potato under the specific environmental and seasonal conditions of the study. In addition, a sensory evaluation was conducted at 90 days after planting to assess consumer-related traits across genotypes. The objective was to examine genotype-wise performance at the two harvest stages and to identify harvest-stage–specific associations between agronomic performance and sensory traits, thereby providing insights into genotype suitability and market potential.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and design

The field experiments were carried out on the char land (riverine island) of the Brahmaputra River near Bangladesh Agricultural University (BAU), Mymensingh, which is 14 m above sea level and located at latitude 24.7240° N and longitude 90.4374° E [27]. The mean monthly temperature throughout the study period varied from 30.6 °C in September to 22.94 °C in December, based on climate records from the Bangladesh Meteorological Department and pertinent literature [27–31] (Table 1, Figure S1). September 2024 recorded the highest temperature at 33.3 °C, while December 2024 experienced the lowest temperature (27.6 °C) during the study period. Rainfall sharply declined from 114.6 mm in September to just 5.3 mm in December, indicating a transition from the post-monsoon to the winter season (Table 1, Figure S1). This seasonal shift likely influenced the physiological growth and yield performance of the sweet potato genotypes under investigation (Table 1, Figure S1). Day and night temperatures followed similar trends, with night temperatures slightly lower than daytime temperatures (Figure S1). Nine sweet potato genotypes, comprising three released varieties and six advanced lines, were evaluated in this study (Table 2). The experiment was conducted from October to December 2024 during the rabi season in Bangladesh, characterized by cooler temperatures, low rainfall, and dry conditions, using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. For the two harvests (H1: 90 DAT; H2: 120 DAT), genotypes were randomized within blocks for each harvest in the same field. Therefore, the Genotype × Harvest interaction reflects within-season, within-field performance rather than comparisons between fully independent factorial plots. Each plot measured 2.5 m by 2.0 m. To ensure optimal growth and management, 25–30 cm long vines with three nodes were planted at a spacing of 40 cm within rows and 60 cm between rows. A total of 24 vines were planted per plot, with a distance of 1.0 m between adjacent plots and 1.5 m between replications.

Experimental materials

This study examined morphological, nutritional, and yield-contributing characteristics of nine sweet potato genotypes. Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute (BARI) provided two check varieties—BARI Sweet Potato-15 and BARI Sweet Potato-17— as part of the experimental materials. The study included six sweet potato genotypes developed through polycross breeding, using seeds obtained from the International Potato Center (CIP), along with the BAU Sweet Potato-5 variety collected from Bangladesh Agricultural University (BAU). A comprehensive overview of the sweet potato genotypes used in this investigation is presented in Table 2.

Data collection

Nine sweet potato genotypes were evaluated for 16 quantitative features representing morphological, nutritional, and yield-contributing traits (including root class). Root class data were collected based on root mass and number of roots. The root size of the nine genotypes was categorized in six classes — Class 1 (30–100 g), Class 2 (100–200 g), Class 3 (200–300 g), Class 4 (300–400 g), Class 5 (400–500 g), and Class 6 (> 500 g) (Figure S2). The contribution of mass and number to each class was recorded after harvesting by a digital weighing scale (SF-400, Camry, Guangdong, China) and manually by counting. Leaf chlorophyll status was estimated using a SPAD meter (SPAD-502 Plus, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), providing a relative index of SPAD value. Data for vine length, leaves per vine, leaf length, leaf width, leaf area, and shoot mass were collected from three plants per plot (Table 3). In accordance with marketability requirements greater than 30 g, yield-related information, such as yield (t ha− 1) and total and economic number and mass of roots per plot, were recorded at harvest. The content of glucose, fructose, and sucrose in fresh tuber samples were determined using three separate digital refractometers (HI96803, HI96804 and HI96801; Hanna Instruments Inc., Woonsocket, RI, USA) according to the procedures described by Keller and Schwager [32] for the determination of individual sugar contents using digital refractometry.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation of nine sweet potato genotypes was conducted with a panel of 15 randomly selected, untrained individuals under blind conditions. Each genotype was assessed in three replications. Roots were washed, peeled, and cut into uniform pieces, then prepared by boiling, baking, and burning, while raw roots were evaluated for crumbliness, aroma, and texture. Seven sensory attributes—aroma, sweetness, hardness, crumbliness, boiled taste, burnt taste, and baked taste—were scored on a 0–10 intensity scale. Medians and ranges were calculated to quantify variation among genotypes, following established descriptive sensory evaluation procedures and guidelines for the use of intensity rating scales [33, 34]. The preparation of sweet potato genotypes for sensory evaluation is presented in Figure S2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Minitab 17 [35] and R 4.4.1 via RStudio [36]. ANOVA and genotype-wise mean comparisons were performed in Minitab, with post hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey’s HSD (agricolae package) for our fully balanced dataset [37]. Effect sizes (η²) were calculated with the lsr package to quantify the proportion of variation attributable to genotype.

Total soluble solids (°Brix) were measured using a refractometer calibrated with distilled water (0 °Brix) and a standard sucrose solution (10 °Brix). Tubers were peeled, chopped, homogenized, and filtered to obtain a clear extract following standardized protocols. Although °Brix primarily reflects total soluble solids, it serves as an indirect indicator of overall sugar concentration in sweet potato. Careful calibration ensured reliable comparative measurements across genotypes [38]. To control for potential Type I errors across 16 traits, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method (stats package). PCA, Pearson correlation, and multivariate analyses were performed using dplyr, tidyr, factoextra, and cluster, with visualizations via ggplot2, corrplot, and RColorBrewer [39–46]. Sensory evaluation data, which did not meet normality assumptions, were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons (pgirmess) [47].

Harvesting time (HT) had a significant main effect (p < 0.05) on SPAD value, leaf area, shoot mass, number and weight of economic roots, glucose content, and total yield (Table 4). Mean total yield increased from 6 to 27 t ha⁻¹ at the first harvest to 11–45 t ha⁻¹ at the second harvest (Table S2).

The genotype × harvesting time (Gen × HT) interaction was significant (p < 0.05) for SPAD value, number of vines per plant, vine length, leaf area, leaf length, leaf width, shoot mass, total root weight, economic root weight, and total yield (Table 4). Yield changes between harvests varied among genotypes, with extremes ranging from a 35% decrease to a 150% increase (Table 4; Table S2). The Gen × HT interaction reflects within-field, within-season responses of genotypes to the two harvest timings, rather than effects from fully independent factorial plots, and should be interpreted accordingly.

Comparison between two harvests

Yield-contributing traits

Significant genotypic variation was observed for yield and its associated parameters (Table 5, Table S2, Fig. 1). At the evaluated harvest stages, G-89 showed the largest relative increase (150%) compared to the first harvest, while other genotypes also demonstrated notable gains. (Table 5, Table S2, Fig. 1). G-184 and BARI Sweetpotato-15 also exhibited substantial increases of 94% and 65%, respectively (Table 5, Table S2, Fig. 1).

The mass of economic roots followed a similar trend, with pronounced increases in G-89 (238%) and G-184 (116%), while BARI Sweetpotato-17 showed a moderate increase of 61.2% (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S4).

Vegetative growth traits

Vegetative traits differed significantly among genotypes and between harvests (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S5). G-276 showed the largest increases across all leaf and shoot traits, while G-89 and G-184 also exhibited substantial leaf area expansion (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S5). G-138 showed moderate increases in vine and leaf length, but shoot mass declined (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S5). In contrast, BAU Sweetpotato-5 and G-193 showed a general decline in most vegetative traits at the second harvest, which may reflect a shift in photosynthate allocation from vegetative growth toward tuber development, consistent with source–sink dynamics in sweet potato (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S5).

Nutritional quality traits

Individual sugar contents varied among genotypes and harvests (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S5). G-276 and G-184 showed notable increases in glucose content (149% and 48%) and sucrose content (72% for G-184), while BARI Sweetpotato-17 exhibited moderate increases in fructose content (44%) and sucrose content (59%). G-138 showed a smaller increase in glucose content (25%), whereas G-89 and G-193 experienced reductions in most individual sugar contents at the second harvest (Table 5, Table S2, Figure S5).

Distribution of root number and mass in two harvests

Root Size Distribution

Root size distribution was evaluated using six predefined classes based on fresh weight (Class 1: 30–100 g, Class 2: 100–200 g, Class 3: 200–300 g, Class 4: 300–400 g, Class 5: 400–500 g, Class 6: >500 g). Roots weighing less than 200 g (Classes 1 and 2) predominated in number, with Class 1 contributing the majority of root counts but less than half of the total root mass (mass-to-number ratio = 0.57) (Fig. 2). Class 2 roots represented a smaller proportion of total root number but contributed disproportionately more to absolute root mass (mass-to-number ratio = 1.42). Medium and large roots (Classes 3–6, > 200 g) were relatively few in number but contributed disproportionately to total root mass, with mass-to-number ratios ranging from 2.83 to 3.5.

Genotype-specific root size distribution

The pattern of root size distribution was generally consistent across genotypes (Figs. 4 and 5). At harvest-1, BAU Sweetpotato-5 and BARI Sweetpotato-15 allocated 20% and 32% of root mass, respectively, to Class 1 (30–100 g), which was lower than most other genotypes (Figs. 4 and 5). By harvest-2, G-193 showed a pronounced increase in Class 1, contributing 83% of total root mass—a 43% rise compared to harvest-1 (Figs. 4 and 5).

For Class 3 (200–300 g), G-184 increased its proportional mass from 3% to 25%, whereas G-276 decreased by 19% (p < 0.05) (Figs. 4 and 5). Minor shifts were observed in larger root classes: G-89 increased by 10% in Class 4 (300–400 g), Class 5 (400–500 g) remained largely unchanged across genotypes, and Class 6 (> 500 g) showed minimal changes (± 1%) in some genotypes (p < 0.05) while other genotypes allocated less mass to the largest root classes overall (Figs. 4 and 5).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PC1 captured variation mainly associated with root number, root mass, and yield, whereas PC2 reflected individual sugar content and shoot biomass (Fig. 6, Table S5). PC1 showed strong positive loadings for total and economic root number and mass, while individual sugar contents loaded negatively (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is an important root crop, with diverse genotypes showing substantial variation in root size, yield potential, and individual sugar composition [27]. As this study was conducted on char land during a single growing season, the interpretations presented here reflect genotype performance under the specific seasonal and environmental conditions of the experiment. Understanding genotype-specific growth patterns and harvest responses is critical for optimizing production, quality, and marketability. Previous studies have documented class-wise root distributions, showing that root size and number significantly influence total yield and economic value [47–49]. Root classification into six size classes revealed that smaller roots represented a major component of the economic root mass, while medium and larger classes together accounted for the remaining proportion (Fig. 2). Some genotypes, including G-89, G-184, G-138, and BARI Sweetpotato-15, accumulated substantial yield by 90 days, reflecting relatively higher yield at the early harvest stage under the environmental and management conditions of this study (Fig. 1). Sensory evaluation conducted at 90 days provided information on palatability and market suitability at this early harvest stage. Integrating yield, root size distribution, and sensory traits provides a framework for evaluating genotype-specific harvest responses and marketable root size selection, which may support decision-making for farmers and breeders under similar production conditions [18, 50–52].

Genotype × harvest-time impact on sweet potato

The interaction between genotype and harvest time significantly influenced vegetative growth, root yield, and individual sugar composition, which together determine both crop performance and market quality (Table 4). Some genotypes accumulated biomass and root yield more rapidly, whereas others exhibited slower increases, reflecting inherent differences in growth patterns and potential adaptability to local environmental conditions (Table 5). Individual sugar composition—including glucose, fructose, and sucrose—varied across harvests, with some genotypes showing marked increases at later stages, reflecting ongoing carbohydrate accumulation and developmental changes that may influence palatability and storage properties [27, 52–55].

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) indicated that yield-related traits largely separated genotypes, whereas traits such as sugar accumulation were more responsive to temporal changes (Fig. 6). These patterns suggest that some traits are relatively stable across harvests, whereas others are sensitive to environmental and developmental factors, consistent with previous reports on harvest-dependent quality variation in sweet potato and other root crops [43, 56–58]. These results underscore the importance of considering both genotype and harvest timing to optimize yield and quality for specific market requirements.

Genotypic variation in response to harvest timing (90 and 120 DAT)

Comparison of harvests at 90 and 120 days revealed notable genotypic differences in yield accumulation (Fig. 1; Table S2). Genotypes G-89, G-184, BARI Sweetpotato-15, and G-138 exhibited increased yields at 120 days, suggesting continued biomass accumulation under the specific environmental and management conditions of this study. Conversely, G-54, G-193, BAU Sweetpotato-5, G-276, and BARI Sweetpotato-17 produced similar or higher yields at 90 days, reflecting relatively higher yield at 90 DAT compared to 120 DAT under the evaluated conditions (Fig. 1). It should be noted that only two harvest times (90 and 120 DAT) were evaluated; therefore, any classification as early- or late-bulking reflects relative performance at these assessed harvest stages under the specific char land agro-ecological conditions of this study, rather than definitive developmental behavior.

In contrast, BAU Sweetpotato-5 (2%) and BARI Sweetpotato-17 exhibited minimal yield gains at 120 days (Table S2), suggesting that, under the evaluated conditions, extending harvest to 120 DAT may offer limited economic advantage for genotypes performing well at 90 DAT due to additional land, labor, and market costs [18]. A decline in yield at 120 days for G-193 may reflect prolonged field retention, physiological deterioration, or environmental stress [4, 59]. These observations highlight inherent genotypic variation in yield accumulation and underscore the importance of considering relative performance at the evaluated harvest stages (90 and 120 DAT) under char land conditions when optimizing production strategies [5, 58, 60].

Differential effects of harvest timing on root mass and root number

Analysis of root size distribution showed that, although the total number of roots remained largely unchanged between harvests (Fig. 3a), the distribution of root mass shifted over time (Fig. 3b). Class 1 roots (30–100 g) dominated numerically, accounting for the majority of root counts but contributing less than half of total root mass. In contrast, medium- and large-sized roots (Classes 3–6, > 200 g), though fewer in number, contributed disproportionately to total mass, highlighting the critical role of root enlargement in determining marketable yield [61–63].

Genotype-specific trends indicated that some genotypes had largely stabilized root mass by 90 days, reflecting higher early-stage productivity, whereas others continued accumulating biomass until 120 days, demonstrating extended yield potential under favorable conditions [5, 60]. These findings underscore that root number alone is insufficient to predict yield, particularly when marketable root mass is the primary target.

Economic significance of smaller root classes

Analysis of root class distribution provided valuable insight into the economic implications of harvest timing. The six root size classes were evaluated, with Class 1 roots (30–100 g) considered “economic” due to their widespread consumption in Bangladesh [30]. Across genotypes, these small roots contributed the largest proportion of total root mass (Fig. 2), underscoring their market importance. Treating them as non-economic would result in substantial yield and income loss, as they are in demand for direct consumption, either boiled or as a vegetable [60]. Consumer preference further supports the economic relevance of small- to medium-sized roots (30–200 g) over very large roots (> 500 g), which are less convenient to cook, portion, and sell [19, 64]. Genotypes that predominantly produce small- to medium-sized roots, such as BAU Sweetpotato-5 and BARI Sweetpotato-17, can therefore enhance marketability and postharvest handling efficiency.

Very large roots are less practical for household use and marketing, requiring longer cooking times, not fitting standard utensils, and being difficult to divide for sale, making them less appealing to consumers and vendors [64]. Sensory evaluation at 90 DAT confirmed that roots harvested at this stage were generally edible and acceptable, with scores ranging from 6 to 10 on a standardized scale (Table 7), although results were based on a single harvest and an untrained panel. This indicates that roots harvested at 90 DAT were generally edible and acceptable under the tested conditions; however, broader recommendations regarding early harvest would require evaluation across additional harvest stages and environments.

Beyond household consumption, small sweet potato roots are increasingly utilized in the food industry [65, 66], where they are processed into flour to partially replace wheat flour in baked products such as bread, cakes, and pastries, enhancing nutritional content and providing gluten-free alternatives [65, 66]. Sweet potato flour is also employed in the production of natural food colorants as a plant-based substitute for synthetic dyes [67]. The substantial contribution of small roots to both total tuber mass and economic value highlights their significance in harvest planning and postharvest processing, improving profitability and sustainability for farmers in Bangladesh [68].

Integrating yield performance and consumer preference

Harvesting at 90 days may capture a substantial proportion of total root mass, but it is crucial that these roots remain edible and palatable. Therefore, this study conducted a sensory evaluation to determine edibility and palatability at 90 DAT (Table 7). Significant variation was observed among genotypes, with BAU Sweetpotato-5, G-89, and BARI Sweetpotato-17 performing well in sensory appeal and palatability (Table 7, Table S2).

Differences in yield and sensory traits suggest that genetic factors contribute to carbohydrate accumulation and starch structure, while environmental conditions and harvest timing also play a role, as seen in strong biomass allocation of G-89 at the later harvest (Fig. 1) [65–67]. Medium-sized roots produced by G-89, G-138, and G-184 at the later harvest provide flexibility for market segments, while harvest at 90 DAT did not compromise root quality, as all genotypes remained edible and suitable for consumption at the evaluated stage (Table 7). Although sensory perception may vary among individuals, traits such as sweetness, aroma, and texture consistently influence consumer preference (Table S5), highlighting the importance of integrating sensory evaluation with yield and root size data to inform breeding programs targeting both farmer profitability and consumer satisfaction [18, 51, 52, 69–71].

Genotype-specific root allocation and harvest recommendations

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) summarized the multivariate variation among sweet potato genotypes based on yield- and quality-related traits [72, 73]. The first two components accounted for the majority of total variation, with PC1 primarily associated with yield-related traits—including total root number, total root mass, economic root number and mass, and total yield per hectare—while individual sugar contents (glucose, fructose, and sucrose) loaded negatively [58, 74–76]. Genotypes with higher PC1 scores exhibited greater root number and mass, whereas genotypes with lower PC1 scores had higher concentrations of individual sugars. PC2 was mainly influenced by vegetative traits and individual sugar accumulation, reflecting variation in secondary characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis further supported these patterns, showing negative associations between yield- and sugar-related traits (Figure S6) [77].

Integration with genetic analysis revealed that traits contributing strongly to PC1—particularly total root mass and economic root number and mass—also exhibited high heritability and genetic advance, suggesting an important contribution of additive gene action and substantial potential for improvement through phenotypic selection [27, 77–81]. In contrast, vegetative traits and individual sugar contents (glucose, fructose, and sucrose) generally showed low to moderate heritability and genetic advance, reflecting strong environmental influence and limited potential for direct selection [78, 82]. Temporal variation in individual sugar accumulation across harvests further underscores environmental effects, such as temperature, rainfall, and char land conditions [82–84]. Consequently, selection focusing on root yield components may achieve higher genetic gains than direct selection for glucose, fructose and sucrose [85–87].

This study raises the question of whether increased root size at later harvests coincides with continued shoot growth. The findings highlight the importance of aligning harvest timing with genotype-specific performance within the evaluated harvest stages under the specific environmental conditions of this study. Further multi-time-point studies are needed to fully understand growth dynamics and optimize production efficiency, profitability, and consumer satisfaction.

Conclusions

Genotypes G-89, G-184, BARI Sweetpotato-15, and G-138 exhibited comparatively higher yields and larger roots at 120 days after planting (DAT), while several other genotypes achieved satisfactory yields and root sizes at 90 DAT under the tested char land conditions. Sensory evaluation at 90 DAT indicated that roots harvested at this stage were generally edible and acceptable for consumption, based on the single harvest and untrained panel evaluated in this study.

These findings suggest that aligning harvest timing with genotype-specific performance at the evaluated stages (90 and 120 DAT) can help optimize both productivity and marketable root quality. The refined root size classification, informed by local market preferences and sensory assessment, provides a practical framework to guide harvest decisions and enhance consumer acceptance in Bangladesh.

While results are promising for specific genotypes, including G-89 at 120 DAT, they should be interpreted as preliminary and context-specific, reflecting genotype × environment interactions. Performance may vary under different environmental conditions, management practices, or production systems, and multi-location validation is recommended before broader application. Overall, this study highlights the potential importance of genotype-specific harvest timing combined with redefined root size categories, reflecting their observed influence on sweet potato yield, root size distribution, and quality traits under the conditions evaluated.
