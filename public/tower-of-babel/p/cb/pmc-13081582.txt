Lipoid pneumonia misdiagnosed as cystic lung cancer: two case reports
Authors: Na Li, Yizhuo Wang, Qi Wan, Xinchun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFocal cystic air space is a rare manifestation of lipoid pneumonia. In this study, we conduct an analysis and synthesis of the characteristics of two cases initially suspected to be cystic lung cancer, but subsequently diagnosed as lipoid pneumonia, with the aim of enhancing differential diagnostic capabilities and minimizing the risk of misdiagnosis.Case presentationBoth patients were found to have ground-glass nodules upon physical examination. Chest CT scans revealed cavitary lesions in the lungs with surrounding ground-glass opacities, consistent with LUNG-RADS category 4A, not excluding cystic lung cancer. Later, surgical intervention was performed, and the pathological examination showed features consistent with lipoid pneumonia.ConclusionsLipoid pneumonia can present as ground-glass nodules and has certain similarities with cystic lung cancer in imaging manifestations. Clinicians should obtain a detailed exposure history, including the use of nasal oils or lip balms, to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure appropriate management. Keywords: Lipoid Pneumonia, Pulmonary nodules, LUNG-RADS, Cystic lung cancer

Background

Lipoid pneumonia is an inflammatory reaction caused by the deposition of lipid substances in the lungs [1]. On high-resolution CT (HRCT), ground-glass opacity is a common manifestation of lipoid pneumonia. The CT manifestations of lipoid pneumonia also include air-space nodules, which can resemble pulmonary tumors [2]. However, they have many differences in etiology, pathophysiology, imaging features, and clinical characteristics [3–5]. Accurate differential diagnosis is crucial for formulating reasonable treatment plans and assessing patient prognosis. Over the past decade, we have observed 2 patients who were suspected of having cystic lung cancer, and underwent surgical resection; histopathology ultimately confirmed lipoid pneumonia.

Case 1

A 52-year-old male was found to have a lung nodule during a physical examination. Laboratory tests showed no significant abnormalities. The patient remained asymptomatic and underwent regular monitoring. A chest CT conducted on July 2022 (Fig. 1A-B), and a PET/CT performed on November 2022 (Fig. 1C), revealed multiple small bullae under the pleura of both lungs. There was no significant change observed in the ground-glass nodule located in the posterior segment of the right lower lobe, which measured approximately 13 × 8 mm. The nodule exhibited a clear boundary and contained multilocular cysts, with no increased FDG uptake. Based on these findings, the nodule is classified as LUNG-RADS 4A, and the possibility of cystic lung cancer cannot be excluded. Later, surgical intervention was performed, and surgical biopsy (Fig. 1D) showed that the alveolar cavities and interstitium contained a large number of histiocytic cells with vacuolar cytoplasm, no obvious atypia in the cell nucleus, mild hyperplasia of the adjacent alveolar epithelium, widening of the alveolar septa, and scattered lymphocytic infiltration, with a tissue pattern consistent with lipoid pneumonia. On further questioning, he stated that he had used nasal oil to relieve nasal congestion.

Case 2

A 43-year-old female was found to have a lung nodule during a physical examination. Chest CT (Fig. 2A-C) showed a ground-glass nodule in the outer segment of the right middle lobe, about 12 × 12 mm in size, with a clear boundary and slightly lobulated edges, with multilocular cysts and blood vessels passing through inside, considering with LUNG-RADS 4A, not excluding cystic lung cancer; multiple small bullae were seen under the pleura of both lungs. Laboratory tests showed no significant abnormalities. The patient remained asymptomatic and underwent regular monitoring, and later underwent surgical intervention. Surgical biopsy (Fig. 2D) showed hyperplasia of the alveolar epithelium, focal proliferation of fibrous tissue in the interstitium, deposition of cholesterol crystals, and lymphocytic infiltration with lymphoid follicle formation, consistent with the tissue pattern of lipoid pneumonia. She was advised to stop using lip balm and lipstick (Fig. 2).

Discussion and conclusions

Lipoid pneumonia is an inflammatory reaction caused by the deposition of lipid substances in the lungs. It can be divided into endogenous and exogenous types [1–3]. Endogenous types are often related to abnormal lipid metabolism or swallowing dysfunction leading to the aspiration of self-lipids; exogenous types are mostly due to the inhalation of exogenous lipid substances, such as mineral oil fog, oil-based medications mistakenly inhaled, etc. Pathologically, the alveolar cavities are filled with a large amount of lipid substances, accompanied by varying degrees of inflammatory cell infiltration and thickening of the alveolar septa [4–7].

The radiographic findings are highly variable, which is why it is essential to include lipoid pneumonia in the differential diagnosis of a range of conditions, including bronchogenic carcinoma, metastases, infectious pneumonia, rheumatoid nodules, and granulomatosis with polyangiitis. The most common radiographic presentations are increased density with ground-glass opacities, a “crazy paving” pattern, and nodules or masses. Consolidation is generally heterogeneous and, when evaluated with mediastinal window settings, shows fat density ranging from -30 to -150 HU, which can be diagnostically helpful [8]. In terms of PET/CT, there have been cases of false positives for malignancy due to localized inflammatory activity [9–10].

LUNG-RADS is a standardized classification system designed to simplify and standardize the reporting of lung findings, particularly in the context of lung cancer screening with CT. It is used to categorize nodules and other lung abnormalities to aid in clinical decision-making [11–12]. Cystic lung cancer refers to a malignant lung tumor that has developed a cavity or hollow space within it. This cavity may be due to the necrosis of tumor tissue or the formation of an air-filled space as the tumor grows. On CT scans, cystic lung cancers often present as lung nodules with a central lucency or air-filled space, which is the cavity. If a cystic lung cancer is detected, it typically falls into a higher LUNG-RADS category, such as 4 or 5, indicating a higher likelihood of malignancy and the need for further diagnostic evaluation and prompt clinical action [13].

Diagnosis is established based on a clinical history that aligns with exposure to agents known to cause lipoid pneumonia, supported by the verification of lipid-laden macrophages in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid or characteristic findings on pulmonary histopathology. BAL may suggest the diagnosis when it yields a turbid white liquid with a surface layer of fat droplets and an aqueous lower phase; nevertheless, the most telling finding is the identification of alveolar macrophages filled with lipids [1–4]. A lung biopsy is often required and can be conducted through fiberoptic bronchoscopy or percutaneous needle aspiration. Surgical biopsy is generally not necessary. In pathological anatomy, when conventional paraffin fixation techniques are employed, lipids are dissolved by xylene, leading to the appearance of “vacuoles” where the fatty substances have been dissolved. Additionally, lipid-laden macrophages are observed to fill and expand the alveoli and interstitium, potentially associated with inflammation and fibrosis [5–7].

In terms of treatment, the initial step when patients are asymptomatic is to ascertain and cease exposure to the causative agent. While there are indications that corticosteroids may alleviate inflammation, this approach is typically reserved for acute cases with significant pulmonary damage. An additional therapeutic option is the implementation of whole lung lavage, a procedure akin to that used in the treatment of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. Ultimately, if there is concurrent bronchiectasis, pharmacological management is advised. Should pharmacological interventions prove unsuccessful, surgical intervention becomes a consideration [14–15].

In both of our cases, surgical intervention was ultimately carried out and the pathological diagnosis confirmed lipoid pneumonia. While a trial of conservative management—including cessation of inciting agents and short-interval CT follow-up—may be appropriate for asymptomatic patients with LUNG-RADS 4A lesions where features suggest lipoid pneumonia, this approach is highly dependent upon the prospective recognition of a clear lipid exposure history, which was not initially identified in our cases. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating a detailed history of lipid exposure into the standard evaluation of indeterminate pulmonary nodules. In such scenarios, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) to detect lipid-laden macrophages may also serve as a useful minimally invasive diagnostic step before considering surgery.

It is noteworthy that lipoid pneumonia presenting as a LUNG-RADS 4A lesion is exceedingly rare, as its imaging features—such as intralesional cystic change and ground-glass opacity—often closely mimic those of malignant processes, particularly cystic lung adenocarcinoma. This rarity emphasizes the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis even for nodules that meet imaging criteria suggesting a higher risk of malignancy. Clinically, this suggests that in patients with LUNG-RADS 4A nodules—especially those without conventional risk factors for lung cancer—clinicians should diligently seek historical evidence of lipid aspiration, such as the use of oil-based nasal products, lip balms, or inhalants. Incorporating this element into the diagnostic workflow could significantly improve risk stratification and patient selection for invasive versus non-invasive management.

Collectively, ground-glass opacity and air-space nodules in chest-CT are rare imaging manifestations of lipoid pneumonia, which is often misdiagnosed as cystic lung cancer due to similar imaging findings. Early recognition of this uncommon radiologic feature of lipoid pneumonia and appropriate management are important.
