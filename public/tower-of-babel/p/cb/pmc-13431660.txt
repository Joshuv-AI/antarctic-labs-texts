Evaluating efficacy of oxytetracycline, lincomycin, and tylosin for treatment of clinical European foulbrood disease
Authors: Midhun S Jose, Harmony Woodrow, Maria Janser, Marina C Bezerra da Silva, Oleksii Obshta, Marcelo P Camilli, Emilio E Tellarini Prieto, Thanuri Edirithilake, Ryan Merkl, Igor Moshynskyy, Elemir Simko, Sarah C Wood, Ellen Klinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotics have been widely used in North American beekeeping for the last 70 yr, contributing to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). European foulbrood (EFB) is a stress-associated, reportedly self-limiting, bacterial disease of honey bee larvae, caused by Melissococcus plutonius (ex-White 1912) Bailey and Collins 1983 (Lactobacillales: Enterococcaceae). Although oxytetracycline (OTC) is the only antibiotic approved for EFB, its continued use in North America requires evidence to justify it, given concerns about AMR despite no confirmed resistance in M. plutonius. Moreover, if AMR emerges in M. plutonius, the efficacy of alternative antimicrobials for treatment of EFB should be determined, such as tylosin (TYL) and lincomycin (LMC), which are approved in North America for managing American foulbrood. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of OTC, TYL, and LMC in treating colonies affected by a natural outbreak of EFB during spring 2024 in Saskatchewan, Canada. Forty-eight infected colonies were split into 4 groups of 12: 1 sham-treated and 3 treated with either OTC, TYL, or LMC per label instruction. Colony strength and EFB symptoms were monitored over 1 mo, and M. plutonius bacterial load was quantified via Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) pretreatment, 1- and 4-wk posttreatment. We found that all antimicrobial-treated colonies had significant improvement in colony strength relative to pretreatment. Adult bees from antimicrobial-treated groups also had a significant reduction in bacterial load 1-mo posttreatment. However, we also observed a spontaneous recovery in sham-treated colonies. Our findings support the clinical efficacy of OTC and highlight the potential of TYL and LMC as effective alternatives for managing EFB.Keywords: honey bee, Melissococcus plutonius, antimicrobial, colony strength, alternative treatment

Introduction

Since 2018 in Canada, and 2017 in United States, veterinarians have become responsible for oversight of antimicrobial use in the beekeeping industry (CMIB 2017, FDA 2017). Accordingly, to ensure good antimicrobial stewardship by veterinarians and beekeepers, it is crucial to provide clinical antimicrobial efficacy data to inform antimicrobial prescribing practices, considering that in many countries in European Union, New Zealand, Uruguay, and Chile, antimicrobial use in beekeeping is prohibited (Formato et al. 2021).

European foulbrood (EFB) is a bacterial brood disease of honey bees which is widely treated with oxytetracycline (OTC) in North America (Richards et al. 2021); however, EFB has historically been regarded as a self‑limiting disease, as colonies may exhibit spontaneous clinical recovery without antimicrobial intervention (Forsgren 2010) which reflects the stress and environment condition‑dependent nature of Melissococcus plutonius (ex-White 1912) Bailey and Collins 1983 (Lactobacillales: Enterococcaceae) infections, which are influenced by nutritional status, brood rearing dynamics, and colony strength. Since M. plutonius is a non‑spore‑forming bacterium, reductions in larval susceptibility and increased survival to adulthood leading to increased availability of nursing bees can temporarily suppress disease expression. Therefore, self-limiting nature of the disease necessitates further evidence to justify ongoing antimicrobial management of this disease. EFB results from oral transmission of the bacterium M. plutonius to honey bee larvae, resulting in larval starvation and death under conditions of colony stress, such as reduced colony nutrition and brood care (Aupperle-Lellbach et al. 2018, Forsgren 2010, Von Büren et al. 2019). Clinical signs of the disease include twisted or contorted larvae with yellowish brown color, uneven brood pattern, and foul odor (Forsgren et al. 2005). Reduced brood survival lowers adult bee populations, leading to decreased honey production and pollination services (Groeneveld et al. 2024), and causing significant economic losses for beekeepers estimated at $0.25 to 35 million annually in Alberta due to equipment replacement or destruction (Laate et al. 2020). Proposed reservoirs of this disease include adult bees (Jose et al. 2025) and equipment (Fowler et al. 2025). These potential persistent sources of infection necessitate an integrated pest management (IPM) approach to EFB management.

Recommended IPM strategies for EFB focus on reducing pathogen load and improving colony resilience. Key practices include regularly replacing brood frames, using the shook swarm method to transfer bees into clean equipment (Waite et al. 2003) and implementing queen caging or re-queening to interrupt brood cycles and strengthen colony health (Mosca et al. 2024), thorough disinfection of all beekeeping equipment is essential to prevent contamination (Ohashi et al. 2020), while provisioning supplemental nutrition supports overall colony health. In critical circumstances, complete destruction of infected colonies and equipment by incineration may be necessary (Mueller et al. 2020). Notably, EFB is considered a self-limiting disease under certain conditions, as colonies can sometimes recover without intervention, particularly when there is sufficient honey and pollen flow (Roetschi et al. 2008, Haynes et al. 2013). Additionally, as part of an IPM approach, OTC is often used as a therapeutic measure (Thompson et al. 2005, Masood et al. 2022, Mosca et al. 2024).

Despite continued OTC use, there are only a few reports of phenotypic OTC resistant strains of M. plutonius (Waite et al. 2003, Burns 2021, Masood et al. 2022, Takamatsu et al. 2023). Interpretation of these reports, however, is complicated by the absence of a standardized methodology for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination in M. plutonius, which raises uncertainty regarding the classification of phenotypic resistance (Masood et al. 2022, Takamatsu et al. 2023). Based on Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI 2010) guidelines applied to related bacterial taxa, M. plutonius isolates with MIC values ≤ 2.5 µg/ml are generally considered susceptible to OTC. However, no species‑specific clinical breakpoints or formal definitions of OTC resistance have been established for M. plutonius, and the genetic determinants underlying OTC resistance in this species remain uncharacterized. Consequently, MIC values for M. plutonius should be interpreted primarily in a comparative context among isolates or treatments, rather than as definitive indicators of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Moreover, considering that OTC is a bacteriostatic antimicrobial, in that it prevents the multiplication of M. plutonius, but does not kill M. plutonius (Jones and Morrison 1962), even M. plutonius strains which are sensitive to OTC may persist in the hive after OTC treatment. This is consistent with clinical observations of Budge et al. (2014) who documented the presence of M. plutonius in adult bees from colonies routinely treated with OTC in England and Wales.

Considering the emergence of genotypic and phenotypic OTC-resistance in another bacterial foulbrood pathogen of honey bees, Paenibacillus larvae (Consignado 2010, Beims et al. 2015, Obshta et al. 2023), the potential for dissemination of similar AMR among M. plutonius populations remains, necessitating the exploration of antimicrobial alternatives for EFB treatment. Tylosin (TYL) and lincomycin (LMC) belong to the macrolide and lincosamide classes of bacteriostatic antibiotics (Fish and Cundliffe 1996, Kumura et al. 2017), respectively, and are approved in North America for managing American foulbrood. Both have demonstrated efficacy for in vitro treatment of M. plutonius infected larvae (Masood et al. 2022); however, their effectiveness at the colony level remains to be investigated.

To determine the therapeutic efficacy of approved and alternative antimicrobials for treatment of colonies affected by clinical EFB disease, herein we describe a controlled clinical trial involving antimicrobial treatment of an outbreak of EFB among 48 nucleus honey bee colonies in Saskatchewan, Canada. Over 4 wk of time period, we evaluated the effect of sham, OTC, TYL, or LMC treatment on (i) colony strength, (ii) clinical signs of EFB, and (iii) M. plutonius bacterial load in larva and adult bees.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

On May 2024, we investigated an EFB outbreak in a commercial beekeeping operation in Saskatchewan, Canada, involving 48—6 frame—nucleus honey bee colonies. One week after diagnosis, the colonies were relocated to 3 apiaries, approximately 4 km apart, with 16 colonies placed at each location.

Study Design

We conducted a controlled clinical trial in June–July 2024 to evaluate the efficacy of an approved antimicrobial treatment (OTC) and 2 alternative treatments (TYL and LMC) for a naturally occurring outbreak of EFB in nucleus honey bee colonies distributed across 3 locations (Fig. 1). After colonies were placed into the 3 yards (12 colonies per yard), they were stratified according to colony strength ranging from highest to lowest score within each yard and then randomly assigned to one of 4 treatment groups: sham, OTC, TYL, or LMC, with 4 colonies per treatment per yard (12 colonies per treatment in total). Additionally, colony strength scores (described below; Supplementary Table S2) did not differ significantly among yards (Supplementary Table S3). Colonies were assessed for clinical signs and colony strength at pretreatment and at 1-, 2-, and 4-wk posttreatment. Larvae and adult bees were also sampled for M. plutonius bacterial load using Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) at pretreatment, 1-wk, and 4-wk posttreatment.

Study design to investigate the effect of antibiotic treatment on clinical EFB. A total of 48 colonies were distributed across 3 apiary yards (n = 16 colonies per yard) and assigned to 1 of 4 treatments: sham (control), OTC, TYL, or LMC. Sham and OTC treatments were administered at 4-d intervals, whereas TYL and LMC treatments were administered at 6-d intervals. Pretreatment assessments were conducted on day 0 (D0). Posttreatment colony assessments were conducted at 1 and 4 wk following treatment: days 17 and 27 for sham and OTC groups, and days 21 and 37 for TYL and LMC groups.

Diagnosis of EFB

In May 2024, a commercial beekeeper, in consultation with a government apiculturist, reported clinical EFB in 48 nucleus honey bee colonies, based on visual clinical signs including twisted or contorted larvae with yellowish brown color, uneven brood pattern, and foul odor. At the time of the initial diagnosis, the operation contained 48 nucleus colonies, all located within a single yard. Additionally, we also collected 3/larvae and 20 adult bees from 6 out of 48 colonies for initial diagnosis by qPCR to confirm the presence of M. plutonius.

According to the beekeeper and our own observations, clinical signs of EFB were confined to this yard; no additional colonies outside this location were reported to be symptomatic. All 48 colonies in the affected yard were examined at the onset of the study. Among these, 28 colonies exhibited visible EFB like symptoms (sick larvae) at the beginning of the experiment. During the course of the study, 9 of these symptomatic colonies died, and because they could not be followed through the full experimental period, they were excluded from the data analysis.

With respect to pathogen detection by qPCR for M. plutonius in larvae and adult bee samples, larvae from 24 colonies and adult bees from 28 colonies were positive for M. plutonius. Up on diagnosis of EFB the colonies were separated from the main operation and relocated to 3 quarantine yards according to The Apiaries Act (2005). After the relocation colonies were randomly distributed across 3 yards; these yards contained only the colonies involved in the study (ie those originating from the original 48 colony yard). Not all colonies were clinically infected: While 28 showed visible symptoms initially, the remainder displayed no clinical signs at the time of diagnosis, although some were qPCR positive.

Appropriate biosecurity measures were implemented during hive inspections and sampling. Hive tools were disinfected between colonies using appropriate sanitization procedures, disposable gloves were changed between hives, and inspections were conducted in a manner that minimized cross‑contamination between treatment groups. Colonies were managed individually, and no equipment, frames, or hive components were shared among hives during the study period.

Antimicrobial Treatments

Colonies were treated with either sham control, OTC at the label dose for EFB, or TYL and LMC at the label dose recommended for AFB disease. In our study, TYL and LMC treatment regimens used were adapted from the AFB label directions (for dose preparation and frequency) solely as a standardized reference because no EFB label exists. The treatments were applied over top bars at the back of brood chamber. For sham treatment, 32 g of icing sugar (Rogers Lantic Inc. Montréal, QC, Canada, Product No.20108812_EA) was applied 3 times at 4-d intervals. For OTC-treatment, 4 g of OXYTET 62.5 (Vita Bee Health Canada Ltd., Canada; Exp. 04–2025, Lot: 881514) was added to 35 g of icing powdered sugar and 32 g (228 mg of OTC/colony) of the mixture was applied 3 times at 4-d intervals. For TYL, 200 mg of Tylan Soluble (Elanco Canada Ltd., Canada; Code: 463070050, lot: AO410945, Exp. 01/2025) was mixed with 20 g of icing sugar and 20.2 g of the mixture was applied per colony (200 mg TYL/colony) per treatment. For LMC, 500 mg of Lincomix (Zoetis Canada Inc.; Exp. 11/2026, Lot: P3502-08) was mixed with 20 g of icing sugar and 20.5 g of the mixture (200 mg of LMC/colony) was applied per application. The differences in total sugar mass therefore reflect compliance with label-directed antimicrobial dosing.

Treatment frequency and the quantity of icing sugar applied differed among antimicrobials in accordance with label-recommended dosing instructions. Sham controls were matched to the oxytetracycline treatment to account for handling and application frequency, as oxytetracycline is the only antimicrobial labeled for EFB.

Colony Strength

The cluster size (number of inter frame spaces covered by bees) and the percentage of brood coverage were recorded pretreatment, and 1 and 4-wk posttreatment, and used to calculate an overall colony strength score, as described below.

Cluster Size

Assessments were made in the morning at temperatures below 15 °C to prevent flight of forager bees according to Nasr et al. (1990). Colonies were opened carefully without shaking frames or disturbing bees. Cluster size was estimated by manually counting the number of inter-frame spaces (open space between the vertical frames [combs] that are installed inside a beehive box) occupied by bees.

Inter-frame bee coverage (cluster size) was quantified using a point-based scoring system. Each inter-frame space completely covered by bees was assigned a score of 1. Spaces that were approximately half-covered or one-quarter covered were assigned scores of 0.5 and 0.25, respectively. The scores from all inter-frame spaces within a colony were summed to obtain the cluster size of the colony. All assessments were performed by a single observer blinded to treatment.

Brood Coverage

Brood coverage was done based on the protocol described by Wang et al. (2020) and Tellarini Prieto et al. (2024) by taking digital images of both sides of the brood frames in each colony using a 16.2-megapixel Nikon D7000 digital camera (Minato, Tokyo, Japan). Photos were taken using a photo box made of white corrugated plastic to hold the frames and protect them from ambient light. The camera was mounted on a tripod with an 18- to 105-mm lens together with a Nikon SU-800 wireless speedlight placed inside the photo box to ensure proper illumination of the brood frame. Total brood area was calculated from the photos using the Honey Bee Complete software (version 4.2, WSCS scientific GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) (Wang et al. 2020). Capped brood area was determined by the auto-recognition function, while the open brood area was calculated based on manual demarcation of open brood, followed by automatic determination of total brood area. For images with irregular brood patterns (brood cells mixed with other cell types, eg pollen, honey), the brood area was calculated by manually selecting each capped/open brood cell. The total brood area per each side of the frame was expressed as percentage, summed for all frames in the colony, and divided by 100, resulting in a number ranging from 1 to 12.

Clinical Signs of EFB

Clinical signs EFB were evaluated at 4 time points: pretreatment, and 1-, 2-, and 4-wk posttreatment. Assessments were performed by counting the number of visibly sick larvae (twisted or contorted larvae with yellowish brown color) within a 6.25-inch2 plastic square placed on the most affected brood frame in each colony (Delaplane et al. 2013b, Dussaubat et al. 2016). In addition to larval assessments, colonies were monitored for queen presence, egg-laying activity, and overall colony survival. Dead colonies were promptly sealed to prevent robbing and disease transmission.

Collection of Adult Bees and Larvae for M. plutonius qPCR

Twenty adult bees were collected from brood frames in each colony at 3 time points: pretreatment, 1-wk posttreatment, and 4-wk post-sham or antimicrobial treatment.

Pretreatment, 3 larvae showing clinical signs of EFB (yellow‑brown discoloration and a deflated appearance) were individually collected using sterile swabs from the most affected frames in each colony. If no visibly diseased larvae were present, 3 healthy‑appearing larvae were collected instead from the brood frame containing the greatest number of larvae. At 1- and 4-wk posttreatment, 9 larvae with clinical signs of EFB were collected individually (if there were no visibly diseased larvae present, 3 healthy-appearing larvae were collected instead from the brood frame) from the 2 most affected frames in each colony. All larval and adult bee samples were transported to the laboratory on ice and immediately stored at −20 °C.

Processing of Adult Bees and Larvae for DNA Isolation and qPCR

Adult Bees

Twenty adult bees per colony (48 colonies in total) were surface sterilized (70% ethanol and then 0.05% sodium hypochlorite) and homogenized in 20 ml sterile PBS. The homogenate was centrifuged for 5 min at 600 RCF; 100 µl-aliquots of the supernatant were later used for DNA extraction.

Larvae

Pretreatment, swabs from homogenized larva of 3 visually diseased larvae per colony were individually processed by adding 2 ml sterile PBS to each swab, vortexing for 30 s, and allowing debris to settle for 15 min. Of the supernatant, 1 ml was transferred to new tubes and stored at −20 °C. From each stored sample, 100 µl was subjected to DNA extraction and qPCR.

At 1- and 4-wk posttreatment, swabs from 9 sick larvae were processed as above. To create pooled samples, 100 µl from each of 3 individual larval swabs was combined, resulting in 3 pooled samples each representing 3 larvae. From each pool, 100 µl was subjected to DNA extraction and qPCR.

DNA Isolation from Adult Worker Bees and Larvae

DNA from adult bee homogenate and swabs from larva homogenates were extracted using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The DNA was eluted in 50 µl nuclease-free water and 2 µl of this DNA was used for qPCR detection of M. plutonius using the assay conditions described by Budge et al. (2010) and the corresponding 16s-rRNA-based primers and probes: EFBFor (TGT TGT TAG AGA AGA ATA GGG GAA), EFBRev2 (CGT GGC TTT CTG GTT AGA), and EFBProbe (FAM—AGA GTA ACT GTT TTC CTC GTG ACG GT—TAMRA). Real-time reactions were performed in triplicates using TaqMan chemistry and Multiplex qPCR reagent kits (QIAGEN, Germany). M. plutonius DNA was quantified by comparing it to a standard curve (Forsgren 2010). For qPCR quantification of M. plutonius, a standard curve was generated using bee and larval homogenates confirmed to be free of contamination. Pure cultures of M. plutonius were grown, quantified by CFU enumeration, and spiked into homogenates at a starting concentration of 107 CFU/ml, followed by 10-fold serial dilutions. DNA was extracted from each dilution, and qPCR was performed to obtain Ct values. A standard curve was constructed by plotting Ct values against the logarithm of CFU concentrations, enabling estimation of bacterial load in unknown samples based on their Ct values.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism (2025 GraphPad Software, version 10.0, Boston, United States). All tests were 2 tailed. Alpha was set at 0.05. We did not include dead colonies in any of our statistical analysis. Raw data and statistical analysis (multiple comparisons) are given in Supplementary Table S3.

Prior to analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and equality of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. All variables satisfied the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. At the beginning of experiment (pretreatment), to compare colony strength scores among treatment groups at the baseline, 1‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used. Subsequently, comparison of colony strength scores in different time points (pretreatment, 1-wk posttreatment, and 4-wk posttreatment) was analyzed with in each group using a 1‑way repeated measures ANOVA (based on Generalized Linear Model (GLM)) with time as a within-subject factor and treatment as a between-subject factor. Where the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Therefore, the reported F-statistics use adjusted (non-integer) degrees of freedom to reduce Type I error rates. Furthermore, Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was applied for post hoc analyses, using pretreatment values as the reference. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a significance level of P < 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Differences in the number of sick larvae were evaluated by comparing pretreatment values with posttreatment time points (1 and 4 wk) within each treatment group. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated that the assumptions of normality were not met. Consequently, nonparametric statistical methods were applied. Within each treatment group, repeated measures across time were analyzed using the Friedman test. When a significant overall effect was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Results are presented as median ± interquartile range (IQR), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

To evaluate the effects of antibiotic treatments on M. plutonius bacterial load in adult bees (CFU/bee) and larvae (CFU/larva), data distributions were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. As assumptions of normality were violated, results are expressed as medians ± IQR. Treatment effects were analyzed using a 2‑way repeated‑measures design for CFU/bee and mixed-model for CFU/larvae with time (D0, 1 wk, 4 wk) as a within‑subject factor and treatment as a between‑subject factor. Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied due to sphericity violations, and inference focused on the treatment × time interaction. Results were corroborated using distribution‑robust model‑based analyses. Multiple comparisons were done by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

Results

Effect of Antimicrobial Treatment on Colony Strength

Over the period of study, we lost 9 colonies (3 colonies in sham treatment, 4 colonies in OTC treatment, and 2 colonies in LMC treatment). Antimicrobial treatment significantly increased colony strength score (2.8 ± 1.1) in 4-wk posttreatment compared to pretreatment (Fig. 2). Sham-treated control colonies also increased in strength score over time, by 1.9 ± 1.2 at 4-wk posttreatment compared to pretreatment; however, this increase was nonsignificant (F(1.57, 12.53) = 2.23, P = 0.15). Pretreatment mean colony strength score (6.7 ± 1.2) was similar across all treatment groups. In the OTC treatment group, a significant increase in colony strength score (2.9 ± 0.7 [F(1.9, 13.6) = 7.6, P = 0.006]) was observed at 4-wk posttreatment compared to pretreatment; however, the colony strength in 1-wk posttreatment was nonsignificantly increased by 0.9 ± 1 (P = 0.06) compared to pretreatment. For the TYL-treated colonies, the significant (F(1.4, 16) = 20.5) colony strength score increase was 2.2 ± 2 at 1-wk (P = 0.006) and 3 ± 1.8 at 4-wk posttreatment (P < 0.0004). Similarly, the LMC-treated colonies showed a significant increase (F(1.6, 14.7) = 9.9) of 2.2 ± 0.3 in 1-wk (P = 0.04) and 2.7 ±0. 3 at 4-wk posttreatment (P = 0.005).

Effect of Antimicrobials on Clinical Signs of EFB

There was no significant difference in visual clinical signs of EFB (number of sick larvae) over time in both control and antimicrobial-treated colonies (Fig. 3A and B). Relative to pretreatment, antimicrobial-treated colonies had a decrease of 62 (OTC), 42 (TYL), and 30 (LMC) percentage of colonies with visually diseased larvae while the sham-treated control had an increase (11%) in proportion of colonies with visually diseased larvae (Fig. 3B).

Effect of Antimicrobial Treatment on Bacterial Load of Adult Bees and Larvae

Relative to pretreatment, at 1- and 4-wk posttreatment, there was no significant difference in bacterial load (F(1.644, 87.12) = 1.768) of larvae from sham-treated control colonies or antimicrobial-treated colonies (Fig. 4). However, relative to pretreatment, adult bees from antimicrobial-treated colonies showed a significant (F(1.524, 44.20) = 24.14, P ≤ 0.001), on average 3.2 to 3.8-log10 fold decrease in M. plutonius CFU/bee at 4-wk posttreatment, while no significant difference was observed in the bacterial load of adult bees from sham-treated controls (Fig. 5).

Discussion

We investigated the effectiveness of OTC, TYL, and LMC for treatment of EFB in honey bee nucleus colonies during a natural outbreak in spring 2024 in Saskatchewan, Canada. All 3 antibiotic treatments were associated with a significant increase in colony strength (Fig. 2) and a significant decrease in M. plutonius colonization of adult bees (Fig. 5). Sham-treated control colonies also increased in strength over time; however, the trend was nonsignificant. Moreover, M. plutonius bacterial load in adult bees from control colonies did not differ significantly over time.

We found that antimicrobial treatment significantly improved colony strength score by 2.7 to 3 in 4-wk posttreatment (Fig. 1), while control colonies experienced a nonsignificant increase in colony strength score (1.9 ± 1.2) by 4-wk posttreatment. Interestingly, colonies treated with TYL and LMC showed significant improvement in colony strength as early as 1-wk posttreatment. Nevertheless, 4-wk posttreatment, colony strength was similar between the antimicrobial and sham-treatment groups, indicating that colonies recovered their strength over time regardless of antimicrobial treatment. The positive effect of antimicrobial treatment on colony growth that we observed in this study is consistent with previous studies demonstrating efficacy of OTC, TYL, and LMC at improving survival of larvae (Masood et al. 2022) and adult bees (Jose et al. 2025) infected with M. plutonius in vitro. Additionally, our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that OTC, when combined with partial shook swarm management, improves colony strength by reducing disease severity (Mosca et al. 2023). Although some studies have suggested that antimicrobial treatment may impair the honey bee gut microbiome and social behavior, potentially leading to reduced colony strength over time (Ortiz-Alvarado et al. 2020), we did not follow the colonies in this study beyond 4-wk posttreatment, so we cannot comment on potential long-term effects of antimicrobial therapy on colony performance.

In contrast to the positive effect of antimicrobial treatment on colony strength, we did not observe a significant effect of antimicrobial treatment on number of larvae with visual signs of clinical disease. One explanation for the lack of a treatment effect is that we stratified colonies into treatment groups based on colony strength, rather than clinical signs of disease, which may have led to treatment groups having an unequal distribution of colonies with visually diseased larvae. Moreover, pretreatment, the colonies had on average only 10 ± 9 number of visually disease larvae per 6.25-inch2 area of worst-affected brood frame, limiting the ability to demonstrate a significant clinical improvement posttreatment.

Although we did not observe a significant improvement in clinical signs in this study, antimicrobial treatment was associated with a significant decrease in M. plutonius bacterial load in adult bees (Fig. 5). This observation aligns with previous findings (Jose et al. 2025), which reported a 96% to 99% decrease in bacterial load (CFU/bee) following antimicrobial treatment in adult bees. Considering that asymptomatic adult bees colonized with M. plutonius are a potential reservoir for EFB, our study suggests that antimicrobial treatment may be beneficial in decreasing the risk of future EFB outbreaks. However, antimicrobial treatment was not associated with a decline in M. plutonius colonization of honey bee larvae (Fig. 4), suggesting that brood may continue to be a source of M. plutonius contamination in the colony. Decreased availability of the antibiotic to the colony’s larvae relative to the adult bees in the colony may explain the absence of a posttreatment decline in M. plutonius colonization of the larvae. Moreover, considering the bacteriostatic, rather than bactericidal, nature of the 3-antimicrobial tested, it may not be surprising that M. plutonius colonization of both adult bees and larvae was still detected at 1- and 4-wk posttreatment in all experimental groups except for the adult bees sampled LMC-treated colonies at 4-wk posttreatment. Our observations align with Budge et al. (2010), who reported that adult bees from OTC-treated colonies continued to carry M. plutonius, although with significantly reduced bacterial loads. Even though infected larvae play a vital role in intra-colony transmission and pathogen persistence, adult worker bees are increasingly recognized as an important reservoir for M. plutonius, (Fowler et al. 2025, Jose et al. 2025). Current evidence indicates that M. plutonius replicates extensively in the gut of young larvae, constituting the primary amplification stage within the colony (Bailey 1960, Forsgren et al. 2013, Grossar et al. 2020). Notably, even after infected larvae are removed, M. plutonius can persist on brood comb, in larval feces, and within hive food stores, facilitating repeated cycles of reinfection and seasonal disease recurrence (Mueller et al. 2020, Fowler et al. 2025). Recent work demonstrates that antimicrobial treatment significantly reduces bacterial loads in adult bees but fails to fully eliminate M. plutonius, consistent with the role of adults as persistent carriers. In contrast, larval bacterial loads may remain unchanged following treatment due to continual exposure from contaminated comb and brood food, coupled with rapid bacterial replication within larvae. Together, these findings highlight that both larvae and adult bees may contribute intra-colony transmission, underscoring the importance of integrating management strategies.

One weakness of this study is that the lack of consistent infection across all colonies since this was a “natural outbreak.” Following initial visual diagnosis by commercial beekeeper, in consultation with a government apiculturist in May 2024, only 6 out of 48 colonies were selected for qPCR to confirm the presence of disease (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, 19/48 of the colonies had no visually diseased larvae at the start of the study in June 2024, although M. plutonius was detected by qPCR in 39 colonies pretreatment. Thus, it is possible that some of the colonies were in recovery prior to initiation of antimicrobial treatment, influencing our results. This is the first stratified and controlled field trial demonstrating efficacy of both approved and non-approved antimicrobials to assist in recovery of honey bee colonies from EFB by significantly improving colony strength and reducing bacterial load of adult bees. These observations support continued, judicious prescribing of antimicrobials by veterinarians for colonies with EFB in North America; however, our observation that both control and antimicrobial-treated colonies achieved similar colony strength posttreatment suggests that spontaneous recovery (a reduction in visibly diseased larvae, fewer larvae showing clinical signs of EFB, and increase in colony strength in untreated control colonies over time) from EFB is also possible, without beekeeper or veterinary intervention. Importantly, this study did not evaluate the efficacy of other cultural control strategies to reduce colony stress and improve colony health, which may be equivalent or superior to antimicrobial treatment for EFB management. Therefore, comparison of cultural control methods to antimicrobial treatment is an important future direction. Moreover, future clinical trials involving antimicrobial treatment of EFB should follow colonies over a year or more posttreatment to understand the effect of antimicrobials on disease recurrence, honey production, and overwinter survival.
