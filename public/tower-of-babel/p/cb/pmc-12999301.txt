Solitary intracranial dural-based Ewing’s sarcoma in a pregnant female: A case report
Authors: Aymen Shahab, Aruba Nawaz Khattak, Khadeeja Anwar, Awais Mustafa, Shahroze Ahmed, Kashif Siddique, Usman Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEwing’s sarcoma (ES) is a rare and highly malignant tumor characterized by small round blue cells, typically originating from bones and soft tissues in children and young adults, although it is rarely found as a primary intracranial lesion. We presented a case of a 26-year-old primigravida presenting with headache, vomiting, blurred vision, papilledema, and abducens nerve palsy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan revealed a large extra-axial cystic-solid mass located in the occipitoparietal region, which resembled an atypical meningioma. Surgical excision was carried out, and histopathological examination, along with immunohistochemistry, confirmed the diagnosis of ES, which tested positive for CD99, NKX2.2, and EWSR1 gene. Whole-body imaging excluded any extracranial primary sites or metastasis, confirming the presence of a solitary primary intracranial ES. After the operation, the patient was treated with vincristine-doxorubicin-cyclophosphamide and ifosfamide-etoposide chemotherapy, which resulted in a partial radiological response. This case illustrates the diagnostic difficulties associated with intracranial ES due to its radiological similarity to meningioma and stresses the importance of immunohistochemical and genetic analysis for a conclusive diagnosis. Early multimodal management is vital for improving survival in such rare cases.Keywords: Extraosseous, Dural-based lesion, Occipitoparietal mass, Papilledema, Multimodal treatment

Introduction

Ewing’s sarcoma (ES) belongs to a group of round blue cell malignant tumors arising mostly from bones and soft tissues, involving children and adolescents, constituting 10-15% of all sarcomas. ES has both osseous and extraosseous origins [1]. The usual sites of origin of ES are lower limbs (45%), pelvis (20%), upper limbs (13%), ribs (13%), and skull (2%) [2]. Extraosseous ES is rare and arises from the axial locations, such as the paravertebral or epidural space. ES has been rarely reported to involve the central nervous system (CNS) primarily. Primary ES affecting the CNS is usually intraparenchymal with supratentorial location or in the spinal cord [3]. Early and precise diagnosis of ES is essential to enable the adoption of a multimodal treatment strategy, including surgery and/or focal radiotherapy, in addition to systemic chemotherapy as soon as feasible, thereby preventing the distant metastases associated with ES. Typically, ES presents in the second decade of life, and achieving a long-term survival poses a significant challenge in management [4]. We presented a case of a solitary intracranial dural-based ES originating from the occipitoparietal lobe in a pregnant female with complaints of vertigo and impaired vision.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old pregnant female of 12 weeks presented with complaints of headache, vomiting, and blurred right eye vision for 1 month. The past medical and family history of the patient was non-significant. On clinical examination, the patient was having a lethargic appearance, bilateral papilledema, and abducens nerve palsy. Hematological investigations were unremarkable (Table 1). The patient was suspected of having raised intracranial pressure, and neurosurgical evaluation was planned.

Computed tomography (CT) could not be performed due to the patient’s pregnancy; therefore, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain without contrast was performed, revealing an extra-axial solid cystic mass measuring 65 × 38 mm in the left occipitoparietal lobe, causing a mass effect on the underlying parenchyma and ventricular system, resulting in a 9 mm midline shift. Corresponding diffusion weighted image (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) revealed diffusion restriction of the solid component of the lesion, indicating its high cellularity. Susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) showed internal flow voids indicating vascularity (Fig. 1). The provisional diagnosis was atypical meningioma or hemangiopericytoma, attributed to the extra-axial location of the lesion. Surgical excision of the lesion was advised, and magnetic resonance venography (MRV) with the time-of-flight (TOF) technique was performed for surgical planning. On MRV, compression of the left transverse sinus was found with resultant collateral formation (Fig. 2). After a 5-week interval from presentation, an obstetric ultrasound was performed, showing a 17-week alive intrauterine pregnancy (Fig. 3).

The STEALTH AXIEM navigated a left occipital craniotomy, and total resection of the tumor was performed at the 5th week of presentation. The tumor cyst, along with the lesion, was drained during the surgery, and the tumor was debulked in stages.

On histopathological examination, small round tumor cells with scant cytoplasm and round, hyperchromatic nuclei were found, arranged in sheets, along with areas of necrosis. The specimen was positive for the ESWR1 gene and tumor markers CD99 and NKX2.2 (Fig. 4). The findings were consistent with Ewing sarcoma.

(A) Hematoxylin and Eosin (H & E) 10X slide showing solid areas of uniform undifferentiated round cells with indistinct cell borders; (B) H & E 20X slide showing Homer Wright rosettes consisting of a central fibrillar core surrounded by concentrically arranged tumor cells; (C) CD99 showing strong, diffuse membranous staining in the tumor cells; (D) NKX2.2 showing strong nuclear staining in the tumor cells.

Post-operatively, an MRI brain without contrast was repeated, and demonstrated left occipital craniotomy changes with residual disease in the left occipital surgical bed. MRI of the spine, both upper and lower limbs, was alsoperformed to locate the primary site of the disease; however, the limb MRIs were unremarkable. A CT chest with abdominal shielding was also performed, and no pulmonary metastasis was found. Therefore, the intracranial solitary tumor was diagnosed as the primary Ewing’s sarcoma. The chemotherapy was planned for the management of the residual disease, and the patient’s pregnancy was terminated at 25 weeks (13 weeks from in-hospital presentation) after a thorough explanation of the disease prognosis with the patient and her partner.

After termination of pregnancy, positron emission tomography (PET/CT) of the whole body was performed, concluding ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) left occipital lesion, indicating residual disease. The rest of the brain parenchyma was normal, and no FDG-avid disease was noted elsewhere in the body (Fig. 5). Chemotherapy treatment was started for the patient. Patient was given 3 cycles of both combinations, vincristine-doxorubicin-cyclophosphamide (VDC) and ifosfamide-etoposide (IE). Post-chemotherapy, an MRI brain with contrast was carried out, with the findings of an interval decrease in the solid and cystic component of the lesion with extension into the tentorium cerebelli, and falx cereberi in the posterior occipital region, indicating an interval treatment response to chemotherapy (Fig. 6).

Discussion

ES was first described by James Ewing in 1921 as a highly malignant bone tumor, accounting for 10% of all bone tumors [5]. Primary cranial involvement of ES is rare, with an estimated incidence of 1%. ES is a neuroectodermal tumor and is predominantly observed in children and young adults, with the highest incidence noted during the second decade of life. It is classified as the second most prevalent type of pediatric bone tumor and exhibits no preference for any gender [6]. ES is predominantly located in the long bones and the trunk. Osseous ES affecting the calvarium usually targets the frontal and parietal convexities. Less frequently, lesions originate from the squamous and petrous portions of the temporal bone. Although these lesions can infiltrate adjacent structures such as the dura and brain parenchyma, most predominantly reside in the epidural space and manifest symptoms due to their mass effect [7]. Extraosseous ES has been reported in locations including the anterior, middle, and posterior fossae, the tentorium, the cavernous sinus, and the cerebellopontine angle [8]. The reported patient was a primigravida young female in her third decade of life during the first trimester originating from the left occipitoparietal region. Bansal et al. reported a primigravida female in her third decade of life with a dural-based ES in the left-occipitoparietal region [9].

Primary intracranial ES typically manifests with symptoms of intracranial hypertension, such as headaches, vomiting, or seizures, along with alterations in consciousness. Headache is the most common symptom, with papilledema as the most common objective sign. Motor deficits, as well as less common visual disturbances and endocrine irregularities, may arise depending on the location [3]. Dural-based ES could present with intracranial hemorrhage in about 50% cases [10]. In this case, the patient also presented with symptoms of headache, vomiting, and blurred vision, along with findings of bilateral papilledema and cranial nerve palsy, indicating the compression symptoms of the mass at its site of origin.

Early diagnosis of ES incorporates radiologic, histopathological, and immunohistochemical data for achieving the best possible clinical outcomes. Primary intracranial ES had two patterns of involvement of meninges, either as a diffuse involvement of leptomeninges without any intraparenchymal mass or as a primary dural-based mass resembling meningioma, as in this case [11]. ES rarely presents as a primary dural-based neoplasm and radiologically resembles a meningioma. The clinical characteristics could lead to a preoperative diagnosis of meningiomas. Meningiomas represent the most prevalent dural-based tumor, constituting roughly 20%-30% of all primary intracranial tumors. They are primarily benign and can be effectively treated through surgical resection alone. Conversely, ES is an uncommon aggressive intracranial tumor that requires a multimodal approach to treatment. The differentiation between these two tumors is crucial for prognostic and therapeutic reasons, as a misdiagnosis can lead to unnecessary delays in the appropriate treatment, under the false impression of meningioma, which can adversely affect patient survival [12]. These tumors usually present as iso-to-hypointense on T1-weighted sequences and demonstrate varying intensity when compared to grey matter on T2-weighted sequences. The involvement of soft tissues and the pressure on surrounding neurovascular structures can be identified using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Cystic or hemorrhagic components within the lesion can also be present [13]. As in this scenario, a solid cystic mass with a compression effect on the surrounding parenchyma and ventricles was observed.

Light microscopy generally shows a non-specific structure of nests or sheets composed of uniform, primitive, round cells with minimal cytoplasm. The differential diagnosis for small blue round cell tumors is extensive; thus, additional tests as immunohistochemical staining, are necessary [8]. CD99 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that is critical in the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells and is a highly sensitive biomarker for primary intracranial ES. However, it is not a specific marker, since high expression of CD99 can also be seen in hemangiopericytoma, lymphoblastic lymphoma, sex-cord stromal tumors, rhabdomyosarcoma, and malignant glioma [14]. NKX2.2, a transcription factor characterized by a homeodomain, has been identified as a target of EWS-FLI-1, the fusion protein linked to Ewing sarcoma. Array-based gene expression analysis has shown that it is differentially upregulated in Ewing sarcoma. This marker is crucial for Ewing sarcoma, demonstrating a sensitivity of 93% and a specificity of 89% [15]. EWSR1 gene rearrangement is regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing ES. The chromosomal translocation t(11;22)(q24;q12) is the most common genetic anomaly and is present in 85%-90% of all ES [16]. The histopathological specimen in this case report was positive for CD99, NKX2.2, and EWSR1 gene.

Due to the rare incidence of primary intracranial ES, definitive treatment guidelines have yet to be established. However, based on the available evidence, the gross total resection with broad margins is recommended to minimize the risk of local recurrence. Regardless of the location, the main surgical objective is to accomplish total excision, as this is a crucial element for long-term survival. The affected bone must also be removed [17]. Surgical resection is essential for the definitive diagnosis and relief of decompression symptoms [3]. The initial chemotherapy combinations for ES are VDC. Furthermore, the addition of IE could raise survival in non-metastatic ES. The use of chemotherapy for ES has resulted in an improvement of long-term survival rates to 70%-80% [18]. Radiotherapy could also improve the median survival time [19]. The patient also had adjuvant therapy after surgical resection with VDC and IE, resulting in an interval treatment response.

Although ES primarily originates from long bones and the pelvis tends to metastasize, however, metastases from primary intracranial ES are rare. It could be attributed to early diagnosis and treatment [8]. Similarly, in this case report, no metastasis was seen on whole body PET/CT. ES in the CNS has a survival duration of 6 months to 3 years. Before the advent of chemotherapy, the sarcoma patients had a survival rate of 10%. However, with the use of radical resection, chemotherapy, and radiation, achieving long-term remission is frequently observed [20].

Conclusion

Primary intracranial dural-based ES is an extremely rare and aggressive malignancy that can closely resemble meningioma both in clinical presentation and radiological findings, thus creating considerable diagnostic difficulties. Early detection through histopathological examination, immunohistochemical analysis, and molecular markers, such as CD99, NKX2.2, and EWSR1 gene rearrangement, is crucial for accurate diagnosis and timely intervention. This case illustrates the necessity of a multimodal treatment plan including surgical excision, systemic chemotherapy, and the potential incorporation of radiotherapy to achieve the best outcomes.
