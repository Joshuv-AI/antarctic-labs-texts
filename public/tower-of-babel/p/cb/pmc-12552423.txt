LorBin: efficient binning of long-read metagenomes by multiscale adaptive clustering and evaluation
Authors: Wei Xue, Zuo Liu, Yaozhong Zhang, Waseem Raza, Yarong Li, Li Jiang, Ye Tao, Jun Qian, Jousset Alexandre, Fang-Jie Zhao, Yangchun Xu, Fritz Sedlazeck, Qirong Shen, Gaofei Jiang, Zhong Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-read sequencing has transformed metagenomics and improved the quality of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). However, current binning methods struggle with identifying unknown species and managing imbalanced species distributions. Here, we present LorBin, an unsupervised binner specially designed to reconstruct MAGs in natural microbiomes. LorBin deploys a two-stage multiscale adaptive DBSCAN and BIRCH clustering with evaluation decision models using single-copy genes to maximize MAG recovery. LorBin outperforms six competing binners in both simulated and real microbiomes, including oral, gut, and marine samples. LorBin generated 15–189% more high-quality MAGs with high serendipity and identified 2.4–17 times more novel taxa than state-of-the-art binning methods. Together, LorBin is a promising long-read metagenomic binner for accessing species-rich samples containing unknown taxa and is efficient at retrieving more complete genomes from imbalanced natural microbiomes.Subject terms: Metagenomics, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Metagenomics has transformed our understanding of microbial communities by shedding light on uncultivable species1. Recently, long-read technologies have revolutionized metagenomics by enabling robust contig assembly, which is essential for the reconstruction of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and accurate taxonomic profiling2. Long-read sequencing enables the tracking of microbial ecological and evolutionary processes at an unprecedented resolution. However, this potential remains limited by the lack of dedicated computational tools, with binning forming the major bottleneck3. Metagenomic binning aims to group DNA fragments (e.g., contigs) originating from the same or closely related genomes into distinct bins4. Accurate binning is critical for reconstructing the microbial genomes within the microbiota and exploring their functional capabilities4. In addition, high-quality binning is essential for downstream analyses, such as microbial community structure and function analysis, and the discovery of novel species5.

Current metagenomic binners struggle to handle long-read data. The continuity and rich information of long reads enable the assembly of low-abundance genomes and improve the assembly to be more continuous with fewer stitching errors. These factors make existing short-read binners unsuitable for long-read binning due to the differences in the properties of assemblies between short- and long-read sequencing. The most advanced binning tools improve the quality of binning by optimizing the critical steps of feature extraction and contig clustering. For instance, VAMB first employs a deep variational autoencoder to encode the k-mer and abundance features6 and was improved by AAMB via an adversarial autoencoder7. SemiBin8 uses a siamese neural network with a pretrained model, whereas COMEBin9 applies data augmentation and contrastive multi-view representation learning to embed contigs. SemiBin210 utilized self-supervised contrastive learning to extract feature embeddings from short-read contigs and was extended to long-read data by incorporating a DBSCAN clustering algorithm. These methods span from encoders6,8,10,11 to algorithms of clustering9,10,12,13 or their integrated models14–16.

Existing binners remain susceptible to biases associated with the intrinsic attributes in natural microbiomes. The process of metagenomic binning is challenged by the prior uncertainty of the species present and their richness and diversity (species complexity), limited genome references (unknown taxa), strain-level variations, and genomic plasticity (intraspecies heterogeneity)17. In addition, other natural attributes of microbial ecosystems, such as imbalanced species distributions (the low abundance of most species), may distort bins and cause incorrect contig assignments. Microbiomes are typically composed of a few dominant species coexisting with thousands of rare species. These rare species play crucial roles in ecosystem functioning18 but make metagenomic data unbalanced, increasing the difficulty of binning. To date, no tools adequately address the above specific challenges. Improving the entire de novo binning process may be the key to achieving efficient long-read binning.

To address these challenges, we present LorBin, an unsupervised deep-learning tool for long-read binning of host-associated and free-living microbial communities. LorBin is designed to solve the challenges of computational efficiency and identify unknown species in public databases, rare microbiomes, and imbalanced species abundance. The LorBin architecture is built in three blocks: (1) a self-supervised variational autoencoder (VAE) to handle unknown taxa and to extract embedded features of hyper-long contigs; (2) a two-stage clustering of multiscale adaptive DBSCAN19 and BIRCH20 algorithms oriented to complex species distributions; and (3) an assessment-decision model for reclustering to improve the confidence of quality control and the number of complete MAGs.

In this work, we demonstrate that LorBin offers a robust approach for handling imbalanced microbial distributions and reconstructing novel species genomes during long-read binning. LorBin consistently outperforms six state-of-the-art (SOTA) binning methods, SemiBin8, SemiBin210, VAMB6, AAMB7, MetaBAT221, and COMEBin9, for recovering high-quality bins across a range of simulated and experimental microbiome data. LorBin consistently retrieves the shared MAGs as competing binners plus a large set of unique MAGs covering diverse main phyla. LorBin particularly excels in biodiverse environments with complex compositions of microbial species, as well as in samples with limited prior knowledge about the species present, such as nonhuman gut or marine environments.

Results

Overview of LorBin

LorBin is a deep learning approach for the metagenomic binning of long-read assembled contigs. The method computes the abundance and k-mer frequencies of each contig to extract their embedded features via a self-supervised variational autoencoder (Fig. 1a). The embedded features are subjected to binning through a two-stage multiscale adaptive clustering process. DBSCAN19 and BIRCH20 were selected as the clustering algorithms from 12 clustering methods because they have the best performance in binning (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Methods). In the first stage, the adaptive DBSCAN algorithm is applied to generate clusters at multiple scales (Fig. 1b and Step 1 in Fig. 1e). These clusters might intersect or overlap, affecting bin quality. To prevent this bias, an iterative assessment was designed to evaluate cluster quality and select the best clusters from the DBSCAN clusters to generate preliminary bins (Step 2 in Fig. 1e). A reclustering decision model was developed to determine whether preliminary bins are of sufficient quality to be retained for bin pooling (Step 3 in Fig. 1e) or whether they are subjected to reclustering (Fig. 1c). In the second stage, the contigs in low-quality bins that were not clustered were subjected to multiscale adaptive BIRCH (Step 4) and iterative assessment (Step 5) to improve contig utilization and complement bin pooling. Eventually, LorBin gathers total bins from the two-stage adaptive multiscale clustering along with strict assessments as the final bin pool for the downstream analyses (Fig. 1d).

LorBin optimization in binning performance

Metagenome binning comprises two default processes: feature extraction and clustering. LorBin comparison experiments were conducted to reduce resource usage and improve binning results (Methods). For feature extraction, we found that the VAE was more efficient at extracting the embedded features of contigs than the other two encoders either using CPUs (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1) or GPUs (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2). Compared with SOTA methods, LorBin was 2.3–25.9 times faster than SemiBin2 and COMEBin under normal memory consumption (Supplementary Data 3–5). In the clustering stage, DBSCAN and BIRCH were determined as the two best approaches out of 12 different clustering algorithms for the embedded features (Supplementary Fig. 1). To further improve the binning performance, we developed a two-stage adaptive clustering model deploying the multiscale DBSCAN & BIRCH algorithms with evaluation-decision models (Fig. 1b, c). Compared with single clustering binners, the two-stage clustering approach yielded more high- and medium-quality bins (hmBins) (Supplementary Fig. 3) due to the complementarity effect between the two clustering stages (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, the iterative process using the quality assessment model generates more confident preliminary bins by analyzing the boundaries, overlaps, and shapes of clusters. The reclustering decision model enabled the complementarity of high-quality bins by determining the free contigs and those in low-completeness bins to be reclustered (Supplementary Fig. 5). This assessment-decision model was found to be more effective than linear regression and random forest models (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). To explore the important features for reclustering, we used Shapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP)22 to calculate the marginal contribution of each feature to the model output (Supplementary Fig. 6). Completeness and its range to purity (|completeness–purity | ) were identified as major contributors to reclustering decisions (Supplementary Fig. 6)8,10–12,23.

Benchmarking on a synthetic dataset

We benchmarked LorBin against SOTA binners, including SemiBin8, SemiBin210, VAMB6, AAMB7, COMEBin9, and MetaBAT221, on the synthetic CAMI II dataset3 comprising 49 samples from five habitats (Fig. 2). LorBin recovered 246, 266, 422, 289, and 164 high-quality bins (hBins) from the airways, gastrointestinal tract, oral cavity, skin, and urogenital tract, respectively (Fig. 2a). The percentages of hBins recovered by LorBin were 19.4% (airways), 9.4% (gastrointestinal), 22.7% (oral), 15.1% (skin), and 7.5% (urogenital) higher than those recovered by the second-best binner SemiBin2 (Fig. 2a). Compared with the other binners, LorBin reconstructed 71.9 ± 16.7% (airways), 42.2 ± 12.3% (gastrointestinal), 76.0 ± 20.6% (oral), 83.9 ± 23.2% (skin), and 43.4 ± 8.5% (urogenital) more hBins (Supplementary Fig. 7a). LorBin showed superior clustering accuracy, which was 109.4 ± 28.7% (airways), 24.4 ± 8.7% (gastrointestinal), 78.0 ± 19.1.0% (oral), 93.0 ± 14.9% (skin) and 35.4 ± 12.3% (urogenital) higher than that of the competing binners (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 7b). We also compared the adjusted Rand index (ARI) and F1 score of the binning performance across different binners. LorBin achieved the highest F1 score in the airway, gastrointestinal, and oral environments and the highest ARI in the oral and skin environments (Supplementary Fig. 8). Overall, LorBin outperforms the available cutting-edge binners in recovering complete genomes from synthetic long-read metagenomes.

Performance on the imbalanced dataset

We investigated the ability of binners to handle imbalanced species distributions by simulating reference communities with increasing imbalance, with evenness ranging from 0.9 (equal distribution of each species) to 0.3 (hyper-dominance of one species). The silhouette coefficient (goodness of clustering) of each binner was used to evaluate its robustness to imbalanced species distributions (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 9). The microbial evenness gradient was constructed by downsampling contigs from the relatively balanced microbiomes in the CAMI II dataset (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b and Methods). We found that the silhouette coefficient of each binner increased from −0.3 to 0.9 with the microbial evenness of perturbed samples, e.g., a reduction in downsampling percentages on the raw metagenomes (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Compared with SOTA binners, LorBin was effective at binning each perturbed sample with the highest silhouette coefficients, which were 0.32 ± 0.11 (airways), 0.19 ± 0.13 (gastrointestinal), 0.38 ± 0.10 (oral), 0.32 ± 0.10 (skin), and 0.19 ± 0.13 (urogenital) times higher than the second-best method (Supplementary Fig. 9c). The area under the silhouette coefficient curve of LorBin (avg. 0.20 ± 0.01) was consistently larger than that of the competing binners in five different habitats (Fig. 2c). The areas of the competing binners ranged from 0.10 ± 0.01 to 0.27 (Fig. 2c). Overall, LorBin outperforms the SOTA binners in addressing imbalanced microbiomes.

LorBin outperforms other binners on a real dataset

We further benchmarked the ability of LorBin to process the real long-read PacBio HiFi metagenomes in 104 human intestine samples (Fig. 3). LorBin reconstructed 224 high-quality bins (hBins) and 455 medium-quality bins (mBins) according to CheckM2. On average, it achieved 106.1 ± 30.0% higher number for hBins and 115.6 ± 72.8% higher number for mBins than any of the other binners (Fig. 3a). LorBin consistently showed better binning performance than MetaBAT2, VAMB, AAMB, and COMEB in more than 50% of the intestine samples (Fig. 3b). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed that the number of hmBins obtained from LorBin was significantly more than that recovered from each competing binner at the sample level (Supplementary Fig. 10). To assess the ability of LorBin to bin imbalanced real datasets, we calculated the size of hmBins (e.g., the number of contigs per bin, Supplementary Fig. 11) from each binner and compared the range of their bin sizes (Fig. 3c). We found that LorBin was stable in addressing the imbalanced real dataset with the widest range of bin sizes among the seven binners (Fig. 3c). The hmBins of LorBin and MetaBAT2 included the highest and lowest number of contigs from the intestine metagenomes, respectively, whereas the former comprised approximately 10% more contigs binned than SemiBin2 and SemiBin (Supplementary Fig. 12). The 29k contigs were used by LorBin for binning hmBins because of the superior performance of two-stage multiscale clustering on imbalanced metagenomes (Fig. 3d).

To demonstrate the capacity of LorBin to handle clusters differing in shape, we developed a transformer classifier to assess the convexity of the embedded features of each bin. Among the obtained hmBins, 24.9% and 17.2% were predicted to be convex and concave clusters, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Additionally, many of the unclassified clusters (~58%) with fewer than 33 contigs (bin size <33) were generated during the first stage of the LorBin workflow (Supplementary Fig. 13b). We visualized their embedded features via UMAP24 (see Methods) to intuitively illustrate the complex shapes of bins in real datasets. The UMAP embedding showed that bin.313 formed a convex cluster (i.e., approximated by a convex polygon), whereas bin.188 exhibited a clearly concave structure (Fig. 3e). In addition to human gut samples, LorBin also performed well in long-read binning of the microbiomes of marine and sheep guts (Supplementary Fig. 14), suggesting the advantage of LorBin in genome reconstruction from complex and diverse natural environments.

LorBin discovers several novel taxa

To investigate the nature of the bacterial genomes recovered by LorBin and current binners, we performed taxonomic profiling of hmBins reconstructed by LorBin and other binners from 104 real human intestinal microbiomes (Fig. 4, Supplementary Data 6). LorBin recovered 34 unique species, approximately 2.4 times more than the second-best binner, SemiBin2 (Fig. 4a). Moreover, hmBins from LorBin presented increased diversity at the species, genus, and phylum levels (Fig. 4b). During the taxonomic assignment, some hmBins could not be annotated or assigned as unclassified species by the GTDB (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 15a), indicating the ability of LorBin to identify potential new species. We calculated the average nucleotide identity (ANI) between unclassified species from LorBin and other binners (Supplementary Fig. 15b). We found that these unclassified new species (ANI > 97) may have originated from different binners, with LorBin identifying the most unclassified species (Supplementary Fig. 15b). Finally, we calculated the serendipity25 (e.g., the ability of a binner to identify novel genomes relative to other binners) of LorBin relative to the other six binners, and the serendipity of LorBin reached 20.4–63.5% (Fig. 4d). This finding suggested that LorBin is superior for constructing rare, novel, and previously unknown species from natural microbiomes.

Discussion

We introduce LorBin, a novel algorithm tailored for the efficient binning of long-read metagenomes. LorBin brings several substantial advances: (1) More high-quality bins are recovered from both rare and low-abundance species. They are overlooked by existing methods due to compositional imbalance. LorBin addresses this issue directly and improves community resolution. (2) It expands phylogenetic coverage by recovering more unique and novel taxa. This highlights the power of LorBin in exploring microbial dark matter and accessing poorly described or unknown lineages. (3) It improves the binning quality and reliability. LorBin can identify and correct misclassified bins via assessment and reclustering steps. This improves both the read/contig utilization and confidence in bin quality. (4) It is efficient in runtime. In feature extraction and learning processes, LorBin requires neither reference genomes nor labeled data, whereas in the clustering process, LorBin deploys adaptive clustering to automatically determine parameters instead of traversing each of them. These factors make LorBin run faster than most supervised or semi-supervised binners. Furthermore, LorBin outperforms the SOTA binners consistently across real-world datasets originating from diverse environments from the human gut to soil to marine samples. These advances make LorBin a robust and promising binner for metagenomic studies.

The strong performance of LorBin stems from a series of algorithmic innovations. As a core, this binner deploys a multiscale adaptive clustering framework. This framework aims to separate the metagenomic contigs based on intrinsic data structures rather than assumptions about species abundance or cluster density. To this end, LorBin integrates the density-based (DBSCAN) and hierarchical (BIRCH) clustering algorithms into two-stage hybrid clustering. These two stages complement each other. DBSCAN is highly adaptable to local density shifts in the data structure and can isolate clusters of irregular shape19. This attention is essential in metagenomics since species vary greatly in abundance and composition, e.g., compositional imbalance in real microbiomes. BIRCH complements DBSCAN by handling large-scale data efficiently via hierarchical summaries20. Owing to the complements, LorBin adapts to varying shapes (concavity and convexity), sizes (rare and low-abundance taxa), and evenness of clusters (imbalanced species distributions). Compared with single clustering algorithms (Supplementary Fig. 3a), LorBin enables the recovery of 10.9–46.0% of high-quality genomes, particularly from rare and low-abundance bacterial lineages. This stands in contrast to available binning tools that assume roughly uniform cluster structures4,8, as shown in our perturbation experiments.

In addition, the reclustering strategy enables LorBin to maximize bin recovery. Instead of discarding low-quality bins or unassigned contigs, LorBin performs an iterative refinement step with an assessment-decision model. The assessment model ensures that clusters are well separated and that each contig is assigned to the most appropriate cluster. Furthermore, the decision model enables the reutilization of low-quality bins and unassigned contigs. This step improves the retrieval of genomes that might be discarded by other binners. These models extract key features from single-copy genes and neural networks by using non-linear relationships to refine the bin quality. Compared with linear regression and random forest, the assessment-decision model reconstructed approximately 8% more high-quality bins. This mechanism substantially increases contig utilization and reduces the constraints of early-stage errors and compositional noise on binning performance. Compared with existing binners on human gut samples, LorBin yielded 10.9 to 202.7% more high-quality bins, particularly 2.4- to 17-fold more unique species. This demonstrated the strong ability of our binner to capture rare or previously undescribed taxa that other methods fail to recover. This capacity to improve both the breadth and depth of microbial genome recovery. These findings suggest the wide application of LorBin for comparative microbiome studies, taxonomic discovery, and ecological inference.

LorBin offers practical advantages in scalability and runtime. Feature extraction is important for binning. Many encoders extract features, such as the adversarial autoencoder26 (AAE)7 and siamese neural networks27, which use the semisupervised (SemiAE)8,10 methods. Compared with the AAE and SemiAE, LorBin applies a self-supervised variational autoencoder (VAE) to learn effective feature embeddings without relying on references or labeled data. The VAE used by LorBin is less time-consuming and reconstructs 14.3–28.6% more high-quality bins than suboptimal encoders. In the clustering process, the parameters of LorBin are dynamically determined through the adaptive optimization strategy based on data distribution characteristics, which effectively improves its adaptability and efficiency for complex data structures. Throughout the entire process, LorBin maintains an acceptable runtime and is 2.3–25.9 times faster than SemiBin210 and COMEBin9 with a 32-core CPU, 64 GB of RAM, and 0.3*NVIDIA A800 GPU (with 24 GB of VRAM). The time and memory consumed by LorBin were found to be linearly correlated with input data sizes (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Despite these advancements, several challenges remain to be addressed in future iterations. One key limitation is the reliance on single-copy genes as the primary metric for assessing bin quality during reclustering. While this approach is efficient, integrating additional context-aware metrics could further improve bin accuracy. For example, coverage correlation coefficients and k-mer co-occurrence patterns have been applied to improve clustering15. Future developments could explore ensemble models that combine multiple genomic features for a more holistic binning assessment. Although LorBin is optimized for large-scale datasets, metagenomic binning remains computationally demanding. In the current metagenomics era, it is important for bioinformatic tools to process millions of contigs. We found that GPU acceleration can achieve a 76-fold speedup (Supplementary Fig. 16). For ultra-large input datasets of millions of long-reads, applying distributed computing or implementing more efficient encoding strategies could further increase the scalability. Another important challenge is the binning of mobile genetic elements (MGEs). For instance, plasmids and prophages often exhibit sequence characteristics distinct from those of chromosomal DNA28–30. The development of binning algorithms for MGEs could improve their recovery and contribute to a better understanding of horizontal gene transfer and antimicrobial resistance (Supplementary Fig. 17). Interpretability remains another key focus of future research, particularly for the structural characteristics of bins and Al-based binners. The shape of clusters in latent space may be associated with genomic features such as GC content, sequence complexity, and contig length or others. Non-convex clusters often indicate genomes with biased compositions or uneven assemblies. Understanding this biological significance can increase the accuracy of metagenomic binning. To increase the reliability of AI models, it is crucial to understand how models make binning decisions. Thus, developing methods to analyse the feature importance of k-mer frequencies, sequence compositions, or evolutionary signals could provide deeper insights into binning decisions. This approach might increase the confidence of LorBin predictions and provide strong biological insights beyond binning performance metrics. Additionally, expanding LorBin to metatranscriptomic and functional annotation pipelines could facilitate a deeper exploration of microbial interactions, gene expression, and metabolic potential.

In summary, LorBin represents a significant advancement in metagenomic binning. It enables high-resolution genome recovery from real-world long-read data, including rare, novel, and understudied taxa. It is accurate, scalable, and applicable to a wide range of habitats and microbiomes. By integrating self-supervised learning, adaptive clustering, and reclustering strategies, LorBin maximizes the value of metagenomic sequencing. Together, LorBin is a promising tool for microbial discovery and microbiome research.

Methods

Overview of LorBin

The framework of LorBin is divided into the following steps (Fig. 1): (1) VAE embedding computes the abundance and k-mer frequencies as the input of a self-supervised variational autoencoder (VAE) to extract the embedded features (Fig. 1a); (2) First-stage clustering uses the multiscale adaptive DBSCAN and an iteration process with a cluster quality assessment model to generate preliminary bins and then uses a reclustering decision model to evaluate the quality of the preliminary bins. High- and medium-quality bins are included in the final bin pool, and low-quality bins are subjected to reclustering (Fig. 1b); (3) Second-stage clustering (reclustering) uses contigs in low-quality bins and unclustered contigs in the first stage to generate bins through multiscale adaptive BIRCH and an iteration process similar to that of first-stage clustering (Fig. 1c); (4) Bin pooling pools the bins of the two stages as the final binning result (Fig. 1d). In addition, we constructed encoder comparison, clustering algorithm comparison, imbalanced data perturbation analysis experiments, cluster shape detection experiments, cluster density statistics, and serendipity experiments to comprehensively optimize and evaluate LorBin.

Computation of abundance and tetranucleotide frequencies

LorBin combines an S-dimensional abundance vector and a T-dimensional composition vector to represent each contig, where S is the number of samples and T is the number of k-mers31. The feature matrix of the contigs is denoted as X∈RN×(S+T), where N denotes the number of contigs. The abundance matrix is denoted as Xa∈RN×S and is obtained by mapping reads (including short reads and long reads) to contigs. The composition matrix of X is denoted as Xc∈RN×T. LorBin uses a sliding window of length k and stride 1 to traverse the contig sequence, generates k-mer fragments, and then counts the frequency of k-mer fragments. The k-mer frequency is denoted as F∈RN×T. The T-dimensional composition vector Xc is generated from the k-mer frequency. LorBin sets k=46–8,10,11 and T=136 (treating k-mers and their reverse complements as equivalents6–10,31).

where Fi∈F represents the frequency of the k-mer feature of the i-th contig and where xic∈Xc is the composition vector of the i-th contig k-mer vectors and is normalized by the length of each contig after the addition of a small pseudocount.

VAE embedding

A variational autoencoder is constructed to embed contigs. The input layer dimension of VAE6 is S + T, and the input matrix is X∈RN×(S+T). The hidden layer of the encoder consists of two fully connected layers, each using batch normalization and dropout (p = 0.2). The output of the last layer is passed to two different, fully connected layers of length Nz, termed the µ and σ layers. The output of the µ layer is the mean of the latent, whereas the output of the σ layer is the variance of the latent. Then, the latent layer, z, is of length Nz and is obtained by sampling the Gaussian distribution using the µ and σ layers as parameters—for example, zi~N(μi,σi) for each neuron i=1,…,Nz. The sampled latent representation is then passed through the hidden decoding layers, which are identical in size to the hidden encoding layers except that they are arranged in reverse order. Finally, the last hidden decoding layer is connected to an S + T fully connected layer, which is split into two output vectors, Xoa∈RN×(S+T) and Xoc∈RN×(S+T). LorBin uses leaky rectified linear units as activation functions except for the µ and σ layers, which use linear and softplus activation, respectively. Furthermore, for the last layer generating the reconstructions, LorBin uses softmax activation to generate Xoa, mimicking a probability distribution and linear activation for Xoc. After training, the input sequences are encoded by passing them through the VAE and extracting the values of the µ layer. The VAE models are trained via the Adam optimizer and one Monte Carlo sample of the Gaussian latent representation.

Two-stage clustering

To handle the imbalance and complex distribution of metagenomic data, we design a two-stage clustering method, including an adaptive mechanism, multiscale DBSCAN clustering, multiscale BIRCH clustering, a cluster quality assessment model, and a reclustering decision model. The adaptive mechanism is used to determine the parameter list of multiscale clustering. Before clustering, LorBin automatically generates a list of Euclidean distances with m1 scales (m1 is constant, set to 5) and a list of cosine similarities with m2 (m2 is constant, set to 10) scales. In the first stage, multiscale adaptive DBSCAN is applied to cluster the embedded features of contigs (Fig. 1b). Lorbin uses the cluster quality assessment model to evaluate and select these clusters to ensure that the contigs are in a unique bin. The entire process of evaluation and selection is iterated as follows. The cluster assessment model evaluates all the overlapping clusters. If other clusters had overlapping contigs with the best cluster, these contigs were considered to belong to the best cluster and thus removed from other clusters. Other clusters are subjected to the next round of evaluation and selection. The evaluation and selection operations are iterated until no best cluster with contamination <50% has a score >0. These best clusters generated in the first stage are subjected to the reclustering decision model to predict the quality score. If the score exceeds a (constant, set to 0.6), the bin is retained for bin pooling; otherwise, it is subjected to reclustering (Fig. 1c). In the second stage, the contigs in the low-completeness bins as well as the unclustered contigs in the first stage (step 3 in Fig. 1e) are subjected to multiscale adaptive BIRCH and iterative assessment to generate complementary bins (Fig. 1c). The multiscale adaptive BIRCH and cluster assessment model in the second stage works similarly to that in the first stage. The bins obtained at the first and second stages constitute the binning result.

Adaptive to determining parameters

Multiscale DBSCAN19 requires a list of neighborhood radii ε, and BIRCH20 requires a list of thresholds τ. Both parameters are related to the intercluster distance and intracluster distance. We design an adaptive mechanism that can automatically determine the list of clustering neighborhood radii ε and the parameter list of the threshold τ based on the overall distance relationship of the input embedded features. The adaptive mechanism first calculates the distance distribution matrix of the embeddings M∈RN×N, Mi,j∈M,Mi,j=d(i,j), where N is the number of objects contained in the datasets and where d(i,j) is the distance from the i-th embedded feature to the j-th embedded feature in the datasets. LorBin sorts the distance matrix M in ascending order and obtains the ordered distance matrix Ms. LorBin selects the n-th, 2n-th, 3n-th, …, kn-th distances to generate a clustering parameter list with length k, where k is a hyperparameter indicating the number of clustering parameter lists and where n denotes the number of contigs.

Model pretraining

Both the cluster quality assessment models and the reclustering decision models were pretrained. The cluster quality assessment model uses the mean square error as the loss function, and the reclustering decision model uses a sigmoid function as the loss function. We extracted 3000 bins as the pretraining dataset from 10 simulated samples at a ratio of 1:2 between high- and medium-quality bins and low-quality bins. These bins in equal proportions were obtained from MetaBAT221, SemiBin8, and SemiBin210, and the F1 score was calculated via the completeness and contamination obtained via CheckM2 (version 1.0.2)33. The F1 score is used as the true label to train the cluster quality assessment model. We recorded high- and medium-quality bins as positive examples, which did not need to be reclustered, and low-quality bins as negative examples. These labeled data were used to train the reclustering decision models. For each training model, the data were divided into five equal parts. One part was selected as the testing set, while the remaining four parts were used as the training set, with no overlap between the training sets and testing sets. This process was repeated five times. The highest accuracy reclustering decision model and the minimum mean square error cluster quality assessment model were applied to LorBin.

Encoder algorithm comparison

To determine the best encoder for contig embedding, we conducted a series of ablation experiments on the SRR13128012, SRR13128013, SRR13128014, and ERR9765783 datasets39. We used three encoders, namely, the adversarial autoencoder (AAE) in AAMB7, the Siamese network (SemiAE) in SemiBin210, and the variational autoencoder (VAE) in VAMB6, with K-means clustering.

The AAE uses the model in the AAMB7. The AAE is composed of three modules: the encoder-decoder module, the z latent space discriminator module, and the y latent space discriminator module. The encoder-decoder module learns the contig features and encodes them into the z nd y latent spaces. The discriminators for the z and y latent spaces restrict the latent space as their priors, thus performing the regularization of the model, which imposes a structure on the latent space that makes it clusterable. The encoder is a sequence of two dense layers with 547 units of dense layers, each with a leaky ReLU activation function and batch normalization. The encoder is connected to the dense layers µ,σ, each with 283 units parameterizing z, and to the y layer with 700 units. Softmax activation is applied to the y latent layer to mimic a probability distribution. The decoder reconstructs the contig features via a sample of the Gaussian distribution z~N(μ,σ) and the y vector. Its architecture is identical to that of the encoder. Softmax was applied to the abundance output units. The two discriminators of the z and y space, Dz, and Dy, are both networks with the same architecture as the decoder model, except without batch normalization, where the final output layer is one single node. Each discriminator is trained to discriminate between samples taken from the latent spaces (z,y) and the prior samples. The prior for Dz is the unit Gaussian distribution N(μ=0,σ=I), and I is a small random vector. For Dy, the prior distribution is the RelaxedOneHotCategorical distribution Cat(t), where the temperature is τ=0.15. The discriminators were optimized for a binary classification task; therefore, the Softmax activation function was used for the output node to interpret the output as a probability. All three modules are optimized with Adam.

SemiAE was obtained from SemiBin210. The training of SemiAE relies on musk-link and cannot-link constraints. We followed the method in SemiBin210 to break longer contigs to generate musk-linked contigs and randomly sampled pairs of contigs and treated them as cannot-link pairs. To control the training time and the ratio between the must-link and cannot-link constraints, we limit the number of cannot-link constraints used in training to min (m,40000), where m is the number of must-link constraints. The inputs to the SemiAE are pairs of features computed from the contigs, whereas its outputs are the fixed-dimensional embedding in R100. The first two layers of the network are followed by a batch normalization layer, a leaky rectified linear unit layer, and a dropout layer (p = 0.2). The SemiAE uses supervised contrastive loss to classify the must-link (positive label) and cannot-link (negative label) constraints. Intuitively, the distance in the embedded space between elements with a must-link constraint is minimized, whereas elements with a cannot-link constraint are kept apart. The loss function is minimized with the Adam optimization algorithm.

Clustering algorithm comparison

We compared 12 clustering algorithms, DBSCAN19, BIRCH20, infomap47, K-means, K-medoid, mean shift48, spectral49, OPTICS50, hierachy, GMM, C-means51, and canopy algorithms6,52, on an in-house simulated dataset of 70 species.

The simulated sample was generated by PBSIM241 via ‘--data-type CCS’ with different abundances, and its references were obtained from NCBI. The long reads were subsequently assembled via metaFlye 2.8.1-b167653, and all the contigs were used. The embedded features of the contigs were extracted via VAE via a pretrained model using samples of CAMI II from the gastrointestinal tract because the pretrained model was more reliable when the number of contigs was relatively small. The infomap was obtained from the python package igraph 0.10.154, the canopy algorithm was obtained from the VAMB6 source code, and the other 10 algorithms were obtained from the python package scikit-learn (version 1.1.2)55. The parameters of the infomap and canopy were obtained from SemiBin8 and VAMB6. The parameters of the other clustering algorithms were optimized via a grid search. The Davies–Bouldin index (DBI)56, an internal evaluation index for clustering that does not require ground truth, was used as the evaluation function.

Perturbation analysis of imbalance

Metagenomic data are characterized by uneven species abundance, with few abundant species coexisting with a highly diverse set of rare species. To compare the impact of imbalanced data on binning and the performance of binners on different degrees of imbalanced data, we generated relatively balanced datasets and relatively imbalanced datasets from the five different environments of the CAMI II datasets3.

For every environment, we counted the cluster sizes of high- and medium-quality bins obtained by LorBin. Then, we specified the cluster size range and randomly selected 50 clusters of similar sizes to generate relatively balanced samples. In addition, their evenness was recorded as the control experimental data of the imbalance perturbation experiment. Then, we perform downsampling on this balanced sample, changing the evenness of this sample so that the imbalance of the sample gradually increases, turning it into a relatively imbalanced sample. In the first perturbation experiment, we randomly selected 10% of the 50 clusters, that is, 5 clusters, and downsampled them to reduce their cluster size to less than 10. In the second perturbation experiment, we randomly selected 20% of the 50 clusters, that is, 10 clusters, and downsampled them to reduce their cluster size to less than 10. We conducted 9 such perturbation experiments on a balanced sample. The proportion of randomly selected clusters increased from 10 to 90% at a stride of 10%. Each time downsampling was performed, the evenness and imbalance relative to the balanced sample of the generated imbalanced sample were calculated. These 9 samples with different degrees of imbalance serve as the data for the perturbation experiments. We used LorBin, SemiBin8, SemiBin210, VAMB6, AAMB7, COMEBin9, and MetaBAT221 to bin these 10 samples with different degrees of balance and obtained the predicted labels and embeddings of the contigs of each sample. LorBin, SemiBin8, SemiBin210, VAMB7, and the embeddings of AAMB7 and COMEBin9 all come from their encoders. Metabat221 uses the concatenation of tetranucleotide frequencies and their calculated depth information. We use embedding and predicted labels to calculate the silhouette coefficient of each binner result on these ten samples. The entire process of relatively balanced sample generation, downsampling to generate relatively imbalanced samples, binning, and calculation of the silhouette coefficients through the binning results and embedding is repeated 10 times in a single environment. This process was repeated 10 times in the gastrointestinal, oral, skin, and urogenital environments.

Explainability of the cluster quality assessment model and the reclustering decision model

Shapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP)22 was used to interpret the cluster quality assessment model and the reclustering decision model prediction. This technique uses game theoretic principles to compute the contribution of each input feature of the model toward the prediction of the cluster and to visualize the impact of each vital feature. The closer the SHAP value is to zero, the smaller the contribution of the feature to the prediction, and a SHAP value far from zero indicates that the contribution of this feature to the prediction is greater. For the trained models, we computed the SHAP values of every feature over-all test set samples in that iteration. We used these aggregated local interpretations to explain the overall model.

Convexity classifier

To directly evaluate the convexity of the embedded features of each bin, we design a neural classifier comprising a transformer encoder57,58 and a classification head with two fully connected layers. The transformer architecture includes a self-attention layer and a feed-forward neural network, with the following parameters: an input size of 32, an embedding dimension of 64, two attention heads per layer, a feed-forward layer size of 128, and a maximum token length of 512. A synthetic dataset containing 10,000 clusters was generated, with a 1:1 ratio of convex to concave clusters. The convex clusters included hyperspheres and hyperellipsoids, whereas the concave clusters consisted of high-dimensional rings, crescents, and other nonconvex geometries. The classifier was trained for 100 epochs via the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001) with fivefold cross-validation (training:validation ratio = 4:1), yielding a validation accuracy of 0.898 ± 0.010.

ERR9765783 was downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena) under project ID PRJEB52977. The simulated CAMI II datasets are available at https://data.cami-challenge.org/participate. The 104 PacBio HiFi real human intestinal datasets were downloaded from the National Genomics Data Center (CNCB) [https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn] (Project ID: PRJCA008836). The marine datasets were obtained from ENA (Project ID: PRJEB52999). The sheep gut datasets were obtained from the NCBI (Project ID: PRJNA595610).

All the codes used for this work are available at LorBin on GitHub (https://github.com/LorMeBioAI/LorBin) and Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.13864645).
