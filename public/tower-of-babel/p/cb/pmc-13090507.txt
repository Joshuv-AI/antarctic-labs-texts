Fermented ginseng by-products regulate calcium metabolism pathways, liver health, immune function and intestinal microbiota to improve eggshell quality and production performance in late-laying hens
Authors: Zhaobin Zhou, Qiming Wang, Weiwei Wang, Sijuan Huang, Denghui Hu, Bin Zhou, Jiannan Kang, Yongxiang Luo, Huiran Kang, Qian Zhang, Jieyang Wu, Zikui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High egg breakage during the late laying phase reduces the economic efficiency of egg production. This study investigated the effects of dietary supplementation with fermented ginseng by-products (FGB) on productive performance, eggshell quality, and related physiological indices in late-laying Roman Gray hens. A total of 360 hens (450 days old) were randomly assigned to four groups: a control group fed a basal diet and three treatment groups fed the basal diet supplemented with 100, 150, or 200 g/T FGB (GF100, GF150, and GF200, respectively). The trial lasted 90 days and was divided into early, middle, and late phases. FGB supplementation reduced the broken egg rate during the early phase, late phase, and overall trial period, increased average egg weight during the middle, late, and overall periods, and improved hen-day egg production during the late phase and across the entire trial. In addition, FGB increased eggshell weight during the early phase and increased eggshell thickness during the middle and late phases. FGB also upregulated several calcium metabolism-related genes in the duodenum and shell gland, particularly during the late phase, without causing consistent changes in serum calcium or inorganic phosphorus levels. Moreover, FGB altered serum biochemical indices in a phase-dependent manner and changed the relative abundance of several cecal genera. Overall, dietary FGB supplementation improved eggshell-related traits and productive performance in late-laying Roman Gray hens and was associated with changes in calcium metabolism-related gene expression, serum biochemical indices, and cecal microbiota composition.

Introduction

Eggs serve as an important source of high-quality protein for humans. As reported by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) (Bermudez-A et al., 2025), China accounted for approximately 34% of global egg production in 2022. To maximize production efficiency, poultry breeding programs and commercial farms continue to pursue greater lifetime egg output ("500 eggs within 700 days") . However, as laying hens age, egg production declines, accompanied by eggshell thinning and reduced eggshell strength (Lu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2023). This leads to a substantial increase in egg breakage, and eggs from 60-week-old hens may exhibit breakage rates up to three times higher than those from hens aged 20 to 29 weeks (Dong et al., 2022; Sirri et al., 2021). Consequently, there is growing interest in feed additives that can improve productive performance and reduce egg breakage during the late laying phase.

Ginseng (Panax ginseng Meyer), a member of the Araliaceae family, has long been used as a medicinal and edible herb in traditional medical systems in China, Korea, and other regions. In 2023, the China State Administration for Market Regulation formally included ginseng in the Catalog of Health Food Ingredients (No. 10) as a functional ingredient for fatigue resistance and immune enhancement (Kim et al., 2024). Studies have demonstrated that ginseng (Panax ginseng) and its key bioactive components, including ginsenosides and ginseng polysaccharides, exert multiple beneficial effects, such as anti-aging activity, hepatoprotective effects, and modulation of gut microbiota composition (Gao et al., 2024; Huo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2018). In commercial production systems, livestock and poultry often experience compromised immunity and impaired organ function, particularly under conditions of aging or physiological stress. Given its diverse biological activities, ginseng may contribute to improving physiological status and productive performance in livestock and poultry.

Annual ginseng production in China reaches 69, 850 tons, generating large quantities of ginseng by-products, such as powder and fibrous roots, during processing each year (Li et al., 2024) . Microbial fermentation has been shown to enhance the release of ginseng polysaccharides and ginsenosides while improving their bioavailability (Kim et al., 2011), thereby increasing the utilization value of ginseng by-products. In poultry, ginseng by-products have been shown to increase egg production in 75-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens (Kang et al., 2016) and reduce mortality in white-feathered broilers (Kim et al., 2014). These benefits may be related to the estrogen-like activity and immunomodulatory effects of ginseng extracts (Seo et al., 2025; Shim et al., 2012). Furthermore, previous studies have shown that ginsenosides can mitigate bone loss in ovariectomized mice (Nguyen et al., 2024) and upregulate osteocalcin expression to help maintain bone strength (Liu et al., 2021). These findings suggest that ginseng by-products may be involved in the regulation of calcium metabolism, particularly in animals experiencing reproductive decline. As laying hens age, reproductive function declines, leading to reduced egg production and increased egg breakage. Consequently, fermented ginseng by-products (FGB) may represent a potential nutritional strategy for improving productive performance and reducing egg breakage in aging hens.

To date, research on ginseng by-products and their fermented derivatives in laying hens remains limited. In particular, studies focusing on eggshell quality in late-laying hens remain scarce, and their potential regulatory effects on calcium metabolism have not been sufficiently clarified. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of FGB on egg production, eggshell quality, and egg breakage rate in Roman Gray hens during the late laying period. Furthermore, to explore the potential associations underlying the observed phenotypic changes, we measured serum calcium and phosphorus levels, examined the expression of calcium metabolism-related genes in the duodenum and shell gland, assessed serum biochemical indices, hepatic antioxidant capacity, immune function, and profiled cecal microbiota composition.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hunan Agricultural University (Changsha, China) (Approval No. HAU ACC 2022176).

FGB and experimental feed

The FGB (moisture content < 12%) used in this study as a feed additive for Roman Gray laying hens was provided by Hunan Microorganism & Herb Biological Technology Co., Ltd. The ginseng by-products used in this study consisted of ginseng stems and leaves and adventitious roots mixed at a ratio of 1:1. The mixture was fermented with Mycobacterium phlei at pH 6.5-7.0 for 60 h. After fermentation, the broth was sterilized at 120°C for 30 min and centrifuged at 10, 000 r/min for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and dried to obtain the final FGB product. The FGB primarily contained three major ginsenosides (Rb1, Re, and Rg1) and ginseng polysaccharides. Specifically, the total ginsenoside content was 0.8%, with Rb1, Re, and Rg1 accounting for 0.13%, 0.09%, and 0.11%, respectively, whereas the ginseng polysaccharide content was 20%.

FGB was added to the basal diet at 100, 150, or 200 g/T (based on total feed) and thoroughly mixed with the laying hen premix. The resulting mixture was then proportionally blended with corn, soybean meal, and other basal diet ingredients. Experimental design

A completely randomized design was used in this experiment, with the supplementation level of FGB as the sole experimental factor. A total of 360 Roman Gray laying hens at 450 days of age (late laying phase), with similar body weight and good health status, were randomly assigned by body weight to four dietary treatments, with six replicates per treatment and 15 hens per replicate. The control group (Control) received the basal diet, whereas the treatment groups received the basal diet supplemented with 100 g/T (GF100), 150 g/T (GF150), or 200 g/T (GF200) FGB. The 90-day trial was divided into three phases: early (0-30 d), middle (31-60 d), and late (61-90 d). Hen-day egg production (HDEP), eggshell quality, and broken egg rate (BER) were recorded every 30 days. In addition, samples of the duodenum, shell gland, serum, liver, and cecal digesta were collected for subsequent analyses. Throughout the experiment, hens were housed in cages with ad libitum access to water and were fed twice daily. Environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, and lighting) followed Roman Gray layer management guidelines, and all other management practices were consistent with standard commercial farm procedures.

Production performance

Body weight was recorded for each replicate after a 12-h feed withdrawal on days 30, 60, and 90 of the trial. Eggs were collected daily, and the numbers of normal eggs and broken eggs were recorded. At the end of each phase, average daily feed intake (ADFI), HDEP, average egg weight (AEW), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and BER were calculated according to the method described by Shang et al. (2020).

Eggshell quality

On days 30, 60, and 90, five eggs were randomly selected from each replicate, resulting in a total of 30 eggs per treatment group at each time point. Egg weight, eggshell weight, and eggshell strength were measured using a digital egg tester (DET6000, NABEL Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), whereas eggshell thickness was measured using a ball-tip micrometer caliper (Tuyttens et al., 2013).

Biochemical indicator testing

On days 30, 60, and 90 of the experiment, after a 12-h fast, one hen was selected from each replicate. Approximately 3 mL of blood was collected from the wing vein and allowed to clot for 1 h. Samples were then centrifuged at 1, 000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting serum was collected and stored at -80°C for subsequent analysis.Serum inorganic phosphorus (IP), calcium (Ca), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were measured using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (BS-200) with commercial reagent kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Research Institute. Serum IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations were determined using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China).

Liver antioxidant indices

On days 30, 60, and 90 of the experiment, after a 12-h fast, one bird from each replicate was selected and euthanized for liver sample collection. Liver samples were immediately stored at -80°C until analysis. For analysis, 0.1 g of liver tissue was homogenized in ice-cold physiological saline at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) using a tissue homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3, 000 r/min for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for the determination of antioxidant indices. Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), catalase (CAT), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using commercial assay kits obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

On days 30, 60, and 90 of the experiment, after a 12-h fast, one hen from each replicate was selected and euthanized. Duodenum and shell gland samples were collected and stored at -80°C until analysis. Total RNA was extracted from the duodenum and shell gland using TRIzol reagent (Accurate Biology, China). RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., China). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg of total RNA using a HiScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Accurate Biology, China). Quantitative PCR was then performed in triplicate using SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM (Accurate Biology, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). 16S rRNA sequencing analysis

On days 30, 60, and 90 of the experiment, after a 12-h fast, one hen from each replicate was selected and euthanized. Approximately 2 g of digesta from the middle portion of the cecum was collected, placed into pre-labeled sterile tubes, and stored at -80°C until analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal digesta samples using the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). DNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., China). Qualified DNA samples were then submitted to Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for high-throughput sequencing and library construction.

The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primer pairs 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) in a T100 Thermal Cycler PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). PCR products were extracted from 2% agarose gels, purified using a PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., China). Sequencing data were processed using standard bioinformatic procedures. Tags were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence similarity threshold using USEARCH software. Taxonomic annotation of OTUs was performed against the SILVA reference database. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses were conducted to assess microbial community composition.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was used to d etermine differences among groups. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

During the middle phase, compared with the Control group, dietary supplementation with different levels of FGB significantly increased AEW (P < 0.05), but did not significantly affect ADFI, HDEP, FCR, or BER (P > 0.05). BER was significantly lower in the GF150 and GF200 groups than in the Control group during the late phase (P < 0.05). Serum Ca and IP levels

Serum calcium and inorganic phosphorus levels are presented in Table 5. During the early and late phases, dietary supplementation with FGB at various levels did not significantly affect serum Ca or IP levels compared with the Control group (P > 0.05). Discussion

Declining reproductive function in aging hens is generally accompanied by a gradual reduction in productive performance during the late laying period (Dong et al., 2022; Sirri et al., 2021). Tajudeen et al. reported that supplementation with ginseng residue extract for 0-8 weeks did not significantly affect HDEP, whereas supplementation for 12 weeks significantly increased egg production in laying hens (Tajudeen et al., 2024). This time-dependent response is broadly consistent with the results of the present study, in which FGB improved HDEP mainly during the late phase and across the entire trial period, while little effect was observed during the early phase. Previous studies have suggested that ginsenosides may exhibit estrogen-like biological activity because of their steroid-like structural features (Fu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024), and such effects may be associated with reproductive regulation (He et al., 2024). However, because reproductive hormones, ovarian morphology, and follicular development were not assessed in the present study, the mechanism responsible for the improvement in egg production remains unclear. One possible explanation for the delayed improvement in productive performance is that the phytoestrogen-like activity of ginsenosides is relatively weak (Lee et al., 2003), and thus prolonged supplementation may be required before this effect accumulates sufficiently to exert a measurable influence on laying performance. Therefore, the present data support the conclusion that FGB improved productive performance after prolonged supplementation, but they do not directly demonstrate that this effect was mediated through regulation of estrogen release or oocyte maturation.

Eggshell deterioration is one of the major causes of economic loss in late-laying hens. With increasing age, the mammillary layer density and palisade layer thickness of the eggshell decline, resulting in reduced shell quality and an elevated BER (Benavides-Reyes et al., 2021). In the present study, dietary supplementation with FGB significantly reduced the BER during the early and late phases, and these changes were accompanied by improvements in eggshell-related traits. Specifically, FGB increased eggshell weight during the early phase and increased eggshell thickness during the middle and late phases, although eggshell thickness was lower in some treatment groups during the early phase. Therefore, the beneficial effect of FGB on eggshell quality appears to be phase-dependent and becomes more evident with continued supplementation. The lower BER observed in the GF150 and GF200 groups may be related to the combined improvement in shell thickness and shell weight. Similar improvements in BER have also been reported for other feed additives, such as astragalus polysaccharides (Lv et al., 2021). Because eggshell thickness and shell weight are important determinants of shell integrity in older hens (Dong et al., 2022; Kowalska et al., 2021), the present findings suggest that FGB may help reduce egg breakage by improving eggshell-related traits.

Systemic calcium homeostasis is maintained through the coordinated regulation of intestinal absorption, transport in blood, skeletal buffering, and deposition in the shell gland (Shanmugam et al., 2024). In the present study, dietary supplementation with 150 g/T FGB did not affect serum calcium or phosphorus concentrations. These findings suggest that systemic mineral homeostasis was generally preserved during FGB supplementation. One possible explanation is that FGB may have enhanced the intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphorus while accelerating their utilization and deposition in bone and/or eggshell, thereby helping maintain overall calcium balance without producing detectable changes in circulating mineral levels. Previous studies have indicated that ginseng-derived products may influence calcium-related physiological processes. Aryal et al. (Aryal et al., 2011) revealed that ginseng extracts maintain intracellular calcium homeostasis by regulating the expression of calcium-binding proteins. Similarly, Ikram et al. (Ikram et al., 2020) demonstrated that ginsenosides can elevate intracellular calcium concentrations by modulating ion channels and cell surface receptors. Calcium absorption and deposition are critical factors influencing eggshell thickness and eggshell weight in laying hens (Xin et al., 2021). Therefore, in the present study, we further investigated whether FGB affected calcium metabolism-related pathways in late-laying hens. Previous studies have also indicated that ginsenosides may affect intestinal absorption and bone metabolism (Kang et al., 2020; Yata et al., 1986), suggesting that ginseng-derived products may influence calcium utilization at the tissue level even in the absence of consistent changes in circulating calcium and phosphorus. Similarly, Jiang et al. (Jiang et al., 2022) reported that different levels of 25-OH-D3 did not significantly alter serum calcium or phosphorus concentrations in laying hens. Therefore, the absence of consistent serum changes in the present study does not exclude the possibility that FGB affected calcium transport or deposition in tissues involved in eggshell formation.

The duodenum is the primary site of intestinal calcium absorption, whereas the shell gland is the key site of calcium deposition during eggshell formation (Bar et al., 2009; Jin et al., 2024). Several calcium metabolism-related genes, including PMCA1b, CaBP-D28k, VDR, and CA2, play important roles in calcium transport and calcium carbonate formation (Berg et al., 2004; Han et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2024). Previous work has shown that ginseng-derived products may affect calcium-regulatory pathways in poultry and other animal models (Fu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023). In the present study, FGB supplementation significantly increased the relative expression levels of several calcium metabolism-related genes in both the duodenum and shell gland, particularly during the late phase. These findings are consistent with the observed improvement in eggshell traits and suggest that FGB may be associated with enhanced calcium transport and shell mineralization. However, because only mRNA expression was measured, the present results indicate an association rather than direct proof that FGB improved eggshell quality through these pathways. Additional studies involving protein expression, calcium transport activity, and eggshell ultrastructure would be required to confirm this mechanism. Regarding the underlying mechanism, it is plausible that ginsenosides present in FGB may induce the expression of osteocalcin and estrogen, thereby promoting calcium absorption and transport (Pajoumshariati et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2025). Additionally, the effect may be associated with ginseng polysaccharides mitigating inflammatory and oxidative stress damage in the intestine and reproductive system (Han et al., 2025; Huo et al., 2025).

The liver is the primary organ responsible for fat synthesis and metabolism, and it is also the main site for the synthesis of yolk precursors. Thus, maintaining hepatic health is critically important for sustaining the laying rate of hens during the late laying period (Wu et al., 2024). Serum levels of ALT, AST, and ALP serve as biomarkers reflecting liver health status (Fang et al., 2023).In the present study, dietary supplementation with 150 g/T FGB significantly reduced serum AST levels during the early phase and ALT levels during the mid and late phases, while simultaneously increasing ALP levels throughout the entire experimental period. Qureshi et al. observed similar results upon supplementing broiler diets with ginseng powder (Qureshi et al., 1983). The observed improvement in production performance during the late laying phase may therefore be a direct consequence of FGB's hepatoprotective effects.

To verify whether FGB exerts hepatoprotective effects by enhancing hepatic antioxidant capacity, we measured endogenous antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT) (Zheng et al., 2026), a key indicator of lipid peroxidation (MDA) (Shi et al., 2025), and T-AOC in the liver. Unexpectedly, FGB had no significant impact on the hepatic antioxidant capacity of laying hens across all three experimental phases. This finding is inconsistent with previous studies by Kim on broilers and Wang on pigs (Kim et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2020), which may be attributed to the fact that their measurements were conducted on muscle and serum samples, whereas ours focused on liver tissue. However, other research has shown that the activity of hepatic antioxidant enzymes in rainbow trout is positively correlated with the dietary dosage of ginseng powder (Liu et al., 2025), suggesting that the dosage of FGB used in this study may not have been sufficient to significantly enhance hepatic antioxidant capacity in late-laying hens.

Avian immunoglobulins, including IgA, IgG, and IgM, are important components of humoral immunity (Al-Rasheed et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that ginseng extracts can improve immune indices in broilers when provided at appropriate doses (Zhang et al., 2024). During the early and middle phases, serum IgM and/or IgG levels were elevated in some treatment groups, whereas during the late phase, serum IgM levels in the GF100 and GF200 groups were lower than those in the Control group. These findings suggest that FGB did not exert a uniformly stimulatory effect on humoral immunity throughout the experimental period. Instead, the immune response to FGB appeared to vary with both dose and supplementation duration. Interestingly, Liou et al (Liou et al., 2004) also observed that increasing the dosage of ginseng extract could reduce certain immune responses in mice. In the present study, the GF150 group showed relatively stable immunoglobulin profiles throughout the trial, without the marked early increase or late decrease observed in the GF100 and GF200 groups. This phenomenon may be explained by the relatively poor immune system function of laying hens during the late laying period (Van de Reep et al., 2018), and the polysaccharide components in FGB possess immunostimulatory activity. Sustained immune stimulation may induce immune tolerance in B cells (Nardini et al., 2024). Therefore, from the perspective of immune stability, 150 g/T may represent a relatively balanced supplementation level under the present experimental conditions. Nevertheless, the biological basis of these dose-dependent immune responses requires further investigation.

The composition of the gut microbiota reflects the physiological state and intestinal health of laying hens. Previous studies have shown that the cecal microbiota becomes relatively stable after hens reach the middle-to-late laying period (Videnska et al., 2014), which may explain why FGB supplementation did not significantly alter the dominant microbiota composition at the top 10 phylum or genus levels in the present study. However, significant differences were detected in several taxa at the top 50 genus level. Specifically, Odoribacter and Collinsella were enriched in the GF200 group during the early phase, Paraprevotella was increased in the GF100 group during the early and middle phases, and Megamonas was increased in the GF150 group during the late phase. These genera have been associated in previous studies with gut homeostasis and the production of fermentation-derived metabolites such as acetate and propionate (Colombino et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024). Previous reports have also shown that ginseng extracts or ginseng polysaccharides can alter the abundance of beneficial gut bacteria, such as Lactococcus and Lactobacillus (Hu et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2024). Taken together, the present findings suggest that FGB may influence the intestinal microenvironment by modulating selected microbial taxa. However, because short-chain fatty acids and other microbial metabolites were not measured in this study, the current results do not establish a direct causal relationship between these microbiota changes and the improvement in eggshell quality or productive performance.

Previous studies have shown that acid-producing or probiotic bacteria may contribute to eggshell quality by improving mineral utilization and shell ultrastructure (Huang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). In the present study, the enrichment of several potentially beneficial genera coincided with improved eggshell traits in FGB-treated hens, suggesting that microbiota modulation may be one of the pathways associated with the beneficial effects of FGB. However, this interpretation remains preliminary. Since intestinal SCFAs and lactic acid were not measured, further studies integrating microbial metabolites, calcium transport, and eggshell ultrastructure are needed to determine whether FGB improves eggshell quality through microbiota-mediated mechanisms.

Conclusion

Dietary supplementation with FGB improved eggshell-related traits and productive performance in Roman Gray laying hens during the late laying phase, particularly during the later stages of the trial. These effects were accompanied by altered expression of calcium metabolism-related genes in the duodenum and shell gland, changes in selected serum biochemical indices, and shifts in several cecal bacterial genera, including Odoribacter, Collinsella, Paraprevotella, and Megamonas. Under the conditions of the present study, 150 g/T appeared to be a relatively balanced supplementation level. The beneficial effects of FGB became more evident after prolonged supplementation, especially after 60 days.
