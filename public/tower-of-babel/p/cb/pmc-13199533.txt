Design-based life cycle assessment of flexible pavements to evaluate embodied carbon footprint
Authors: Praveen Bodhanam S, Ramu Baadiga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Material-intensive infrastructure systems, such as flexible pavements, offer significant opportunities for reducing resource consumption and associated life-cycle environmental impacts through design-level interventions. This study integrates structural performance metrics, specifically modulus improvement factor (MIF), into life cycle assessment (LCA) decision-making for pavements. The life cycle carbon emissions of conventional and sustainably reinforced pavements were evaluated. Besides, the inclusion of acidification potential and abiotic fossil resource depletion was evaluated for pavement at the material stage. Further, sensitivity analysis and field verification were performed. Results reveal that material production and manufacturing contribute more than half of total emissions, while transportation contributes to one-third, with the least at the construction stage. A carbon emission reduction factor (CERF) was formulated to compare reinforced and unreinforced materials, demonstrating that geogrid inclusion reduces embodied carbon across all subgrade conditions. The proposed LCA framework demonstrates a practical pathway for structural benefits along with sustainability evaluation into pavement design.

Introduction

Background on flexible pavements and sustainability

Flexible pavements are the most widely used pavement system throughout India and much of the world due to their flexibility and usability across a range of traffic volumes, including low-volume roads and high-volume highways. Although they provide cost savings during construction and maintenance simplicity, traditional flexible pavements are most prone to early distresses like rutting, fatigue cracking, and differential settlements when they are built over variable or soft subgrades1–5. These performance issues are further aggravated by non-uniform traffic load trends and increasing demand for prolonged service life, placing a renewed emphasis on design strategies that bolster structural resilience and optimize material utilization6–8.

At present, the pavement industry is in transition and moving towards incorporating sustainable practices in pavement construction. One of the noticeable trends for such practice is that during the tendering stage itself, submission of estimated life cycle emissions during the life cycle of the pavement by construction firms9,10. Simultaneously, the pavement construction industry is faced with a different array of issues that are expected to become more severe in the near future. The global pavement infrastructure encompasses nearly 16.3 million kilometres, and approximately 25 million kilometres of new roads are anticipated to be built by 205011. In the current scenario, the status of India remains critical. Owing to the second-largest road network globally, India encounters severe material supply constraints, aggravated by its national policy to extend the National Highway network to over 237,000 km by 204712.

Sustainability of pavements can be linked to their carbon footprint. Hence, a detailed life cycle assessment is necessary to evaluate the environmental impacts associated with pavements throughout their life cycle. Such a need arises due to the global concerns about anthropogenic pollution and its adverse impacts on the ecosystem and human health13,14. To address these issues, international organizations such as the United Nations have established targets to reduce greenhouse gas emissions through the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Climate Agreement, which collectively mandate a concerted effort to reduce atmospheric CO2 levels and pollution.

Importance of geogrid reinforcement

In recent years, geosynthetic materials, particularly geogrids, have emerged as a practical engineering solution for incorporating sustainable waste materials, reducing material requirements, and mitigating the embodied environmental impacts associated with pavement construction. Geogrids provide lateral confinement to the aggregates of granular layers, enhancing the composite stiffness of the reinforced layers and thereby reducing both fatigue and rutting strains15–18. Practically, this improved mechanical response enables designers to achieve equivalent or improved structural performance with thinner granular and asphalt layers, also simultaneously extending service life and reducing the mass of energy-intensive materials (notably asphalt binder) that account for the largest shares of life cycle emissions. The benefits of the reinforcement can be observed particularly in soft to medium subgrades, where the absolute reductions in layer thickness (and thus embodied carbon) are largest19.

Design practice translates the reinforcing effect into the modulus improvement factor (MIF) or related indices such as the layer coefficient ratio (LCR), which quantify the effective increase in stiffness of the reinforced granular layer, enabling the direct conversion of stiffness gains into reduced layer coefficients or thinner designs. Measured MIFs vary with geogrid mechanical properties, aperture geometry, installation depth, compaction quality, and the stiffness difference between the granular layers and the subgrade. Hence, the optimal geogrid position is context dependent, commonly near the subgrade–granular subbase (GSB) interface or GSB-base layer interface or within the upper portion of the base or wet mix Macadam (WMM) for many practical thicknesses, though thicker bases or different loading footprints may shift this depth20–26. Because MIF values are strongly influenced by site conditions and construction quality, reliable design outcomes and defensible claims about environmental savings demand careful selection of geogrid properties and empirical field verification.

Numerous laboratory and numerical investigations have demonstrated that geogrid reinforcement enhances the mechanical behaviour of flexible pavements, increasing composite stiffness, load-bearing capacity, and resistance to rutting18,27–32. Large-scale laboratory tests and numerical models provide controlled evidence of modulus improvement, forming the basis for MIF-based design methods. However, these approaches may report optimistic values due to their inability to incorporate real-time scale effects and site variability. Factors such as compaction efforts, gradation of the materials, and accuracy of geogrid placement often differ from laboratory to field conditions33.

Field plate load test is a viable method for assessing actual stiffness improvements, as it captures the response of the pavement under realistic construction and environmental conditions. Although field plate load tests are laborious and resource-intensive, selective in situ verification is justified when design decisions depend on assumed MIF values, especially for lower subgrade CBR values, where reinforcement can provide the maximum thickness reductions. Accordingly, this study includes an in situ static plate load test at a representative site with a CBR of 10% to empirically validate the reinforcement assumption during the pavement design and strengthen confidence in the reported environmental benefits.

Role of life cycle assessment in flexible pavements

Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a standardized method for quantifying the environmental burdens associated with a product or system throughout its entire life cycle, from raw material extraction through production, transportation, construction, use, maintenance, and end-of-life management34,35. LCA translates inventory flows into impact indicators such as global warming potential, acidification potential, and abiotic depletion of fossil resources, which permit consistent comparison of alternative designs on an equivalent functional basis36,37. The literature commonly presents the LCA procedure in four stages, which are goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory, impact assessment, and interpretation of results. These stages ensure that objectives are set clearly, the necessary data are gathered in a systematic way, impacts are calculated consistently, and the findings are interpreted with transparency. The four-stage workflow of LCA is shown in Fig. 1. In the pavement context, LCA is indispensable because the environmental performance of a project depends not only on in-service emissions but also on the upstream impacts embodied in materials and logistics. Recent studies on LCA reported that LCA practice for infrastructure must prioritise transparent system boundaries, consistent functional units, and sensitivity testing, because these choices substantially influence reported outcomes38–41.

Empirical LCA studies of asphalt pavements repeatedly identify the material production stage, and especially asphalt binder manufacture and aggregate processing, as the dominant source (about 70–80%) of embodied carbon42,43. Transport of large volumes of heavy materials is commonly the second largest contributor, owing to frequent heavy-truck haulage and sometimes long distances between quarries, asphalt plants, and work sites44. Recycling reclaimed asphalt pavement reduces emissions by approximately 15%45 and displaces the use of virgin binder and aggregates. The extent of these credits depends on recovery rates and actual substitution for primary production46. These findings offer insight into the rationale behind mitigation measures, including reducing material quantity, increasing recycled content, and optimizing supply chain logistics47. Geogrid inclusion helps to achieve these goals by improving the stiffness of the reinforced layer, which enables reductions in both granular and asphalt thickness, and by facilitating the use of recycled polymers, such as polypropylene terephthalate (PET), in geogrid production. Therefore, incorporating geogrid reinforcement in flexible pavement design is recommended to achieve material savings and to quantify the resulting environmental benefits.

Past LCA studies translated these benefits into environmental terms by estimating the embodied carbon (EC), the total greenhouse gas burden of a product or system, expressed in kilograms of CO2 equivalents (kg CO2 e), for reinforced pavements. In pavement LCAs, EC is driven by raw material extraction and processing, the energy intensity of manufacture, on-site fuel use during construction and maintenance, and transport distances between quarries, plants, and worksites. Geogrid-reinforced pavements exhibit a reduction in EC values compared to unreinforced pavements, with reductions ranging from 7% to 24%, depending on subgrade strength and other factors19. These results may vary based on the aperture size and shape of the geogrid. A study by Mazurowski48 reported a reduction in EC values of about 16%−17% for a hexagonal geogrid for soft subgrade conditions.

A range of literature values for unit EC is available and is commonly applied as an inventory input when translating design quantities into life cycle emissions. Representative ranges reported in prior studies include bituminous mixes about 0.029 to 0.135 t CO2 e/t49–52, crushed aggregates about 0.005 to 0.0176 t CO2 e/t50,53,54, and geogrid products reported between about 1.98 and 4.49 t CO₂ e/t55–58. Diesel combustion as 2.9 kg CO2 e/L59–61. Variation in these unit values reflects differences in fuel type, production technology, plant efficiency, geographic context, and assumed transport distances. Researchers use these factors to convert the layer thicknesses implied by MIF-based designs into embodied carbon estimates and to compare reinforced and unreinforced alternatives on an equivalent functional basis.

Beyond global warming potential, other impact categories are relevant for a comprehensive sustainability assessment. Acidification potential, typically expressed in sulphur dioxide equivalents, is driven largely by combustion and process emissions from material production and transport62. Abiotic depletion of fossil resources or fossil energy demand quantifies the non-renewable energy embodied in material production and logistics, and is particularly sensitive to asphalt volume and fuel consumption. Comparative studies that include these categories generally find that the same material and transport drivers that dominate embodied carbon also tend to dominate acidification and fossil-energy indicators, although the proportional contributions of individual materials can differ36,39. Including these indicators, therefore, gives a more complete picture of environmental impacts when evaluating geogrid reinforcement.

Research gaps and objectives

Despite an expanding literature, existing LCA studies on geogrid-reinforced pavements still show notable limitations. Most existing LCA investigations focus on conventional flexible pavements, while studies of geogrid-reinforced designs remain scarce. Furthermore, available studies often concentrate on soft subgrades, rely on layer coefficient ratio (LCR)-based methods, or consider isolated material changes rather than systematic comparisons across a representative range of subgrade strengths. In addition, field verification of MIF values is uncommon, and end-of-life credits for reclaimed asphalt pavement are treated inconsistently in prior work.

To address these shortcomings, the current study employs MIF-based designs for varying subgrade strengths and conducts LCA for all the pavement life cycle stages. The study compares emissions over the life of reinforced and unreinforced pavement sections across a range of subgrade strengths (CBR 6%–15%). It incorporates a field plate load testing to verify the assumed stiffness improvement for a representative subgrade condition. In addition, the study introduces a performance-based indicator to quantify the environmental benefits of reinforcement, thereby establishing a direct link between structural design improvement and sustainability assessment. Furthermore, the study also evaluates acidification potential and fossil resource depletion at the material stage to provide a broader perspective on environmental performance.

Methodology

This study presents a design-based life cycle assessment framework that incorporates the MIF, a performance quantifier, into LCA to evaluate the environmental implications of geogrid-reinforced pavements. By aligning structural performance with LCA outcomes, the method enables more informed pavement design decisions over different soil subgrades. Pavement designs were produced in IITPave using IRC 3763 and IRC SP5964 procedures for subgrades (CBR 6%, 8%, 10%, and 15%). A life cycle inventory covered material production, transport, construction, maintenance, and end-of-life. Besides LCA, impacts reported include acidification potential and abiotic fossil depletion. To verify the thickness reductions due to the design MIF value, a static plate load test at a CBR 10% site provided an empirical check of the design MIF. In-situ verification of MIF across all subgrade conditions was not undertaken in the present study. Future research should include field validation of design MIF values for a broader range of subgrade conditions to reduce uncertainty in design-based life cycle CO₂ emissions.

Pavement design

Pavement sections were designed using mechanistic-empirical methods following IRC 3763 and the reinforcement provisions of IRC SP5964. Design traffic was 100 million standard axles (MSA), representative of high-volume highways. Four subgrade strengths (CBR = 6, 8, 10, and 15%) were examined to cover weak to moderately strong conditions. For each CBR value, a conventional flexible pavement was first produced in IITPave. Geogrid reinforcement was represented by a polypropylene geogrid (PP3030) placed within the granular layer. For this study, the geogrid is assumed to be located at the GSB–WMM interface, as this location is commonly adopted in most field projects. Reinforcement effects were modelled using an MIF that increases the effective modulus of the reinforced layer to capture confinement and improve load distribution. MIF values were selected from manufacturer information and the literature, and they varied inversely with CBR (study range approximately 1.85 at CBR 6% to approximately 1.40 at CBR 15%). The improved layer modulus was entered into IITPave, and the pavement was reanalysed to update layer thicknesses. Designs were evaluated using limiting-strain criteria, that is, tensile strain at the bottom of the bituminous layer (fatigue) and compressive strain at the top of the subgrade (rutting). Pavement thicknesses were thereby optimized for each CBR and reinforcement condition. The resulting layer compositions and corresponding strain values are reported in Table 1.

Calculation of life cycle emissions for designed pavement sections

Life cycle greenhouse gas emissions, expressed as CO2 equivalents, were estimated for unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced flexible pavements following ISO 1404065 and ISO 1404466, and incorporating construction sector recommendations from ISO 21931–267 to ensure methodological transparency and comparability with prior pavement LCAs. Functional unit and system boundaries are the most important parameters that affect the results. The explicit specification of the functional unit and system boundary prevents inconsistent coverage, the omission of relevant stages, or double counting in comparative assessments. For the current research, the functional unit is defined as one kilometre of a 3.5 metre wide road, a practice commonly used in pavement LCA studies to facilitate comparison across studies38. This functional unit captures the main performance attributes of the pavement system and is evaluated over a 20-year design life. In this context, geogrid reinforcement may increase the service life of the pavement or allow the same design life to be achieved with reduced material consumption, thereby improving resource efficiency. The present analysis adopts a uniform 20-year design period for both reinforced and unreinforced pavement sections to maintain a consistent functional basis for comparison. While reinforced pavements may exhibit longer service life under field conditions, the current framework evaluates performance within the selected design period. Functional units based on equivalent service life or traffic loading (e.g., per standard axle load) can represent such performance differences more explicitly and may be considered in future studies. A similar approach of adopting an identical functional unit for both pavement sections has been used in previous studies19,57, providing a consistent basis for comparison of environmental impacts. EC intensities were determined for each life cycle stage in terms of kilograms of CO2 equivalents per square metre and then converted to kilograms of CO2 equivalents per lane-kilometre for all unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced pavement designs across subgrade CBR values of 6%, 8%, 10%, and 15%.

System boundaries

A cradle-to-grave system boundary was adopted for all pavement sections considered in the current study to ensure consistent treatment of inputs and outputs. The assessment therefore covers material production (raw extraction, processing and manufacture of aggregates, asphalt mixtures, and the polypropylene geogrid), transport to site (haulage of raw and processed materials), on-site construction (fuel use and emissions from excavators, pavers, rollers and loaders), repairs and maintenance phase (scheduled overlays, patching and routine localized repairs over a 20-year service life) and end-of-life operations (milling, recovery and transport of reclaimed asphalt pavement to recycling, and allocation of reuse credits). Figure 2 illustrates the system boundaries considered in this study for assessing CO2 emissions from different life cycle stages of flexible pavements. The quantification of carbon emissions is carried out within these boundaries. Emissions during the use phase were excluded from the current scope of study, as the functional unit and traffic loading on the unreinforced and reinforced sections were assumed to be identical. It is to be noted that the use phase may also involve additional indirect environmental impacts associated with pavement characteristics, such as rolling resistance, surface roughness, road albedo, and drainage characteristics, which may influence use-phase emissions. In particular, geogrid reinforcement may improve long-term pavement performance by reducing rutting and maintaining smoother surface conditions, potentially influencing vehicle fuel consumption and associated emissions. Quantification of these effects requires advanced vehicle-pavement interaction models and long-term performance data, which are beyond the scope of the present study and are not included in the analysis. Applying identical boundaries to both unreinforced and reinforced sections remove boundary-driven bias and supports an equitable comparison of life-cycle impacts55,68. Such consistent emission boundaries are crucial for ensuring the objectivity and validity of the results. Besides, defining clear and transparent boundaries in LCA studies enables the broader application of findings to various situations.

Inventory data sources

The life cycle inventory (LCI) was compiled from existing literature on LCA studies and was supplemented with operational data obtained directly from local Indian suppliers, manufacturers, and contractors. Selected material data, including representative densities and geogrid mass, were referenced from the Raipur bypass (NH-53) project, India. The specific emission factors used in the inventory are summarised in Table 2. These emission factors were adopted from existing LCA literature and were to represent typical values within the ranges commonly reported for asphalt mixtures, aggregates, diesel (fuel), and geogrid products. For main pavement materials such as asphalt mixtures and aggregates, the selected emission factors fall within the ranges reported in previous studies and represent typical production conditions. Operational fuel consumption values for construction activities were derived from contractor equipment usage data. It is recognized that emission factors reported in different studies may vary depending on production technologies, fuel sources, plant efficiencies, and transport logistics. Therefore, to account for this variability, the study also evaluates the sensitivity of the results to changes in key inventory parameters, which provides insight into the potential uncertainty associated with the adopted emission factors.

EC values were computed separately for each life cycle stage and expressed initially as kilograms of CO2 equivalents per square metre (kg CO2 e/m²). Stagewise results were then aggregated to the functional unit and reported as kilograms of CO2 equivalents per lane-kilometre (kg CO2 e/lane-km) for all pavement sections across the considered subgrade strengths (CBR = 6%, 8%, 10%, and 15%). Equations (1) to (5) were applied to calculate emissions for the respective life stages.

The next stage includes repairs and maintenance. Maintenance activities, although intermittent, can have a significant impact on life cycle impacts over a 20-year period and are therefore included in the inventory, consistent with ISO provisions, as well as IRC guidelines and field practice. Accordingly, a 50 mm asphalt overlay is applied at year 10 for unreinforced sections and at year 15 for geogrid reinforced sections (delayed cracking) to maintain serviceability. Minor patching and localized repairs are modelled every five years. Cumulative patching over the design life is estimated to be 15% of the lane area, at a thickness of 20 mm, for unreinforced sections and 10% of the lane area, at a thickness of 20 mm, for reinforced sections. The lower patching fraction for reinforced pavements is attributed to improved load distribution, confinement, and base layer stiffness provided by the geogrid, which delays fatigue and rutting and reduces the incidence and extent of localized failures. Emissions associated with overlay include the production of the overlay asphalt, the transport of the overlay material to the site, and the construction of the overlay. Repair emissions are calculated using Eq. (5) as a proportion of overlay emissions.

Finally, for the end-of-life (demolition and reuse) stage, emissions from demolition were omitted because they are assumed similar for unreinforced and reinforced sections and would therefore largely cancel out in a comparative assessment. Consequently, only the reuse-related emissions were represented. Reuse stage calculations included milling of the asphalt layers, transport of the milled reclaimed asphalt pavement to recycling facilities, and allocation of credits for substituted virgin material. A RAP recovery rate of 30% was assumed for unreinforced sections and 35% for reinforced sections. This estimate is based on literature and field data that support the assumption that reinforced sections tend to have a longer lifespan. Consequently, their strength and quality deterioration, compared to unreinforced sections, will be lower. Therefore, this study has taken into account the minimal variation in RAP recovery. The influence of geogrids on RAP recovery depends on the type and location of reinforcement. When geogrids are used for asphalt layer reinforcement and placed within or near bituminous layers, milling operations may lead to interaction between the reinforcement materials and reclaimed asphalt pavement, potentially affecting recovery efficiency and material quality. Such a problem does not arise in the current study because the geogrid was located below the asphalt layer (at the GSB-WMM interface). As a result, the reinforcement does not interfere with asphalt milling operations, and the assumed RAP recovery rates are specific to this configuration. The transportation of milled RAP to the site is assumed to be approximately 20 km on average. The same sequence of calculations was applied to every designed pavement section across the CBR cases, and final life cycle emissions were reported by summing the contributions from all stages for both unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced pavement sections.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis

To assess the robustness of the embodied carbon estimates and identify the inventory parameters that most strongly affect life cycle outcomes, a sensitivity analysis was performed for pavement sections on a subgrade with a CBR of 6% and 15%. Sensitivity testing is a standard element of transparent LCA reporting because life cycle studies depend on many inputs that are uncertain or site-specific. Examining how outputs change with input variation improves result interpretation and highlights priorities for data improvement66. All inventory variables were varied by plus or minus 25%, while transport distance was varied by plus or minus 50%, as haul distances can differ significantly depending on source location and project logistics. The inputs examined were transport distance, truck transport emissions to the site, emission factors for WMM, GSB, and asphalt mix, the diesel combustion emission factor, diesel consumed for asphalt placement, and the PP3030 geogrid emission factor. A one-at-a-time screening approach was used to isolate and rank the influence of each input on the total kg CO₂ e per lane km. The sensitivity analysis was initially performed for the CBR 6% condition and subsequently repeated for the CBR 15% case to verify the consistency of the observed trends across different subgrade strengths. This additional case was evaluated to examine whether the sensitivity behaviour observed for the weaker subgrade remains consistent for stronger subgrade conditions within the design range considered in the study. Reporting results for the two CBR conditions (6% and 15%) is justified because the relative sensitivities, expressed as percentage deviations from the baseline, were essentially invariant across the other CBR values. Therefore, these two CBR conditions are sufficient to characterize the responsiveness of the system.

Other impact categories: acidification potential and abiotic depletion

In addition to LCA in terms of carbon emissions, other impact categories, such as acidification potential (AP) and abiotic depletion of fossil resources (ADFR), also known as fossil energy demand, were assessed for the unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced sections. AP measures how emissions from pavement materials contribute to the formation of acidifying compounds and is reported here as kilograms of sulphur dioxide equivalent per unit mass. ADFR is represented by fossil energy demand, which is the amount of non-renewable energy (such as coal and oil) consumed during each life cycle stage of flexible pavement construction, reported in megajoules per kilogram to reflect the non-renewable energy embodied in pavement materials. Studying these factors in geogrid-reinforced flexible pavements is crucial because it helps engineers understand and minimize the environmental harm associated with material choices and construction processes, ensuring more sustainable and resource-efficient roads.

Due to the lack of reproducible characterization factors in the literature for calculating AP and fossil energy demand for all life cycle stages of flexible pavements, the chosen factors for the assessment are confined to the material stage. Material masses were calculated from the designed compacted layer thicknesses and representative densities and then multiplied by the selected per-material factors. Results are reported both per square metre and per lane-kilometre. The same set of factors was applied to unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced pavement sections to ensure a fair comparison. All assumed factors are provided in Table 3. The outcomes for these two impact categories represent material-stage environmental burdens and highlight the need for targeted data collection to extend the assessment to additional life-cycle stages in future investigations. In contrast, carbon emissions are evaluated over the full life cycle, and therefore, the indicators are presented using different assessment scopes and evaluated under different system boundaries. The results can be compared across indicators, but their interpretation reflects the extent of life cycle coverage considered for each impact category, with AP and ADFR representing partial impacts and carbon emissions reflecting a more comprehensive life cycle assessment.

In-situ testing

An in situ static plate load test was carried out at the test section prepared at the site of the ongoing Raipur bypass (NH-53) project, India. Pavement layer thicknesses used for the in-situ MIF evaluation are shown in Fig. 4. A rigid, circular steel plate, 300 mm in diameter and 25 mm thick, was placed on the carriageway and loaded with a hydraulic jack rated at 500 kN. A 25-tonne excavator served as the reaction support for the jack (see Fig. 5). Loading was applied in stages to produce bearing pressures of 141, 283, 566, 990, and 1414 kPa, the last corresponding to the maximum test load of 100 kN. Settlement was recorded using dial gauges with a 0.01 mm least count and a 25 mm range, and the mean settlement at each load step was used. Bearing pressure versus settlement curves were plotted for the unreinforced and the PP3030 geogrid-reinforced sections to derive the field MIF and compare it with the design value.

Results and discussions

Life cycle emissions from LCA

EC intensities were determined for each life cycle stage in kg CO2 e/m² and then converted to the metric of kg CO2 e per lane-kilometre for all unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced pavement designs across subgrade CBR values of 6%, 8%, 10%, and 15%. To illustrate the methodology and contribution breakdown, the results for CBR of 6% are presented in Tables 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11. The same computational logic applies to the other cases, such that the single CBR of 6% dataset sufficiently exemplifies the full set of results. From Fig. 6, it is immediately evident that the raw material extraction and manufacturing stage dominates the carbon footprint, contributing more than 55% of the total embodied emissions in every design. This dominance stems from the inherently high energy intensity of asphalt production combined with the substantial mass of binder and aggregates required per square metre of pavement. These upstream energy and emissions burdens outweigh almost all downstream contributions. Following this, transport accounts for just over 30% of embodied carbon, underscoring how the haulage of large volumes of heavy materials (aggregates, asphalt mixes, geosynthetics) from quarries or processing plants to the site becomes a major emissions driver. The multiplicative effect of multiple heavy load trips with significant haul distances magnifies the transport contribution.

Maintenance and repair, cumulatively contributing about 8–10%, reflect the repeated application of materials over the design life of the pavement, together with the associated transport and construction energy each time maintenance is undertaken. By contrast, the on-site construction phase barely registers in the carbon balance, contributing only a little over 1%. This is because the construction period is short relative to the design life of the pavement, and the energy consumed in laying, compacting, and ancillary site operations is minor relative to the embodied burden already embedded in the materials.

Interestingly, the end-of-life demolition and reuse phase contributes a negative emission credit, due to the recovery and reuse of reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP). In unreinforced sections, this credit accounts for about 3% of EC, while in geogrid-reinforced sections, it is marginally higher (about 4%), particularly in designs under low subgrade strength (at CBR = 6%). The slightly greater recovery credit in reinforced pavements likely arises because reinforcement permits shallower interventions of loads by better distribution of wheel loads. It makes the asphalt layer retain its original structure with fewer fatigue cracks, resulting in good recovery with the purity of recoverable asphalt materials.

When viewed in aggregate (see Fig. 7), the total embodied carbon per lane-kilometre is consistently higher for unreinforced pavements and steadily decreases as subgrade CBR increases. It is because the weaker subgrades demand thicker structural layers, particularly asphalt, which magnifies the dominant upstream emissions. By contrast, geogrid reinforcement enables reductions in both granular and asphalt layer thicknesses while preserving structural performance, thus reducing material volumes and total embodied emissions. The relative EC savings from reinforcement are especially pronounced at the lower CBR of 6%, where thickness reductions are largest. At 15% CBR, the baseline design thicknesses are already lower, so marginal savings shrink.

To relate these results to a simple, design-ready novel sustainability metric, the Carbon Emission Reduction Factor (CERF) is introduced. It quantifies the relative reduction in life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions achieved through the use of geogrid reinforcement. CERF is defined as the ratio of the total carbon emissions released over the design life of a geogrid-reinforced flexible pavement to the total emissions associated with the corresponding unreinforced section designed for the same service period. CERF is not intended to replace existing geogrid-reinforced pavement design procedures. Instead, it is proposed as a post-design decision-support indicator that can be applied after feasible design alternatives are obtained using standard design methods. Such a factor is introduced because pavement design methodologies worldwide continue to rely predominantly on structural and functional criteria, with environmental performance still largely treated as an external consideration rather than an integrated design parameter. In contrast, several sectors, such as building construction, have already incorporated sustainability indicators into their design and assessment frameworks. Examples include the Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design (LEED) in the United States and BREEAM certification systems in the United Kingdom, which quantify energy and carbon performance to guide design decisions. The application of similar indicators in pavement design is not in practice, particularly for geogrid-reinforced flexible pavements, as reinforcement can significantly reduce material usage and emissions through design optimization. Hence, CERF can fill that gap of including a sustainability indicator in the design of geogrid-reinforced flexible pavements. The values of CERF range from 0 to 1, where values approaching zero are associated with greater emission reductions, which means higher sustainability benefits. CERF can be directly compared to environmental efficiency in various design conditions and pavement designs.

As per the total emissions from the LCA study, CERF increases with the subgrade strength. A lower value of CERF can be observed at CBR 6%, indicating a higher reduction in total emissions due to the significant material reduction contributed by the inclusion of the geogrid at lower subgrade strengths. At the same time, at a CBR of 15%, the higher CERF value was obtained, where reinforcement benefits are minimal, resulting in fewer reductions in emissions. The observed increase in CERF with subgrade strength can be attributed to the reduced structural contribution of geogrid reinforcement as the subgrade strength increases. Reinforcement primarily improves confinement and load distribution within the granular layers, which is most beneficial when the underlying subgrade is relatively weak. Under these conditions, reinforcement produces greater modulus improvement and allows larger reductions in layer thickness during pavement design. As the subgrade becomes stronger, the pavement system already benefits from higher inherent stiffness, and the additional structural contribution of reinforcement becomes comparatively smaller. Consequently, the potential reduction in material quantities and associated life-cycle emissions decreases, leading to higher CERF values at higher CBR levels.

The results highlight the usefulness of CERF as a decision-support tool, which can help design engineers to compare environmentally efficient alternative pavement configurations and support sustainability-informed decision-making at the project planning stage. Although the current research presents the concept of CERF based on a limited set of subgrade strengths, it offers significant potential for broader application. It should be noted that CERF is not inherently limited to a specific traffic level or design scenario. In this study, a single representative traffic loading condition was maintained to ensure a consistent basis for comparing reinforced and unreinforced pavement alternatives across different subgrade strengths. Because CERF is derived from the relative difference in life-cycle emissions between alternative structural designs, the indicator can also be applied to other traffic levels once the corresponding pavement designs and inventory data are established. The present analysis therefore demonstrates the applicability of CERF within the investigated design conditions while highlighting its potential use as a broader sustainability decision-support indicator. Further research and standardization would enable CERF to be incorporated into pavement design codes worldwide, supplementing current structural design methods with an emphasis on incorporating sustainability metrics into the design of geosynthetic-reinforced flexible pavements.

Findings from the sensitivity analysis

Findings from the sensitivity analysis can be used to prioritise inputs for improved data collection and for any further uncertainty analysis. Figure 8 summarises the percentage deviations in EC induced by each parameter variation for the pavement section design with a subgrade CBR 6%. As shown in Table 12, comparable percentage deviations were observed for the CBR 15% condition, indicating that the sensitivity ranking of the key inventory parameters remains consistent across the considered CBR range. Transport distance emerges as the dominant sensitivity parameter. A ± 50% change in transport distance produces the largest EC deviation of around ± 18% for both subgrade cases. This finding is consistent with previous pavement LCA studies, which highlight the multiplicative effect of hauling large material volumes and the long distances often involved in aggregate and asphalt supply chains. At the other extreme, diesel consumption for on-site asphalt placement has a negligible influence on embodied carbon with approximately 0.35–0.37% variation from baseline values. This reflects the relatively small contribution of short-duration construction fuel use compared to upstream production and transport. Across all tested variables, percentage changes from the baseline were strikingly similar between unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced designs, indicating that the relative ranking of key drivers does not depend on reinforcement. The same inputs that most affect the unreinforced design also dominate the reinforced case, albeit at different absolute magnitudes.

Acidification potential of geogrid reinforced pavements

The distribution of acidification contributions from individual material constituents is presented in Figs. 9 and 10, while the overall variation in AP with CBR values is illustrated in Fig. 11. According to the findings, the asphalt mix contributes to the highest AP, accounting for more than 86% of the total impact across all pavement sections, regardless of subgrade CBR and reinforcement type. While both the granular layers, WMM and GSB, were found to contribute significantly, ranging approximately from 6% to 8% and 4% to 6%, respectively. This material-wise contribution towards AP highlights the critical role of asphalt layer thickness in determining the acidification footprint of flexible pavement.

A comparative assessment between unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced pavements reveals a consistent reduction in AP in the latter. The higher AP values in unreinforced sections arise primarily from the requirement of greater overall pavement thicknesses, especially thicker asphalt layers, to meet design performance criteria at lower subgrade CBR values. Incorporation of geogrid reinforcement not only reduces the thickness of granular layers but also permits a reduction in the asphalt layer, thereby lowering the acidification impact. This trend is particularly evident in low CBR conditions (CBR of 6%), where the benefits of reinforcement are more pronounced.

Field test results-MIF validation

MIF value was determined following the established procedure reported in the literature. Initially, the equivalent modulus (Eeq) of the two-layer system was calculated from the bearing pressure–settlement response obtained from the field static plate load test, considering the initial linear-elastic portion of the curve for reinforced and unreinforced sections (refer to Fig. 13). Using the evaluated Eeq, the modulus of the granular layer was back-calculated separately for reinforced and unreinforced configurations75. Subsequently, the MIF was computed as the ratio of the elastic modulus of the reinforced section to that of the unreinforced section16,76,77. The measured MIF from the field is 1.72, compared with the design MIF of 1.6, which corresponds to a positive margin of 0.12 (about 7.5%). This indicates that the in-situ stiffness improvement provided by the geogrid at the test location exceeds the conservative value used in the design. Since the adopted MIF governs the reduction in base layer thickness in the reinforced pavement section, this agreement provides confidence in the resulting material quantity estimates. Consequently, the environmental benefits quantified in this study are based on design changes that reflect field behaviour, rather than on assumed performance alone.

Conclusions

The current study reveals a detailed life cycle assessment (LCA) of flexible pavements reinforced with geogrid. A comparative analysis was conducted to evaluate the environmental performance with and without reinforcing the sections across the subgrade, as measured by the California Bearing Ratio (CBR), at 6%, 8%, 10%, and 15%. Embodied carbon emissions were calculated for all major life cycle stages. Besides LCA, assessments of acidification potential (AP) and abiotic fossil resource depletion (ADRF) were conducted during the material production and manufacturing stages. A new sustainability metric, the Carbon Emission Reduction Factor (CERF), was introduced to summarize the relative reduction in life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions achieved by geogrid reinforcement. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to investigate the impact of changes in major input parameters of the data inventory, providing additional assurance on the strength of the results.

Through this integrated approach, the research advances the current understanding of how geosynthetic reinforcement influences the environmental footprint of pavements and provides a framework that links structural design decisions with sustainability outcomes. The major conclusions drawn from this work are as follows.

LCA results for both unreinforced and geogrid-reinforced sections across all CBR conditions reveal that the material extraction and manufacturing stage contributes the majority of the life cycle emissions, followed by transportation, reflecting the energy-intensive asphalt, aggregate production, and material hauling. With broader datasets, this approach can support future global pavement design guidelines that explicitly incorporate life cycle carbon and related environmental indicators.Sensitivity analysis highlighted that material inventories and transport distances exerted the strongest influence on results, while the construction phase had a minor effect.Field plate load test results showed the measured stiffness aligns with the design MIF for the tested subgrade, providing confidence in the adopted design framework.Additional environmental indicators, AP and ADRF, followed a similar pattern to the carbon emissions trend, with the material production and transportation stages contributing most to the overall impact. These findings also pave the way for developing analogous sustainability indicators to assess multiple environmental burdens alongside life cycle carbon emissions.
