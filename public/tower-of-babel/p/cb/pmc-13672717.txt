Parkinson’s disease focused multimodal wearable vision based gait monitoring system with real time feedback to support mobility and caregiver well-being
Authors: Anusha M. U., Uma Rani K., Puneeth N., Kishor M., Nemichandra S.C., Deepak Dharrao, Anupkumar M. Bongale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A chronic and progressive neurological disorder, Parkinson’s disease (PD) has a significant influence on patients and their caregivers. Motor symptoms such as postural instability, tremors, and gait dysfunction lead to increased dependency, high caregiver vigilance, and chronic psychological distress in terms of stress, anxiety, and reduced quality of life. Caregivers of patients with PD experience chronic emotional and cognitive distress due to the uncertainty and unpredictability of motor symptoms in daily life, which are not effectively managed by traditional episodic clinical evaluations. To address this gap, we propose a multimodal gait analysis and feedback system that objectively evaluates motor dysfunction and provides remote supervision capabilities that may potentially support caregiver monitoring and reduce the need for constant physical vigilance. The system combines wearable sensors IMUs and flex sensors with a vision-based pose estimation algorithm to continuously monitor tremors and gait dysfunction in a home setting. When deviation exceeds clinically defined thresholds, real-time haptic, visual, and auditory feedback is provided, and wireless data synchronization enables remote monitoring by caregivers and clinicians. In a pilot trial conducted on six patients with PD (Hoehn and Yahr stages 2–3) and one healthy control, the gait classification system achieved a mean accuracy of 88.0% across PD participants and an average feedback response time of approximately 210 ms. The alignment of sensor-derived outputs with standardised clinical scales (MDS-UPDRS Part III) supports the clinical interpretability of this system in a home monitoring context. This sensor-integrated monitoring platform is designed to reduce caregiver monitoring burden and may, in future studies with formal caregiver outcome measures, demonstrate benefit to caregiver mental health and quality of life.

Introduction

Parkinsons Disease (PD) is a progressive disorder caused by loss of neurons in the substantia nigra, a brain region that helps regulate movement through brain circuits connecting the basal ganglia, thalamus, and cortex. The decreased levels of dopamine will result in imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory pathways leading to difficulty with starting and controlling the movement. Worldwide, more than 10 million people have PD, and the majority are over 60 years old [1, 2]. Overall, due to increases in life expectancy, the health burden from PD and the need for caregivers will increase overall as well.

The primary motor symptoms of PD include tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability [3]. These symptoms cause gait dysfunction in the form of reduced step length, shuffling gait, increased variability, freezing of gait, and turning difficulty. These symptoms significantly impair a patient’s independence and are a cause of falls, fractures, hospitalization, and poor quality of life [4]. With the progression of the severity of the disease, patients require increasing supervision and help with activities of daily living.

In addition to motor symptoms, Parkinson’s disease has significant psychosocial and emotional consequences for the patient’s family, spouse, or adult child (as the primary caregiver) that encompass ongoing monitoring of mobility, preventing falls, administering medications and providing physical support. The long-term experience of caregiving has been linked with higher levels of psychological distress including anxiety, chronic stress, sleep disturbance, emotional exhaustion/overload and depression. The continuing uncertainty about the occurrence of sudden changes in motor function, freezing episodes and falling is linked to hyper-vigilance and ongoing mental distress. The burden of caregiving will ultimately impact family wellbeing and family members’ ability to cope positively with Managing Chronic Neurological Illness over time.

The standard way of treating a patient in a clinical setting is based on the subjective evaluation of patients, clinician observations, or using a standard evaluation scale, such as the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) [1, 2]. Standardized evaluations are very beneficial to clinicians, however, they are limited and not applicable to the patient in their home because the data is only evaluated during an outpatient appointment or does not capture the patient's daily motor fluctuations due to the fact that they haven't been continuously recorded [5, 6]. The absence of ongoing, objective, and multi-dimensional home monitoring of patients not only results in incomplete definition of the individual’s motor symptoms, it also creates anxiety for their caregivers who are required to manually monitor the motor symptoms without the ability to provide the caregiver with any meaningful feedback.

Some of the promising areas for the future of managing Parkinson’s disease (PD) include recent advances in digital health technologies such as wearable sensors, inertial measurement units (IMUs), vision based pose estimation using deep learning frameworks and Internet of Things (IoT) based telemonitoring. The objective and real-time analysis of gait makes it possible to quantitatively measure stride length, joint kinematics, tremor amplitude and postural abnormalities, parameters that enable the accurate and quantifiable assessment of gait dysfunction, which is an early symptom of PD. Therefore, parameters related to joint position, step asymmetry, posture stability, and dual-task performance have the potential to be used as digital biomarkers for the diagnosis of PD (and other disorders) and for treatment planning purposes [7, 8]. Sensor based remote monitoring systems are expected to not only offer clinically valid documentation of patients’ motor symptoms, but they may also improve patients’ feelings of safety, reduce the need for caregivers to be vigilant, and improve patients’ mental health status through continuous monitoring and consistency of care.

To address the limitations of subjective clinical evaluation and single-modality measurement approaches, we propose a multimodal wearable and vision based gait monitoring system that integrates deep learning based pose estimation for skeletal landmark detection with sensor-based kinematic analysis. This system will include: real-time correctional feedback, real-time connectivity to remote sites. Related work

Wearable sensors have a pivotal role in the management of Parkinson’s disease (PD), enabling objective and continuous monitoring of motor symptoms such as tremor, bradykinesia, dyskinesia, and gait dysfunction. More extensive reviews highlight the diagnostic, long-term home monitoring, and quantification of motor symptoms roles of wearable devices, with calibration against clinical rating scales like the UPDRS. Commercial products such as PDMonitor®, PKG®, STAT-ON, and FeetMe® have shown that wearable IMUs can accurately record tremor, bradykinesia, dyskinesia, freezing of gait, and fluctuations in medication with the accuracy being greater than 90% versus clinical rating [9, 10]. Prototype upper-body clothing with accelerometers and gyroscopes have also been tested for monitoring motion of the trunk and the limbs in neurological disorders with the demonstration of feasibility and patient acceptance [11].

Real-time, marker-free pose-estimation methods have recently presented themselves as a complementary, contactless option to gait and posture analysis [12]. MediaPipe and other pose-based systems support robust, real-time gait monitoring from standard video feeds or smartphone cameras, extracting joint-angle and spatiotemporal features to assess PD [13]. Smartphone pose estimation has the capability of generating joint-angle measurements within clinically acceptable error (≈5°) of conventional marker-based systems when optimal placement of cameras is used [14]. Validation research also shows that computer vision methods can measure tremor and dexterous upper-limb movement with encouraging concordance to clinician assessment and laboratory measures [15, 16]. Patient-focused research points out that ease of use, comfort, and lack of obtrusiveness are important factors for long-term uptake [17, 18].

Tracking joint kinematics using flex sensors has been investigated for quantifying fall risk in PD. Hardware tracking delays in knee bending angle and phases of gait have established that perturbed joint movement is associated with impaired gait and a greater fall risk [19]. Tremor measurement using IMU sensors on the hand or wrist consistently measures rest and kinetic tremors, including in individuals with deep brain stimulation [20, 21]. IoT-enabled wearable systems provide real-time quantification of tremor severity and support predictive analytics for clinical decision-making [22, 23].

Few studies have integrated wearable sensor measurements and vision-based parameters to classify gait impairments automatically, evaluate motor severity, and forecast fall risk in PD[24].It shows how flex-based knee angles, glove-worn IMU tremor measurements, and camera-acquired pose trajectories can be used for quantitative diagnosis and decision support [25]. A review of gait and postural assessments related to Parkinson’s disease, summarized in Table 1, has shown the continued prevalence of wearable devices and an increasing trend toward using vision-based systems for objective measurement of locomotion and posture deficits. While there have been successful implementations of these technologies for quantitatively measuring gait and tremor-related variables, the present systems remain largely passive and do not provide users with real-time feedback or visual representation of their performance. By utilizing a multimodal approach to measure tremors, posture and joint kinematics with both wearable and vision-based devices, the resulting product will be more robust than either measurement modality alone and will yield more complete information regarding a person’s gait. Specifically, this monitoring system will consist of tremor measurements obtained through inertial measurement units (IMUs), knee kinematics measured via a flex sensor and MediaPipe pose estimation, along with a graphical user interface for providing real-time haptic and auditory feedback to enable continuous monitoring and active correction of a person’s posture while they are living with Parkinson’s disease.

It is worth noting that none of the existing systems reviewed above provide real-time corrective feedback to the patient, a critical gap that the proposed system addresses directly, delivering immediate multimodal alerts that enable in-the-moment posture correction and potentially reducing fall risk, particularly in early to mid-stage PD patients who retain sufficient motor control to respond to postural cues. Beyond patient safety, continuous objective monitoring with remote alert capability is designed to reduce the need for constant caregiver vigilance, addressing the psychological burden of uncertainty that caregivers of PD patients routinely experience.

Methodology

The proposed system offers a multimodal platform consisting of wearable sensors and computer vision; the combination of the two allows for the analysis of gait in real time, as well as correcting gait deviations. This system will include IMU and flex sensors to collect kinematic data; a webcam for upright and lean assessments; a microcontroller to process the above-mentioned data; and a haptic feedback device to inform the user if there is an abnormal gait.

Data transmission and monitoring on cloud

For facilitating remote monitoring by clinicians and caregivers and for enabling real-time wireless data exchange between the wearable module and the monitoring interface, the proposed framework incorporates a cloud-based communication architecture developed using the MQTT protocol [39], which ensures secure and low-latency transmission of sensor data.

The steps involved include the programming of the NodeMCU with Arduino IDE as the main processor. Sensor data is acquired and analyzed in real time through pre-written libraries and custom algorithms. The IDE's debugging and update features streamline the integration of sensors like IMU and flex sensors. Using the IDE library for stacking WiFi modules enables data communication to and from the IoT device and/or communicating with the MQTT broker where the data will then be stored. Additionally, the feedback mechanisms (including the vibration motors) are controlled using processed data values and can create an ongoing dynamic feeding-back mechanism to assist with posture correction.

The patients were selected and staged in consultation with neurologists and classified as Hoehn and Yahr stages 2–3 mean age of 67 ± 5 years. Each patient was asked to walk for 10 min while wearing the device.

The two key measures used for the system's performance were Gait Detection Accuracy (%) and Average Feedback Response Time (ms). Discussion and conclusion

The system developed is capable of accurately identifying gait irregularities associated with Parkinson’s disease, postural irregularities, tremors, and fall instability factors. With its flex sensors, IMU modules, vibration feedback, and wireless connectivity, it can offer real-time measurement and correction of gait patterns with an inbuilt remote monitoring option. Based on trials with Parkinson's patients, the mean gait detection accuracy was 88.0% across PD participants and the response time of feedback was fast at ~ 210 ms thus, patients found the system usable and comfortable.

The system combines vision and wearable sensors to provide both corrective feedback and active motor assistance versus the single mode of operation of the single sensor solution that only provides passive monitoring. The alignment of system derived measures with MDS-UPDRS Part III items increases the clinical interpretability of this framework and suggests that it may serve as a vision and sensor based digital motor monitoring framework for remote patient assessment. While the current classification is rule based, the modular architecture supports future integration of learned classifiers without redesigning the sensing or feedback components.

The system is designed to function as a digital reassurance mechanism providing caregivers with continuous, objective visibility of the patient's motor status without requiring physical presence. Caregiver stress in PD is well documented to be driven primarily by uncertainty around falls, freezing episodes, and sudden changes in mobility. By offering remote access to real time gait data and threshold based alerts, this platform is intended to reduce that uncertainty, though it must be emphasised that no formal caregiver outcome measures were administered in the current pilot study.

During the pilot sessions, caregivers who were present informally observed that the real time GUI display showing live tremor intensity, knee flexion values, and gait category reduced their need for constant physical proximity to the patient. Several caregivers verbally noted that the visual alert system gave them a greater sense of situational awareness and control. Specifically, during the 10-min walking trials, caregivers observed the live GUI display showing real-time gait category, tremor intensity readings, and threshold alerts and informally reported that this visibility reduced their perceived need to physically follow or supervise the patient during ambulation, suggesting a potential reduction in hypervigilance-related caregiver burden. These observations are anecdotal and should not be interpreted as evidence of caregiver mental health benefit. A dedicated follow-up study is planned in which validated caregiver outcome instruments specifically the Zarit Burden Interview and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) will be administered before and after a four week home monitoring period to formally evaluate the psychological impact of system use on caregivers. This will allow the caregiver well-being hypothesis, which motivated the design of this system, to be rigorously tested.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the pilot sample (n = 6 PD participants) is small and does not support statistical generalisation, the reported accuracy and response time figures should be treated as indicative pending validation in a larger cohort. Second, the gait classification module relies on fixed rule based angular thresholds rather than adaptive machine learning models, which limits its ability to accommodate the natural heterogeneity of PD gait across individuals and disease stages. Third, the study was conducted in a controlled indoor environment, and performance in real home settings with variable lighting, camera placement, and floor surfaces has not yet been assessed. Fourth, and importantly for the framing of this paper, no formal caregiver psychological outcome data was collected and the caregiver well-being benefits described are anticipated design outcomes rather than demonstrated findings.

In future work, large longitudinal studies will be conducted measuring PD severity across disease stages, with additional spatiotemporal gait parameters incorporated into the monitoring framework. Systematic quantitative analyses will evaluate the impact of caregiving on caregiver quality of life and emotional health. The current classification uses clinician validated, rule-based thresholds, a deliberate choice for pilot transparency. Future iterations will follow three phases: (1) near-term—patient adaptive, gradient boosted classifiers trained on longitudinal sensor data (2) long-term—LSTM networks capturing temporal gait dynamics, with transfer learning from PhysioNet PD datasets. The term 'AI-driven' reflects both the current use of MediaPipe's deep learning-based pose estimation and this intended trajectory toward fully learned classification.
