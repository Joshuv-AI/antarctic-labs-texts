Effects of Nurse-Led Preoperative Visit on Postoperative Pain and Recovery among Surgical Patients in Nigeria: A Quasi-experimental Study
Authors: Demilade Raqeebat Gbadamosi, Lucia Yetunde Ojewale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Surgical patients' attitudes and perceptions of pain often impact their post-surgical pain experience and treatment effectiveness. Inadequate education and unclear postoperative pain expectations have been associated with heightened pain perception. The objective of this study was to assess the effects of perioperative nurse pre-visits on the pain and recovery level of surgical patients.Methodology:A quasi-experimental design was employed among 128 surgical patients (64 in each of the experimental and control groups), scheduled for surgery at the University College Hospital in Ibadan, Nigeria. Ethical approval was obtained from the institution's ethical review board with approval number UI/EC/24/0180 (08/4/2025-7/4/2025). The experimental group were pre-visited by the operating room nurse and received individual preoperative education and counselling. The patients in the control group received the routine information and education from generalist nurses on the ward. Using the visual analogue scale, post-surgical pain was measured within one and 24 hours post-operation. Patients' recovery level was assessed 48 to 72 hours after surgery, using a post-recovery scale. The data was analysed utilising the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. The hypotheses were evaluated using the independent t-test.Result:Patients in the experimental group had a significantly lower pain score than the control group one hour and twenty-four hours following surgery (p < 0.001). Participants' post-operative assessments showed a significantly satisfactory recovery in 96.9% of the experimental group against 82.8% of the control group (p = 0.008).Conclusion:Pre-operative surgical nurse pre-visits had a significant effect on pain and recovery among surgical patients. Therefore, measures should be taken to incorporate it into clinical nursing protocols to improve patient satisfaction and recovery.

Introduction

Peri-operative nursing encompasses ensuring a safe patient environment pre-, intra- and post-operatively, and this includes providing adequate surgery-related information.[1] The pre-operative nursing visit is a productive and secure way to provide educational and psychological support to individuals admitted for surgery.[2] It forms an integral part of nursing care and is fundamental to the physical and psychological preparation of patients, thereby positively impacting patients' anaesthesia and surgery experiences.[3] The nursing pre-visit is also an effective strategy for helping hospitalised patients and their family members cope with stress.[4]

The perioperative nurse is expected to visit before surgery to provide education and necessary information about the surgery and ongoing activities.[5] Pre-operatively, during the visit, a detailed history and pre-operative assessment, such as physical examination and ensuring pre-operative investigations, are incorporated.[6]

Pain has been described as an unpleasant bodily sensation, and patients' attitudes and perceptions of pain have an impact on pain experience and treatment effectiveness.[7] Post-surgical pain is a disagreeable, bothersome sensation and psychological experience that is thought to be brought on by nerve damage and neuroplastic changes in the central nervous system.[8] For surgical patients, postoperative pain continues to be a major source of worry, and its management could sometimes be difficult. [8,9] Yet, 80% of the 230 million surgical procedures performed annually worldwide result in postoperative discomfort, a problem affecting both industrialised and developing nations.[8] In Western countries, the prevalence of moderate to severe pain varies from 14% to 55%, with the largest frequency occurring on the first post-surgical day.[10] Additionally, it was reported that between 50 and 75 per cent of patients get insufficient pain alleviation after surgery[11], and patients still experience mild to severe post-operative pain despite analgesic administration.[8, 11]

Poorly managed post-operative pain often results in prolonged hospitalisation, increased complications, increased risk of chronic pain, lower quality of life and mortality.[8] Decreased post-operative pain can be achieved through familiarity with the operating room and surgical procedures.[12] Preoperative discomfort, anxiety, young age, and gender, specifically female gender, have all been associated with increased sensitivity to postoperative pain.[9] Pre-operative training has a significant impact on reducing post-operative pain by positively influencing an individual's behaviour and attitude.[13]

Postoperative recovery commences when surgical procedures and delivery of anaesthesia are followed by transfer to the post-anaesthesia care unit or critical care environment.[14] Recovery is defined by patients who perceive a complete return to a pre-operative state.[14] Pre-operative nursing visits have been proven to alleviate patients' adverse factors, such as psychological stress, and increase the success rate and improve recovery.[15] It has also been found useful in reducing the need for narcotic medication, post-operative complications and improving the physiological parameters of the patient.[15] Pre-surgical education conducted during pre-visit helps to alleviate suffering by raising awareness of the surgical procedure intended, pain management strategies, post-operative discomfort and anticipated signs of sepsis.[16]

In Nigeria, a few descriptive studies have been carried out on pre-operative pre-visits led by professionals in fields outside nursing.[17] However, to our knowledge, none have utilised a quasi- experimental design, limiting the ability to establish causal effects and support effective implementation of effective solutions. This study, therefore, addressed the gap by generating evidence in the local context by assessing the effects of perioperative nurse pre-visits on the pain and recovery level of surgical patients in Ibadan, Nigeria.

Conceptual framework.

The study was underpinned by Imogene King's goal attainment theory, developed in 1981 [18]. The theory presents a dynamic model centred on nurse-patient collaboration to achieve optimal care outcomes. It proposes that nurses and patients interact, develop mutual understanding, and establish shared goals through three interconnected systems: personal, interpersonal, and social. In surgical care, the personal system addresses patients' fears and perceptions of surgery, while the interpersonal system involves perioperative nurses providing education, information, and support regarding postoperative pain management. The social system highlights the nurse's advocacy role in ensuring access to resources that facilitate effective pain control and recovery. The model is illustrated in Figure 1.

Material and Methods

The study is part of a larger study on the "Effects of Peri-operative Nurse Pre-visit on Anxiety, Pain and Recovery Level of Surgical Patients in the University College Hospital, Ibadan, Nigeria". An aspect of the study that examined pre and post-operative anxiety levels has been published and is titled: 'Effects of Peri-Operative Nurse Pre-Visit on Anxiety Level of Surgical Patients in University College Hospital, Ibadan, Nigeria: A Quasi-Experimental Study' [19]. This study reports the effects of the intervention on the other two outcomes: Pain and Post-operative Recovery Level. The study adopted a pre-test post-test quasi-experimental design with two groups of surgical patients, which were the experimental and the control groups, assigned as clusters.

This study was conducted at selected surgical wards of the University College Hospital (UCH), Ibadan, Oyo State, from 23rd February to 15th August, 2024. The University College Hospital is a Federal health institution which was established in 1957 by an Act of Parliament in August 1952. It is situated between the Oyo State Secretariat and Agodi Gate. The hospital also has 12 units, 43 wards, a 946-bed capacity and five operating theatres. There are 7 adult surgical wards, namely West 1, West 2, South West 1, South East 1, burns unit, East 1 and endoscopy. In the neurosurgical division are the Frank D. Martison ward, Northwest 1, West-West 2 and NW2.

Population

Adult surgical patients above 18 years, who were admitted at least 24 hours before the surgical procedure, undergoing elective surgery using a general, spinal or combined spinal epidural and who signed an informed consent, were included in the study. Patients who had emergency surgeries and needed intensive care unit admission following surgery were excluded.

Sampling

Six surgical wards were selected using purposive sampling. A paper-ballot method was then used to assign the ward units into either a control or experimental group. The six wards were labelled, picked and placed in a jar labelled 'control and experimental'. Thus, cluster rather than individual randomisation was employed to prevent intervention contamination by having intervention and control group participants in the same ward, which could lead to exchange of information. All eligible surgical patients were selected using a consecutive sampling technique.

Data collection instrument

A structured questionnaire for socio-demographic and clinical data was developed by researchers after reviewing related and relevant published studies. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was used to assess the pain level. VAS is a pain rating scale used to quantify the patient's pain/discomfort and assess its intensity. It is on a scale of 0-10 with severity level categorised as 0=No pain, 1-3 =mild pain, 4-6= moderate pain and 7-10= severe pain.[21, 22]

The post-operative recovery scale was designed by Lobo in 2016[23] and has been validated in a study by Intekhab et al [24]. The instrument was assessed for face and content validity by an experienced perioperative nurse and three experts in medical-surgical nursing. The initial 60 items were categorised into three sub-scales: psychological status, physical independence, and pain. Since post-operative pain was assessed with the visual analogue and anxiety/psychological status was assessed using another scale (reported in another study), only the physical independence section of the post-operative recovery scale was used in the current study. This section was made up of 18 items and was revalidated in the present study by conducting a pilot study among 12 surgical patients who did not participate in the larger study. The study tools and preoperative interaction module were interpreted in English and the Yoruba language. The reliability of the post-operative recovery scale was tested using a Cronbach's alpha test. The English version reliability value was 0.947, and the Yoruba version was 0.925, making the tool very reliable.

On a "5-point" Likert scale, responses were: Never = 1, occasionally = 2, Sometimes = 3, frequently = 4, and Almost always = 5. Items that were negatively worded were reversed during the scoring. Scores were classified into (i) 18-35 = Poor recovery, (ii) 36-53= Moderate Recovery, (iii) 54-71= Good recovery, and (iv) 72-90 = Excellent recovery.

Procedure for Data Collection

Before data collection, three trained research assistants, who were nurses not involved in surgical patient care, received training on the study instrument to minimise bias. Following ethical approval, institutional permission, and written informed consent, baseline data were collected from participants in their respective wards. Patients in the intervention group received a 25-minute preoperative visit by an operating room nurse, who provided individualised education and counselling using a literature-based need-specific module. The session covered surgical procedures, operating room processes, postoperative expectations, pain management, analgesia, wound care, mobilisation, nutrition, and recovery exercises. The control group received routine preoperative information from ward nurses. Contamination bias was minimised by separating intervention and control wards and preventing interaction between groups. Postoperative pain was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale within one hour of recovery room admission and again at 24 hours after surgery. Functional recovery was evaluated 48–72 hours postoperatively using the Post-operative Recovery Scale, with assessments conducted at comparable intervals between groups.

Data Analysis

The data were entered into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 23 for analysis. Frequency, percentages and mean values were used to describe the study participants. The surgical patients' socio-demographic, comorbidity status and type of anaesthesia were analysed utilising chi-square. An independent t-test was used to compare the mean values in both groups for post-surgical pain and recovery.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Ibadan/University College Hospital (UI/UCH) ethical review committee with approval number UI/EC/24/0180(08/04/2024-07/04/2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sociodemographic characteristics

In this study, a total of 128 participants were recruited, with a 100% response rate. The majority of the participants were in the 18 - 37 years age range (43.7% and 36%; p > 0.05), close to half in each group had co-morbidity (45.4% and 42.2%; p > 0.05). Also, most were booked for general anaesthesia (68.3% and 79.7%, p > 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference at baseline in other sociodemographic characteristics, including marital status, level of education, and age.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the impact of a pre-surgical perioperative nurse visit on the pain scores and recovery levels of surgical patients. Both pain control and recovery were significantly better in the experimental group.

Findings revealed that, one hour postoperatively, over half of both groups experienced only mild pain, with some having moderate pain, but none of the patients had severe pain. This could have been due to the effect of anaesthesia in the early postoperative phase. There was a significant difference in the mean value of pain scores, with the experimental group's mean value being lower than that of patients in the control group. This finding is comparable to a previous one where the experimental group also reported a lower pain score than the control group following a pre-surgical nurse-led educational intervention among patients undergoing maxillofacial surgery in China.[20] Similar findings were reported in another study among patients undergoing decompression surgery in Athens, Greece, although a numerical pain rating scale was used for pain assessment. different pain rating scales.[25] Other authors who conducted studies in Iran [12]and Israel[26] have reported a significant reduction in pain intensity in favour of patients in the intervention group following a perioperative nurse's presurgical visit.

In contrast with the present study, one study conducted in China showed an increased demand for analgesia by patients in the experimental group following an anaesthetist's presurgical visit [27]. The authors opined that the information given during the previsit likely sensitised the patients to experience more pain. This was not the case in the current study.

Differences across studies may reflect the subjective and multidimensional nature of pain, influenced by cultural factors, educational approaches, personnel, protocols, surgical procedures, and anaesthesia types. Healthcare systems should develop patient-centred, culturally sensitive educational interventions that provide information, reduce anxiety, and support non-pharmacological pain management strategies.

Although there was a difference between the control and experimental groups, both groups recovered well after surgery, which may be associated with the high quality of medical care provided by nursing and medical staff. This result is consistent with a study on the impact of education on lowering post-operative complications, which found that the experimental group used fewer antiemetics than the control group due to a lower incidence of nausea and dizziness, which translated into a higher recovery for the experimental group.[28] In another study, the majority of surgical patients (93%) did not have complications.[29] Similarly, a study conducted on the effect of pre-operative education on quality recovery for patients undergoing lumbar discectomy shows a significant impact on recovery in terms of the ability to breathe easily and the ability to carry out the activities of daily living.[30]

Additionally, in a study on the effect of a pre-operative nurse-led orientation program on post-operative delirium following cardiovascular surgery, there was a difference between the two groups in terms of length of stay and decreased delirium, in favour of the experimental group.[31] Similarly, another study showed a better recovery in the experimental than in the control groups following adequate pre-operative education.[32] The present study, however, differs from that of Kassahun et al [33] in which there was no difference in the recovery level of the control and intervention groups.

While this study adopted an in-person surgical-nurse previsit and education, other studies have utilised video-assisted pre-operative teaching. However, patients exposed to such video-assisted pre-operative teaching did not have a significantly higher level of post-surgical recovery compared to the control group. [34, 35] This suggests that interactive and tailored preoperative education may have a greater impact than digital education, which is passive and lacks emotional support.

The difference in pre-education teaching module content and its method of delivery, or absence of it, may be associated with the discrepancies in recovery level across various settings. It could also be linked to the fact that education given before the surgical procedure did not affect the quality of care given to both groups, or it did not have an effect on either group. The difference in recovery can be directly influenced by the type of procedure done or the individual's pre-morbid condition, influencing the post-operative recovery.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study is the first quasi-experimental study in Nigeria evaluating the effect of perioperative nurse pre-surgical visits on postoperative pain and recovery among surgical patients. Its findings may guide healthcare stakeholders in developing strategies to improve surgical outcomes. The use of a pilot-tested research instrument enhanced reliability. However, the study was limited by its single-centre design, which may affect generalisability. Other limitations included absence of baseline pain assessment, potential selection bias due to non-randomised groups, variations in analgesic use, and possible observation bias. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the effect of pre-surgical perioperative nurse visits on postoperative pain and recovery using a two-group pre-test post-test cluster quasi-experimental design. Patients who received the intervention experienced significantly lower pain scores and improved recovery compared with the control group. The findings highlight the importance of perioperative nurse education and pre-visit counselling in enhancing pain management, promoting recovery, improving postoperative quality of life, and potentially reducing hospital stay duration. Incorporating structured perioperative nurse visits into hospital protocols may therefore contribute to improved surgical care outcomes.
