Academic distress and help-seeking behavior among first year university students: A correlational study of domestic and international students in the Ethiopian higher education ecosystem
Authors: Dinaol Urgessa Gita, Sintayehu Oljira Bekele, Shambel Leta Birae, Sintayehu Abebe kebede, Aschalew Terefe Refu, Bonsa Shume Tefera, Fisseha Mikre Weldmeskel, Bekalu Ferede Tefera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transitions to a new educational ecosystem result in students' academic distress, particularly at the university level. Thus, this study aimed to examine academic distress and help-seeking practices among first-year domestic and international university students in the fields of Engineering and Health Sciences. The study mainly focused on academic distress and help- seeking behavior abided by the help-seeking process model and mind sponge mechanism. A quantitative correlational research design was used. A total of 361 students were selectedusing simple random sampling. Standardized questionnaires were used to measure the variables. Descriptive statistics and Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) were used to achieve the study objectives. The results revealed that University students had moderate levels of help-seeking behavior and academic distress. Moreover, male students had higher academic help-seeking behavior and lower academic distress than their female counterparts; whereas academic help-seeking behavior and academic distress were lower for female students. Statistically significant sex differences were also noted in academic distress. Based on the mind sponge theory, measures for the prevention of academic distress should be targeted at the optimization of help-seeking behavior with a special focus on female students who manifest a high vulnerability to academic distress. Moreover, ways such as digital-based interventions that assist in addressing a large number of students at once also merit attention.

Introduction

This part of the article presents the background, theoretical foundations, and objectives of the study.

Background

The 21st-century academic landscape is defined by globalization and increased student mobility. These factors frequently trigger academic distress during the transition to university [3, 32]. To ensure construct precision, this study distinguishes academic distress from general psychological distress, such as clinical anxiety or depression [23]. Academic distress is specifically defined as the strain resulting when academic requirements exceed an individual’s adaptive resources [50].

By focusing on academically rooted stressors like heavy course loads and information overload, this research avoids overgeneralizing results into the realm of psychiatric intervention [26, 36]. Evidence indicates that academic distress is a chronic condition for many students, showing little variation throughout the year (Stallman, 2010). It is prevalent among international students [16], particularly those in high-pressure disciplines such as health sciences and engineering [4, 51].

Modern university education intensifies this experience. Students face a barrage of stressors, including unclear assignments and strained teacher-student relationships [18]. Ultimately, these psychological and behavioral manifestations are associated with a negative influence on academic performance. This necessitates a clearer understanding of how students manage specific academic demands [46].

Academic distress arises when cognitive and external resources are overwhelmed by factors such as poor time management, intense competition, and rigid assessment deadlines [1, 8, 28, 39]. While help-seeking behavior serves as a critical protective factor, it remains poorly addressed in university settings compared to the general population [5, 15]. Research indicates that despite high rates of depression and suicidal ideation among students, less than one-third seek professional support [22, 43].

Failing to seek help can exacerbate negative outcomes, including substance abuse and self-harm, with risks appearing significantly higher among junior students [12, 40, 49]. Existing literature, however, predominantly examines help-seeking from a healthcare perspective rather than an academic one and frequently overlooks gender disparities [6, 7]. This study addresses these gaps by examining the specific relationship between academic distress and help-seeking behavior within the student population.

In the present study, along with a new organizational ecosystem for both domestic and international students, system changes as a result of new education policy development in Ethiopia were assumed to be linked with fluctuations in distress. Thus, understanding students’ help-seeking practices is associated with a potential reduction in academic distress. The current study, focused on academic distress and help-seeking behavior, is conceptually guided by the help-seeking process model and mind sponge mechanism. Thus, the underlying objectives of the study were to (1) identify the practice of university students’ help-seeking behavior based on a help-seeking process model, (2) examine gender differences in academic help-seeking behavior and academic distress, and (3) identify differences in academic distress and help-seeking practice among domestic and international students.

Theoretical foundation

Documented evidence suggests that all students at different levels of schooling, particularly at the university level, possess different socio-demographic profiles, identities, career ambitions, and information. As a result, when they enter university, which is a new education ecosystem, many develop a sense of competition to join a discipline in which they perceive that they could get better employment opportunities and fulfill their career aspirations. Amidst this, mental distress is common among the student population [6] and help-seeking behavior and/or suicidal behavior represent two extreme options [34].

The current study utilizes a dual-theoretical approach to interpret the relationship between academic distress and student behavior. First, Nelson’s [33] Academic Help-Seeking Model provides a structured, stage-based taxonomy ranging from the internal awareness of need to the behavioral reaction to help-seeking attempts. Second, the Mind sponge Theory [47] is employed as a heuristic framework to contextualize how environmental pressures are absorbed or ejected through a student’s cognitive comfort zone.

Critically, given the cross-sectional, correlational design of this study, these frameworks are not treated as actively tested causal models or structural pathways. Instead, they serve solely as a qualitative, interpretive lens to conceptually discuss the awareness-to-reaction gap and to understand the psychological evaluations students undergo when navigating systemic higher education pressures.

In the context of Ethiopian higher education, this framework is useful for interpreting how students perceive the value of specific disciplines (e.g., Health Science, Engineering, or Business fields) versus the stressors they encounter. Furthermore, cultural and ideological changes in the educational curriculum, as well as a wide-open competition to join disciplines that help them earn more money, necessitate significant effort, dedication, and sacrifice on the part of these students.

Help-seeking process model

This conceptual diagram illustrates Nelson’s five distinct layers of help-seeking behavior among students, mapping out a sequential process that progresses from internal cognition to final behavior. The process originates at the first, initial layer, where a student becomes aware of the need for help. It then moves forward through the second layer, deciding to seek help, and the third layer, identifying potential helpers. From there, the student enters the fourth layer by employing specific strategies to elicit help, before finally culminating in the fifth and final behavioral layer: reacting to the help-seeking attempts (See Fig. 1 below).

University student’s mind-sponge contextual framework

Mind sponge model is also an interpretive framework for both international and domestic university student experiences, utilizing a pie-shaped diagram structured by distinct, color-coded layers. The outermost perimeter of the pie, shaded in yellow, denotes the cultural and ideological macro-settings surrounding the student. Moving inward, the intermediate blue zone depicts the student’s psychological comfort zone, while the innermost red-colored nucleus represents their core mindset and set of values. Inward-pointing arrows reflect appropriate, incoming contextual and academic information evaluated for absorption, whereas outward-pointing arrows represent unaligned values, inappropriate information, or stressors evaluated for ejection from the comfort zone and mindset. Like all individuals [10, 48], university students possess a mindset shaped by cultural values [42, 45] and personal predispositions. These include states of reactance [29] and reluctance [13], as well as self-reliance beliefs, feelings of inferiority [14, 44], and self-inflicted stress [35] all of which may correlate with help-seeking behavior. Conversely, while students face internal distress, external factors such as social circles (peers, family, and administrators), institutional accessibility, service modality [14], and general life challenges [20] are critical. These environmental elements help students evaluate their needs and decide whether to discard maladaptive mindsets in favor of seeking support.

Objectives

Thus, this study attempts to align the help-seeking process model with the mind-sponge process as follows: First, the help-seeking processes that university students undergo as a means of self-preservation are important and need to be encouraged by all education partners. In this regard, when students have experienced and accessed important help-seeking information, they eventually empower themselves against academic distress. However, according to the help-seeking process model, students master different levels of help-seeking because not all students go through the five stages in a fully-fledged manner. Thus, students who exercise the first stage of the help-seeking process (becoming aware of the need for help) are inevitably less advanced than those who exercise the last process, which is reacting to help-seeking attempts.

Moreover, when people encounter serious distress, help-seeking and suicidal ideations are two extreme options, even though there are other coping mechanisms such as substance abuse and escape or avoidance of the situation. Thus, this study was interested in examining the help-seeking behavior of university students as a cost-benefit judgment between living with academic distress and help-seeking behavior.

Materials and methods

Study design

A correlational research design of the quantitative approach was used to examine help-seeking behavior and academic distress among health and engineering university students. Curtis et al., [11] state that correlational research establishes statistical patterns between two seemingly interrelated variables. Thus, a correlational design was employed to examine the relationship between academic distress and help-seeking behavior.

Study participants and sampling strategy

The current study was conducted among health sciences and engineering university students enrolled in regular first-year Jimma University programs. Therefore, the data source was an original survey conducted in Ethiopia. The total number of students enrolled in regular health and engineering programs was 2,049, of which 1,617 were males and 432 were females. A simple random sampling technique was applied to provide equal and non-zero chances to all study participants. To this end, 361 students (225 males and 136 females) were sampled as study participants.

Measurement tools

Data were analyzed using SPSS and AMOS Version 25. While AMOS was utilized for basic data management and covariance assessments, formal Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were not conducted due to the exploratory nature of adapting the Help-Seeking Scale to the Ethiopian ecosystem.

As a result, internal consistency was established via Cronbach’s alpha (alpha = 0.731) and Composite Reliability {CR} = 0.734). The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) fell below the ideal 0.50 threshold, yielding a value of 0.414. In line with established psychometric literature, an Average Variance Extracted {AVE} < 0.50 accompanied by a composite reliability composite reliability (CR) = 0.70 remains adequate for establishing basic construct reliability in initial or highly localized adaptations. However, this measurement threshold explicitly indicates that a notable portion of variance is attributable to measurement error. Consequently, this limitation dictates that subsequent inferential group differences (such as those identified via MANOVA) was interpreted as preliminary and suggestive rather than definitive, as observed variances may partially reflect measurement error rather than purely substantive differences.

Academic distress was measured using the University Stress Scale (USS), validated by Stallman and Hurst [41], which yielded a pilot study reliability of alpha = 0.83 and a final pilot coefficient of alpha = 0.81. For the construct of Academic distress in this study, the results indicated acceptable internal consistency, with alpha = 0.735 and composite reliability {CR} = 0.736, respectively, though its individual Average extracted {AVE} was 0.320. To ensure construct precision, all items were positively stated and focused on academically rooted stressors.

Academic help-seeking behavior was assessed using a scale adapted from Payakachat et al. (2013), structured into five subscales corresponding to the five stages of Nelson’s [33] model. Regarding the original scale’s relatively weak reliability (alpha = 0.66), a pilot study was conducted among 31 first-year students. Following this pilot, the refined scale demonstrated an improved and acceptable internal reliability of alpha = 0.73. Specifically, help-seeking behavior demonstrated adequate reliability, yielding alpha = 0.731 and composite {CR} = 0.734. According to Hair et al. [19], values exceeding the 0.70 threshold are considered indicative of a reliable instrument, suggesting that the items within each scale consistently measure their respective constructs. All items are positively stated (Table 1).

Operationalization of help-seeking stages

Each stage of the help-seeking process was measured by multiple items on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Rather than converting responses to percentages, Mean Scores (M) and Standard Deviations (SD) were calculated for each subscale stage to maintain interval data integrity for the subsequent MANOVA and correlational analyses. Finally, to manage potential barriers to understanding, participants were provided with questionnaires in their preferred language version (English, Afan Oromo, or Amharic), ensuring the items accurately captured the intended constructs. All researchers reviewed the questionnaires to check their content and face validity. Construct validity was also assessed, and a moderate factor load was observed.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were double-entered into EpiData 4.6.0.6 for quality management and then exported to the Statistical Package for Social Science Analysis of Moment Structures (SPSS AMOS) Version 25. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were used to examine the sociodemographic characteristics of study participants and to evaluate students’ help-seeking practices across the model’s five stages.

Pearson correlation was used to examine the relationships between the study variables. Separate one-way multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA) were conducted to test for mean differences in academic distress and help-seeking behavior across gender (male vs. female) and residency status (domestic vs. international). Descriptive statistics for the four sub-groups resulting from crossing these independent variables were also compiled to provide a comprehensive baseline for evaluating sub-group patterns.

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations received meticulous attention since the study was conducted on human subjects. The research protocol was submitted to the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the psychology department prior to data collection. After thorough evaluation, comments, and guidance, an approval letter (Ref: PSYC/37/2021) was obtained from the Department of Psychology. All ethical, legal, and contextual norms and standards that care for human subjects were carefully applied. The privacy and confidentiality of the respondents were ensured, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

First-year students’ help-seeking practice

Students’ academic help-seeking practices were examined using the subscale mean scores corresponding to the Nelson [33] help-seeking process model. This model captures the cognitive and behavioral phases of students’ help-seeking paths. Rather than utilizing frequencies or raw percentage boundaries, the distribution of responses was evaluated via interval data properties (M, SD) and the binary probability distributions derived from the sample (N = 361).

The empirical results reveal that the internal cognitive components of the help-seeking model yielded the highest mean engagement. Specifically, becoming aware of the need for help reached a mean score of M = 87.80 (SD = 32.73, SE = 1.72), and deciding to seek help yielded a mean score of M = 84.21 (SD = 36.46, SE = 1.92). However, as the process shifted toward active behavioral selection and execution, engagement systematically declined. Identifying potential assisters dropped to a mean of M = 45.15 (SD = 49.76, SE = 2.62), while employing a strategy to elicit help resulted in a mean of M = 42.66 (SD = 49.46, SE = 2.60). The final step, reacting to help-seeking attempts, demonstrated the lowest level of engagement (M = 40.99, SD = 49.18, SE = 2.59). This distinct downward trajectory indicates that as students move from the internal cognitive processing of academic help-seeking to external behavioral implementation, execution scores drop by half (Table 3).

Gender differences in academic distress and help-seeking practice

Sex differences across the primary study variables were examined to evaluate data patterns relevant to designing supportive interventions [2].

The internal consistency of the scales was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and Composite Reliability (CR). Both academic distress (alpha = 0.735, CR = 0.736) and help-seeking behavior (alpha = 0.731, CR = 0.734) demonstrated acceptable reliability, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. However, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for both constructs fell below the 0.50 benchmark Average variance extracted {AVE} = 0.320; = 0.414). This confirms that while the items are cohesive, a greater portion of the variance captured by the indicators is due to measurement error than to the underlying construct. Consequently, the following inferential statistics was interpreted as preliminary patterns rather than definitive conclusions.

A series of one-way ANOVAs within the MANOVA framework examined the effect of gender on students’ academic distress and help-seeking behavior. The results for academic distress indicated a statistically significant difference between groups, F (1, 359) = 11.324, p = .001, eta_p^2 = 0.031. Specifically, male students reported higher levels of academic distress (M = 2.90, SD = 0.75) compared to female students (M = 2.63, SD = 0.75). The partial eta-squared value (eta_p^2 = 0.031) indicates that gender accounts for only 3.1% of the variance in academic distress, representing a small effect size.

In contrast, the analysis of overall help-seeking behavior revealed no statistically significant difference between male participants (M = 3.22, SD = 0.72) and female participants (M = 3.33, SD = 0.67), F (1, 359) = 2.224, p = .137, eta_p^2 = 0.006. These findings suggest that gender does not show a statistically meaningful relationship with general help-seeking tendencies within this sample (Table 4).

Differences in academic distress and help-seeking practice among domestic and international students

Because university students navigate a complex matrix of external environmental conditions and internal behaviors, academic experiences frequently differ by residency background. A one-way MANOVA was conducted to test whether there was a significant difference between domestic (n = 290) and international (n = 71) students across the primary measures.

A significant multivariate difference was observed between domestic and international student populations, Wilks’ Lambda = 0.758, F (2, 358) = 57.12, p < .001. Univariate follow-up testing indicated a statistically significant difference in academic distress scores, F (1, 359) = 109.34, p < .001, eta_p^2 = 0.233. Domestic students reported notably higher scores for academic distress (M = 2.91, SD = 0.69) than their international counterparts (M = 1.99, SD = 0.57). The partial eta-squared (eta_p^2 = 0.233) indicates a strong multivariate effect size, showing that residency status accounts for 23.3% of the variance in distress scores. No statistically significant differences were observed for general help-seeking behavior between domestic (M = 3.27, SD = 0.65) and international students (M = 3.35, SD = 0.83), F (1, 359) = 0.714, p = .399, eta_p^2 = 0.002 (Table 5).

Discussion

Academic distress and help-seeking appear to be complex outcomes within higher education settings, where internal student characteristics interact with broader environmental pressures. This study highlights an awareness-to-reaction gap [33]; while distress is common during academic transitions, it frequently fails to correspond with practical help-seeking behavior. Consistent with general literature reporting non-utilization tendencies between 58% and 63% [13] and specialized service utilization as low as 12.9% [17], this research found that most students demonstrate a high cognitive awareness of help requirements but lower scores on behavioral implementation.

The lack of a statistically significant association between overall academic distress and proactive help-seeking behavior can be conceptually interpreted through Mind sponge Theory [47]. Specifically, the mind sponge framework can be used as a heuristic lens to suggest that pre-existing cultural or social values might act as institutional filters, leading students to reject help-seeking options before they translate into concrete behaviors [48]. However, because this study did not include direct empirical measures of internal cognitive filtering, information processing mechanisms, or cultural value orientations, these cognitive filtering explanations must be considered speculative, theoretically plausible hypotheses rather than empirically verified conclusions.

This shared pattern appears to cross gender boundaries. Although this study found that male students reported slightly higher academic distress scores in this sample which contrasts with some literature noting elevated vulnerability in female cohorts [25, 31] it aligns with local assessments showing high stress in masculine academic subcultures [3]. Crucially, no statistically significant gender differences were observed in actual help-seeking behaviors. This suggests that local systemic pressures and institutional barriers may be pervasive enough to suppress typical gender-based variations, resulting in a uniform pattern of help-seeking underutilization across the student body.

The finding that international students experienced lower academic distress (M = 1.99) than domestic students (M = 2.91) represents an intriguing divergence from standard international student migration literature, which typically records elevated acculturative distress. Conversely, this outcome uncovers a unique pattern regarding international students. Contrary to the traditional leveling effect where international distress usually matches or exceeds domestic levels [24], the international students in this cohort exhibited lower distress scores. A review study that includes 283,412 university students corroborate the current finding indicating that in high quality paper included for review, international students had lower anxiety and stress when compared to domestic students [52]. A study by LaMontagne et al., [27], depicted that international students had better help-seeking propensity and lower depression compared to domestic students. In contrary, Farnaz et al., [16], for instance, dictates international students demonstrated higher academic distress because of lower social support, housing and cultural adjustments. While such contradicting evidence are salient, the perspective from which literature make assertions vary.

From a purely conceptual perspective, utilizing the Mind sponge framework, one could theorize that the extensive preparation required for international academic mobility equips these students with flexible cognitive strategies and a structurally more open comfort zone for absorbing institutional support [32]. However, because this study lacked empirical data on essential contextual confounding variables such as country of origin, socioeconomic status, financial support structures, prior academic achievement, or specific language proficiencies this resilience based interpretation remains highly speculative.

Alternative explanations must be highlighted: first, the international cohort may represent a highly select academic or socioeconomic elite possessing pre-existing support structures unavailable to local domestic peers. Second, the prominent sample size asymmetry (290 domestic vs. 71 international students) may introduce sampling variance that could affect group comparisons. Consequently, this finding should be interpreted with caution and might not be good enough to generalize beyond the current sample.

Finally, the psychometric limitations of the adapted instruments require careful consideration. The low Average Variance Extracted values (AVE = 0.320, AVE = 0.414) indicate that a significant portion of the variance in these measures is due to measurement error rather than the intended constructs. As a result, the observed differences between genders and residency groups may partially reflect measurement error rather than purely substantive behavioral differences. These comparative findings should therefore be treated as preliminary and illustrative rather than definitive findings.

Conclusion

The mind sponge mechanism, which considers an individual’s mindset, comfort zone, and information processing qualities, was considered a useful conceptual lens for the present study. When students enter higher education institutions and experience a sense of pressure, receiving assistance from others is linked to positive outcomes. Based on our descriptive findings, we conclude that university students in this sample experienced moderate academic distress, and their help-seeking scores reflected higher cognitive awareness than behavioral execution.

Measures for managing academic distress should focus on supporting actual help-seeking behaviors and promoting assertive behavior, with appropriate attention to vulnerable student groups. Furthermore, student-centered interventions merit attention in managing distress and boosting help-seeking practice. In support of this, digital interventions focusing on students’ help-seeking behavior [9, 21, 30, 37, 38] suggest that digital support tools can contribute to more effective help-seeking practices among students. To achieve this, the collaborative roles of educators, peers, administrators, and educational partners are valuable.

Consequently, this study suggests that institutional transition programs should account for help-seeking and help-giving dynamics. Utilizing the mind sponge model as a qualitative framework helps outline how students weigh the perceived costs and benefits of transition, highlighting information processing as an area for further study. Ultimately, it is essential to reiterate that the Mind sponge framework and the help-seeking phases were not empirically modeled as structural pathways in this study; instead, they functioned exclusively as interpretive tools to contextualize quantitative trends.

Limitations and ways forward

While this study offers valuable insights, its contributions must be considered alongside some key limitations. First and foremost, the cross-sectional framework relies on a correlational design that provides merely a temporal snapshot; consequently, it precludes the ability to track the evolution of academic distress and help-seeking behaviors across an entire academic year. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reporting measures introduces potential social desirability bias, meaning that participants may have under-reported distress or over-reported help-seeking intentions to align with perceived social norms. On a psychometric level, the reported Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values fell below the standard 0.50 threshold, which indicates that the variance may partially reflect measurement error rather than purely substantive differences; as a result, the reported group differences must be interpreted as preliminary rather than definitive findings. This measurement constraint is compounded by a distinct sampling asymmetry specifically, an imbalance between domestic (n = 290) and international students (n = 71) which ultimately constrains the generalizability of the comparative findings.

Moreover, because the study lacked empirical metrics on unmeasured contextual confounders such as international students’ country of origin, linguistic backgrounds, socioeconomic status, and prior academic history, any resilience-related interpretations remain tentative hypotheses that require replication in more balanced populations. Finally, the statistical design did not evaluate formal inferential interaction effects between gender and residency status, thereby limiting the ability to determine if residency effects function uniformly across both genders.

To overcome these limitations and achieve robust empirical validation of these cognitive mechanisms, future research should employ longitudinal designs and incorporate Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to formally test structural pathways over time. By moving toward these advanced methodologies, researchers can explicitly measure and control for socioeconomic backgrounds, language metrics, and academic history. Ultimately, this approach will make it possible to determine whether student resilience is a product of pre-existing resources or a result of specific cognitive filtering processes.
