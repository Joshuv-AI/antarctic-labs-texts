Collective surfing of single cells on a chemo-attractant wave using multiscale Eulerian velocity vector field
Authors: Sulimon Sattari, Md. Motaleb Hossain, Udoy S. Basak, Mikito Toda, Takeharu Nagai, Satoshi Sawai, Kazuki Horikawa, Tamiki Komatsuzaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wavelike motion mediated by chemotactic signaling occurs in various biological phenomena including neutrophil swarms, wound healing, and amoeba aggregates. However, the macroscopic transition from independent to collective cellular behavior remains unclear, including how to quantify the response of individual cells to a developing chemotactic wave. Recent advances in molecular imaging allow concurrent observation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) concentrations and cell movement at individual resolution. Employing particle image velocimetry (PIV), a scheme to extract Eulerian velocity vector fields in fluids, we derived velocity fields at different Gaussian blurring levels and found that while original fluorescent images reflect cell movement, blurred versions highlight cAMP wave propagation. We identified the phase of cAMP signal wave dynamics and analyzed the interplay between single-cell motility and cAMP wave development. The extracted velocity fields at single-cell resolution show an almost antipodal relationship to those of the cAMP wave characterized by the blurred image, with an angle close to 180° during the rise of the wave when the spiral wave is well developed. Furthermore, single-cell dynamics collectively move toward the crest of the coming cAMP wave but rest (with randomized directionality) in the troughs between waves, akin to “surfing of the collectives”.

Introduction

Collective cell migration underpins several crucial biological phenomena, including wound healing1, tissue morphogenesis2–5, bacterial colonization6, and tumor growth7. Cells in an autonomous state can spontaneously begin to migrate collectively, triggered by external or internal stimuli such as starvation. Traveling wave chemotaxis, or the movement of cells in response to the chemical gradients perceived in the passing of a chemical wave, plays an essential role in collective motion in neutrophils8,9, epithelial cells10, and the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum (Dd)11,12.

Dd is regarded as a prime model for exploring cell collective migration via traveling wave chemotaxis11,12. Chemotactic cell migration is essential for the transition of Dd cells from a unicellular to a multicellular mode of life, a process that allows adaptation to unfavorable conditions and ultimately promotes survival and reproductive success through spore formation and dispersal. Upon starvation, individual cells initiate a multicellular developmental program driven by the periodic relay of cAMP signaling9,11–15. During aggregation, the cells assemble into a multicellular structure that subsequently differentiates into a fruiting body composed of a stalk and a spore mass. In this work, we focus on the velocity and cAMP dynamics of cells in between starvation and aggregation. During this transition, cAMP is synthesized inside cells upon external stimulation by cAMP released by nearby cells, and the Flamindo2 fluorescent indicator of cAMP concentration detects the change of cAMP concentration inside of each single cell16. How a sequence of cAMP signals emitted by individual cells along the development of a spiral wave pattern makes the transition from independent to collective behavior remains unclear.

The directionality of cell motility against cAMP wave propagation has been recently addressed using a chemotactic index (CI) that measures the ratio between the difference in cell position after a finite time interval and its integration of cell displacement in the direction of the cAMP source. By designing a pseudo-1D geometry confinement of cell movement at different timescales of wave propagation, it was found that cells tend to align to the wave front of the incoming cAMP wave (sensing the concentration gradient in front) while they do not follow the gradient backward when the cAMP wave passes, which were insensitive to the speed of wave propagation at least in the range investigated17. However, CI computation requires individual cell tracking, which is often challenging to automate in Dd aggregates as an increase in cell density, as well as quantification of the wave direction — performed either by designing a wave in a 1D channel17 or by fitting transient wave patterns along the sequence of cell stages to the well-developed spiral wave 18.

In this report we will show that the introduction of particle image velocimetry (PIV), together with analysis of cAMP dynamics in their unblurred and blurred fluorescence images, enables a systematic quantification of cell motions relative to local wave phase across a live colony over a broad range of developmental stages, from approximately two hours after the onset of starvation up to ∼17 hours after starvation. To our knowledge, this provides the first systematic, phase-resolved examinations of this behavior, despite an established record of qualitative and semi-quantitative observations13,14,19–22.

It is of crucial importance to quantify the interplay between the global feature of cAMP waves and local cell entities from fluorescent images, including the movements of cell entities, the direction of cAMP wave propagation, and their mutual relationship along each cell stage from starvation in 2D space. Here, we present a scheme to characterize the overarching trends in cellular motility under the influence of a developing cAMP wave. The crux is the combination of PIV, wavelet-based multiscale phase analysis, and Gaussian blurring (coarse graining). PIV computes Eulerian dynamics (flow field) from sequences of fluorescent Dd images24,25 at different blurring scales. In summary, we found that the larger the spatial scale in the blurring, the more the details of the single cells are masked, and, instead, the more the mesoscopic cAMP wave patterns are highlighted. That is, the PIV vector field of the nonblurred image reflects the cell dynamics at the single cell scale, whereas that of the blurred image represents the propagation of the cAMP wave. An almost antipodal relationship exists between the vector field associated with the blurred and that with the non-blurred images. Especially near the rising part of the cAMP wave where significant concentration gradients exist, the antipodals are most enhanced (as shown in Fig. 1). We further identify the phase of the cAMP wave, based on a wavelet multiscale decomposition along the development of the cAMP wave pattern after the starvation of Dd, and scrutinize how cell motility depends on the phase of the cAMP wave. It is suggested that single cells change their orientation with increasing their velocities toward the normal direction (say \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vec {n}$$\end{document}) of cAMP propagation near the rising part (in concentration c) of the cAMP wave, but, after the cAMP wave passes by, single cells cannot adapt their motility to the change in sign of cAMP concentration gradient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial c/\partial \vec {n}$$\end{document}).

Computing Eulerian velocity fields at different blurring scales

To blur cAMP intensity profiles, we employ a simple Gaussian filtering: we compute a blurred cAMP intensity profile \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_b(x,y;\sigma )$$\end{document} at each pixel (x, y) by taking the weighted mean1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I_b(x,y;\sigma ) = \frac{\sum _{x',y'}{G(x'-x,y'-y;\sigma ) I(x',y')}}{\sum _{x',y'}{G(x'-x,y'-y;\sigma )}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the summations are taken over all pixels (x', y'), I(x',y') quantifies cAMP intensity (cAMP concentration) inside single cells at the pixel position (x',y') —computed from Flamindo2 fluorescent signal B(x',y') by multiplication of minus because lower Flamindo2 fluorescent signal corresponds to higher cAMP intensity (see also Supplementary Materials II), and G(x'-x, y'-y) is a Gaussian function centered at (x, y)26: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G(x'-x, y'-y;\sigma )=\frac{1}{2\pi \sigma ^2}e^{-[\frac{(x'-x)^2+(y'-y)^2}{2\sigma ^2}]}$$\end{document} where σ is a parameter that defines the spatial scale of blurring. When blurring is not applied (which we will refer to as σ =0), or when σ is set to a value lower than the typical size of single cells, the cAMP intensities are anticipated to reflect the movement of individual cells in subsequent frames. As σ increases, the positions of cells begin to be “blurred out,” and at values of σ much larger in size than the resolution of single cells, they become largely “blurred out” and only the local cAMP intensity due to a group of single cells is resolved within the given large σ. In that case, only the propagation of the cAMP wave is observed for subsequent frames (when σ becomes much larger than the wavelength of the cAMP wave, one cannot even differentiate the wave pattern).

Here, the Eulerian velocity flow fields are evaluated using the most commonly studied PIV27–30. In short, PIV first partitions consecutive image frames into uniform grids and then employs cross-correlation to establish cell displacement vectors (see Supplementary Materials I for the description of PIV analysis. See Supplementary Fig. S1 for a comparison of cell images in two frames and the corresponding extracted PIV vectors. See also Supplementary Fig. S2, where two consecutive frames (90 seconds apart) are superimposed in two colors with the corresponding PIV vectors overlaid, directly confirming at the single-cell scale that the PIV vectors capture the displacement of individual cells.). The acquired velocity vector field reflects the underlying system motility. As recently shown, PIV mimics velocity vectors of the underlying particles with varying levels of coherence with their neighbors31. Note here that only the difference between Flamindo2 fluorescent images B(x, y) and cAMP intensity profile I(x, y) are the multiplication by a minus sign such as B(x,y)=-I(x,y) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_b(x,y;\sigma )=-I_b(x,y;\sigma )$$\end{document}, and the directions and sizes of PIV vectors are exactly the same for the usage between B(x, y) and I(x, y) because the cross-correlation in PIV analysis is invariant under the change of sign of the 2D image.

Note that (local) cell density is not homogeneous and is dependent on stages, especially at very later stage where the spiral wave is well developed and single cells are aggregated to form a united unit called a mound. Although cell density is not strictly homogeneous over the space, change in cell density per grid element (134 μm × 162 μm) along consecutive neighboring time frame (recorded every 1.5 minutes) is negligible in comparison to the timescale of the spiral wave firing dynamics causing variations in blurred intensity because the typical timescale of cell movements is ∼5 μm/min18.

cAMP frequency in Dd cells varies over time and space. In the early stages (around t<4 hours), no apparent oscillatory behavior was observed in the cAMP signal. It was reported that individual cells﻿’ cAMP intensities are almost constant just after starvation33. Slow fluctuations in cAMP during this regime may arise from a changing baseline dependent on space due to cells coming in and out of the window dependent on the location of grid elements. Roughly after t ≃ 4 hours, fast oscillations start to be visible, and their frequency continues to vary slightly over time and space (see Fig. S6 in Supplementary Materials IV).

Outlook

The correlation between the cAMP wave and cell movements has been realized in early studies13,14 as well as more recently17,18,21. However, despite these observations, there has been surprisingly little systematic quantification of the correlation between local cAMP wave phase and cell motions from starvation to developing spiral cAMP wave. This work may be regarded as the first systematic and quantitative elucidation of their relationship.

Our analysis may suggest that the relationship of single cell motility with cAMP wave is regarded as “surfer” aggregates collectively move (actively) toward the direction of the wave coming whose direction is recognized by visual sense (in the case of cells, it is recognized by cAMP gradient), but, just after the wave passed, along which surfers had (passively) followed by riding on it, no surfer moves toward the direction of the wave leaving and their collective patterns disappear in the trough until being able to see the next approaching wave.

It should be noted that the relationship between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle \cos \theta \rangle$$\end{document} and the phase we characterized from 2D fluorescent images at the late stage where the cAMP spiral wave is well developed is consistent with the result of the chemotactic index and time in the 1D channel experiment making cells approximately confined in a 1D space where the cell movement and the cAMP wave direction are well identified17. The work in this report not only confirms the 1D confined experiments but also generalizes the method to situations where neither cells are confined in the limited space nor the spiral cAMP wave is well-developed.

Apparent mismatch reported in earlier literature between extracellular and intracellular cAMP dynamics–where intracellular cAMP appears to lag behind extracellular waves–has been noted in classic cell suspension assays39, which showed an approximately one-minute shift between peaks of intracellular/optical density signals and extracellular cAMP pulses. However, this delay is thought to arise from methodological artifacts in the experimental procedures. More recent measurements (Ref.40, Fig. 4) do not show such a temporal shift, although they do indicate that extracellular cAMP decays more slowly. Consequently, observations from the Flamindo2 reporter, which reports intracellular cAMP, that seem to suggest cellular inertia–cells continuing to move after passage of the wave crest–may instead reflect persistence of an extracellular cAMP gradient that is not directly detected by the reporter.

We anticipate that this framework is capable of differentiating different types of collective migration, chemokinesis41 from chemotaxis, which is not necessarily considered to accompany directional collectives in response to cAMP, and also elucidating the roles of individual cells such as leader and follower cells in the development of cAMP wave31,42. They are among the forthcoming subjects to be uncovered using higher-resolution fluorescent images, coupled with a trans-scale optical imaging microscope developed recently with a field of view of more than a centimeter and a spatial resolution of a few micrometers, detecting more than a million single Dd cells over many hours33,43,44.
