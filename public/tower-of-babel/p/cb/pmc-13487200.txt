Temporal self-similarity reveals percolation universality classes in complex networks
Authors: Sheng Fang, Jun Meng, Qing Lin, Bingsheng Chen, Gaogao Dong, Chenglin Bai, Jan Nagler, Youjin Deng, Renaud Lambiotte, Jingfang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCatastrophic fragmentation and structural transitions are ubiquitous in real-world complex systems, yet their underlying universality classes remain largely elusive due to strong structural heterogeneity and the absence of well-defined critical thresholds. Here, we discover a robust phenomenon of temporal self-similarity governing the dynamic percolation process across diverse complex networks. By tracking the full statistics of incremental growth events, we reveal that fragmentation dynamics are governed by two independent Fisher-type critical exponents, τc and τs. These exponents uniquely characterize the system’s universality class, from which all other standard critical exponents can be derived through newly established scaling relations. After rigorously validating this framework on canonical network models, we apply it to extensive empirical datasets. Strikingly, our analyses across biological, social, and infrastructural systems demonstrate that real-world networks systematically exhibit universality classes distinct from those predicted by idealized network models, reflecting the influence of higher-order structural features. Our findings establish a dynamic paradigm that bridges statistical physics and real-world resilience, offering a parameter-free, highly scalable approach to classify and characterize structural vulnerabilities in inherently heterogeneous systems.Subject terms: Complex networks, Phase transitions and critical phenomena, Statistical physics

Introduction

Understanding how large-scale connectivity is lost in heterogeneous systems is a central challenge across network science, statistical physics, biology, and infrastructure research1–8. In these diverse settings, a key concept is resilience: the ability of a system to maintain global connectivity and functionality under external perturbations9–13. Percolation theory provides a fundamental and widely adopted framework to model such connectivity transitions, predicting that near a critical threshold, systems exhibit abrupt macroscopic breakdowns and scale-invariant behaviors7,14–20. As shown in Fig. 1, progressive removal of bonds or nodes can trigger the breakdown of a giant connected component with a critical threshold qc. Near qc, the size of the largest connected component follows characteristic scaling laws governed by critical exponents such as β. While different networks may exhibit distinct values of qc and β depending on their topological structure, percolation theory predicts that systems differing in microscopic details can nonetheless display identical scaling behavior, a hallmark of universality.

In these systems, both critical thresholds and critical exponents are generally non-universal and must be determined empirically.

While traditional percolation theory has yielded elegant universal scaling laws for idealized model systems8,21,22, extracting such universal behavior from real-world networks remains highly problematic23. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a)-(c), for canonical systems such as two-dimensional lattices (2D), Erdös-Rényi (ER) networks, and scale-free (SF) networks, the critical thresholds and exponents of bond percolation can be obtained through analytical approaches or numerical finite-size scaling (FSS) analysis. Despite differences in lattice geometry, dimensionality, or degree distribution, such systems exhibit robust universal behavior near criticality, characterized by several exponents, such as the order-parameter exponent β and correlation-length exponent ν24–26. However, empirical systems are inherently finite, strongly heterogeneous, and lack well-defined spatial locality. Moreover, recent studies reveal that percolation in empirical systems is often heavily modulated by feature-enriched interactions, adaptive local feedback, and higher-order coupling patterns27–31. In parallel, recent work on cascading dynamics has revealed that complex systems can exhibit hybrid or system-specific universality classes beyond classical expectations32. These structural and dynamic complexities completely obscure the true critical thresholds, rendering standard static FSS procedures ineffective for extracting critical exponents, as shown in Fig. 1d. Consequently, a crucial question remains unanswered: what universality classes actually govern the catastrophic collapse of highly heterogeneous, real-world complex systems?

To overcome these fundamental limitations, we propose an event-based gap-statistics framework that characterizes percolation through the ordered sequence of bond-addition events. The gap-based perspective was introduced to study the largest jump of the largest connected component during the percolation process33. In that framework, the largest gap was treated as a single extreme-event observable: its location identifies a pseudocritical threshold, while the finite-size scaling of its magnitude, location, and associated cluster size provides information about the critical behavior of the transition. Building on this foundation, the present work moves beyond the largest-gap observable by generalizing the analysis to the full statistical distribution of growth gaps, n(G, V, r), accumulated along the percolation trajectory. This distribution-level formulation reveals a robust form of temporal self-similarity in both the critical and supercritical regimes. Specifically, the gap-size distribution exhibits persistent power-law scaling governed by two independently measurable Fisher-type gap exponents, τc and τs, characterizing the critical and supercritical regimes, respectively. Through the scaling relations derived below, these exponents provide a compact gap-based parametrization of percolation universality classes and allow other standard critical exponents to be inferred within the framework of renormalization-group scaling theory34–38. This approach bypasses the need for exact critical thresholds or multiple system sizes, making it particularly useful for empirical networks. Here, the terms “dynamic” and “temporal” refer to the ordered sequence of bond-addition events in the percolation growth process, rather than to an autonomous physical time evolution.

Strikingly, by applying this dynamic theoretical framework to extensive empirical datasets, we reveal that real-world networks exhibit profound deviations from classical theories. Although many empirical networks feature heterogeneous degree distributions resembling SF networks, their measured critical exponents (τc, τs) significantly diverge from the predictions of idealized SF models. This result indicates that real-world networks cluster into distinct universality classes that are not captured by idealized SF models. The specific values of these dynamic exponents reflect how structural correlations and community structure can substantially limit cascading failures or accelerate systemic collapse, offering a direct physical interpretation of resilience in heterogeneous topologies.

Our results demonstrate that the dynamic gap-size distribution captures universal features of fragmentation processes across a wide range of complex systems. Importantly, the proposed method enables the extraction of universality classes without requiring prior knowledge of critical thresholds or access to multiple system sizes. By unifying theoretical analysis, extensive numerical validation, and application to real-world biological, social, and infrastructural networks, our work establishes a scalable and physically interpretable framework for probing universality, resilience, and structural transitions in heterogeneous complex systems.

This observation naturally raises a fundamental question: how many gap events follow the same scaling laws, and whether such regularity reflects a deeper form of self-similarity in the percolation dynamics.

This result implies that, for any finite m, the top m largest clusters exhibit identical scaling behavior, reflecting a form of spatial self-similarity at criticality. This case is very similar to our observation above that the unnormalized top-m (m = 5) gap values have the same scaling behavior VΔ¯m~Vdf with df = 1 − β/ν = 1 − β1. In contrast to the common static viewpoint, the gap events considered here arise from the temporal evolution of the dynamic percolation process. This distinction motivates the central hypothesis of our work: if spatial self-similarity governs the statistics of large clusters at criticality, then an analogous form of self-similarity may emerge in time through the sequence of growth gaps experienced by the largest connected component. We refer to this dynamic counterpart along the event-based percolation trajectory as temporal self-similarity.

Guided by this intuition, we hypothesize that the scaling behavior observed for the top five gaps can be generalized to the top m gaps for any finite m. To test it, we introduce the gap-size distribution n(G, V, r), defined at bond fraction r as the cumulative distribution of all gap events of magnitude G occurring during the percolation process from r = 0 up to r. This construction parallels the cluster-size distribution n(s, V), but characterizes the statistics of dynamic growth events rather than static cluster configurations.

An important advantage of the dynamic gap-scaling framework is that it enables the extraction of two independent critical exponents, τc and τs, without requiring precise knowledge of the percolation threshold. In particular, the exponent τs can be robustly obtained solely from the supercritical regime, where the largest connected component is already established. This feature is especially relevant for real-world systems, in which the critical point is often inaccessible or ill-defined. Here, the two Fisher-type exponents τc and τs should be understood as two independently measurable gap-based descriptors, rather than as new fundamental exponents outside standard scaling theory. They are obtained from two distinct regimes of the gap-size distribution: τc characterizes the critical regime near r = r1, whereas τs characterizes the supercritical regime at r = rE. Through the scaling relations derived below, these exponents provide a compact gap-based parametrization of conventional critical behavior and offer a practical route for inferring universality classes from gap statistics.

To elucidate the physical origin of the two distinct exponents, we consider the dynamical evolution of gap formation throughout the percolation process. As the bond occupation probability increases, the largest connected component grows through a sequence of merging events whose statistics depend strongly on the proximity to criticality. In the subcritical phase (r < rc), clusters remain small and disconnected. Both the size of the largest cluster and the magnitude of its growth increments are bounded by O(ln V), leading to a limited number of significant gap events, with NG ~ O(1). At this stage, the probability that a newly added bond contributes to the growth of the largest cluster scales as 1/V, so only a few bond additions produce noticeable changes in C. As the system approaches the critical point, entering the critical regime, both the size of the largest cluster C and the typical gap magnitude G increase sharply from O(lnV) to O(Vdf). This rapid growth is accompanied by a dramatic increase in the number of gap events, with NG rising from O(1) to O(V1−κ). In this regime, bond additions are increasingly likely to trigger macroscopic merging events, reflecting the emergence of long-range correlations and the onset of critical behavior. In the supercritical phase (r > rc), the largest connected component spans the entire system and occupies a finite fraction of all nodes. As a consequence, nearly every newly added bond contributes to its growth, leading to NG ~ O(V). However, the incremental increase in cluster size becomes progressively smaller, preventing further growth of the cut-off scale in the gap-size distribution. As a result, the system enters a saturated regime in which gap formation remains frequent but no longer generates large fluctuations.

These dynamical regimes are directly reflected in the numerical gap-size distributions shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, which clearly distinguish subcritical, critical, and supercritical behavior. Taken together, the sharp increase of both the largest cluster size and the number of gap events near criticality, followed by saturation in the supercritical phase, encodes the intrinsic dynamics of the percolation transition. This structure naturally gives rise to two independent scaling exponents, enabling a dynamic characterization of universality beyond traditional static approaches.

Percolation on real-world networks

A central advantage of the gap-size distribution is its ability to characterize critical phenomena in percolation and related complex systems through dynamic statistics rather than static configurations. The two Fisher-type exponents, τc and τs, are independent and together form a complete set of critical descriptors, from which other standard critical exponents can be derived through scaling relations rooted in RG theory. Importantly, the estimation of τc and τs does not require prior knowledge of the critical threshold rc and is independent of system size, thereby circumventing the limitations of traditional FSS approaches. This feature makes the method particularly suitable for real-world networks, where only a single system realization is typically available.

We apply the framework to bond percolation on a diverse set of real-world networks and analyze the gap-size distribution n(G, V, r) evaluated at the location of the largest gap r1 and at full bond occupation r = rE. Compared with model-generated networks, real systems exhibit far greater structural complexity and heterogeneity. As illustrated in Fig. 1, different real-world networks possess markedly distinct topological characteristics. For example, social networks often contain highly connected hubs, resembling SF structures, whereas road networks display narrow degree distributions constrained by spatial embedding, similar to planar lattices. Recent studies on modular interacting infrastructures and multimodal transport systems further show that interconnected design can either mitigate or amplify critical fragmentation, depending on coupling architecture and flow constraints4,11.

Using the network datasets compiled in Ref. 43, we perform percolation simulations on networks drawn from a broad range of domains, including biological, citation, coauthor, interaction, recommendation, social, scientific computing, road, and gene networks. In Fig. 4(j), we summarize the landscape of universality classes by plotting τs versus τc. The gray dashed line indicates the theoretical universality class of SF networks with degree exponent γ ∈ [3, 4), for which the relation τs = τc + 1 holds via prediction of critical exponents from uncorrelated SF networks44 (see “Method”). Notably, most real-world networks cluster closer to the universality class of SF networks than to that of high-dimensional lattice systems. This tendency is particularly pronounced for social, citation, and recommendation networks, whose degree distributions are broadly heterogeneous and often exhibit scale-free characteristics. However, although many empirical networks possess degree exponents in the range γ ∈ (2, 3), for example, the recommendation network analyzed above has γ ≈ 2.5, their measured critical exponents (τc, τs) deviate significantly from the values predicted by idealized model-generated SF networks. This discrepancy highlights that real systems cannot be fully characterized by degree distributions alone. Recent studies have shown that the effective structure of complex networks and clustered core-periphery organization can substantially reshape percolation behavior even when degree statistics appear similar45,46. Instead, higher-order structural features, such as meso-scale modularity, tight-knit communities, and hierarchical core-periphery structures, can substantially alter the pathways of connectivity loss, leading real-world networks to cluster into distinct universality classes.

Interestingly, the empirical networks considered here appear to occupy a constrained region of the (τc, τs) plane. Road networks lie close to the low-dimensional region, consistent with their spatial embedding and relatively narrow degree distribution, whereas many social, citation, and recommendation networks lie closer to the region occupied by heterogeneous SF networks. Moreover, few empirical systems extend substantially beyond the broad envelope traced by SF networks with different degree exponents. This observation suggests that common structural constraints in real-world networks, such as sparsity, degree heterogeneity, clustering, assortativity, spatial embedding, and growth mechanisms, may restrict the accessible region of universality classes. Whether this envelope represents a general constraint on empirical network universality remains an interesting question for future work.

Further, the location of a system in the (τc, τs) plane has a direct physical interpretation. Since τs coincides with the Fisher exponent τ, larger values of τs correspond to a narrower critical cluster-size distribution, whereas smaller values indicate broader cluster statistics. By contrast, τc = 2 − σ, where σ controls the divergence of the characteristic cluster size; larger τc, corresponding to smaller σ, indicates stronger fluctuations and more pronounced multiscale organization. This interpretation is consistent with the positions of model systems in Fig. 4(j): low-dimensional lattices exhibit relatively large τc and smaller τs, reflecting strong fluctuations and broad cluster distributions, whereas high-dimensional and random-graph-like systems display smaller τc and larger τs, consistent with more mean-field-like behavior. SF networks occupy a distinctive region in which both exponents can be large, suggesting a combination of heterogeneous cluster statistics and strong fluctuation effects. This perspective helps explain why FSS in SF networks can be particularly rich and sensitive to structural details such as the degree exponent and cutoff.

Moreover, the critical exponents extracted from real-world networks are not artifacts of specific modeling assumptions, but instead reflect intrinsic structural properties of the underlying systems. Networks that share similar topological organization exhibit consistent values of (τc, τs), thereby belonging to the same universality class. This robustness is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 6, where multiple Facebook network subsets drawn from Ref. 43 are analyzed independently. Despite substantial differences in network size, density, and community composition, the critical exponents obtained from these distinct social systems converge to nearly identical values, demonstrating the stability of universality classification across heterogeneous realizations.

Beyond static network structure, the dynamic scaling framework introduced here further enables characterization of time-evolving systems. We apply the method to temporal journal citation networks47, in which networks are constructed from citation records over successive decades. As shown in Fig. 5, percolation data derived from networks corresponding to different time intervals collapse onto a single universal scaling curve with high precision. This result indicates that citation networks across time belong to the same universality class, despite continuous growth and structural evolution.

Discussion

In this work, we develop a dynamic framework for percolation on complex networks that reveals previously inaccessible universal features of fragmentation processes. By analyzing the gap-size statistics arising during the growth of the largest connected component, we uncover temporal self-similarity in both the critical and supercritical regimes. This behavior is characterized by two independent Fisher-type exponents, τc and τs, which can be related to other common critical exponents through the scaling relations τc = 2 − σ and τs = 1 + 1/df, respectively. These exponents form a complete set of descriptors from which the remaining standard critical exponents can be derived through established scaling relations. As a result, the universality class of percolation can be determined for both model-generated and real-world networks, even when only a single system size is available and the critical threshold is unknown. Notably, the present framework builds on the previous largest-gap approach33, but moves beyond it by shifting the object of analysis from a single extreme-event observable to the full statistical ensemble of growth gaps. While the largest gap provides an effective indicator of the pseudocritical point, the full gap-size distribution captures the statistics of growth events over the percolation trajectory. This distribution-level generalization reveals temporal self-similarity and enables the regime-based gap exponents (τc, τs) to serve as practical descriptors of universality classes, especially for empirical networks where conventional finite-size scaling analysis is difficult to implement.

The effectiveness of this dynamic approach originates from three key factors. First, by resolving the full percolation trajectory rather than isolated configurations, our framework continuously characterizes connectivity changes across the entire occupation range, from r = 0 to r = rE (fully occupied). Second, growth gaps of the largest connected component serve as highly sensitive probes of critical behavior, capturing the emergence of macroscopic connectivity. The locations of large gaps accurately identify pseudocritical thresholds, whereas their magnitudes and frequencies encode information about the system’s fractal dimension and the width of the critical window. Third, the self-similar scaling structure of the gap-size distribution n(G, V, r) allows critical properties to be extracted from a broad ensemble of intermediate gap events, rather than relying exclusively on extreme fluctuations.

Beyond standard percolation, our framework exhibits considerable generality. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 5, similar dynamic scaling behavior arises in explosive percolation on Erdös-Rényi networks, despite the markedly different nature of the transition14,39,48–52. These results suggest that gap-based scaling captures fundamental dynamical features of connectivity transitions that extend beyond classical continuous percolation models. While the present study focuses on single-layer networks with pairwise interactions, the framework can be naturally generalized to more complex interaction structures. Recent studies have shown that higher-order interactions can turn percolation into a genuinely dynamical process29, while feature-enriched and adaptive networks can substantially reshape critical thresholds and fragmentation pathways27,28. Potential extensions therefore include higher-order networks and triadic interactions29,53 and multilayer or multiplex systems54–56, where critical phenomena often emerge from the interplay between multiple interaction channels. Recent work on systemic cascades has revealed hybrid universality classes beyond classical expectations32, abrupt transitions driven by internal random cascading mechanisms in interdependent systems57, and percolation transitions in non-ideal physical systems such as entangled granular networks58. These developments suggest that dynamic scaling may provide a general route for identifying universality classes in complex systems where conventional renormalization or finite-size approaches are difficult to implement.

More broadly, this dynamic scaling perspective provides a useful mathematical bridge between statistical physics and the prediction of real-world catastrophic transitions. Many critical earth and ecological systems exhibit abrupt structural collapses driven by cumulative localized failures, yet they often lack perfect replicas or well-behaved control parameters. Representative examples include ecological fragmentation in coastal and tropical forest systems2,59, the catastrophic melting of Arctic sea ice60, and dynamical percolation transitions in urban traffic and climate networks3,61. Data-driven dismantling and early-warning analyses, together with recent evidence of nucleation-driven collapse in spatial k-core systems, further emphasize the need for dynamic diagnostics beyond static observables62,63. In these highly critical contexts, our gap-based dynamic framework offers a tractable, rigorously grounded diagnostic tool to quantify resilience loss and anticipate systemic reorganization. By decoding the temporal footprint of structural merging, this approach extends the predictive power of statistical mechanics to empirical complex systems.

Dynamic gap scaling offers a unified and scalable framework for diagnosing universality in complex systems. By leveraging temporal information encoded in growth processes, this approach extends universality analysis from idealized models to empirical and evolving networks, opening new avenues for understanding critical transitions in heterogeneous systems.

Simulation

We simulate percolation using the Newman–Ziff algorithm22 combined with a union-find data structure, which enables efficient tracking of cluster merging during the dynamic addition of bonds. Bonds are added sequentially, and the size of the largest connected component is recorded at each step of the percolation process.

For the mth-largest gap, its location is defined as rm = Tm/E, where Tm corresponds to the step at which the gap Δm occurs and E is the total number of edges. The corresponding largest cluster size is given by Cm = C(Tm). This dynamic percolation process is demonstrated in the Supplementary Movie 1.

In addition, we also record all unnormalized growth gaps of the largest connected component and construct the gap-size distribution n(G, V, r) over the percolation process, which is defined as the gap number density during the bond-adding process with bond fraction from 0 to r with given unnormalized gap size G. Here, we note that the ordered largest-gap analysis in Fig. 2 uses the normalized quantity Δm, whereas the gap-size distribution n(G, V, r) is constructed from the unnormalized gap size G. Under this convention, the cutoff of n(G, V, r) scales as G~O(Vdf).

For simulations on various networks, bonds are stored using a linked-list data structure rather than an adjacency matrix, which substantially reduces memory requirements and allows simulations of large systems. Random-regular and scale-free networks are generated via the configuration model. All real-world networks are sourced from publicly available datasets43,47. Each network is treated as unweighted, with self-loops and multiple edges removed during preprocessing. The structural properties of the processed networks are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. For system sizes V > 106, more than 105 independent realizations are generated, while for V < 106, at least 106 realizations are used to ensure statistical convergence.

Data analysis

To extract the critical exponents τc and τs, we perform a nonlinear fitting via the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. For model-generated networks, where multiple system sizes are available, the exponents τc and τs are estimated through FSS analyses of the total number of gaps NG = V∑Gn(G, V, r) at r1 and the second moment of the gap-size distribution S2 = ∑GG2n(G, V, r) at rE, respectively. Systematic uncertainties are assessed by employing multiple fitting forms and comparing the resulting exponent estimates. In contrast, for empirical networks, where only a single system size is typically available, conventional FSS analysis cannot be performed. We therefore estimate τc and τs directly from the power-law regimes of n(G, V, r1) and n(G, V, rE), respectively. The fitting is restricted to the intermediate scaling range, and the uncertainties are estimated by varying the fitting interval.

Details for Fig. 1

The resilience curves shown in Fig. 1a are obtained from Monte Carlo simulations of bond percolation on two-dimensional lattices with system volume V = 10242. Different curves correspond to distinct lattice geometries, including square, triangular, and hexagonal lattices. For these planar lattices, the exact critical thresholds are known and can be derived from duality and star–triangle relations64. For Fig. 1b and Fig. 1c, the normalized size of the largest connected component, denoted as C~, is calculated using the self-consistent equations of the generating function formalism8. In Fig. 1b, the curves correspond to Erdös–Rényi networks with mean degrees 〈k〉 = 3, 4, and 6, respectively. The associated critical thresholds are given by qc = 1 − 1/〈k〉. For scale-free networks shown in Fig. 1(c), the average degree cannot be prescribed directly. Instead, networks are generated by fixing the minimum degree, while the maximum degree is set to kmax=100 for all realizations. The critical threshold is calculated from qc=⟨k⟩⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩.
