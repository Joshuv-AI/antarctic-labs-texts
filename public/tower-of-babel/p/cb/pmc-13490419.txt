UiO-66(NH₂)-based metal organic framework interpenetrated polyurethane matrix as a catalytic microreactor for multicomponent condensation reactions
Authors: Susan Sarvarian, Moslem Mansour Lakouraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroreactors, characterized by their internal microstructures and high surface-area-to-volume ratios, offer significant advantages in chemical synthesis, including enhanced heat and mass transfer, precise reaction control, and scalability. In this work, a polyurethane foam (PUF)-based catalytic microreactor was fabricated through the in-situ incorporation of the zirconium-based metal–organic framework UiO-66-NH₂ during foam formation. Structural characterization confirmed the successful integration of UiO-66-NH₂ within the porous polyurethane matrix while preserving its crystallinity, thermal stability, and catalytic functionality. The resulting MOF–PUF composite exhibited improved surface area and hierarchical porosity, having an average pore diameter of 5.59 nm compared with pristine polyurethane foam, providing accessible catalytic sites and efficient mass transport. The hybrid microreactor was successfully applied to Knoevenagel condensation reactions for the synthesis of α-cyanoacrylonitriles and to the one-pot multicomponent synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives. A broad range of aromatic aldehydes was converted under mild conditions in ethanol, affording the desired products in 80–99% yields. The catalyst also demonstrated excellent reusability, retaining more than 90% of its initial catalytic activity after three consecutive cycles with minimal structural changes. The results suggest that the UiO-66-NH₂/PUF composite can serve as an effective and recyclable microreactor for Knoevenagel condensation and the synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55592-9.Keywords: Polyurethane composite, UiO-66-NH₂, Microreactor, Knoevenagel condensation, Catalysis, MOF–polymer integrationSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Catalysis plays a pivotal role in modern chemical manufacturing, contributing to over 90% of industrial processes. With the increasing demand for cleaner and more efficient production routes, the development of catalytic systems that are not only highly active but also recyclable, selective, and environmentally benign has become a major focus. In this context, green catalysis, which aims to minimize hazardous byproducts and energy consumption, is essential for advancing sustainable chemistry and supporting circular economy strategies1–4. Despite these advances, conventional catalytic systems, particularly homogeneous and slurry-phase processes, still suffer from significant drawbacks, including difficulties in catalyst recovery, leaching, and reduced reusability, which can lead to environmental concerns and loss of efficiency5. To overcome these limitations, microreactor technologies have emerged as promising platforms for heterogeneous catalysis. Due to their high surface-area-to-volume ratios, microreactors provide enhanced heat and mass transfer, precise reaction control, and improved operational safety. Their modular design and compatibility with continuous-flow processes make them attractive for process intensification and decentralized production6,7. Among various configurations, monolithic and foam-based microreactors are particularly appealing due to their hierarchical porosity, low pressure drops, and efficient multiphase interactions, which collectively improve catalytic performance and simplify product separation8. However, conventional inorganic supports and microreactors present inherent limitations. These include relatively low catalyst loading capacity, restricted accessibility for bulky or complex organic molecules, and limited mass transfer efficiency. In addition, their mechanical brittleness makes them prone to structural damage under stress, while their rigid frameworks complicate shaping and processing for practical applications9. Uneven distribution of active sites within these materials can further reduce catalytic efficiency, and their chemical incompatibility with certain reaction media limits their broader applicability. Moreover, catalyst recovery and reuse in such systems, particularly under continuous-flow conditions, remain challenging10,11. To address these issues, alternative support materials with improved flexibility, porosity, and chemical compatibility are required. Porous polymers have attracted increasing attention in this regard due to their tunable pore structures, high surface areas, excellent processability, and ability to uniformly immobilize active catalytic species12. In parallel, microreactor systems have demonstrated high efficiency in carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions such as Knoevenagel condensation, where enhanced mass transfer and controlled environments lead to improved reaction performance compared to conventional batch processes13.

On the other hand, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), particularly UiO-66-NH₂, have also been widely explored as heterogeneous catalysts due to their large surface area, tunable porosity, and accessible active sites14–18. Nevertheless, their practical application is often hindered by limitations such as low mechanical stability, restricted permeability toward bulky substrates, and challenges in achieving uniform dispersion and efficient recovery19,20. These drawbacks highlight the need for hybrid systems that combine the structural advantages of MOFs with the flexibility and processability of polymeric supports.

In this work, we report the design and fabrication of a polyurethane (PUF) foam-based catalytic microreactor via the in-situ incorporation of UiO-66-NH₂ during foam formation (Fig. 1). Polyurethane was selected due to its tunable mechanical and thermal properties, providing a flexible and porous scaffold that enhances mass transport while maintaining chemical resistance21–23. The incorporation of UiO-66-NH₂ into the PUF matrix results in a hybrid structure that integrates high catalytic activity with mechanical stability and improved accessibility of active sites. The catalytic performance of the resulting PUF/MOF nanocomposite was evaluated in multicomponent condensation reactions as representative carbon–carbon bond-forming processes. The system demonstrated efficient catalytic activity under mild conditions, along with good reusability and selectivity. This study presents a scalable strategy for developing MOF–polymer hybrid microreactors and highlights their potential for sustainable catalysis, particularly in continuous-flow systems and green chemical transformations.

This study presents a robust MOF–polymer hybrid microreactor that overcomes the intrinsic limitations of conventional MOF catalysts, including poor processability and limited mass transfer. The integration of UiO-66-NH₂ within a flexible PUF matrix enhanced the catalytic efficiency, structural stability, and practical applicability in continuous-flow systems.

Experimental section

Materials

Zirconium chloride (ZrCl₄), 2-aminoterephthalic acid, aldehydes, dimedone, malononitrile, and dibutyltin dilaurate were purchased from Merck. Other reagents, including N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), acetic acid, glycerol, silicone surfactant, 1,4-diazabicyclo[2.2.2]octane (DABCO), 2,4-toluene diisocyanate (TDI), and polyether polyol (Mw 3000), were obtained from commercial suppliers in China. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of UiO-66-NH₂

UiO-66-NH₂ (Scheme 1a) was synthesized via a solvothermal method. Typically, 1 mmol of ZrCl₄ and 1 mmol of 2-aminoterephthalic acid were separately dissolved in 50 mL of DMF. The solutions were then combined, followed by the addition of 0.5 mL of 1 M acetic acid as a modulator. The resulting mixture was sonicated for 20 min to obtain a homogeneous solution and subsequently heated at 120 °C for 24 h. The resulting yellow solid was collected by vacuum filtration, thoroughly washed with DMF and acetone, and dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 6 h 24.

Illustration of chemical reaction pathway for (a) UiO-66-NH₂, and (b) PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite.

Fabrication of PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite

The PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite (Scheme 1b) was prepared via in situ polyurethane formation using a two-component system. Component A consisted of 10 mL of polyether polyol, 1 mL of deionized water, 1 mL of glycerol, 1 wt% silicone surfactant (relative to the polyol), and catalytic amounts of DABCO and dibutyltin dilaurate (0.001 wt% each)25. To incorporate the MOF into the polymer matrix, 0.1 g of UiO-66-NH₂ powder was dispersed in Component A using ultrasonication for 2 min to ensure uniform distribution. Subsequently, 2 mL of TDI (Component B) was added, and the mixture was stirred mechanically at 800 rpm for 1 min. The resulting mixture was poured into a silicone mold and allowed to cure at ambient temperature. After complete expansion and solidification, the composite foam was cut into uniform pieces (1 cm × 1 cm × 0.5 cm) for subsequent characterization and catalytic evaluation.

Characterization techniques

A range of analytical techniques was employed to evaluate the structural and physicochemical properties of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite microreactor. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to confirm the crystallinity and structural integrity of UiO-66-NH₂ nanoparticles before and after incorporation into the polymer matrix. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured to determine the surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was utilized to identify functional groups and investigate interfacial interactions between the MOF and the polyurethane matrix. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to evaluate thermal stability and decomposition behavior under relevant conditions. Surface morphology and the dispersion of MOF particles within the polymer network were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), which revealed uniform distribution and effective integration. Additionally, product identification and structural confirmation, particularly for Knoevenagel condensation products and 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives, were carried out using 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Bruker, 250 MHz), providing clear evidence for the successful formation of the target compounds.

General procedure for catalytic reactions

All catalytic reactions were carried out in a two-neck round-bottom flask equipped with a reflux condenser under continuous magnetic stirring. The PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ microreactor (1 × 1 × 0.5 cm3) was directly immersed in the reaction medium. Due to its porous, lightweight structure, the foam remained suspended or gently immersed in the solvent, allowing efficient contact between the reactants and catalytic sites without requiring additional fixation. The progress of the reactions was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC).

Knoevenagel condensation reaction

In a typical procedure, aldehyde (1 mmol) and malononitrile (1 mmol) were dissolved in ethanol (50 mL), followed by the addition of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ foam catalyst. The reaction mixture was stirred at 50 °C. After completion (monitored by TLC), the catalyst was removed, and the product was isolated by filtration. The crude product was purified by recrystallization from ethanol.

Synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives

A mixture of aldehyde (1 mmol), malononitrile (1 mmol), and dimedone (1 mmol) was dissolved in ethanol (50 mL), and the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ catalyst was added. The reaction was carried out under magnetic stirring at 60 °C. Upon completion, the catalyst was separated, and the product was purified via recrystallization.

Results and discussion

FT-IR characterization

The FT-IR spectrum of UiO-66-NH₂ confirms the successful incorporation of amino-functionalized organic linkers into the MOF framework (Fig. 2). A broad absorption band in the range of 3440–3370 cm⁻1 is attributed to N–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of –NH₂ groups. A sharp band at 1658 cm⁻1 corresponds to the C = O stretching of coordinated carboxylate groups, while the band at 1568 cm⁻1 is assigned to aromatic C = C stretching vibrations. Additional bands observed at 1337 and 1256 cm⁻1 are related to C–N stretching and bending modes, further confirming the presence of amino functionalities. Out-of-plane C–H bending vibrations appear at 797 and 766 cm⁻1, and a band around 667 cm⁻1 corresponds to Zr–O stretching vibrations, characteristic of the metal–oxo clusters in the UiO structure. These spectral features confirm the successful synthesis of UiO-66-NH₂ with preserved structural integrity, consistent with previously reported data26. FT-IR analysis was also performed to confirm the formation of the polyurethane (PUF) matrix and the successful incorporation of UiO-66-NH₂. The spectrum of neat PUF shows a broad band at 3300–3400 cm⁻1, corresponding to hydrogen-bonded N–H stretching vibrations. A strong absorption band at 1700 cm⁻1 is assigned to the C = O stretching of urethane linkages, confirming successful polymerization. Additional bands in the 1100–1250 cm⁻1 region correspond to C–O–C stretching vibrations of urethane and polyol segments. Notably, the absence of any peak near 2270 cm⁻1 indicates complete consumption of isocyanate (–NCO) groups. In the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite, all characteristic PUF bands are preserved, along with additional features corresponding to the MOF structure. The N–H stretching band becomes broader and more intense, indicating hydrogen bonding between the PUF matrix and MOF functionalities. Furthermore, weak bands in the 750–670 cm⁻1 region are attributed to Zr–O vibrations, confirming the presence of UiO-66-NH₂ within the composite. Overall, these results demonstrate the successful fabrication of the hybrid PUF/MOF structure.

Morphology of materials

FE-SEM images of pristine polyurethane foam (Fig. 3) reveal a highly irregular and porous microstructure. At lower magnifications (10,000 × –25,000 ×), the structure appears as loosely packed amorphous domains ranging from approximately 0.5 μm to several micrometers in size. At higher magnifications (≥ 50,000 ×), nanoscale features such as protrusions and voids (100–500 nm) become clearly visible. As shown in Fig. 4, UiO-66-NH₂ particles exhibit a polyhedral morphology and tend to form aggregated clusters ranging from 100 nm to several micrometers. At higher magnifications, individual nanocrystals with sizes between 50 and 200 nm can be observed. This morphology is attributed to the presence of amino functional groups, which influence nucleation and crystal growth during synthesis27. Although this structure provides a high density of active sites, particle aggregation may limit dispersion efficiency.

FE-SEM images of PUF at different magnifications.

FE-SEM images of UiO-66-NH₂ at different magnifications.

SEM images of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite (Fig. 5) clearly demonstrate the successful incorporation of MOF particles into the polymer matrix. The UiO-66-NH₂ nanoparticles are uniformly distributed throughout the PUF structure, with sizes typically in the range of 100–300 nm. Compared to the pristine MOF, aggregation is significantly reduced, indicating that the PUF matrix effectively stabilizes particle dispersion. This improved distribution enhances surface accessibility and mass transfer, thereby contributing to improved catalytic performance.

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to evaluate the crystalline structure of UiO-66-NH₂, pristine polyurethane (PUF), and the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite (Fig. 6). The XRD pattern of UiO-66-NH₂ exhibits characteristic sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 7.4°, 8.5°, 17.1°, 25.6°, and 30.7°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (222), (400), and (331/533) planes, respectively. These reflections confirm the formation of a highly crystalline framework with long-range structural order, consistent with previously reported data28. In contrast, the XRD pattern of pristine PU foam displays a broad amorphous halo in the range of 2θ ≈ 15–30°, indicating the absence of long-range order due to the flexible polymeric nature of polyurethane. The XRD pattern of the PU/UiO-66-NH₂ foam composite exhibits features from both components. The presence of distinct diffraction peaks at low angles (2θ ≈ 7–10°) confirms the successful incorporation and structural preservation of UiO-66-NH₂ within the polymer matrix. It should be noted that the relatively high intensity reflects the strong crystallinity and high scattering efficiency of UiO-66-NH₂ in this region. At higher diffraction angles (2θ ≈ 15–30°), the broad amorphous halo of the PU matrix dominates the pattern and overlaps with weaker MOF reflections, particularly the peak around 2θ ≈ 25°. As a result, this peak is not clearly distinguishable in the composite. Additionally, the relatively low loading of UiO-66-NH₂, as supported by TGA analysis, and the partial embedding of MOF crystallites within the polymer network contribute to the attenuation of higher-angle diffraction peaks. Importantly, no significant peak shift or disappearance is observed for the main low-angle reflections, indicating that the crystalline framework of UiO-66-NH₂ remains intact after incorporation into the PUF matrix. These findings confirm that the composite consists of crystalline MOF domains uniformly embedded within an amorphous polymeric network, resulting in a hybrid material with combined structural characteristics.

XRD patterns of (a) UiO-66-NH₂, (b) PUF, and (c) PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were carried out to evaluate the textural properties of UiO-66-NH₂ and the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite. Surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter were calculated using the BET method. The pristine UiO-66-NH₂ exhibits a high specific surface area of 581.95 m2/g, a pore volume of 0.3479 cm3/g, and an average pore diameter of 2.39 nm. The adsorption isotherm corresponds to a mixed Type I/IV profile, indicating the presence of hierarchical micro- and mesoporosity. The high BET constant (C = 1444.3) reflects strong adsorbate–adsorbent interactions, consistent with the highly porous MOF structure (Fig. 7).

BET plot and Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of UiO-66-NH₂.

After incorporation into the PUF matrix, the composite shows a significantly reduced surface area (3.7 m2/g), pore volume (0.0051 cm3/g), and an increased average pore diameter (5.59 nm). The isotherm resembles a Type IV profile, typical of mesoporous materials. The decrease in surface area is attributed to partial coverage of MOF particles and pore blockage within the polymer matrix. The lower BET constant (C = 17.23) suggests weaker nitrogen adsorption due to the presence of the polymer phase (Fig. 8).

(a) BET plot and (b) Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ Composite.

Despite this reduction, the formation of a mesoporous structure and the accessibility of MOF active sites contribute to improved catalytic performance, particularly for reactions involving larger molecules.

Thermogravimetric analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to investigate the thermal behavior of pristine PUF, UiO-66-NH₂, and the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite (Fig. 9). These analyses provide valuable insights into the decomposition patterns and thermal stability of each material. The TGA curve of pristine UiO-66-NH₂ nanoparticles shows a gradual mass loss over a wide temperature range, starting from approximately 100 °C up to 900 °C, with about 40% of the initial weight remaining at high temperatures. The initial weight loss below 150 °C is attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and residual solvent molecules trapped within the porous framework. The continuous mass loss at higher temperatures is associated with the progressive decomposition of the organic linkers, while the remaining residue corresponds to thermally stable zirconium oxide (ZrO₂). This behavior confirms the inherent thermal robustness of the UiO-66-NH₂ framework29. In contrast, both pristine polyurethane foam and the PUF-supported MOF nanocomposite exhibit a distinctly different thermal degradation profile. In these samples, the onset of thermal decomposition begins at approximately 250 °C, followed by a rapid and significant mass loss within the temperature range of 300–400 °C. This sharp degradation step is characteristic of the breakdown of the polyurethane backbone and indicates the dominant influence of the polymer matrix on the overall thermal behavior of the composite30. Notably, the TGA curve of the composite closely resembles that of pristine PUF, suggesting that polyurethane constitutes the major weight fraction of the hybrid material. The incorporation of UiO-66-NH₂ does not significantly alter the degradation temperature range of the PUF matrix, which is expected due to the relatively low loading of the MOF component. However, the presence of UiO-66-NH₂ contributes to the functional performance of the composite without compromising the intrinsic thermal properties of the polymer scaffold.

TGA curves of (a) UiO-66-NH₂, (b) PUF and (c) PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite under nitrogen atmosphere.

Overall, the TGA results demonstrate that the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ nanocomposite retains the characteristic thermal behavior of polyurethane while benefiting from the structural robustness of the MOF component, making it suitable for catalytic applications under moderate thermal conditions.

The catalytic performance, including efficiency, selectivity, and recyclability, was systematically investigated under mild and environmentally friendly conditions.

The reaction products were identified, and their structures were confirmed using 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, demonstrating high purity and successful formation of the desired compounds (see Supplementary Information).

The pristine PUF showed no catalytic activity under identical reaction conditions, confirming that the polymer matrix itself is catalytically inactive. The reactions were performed in the presence of pure UiO-66-NH₂ (in the absence of PUF); however, it required significantly longer reaction times (approximately 0.5 h longer than the composite), indicating lower efficiency due to aggregation and limited mass transfer. In addition, poor catalytic performance was observed for the physical mixture of PUF and UiO-66-NH₂ nanoparticles, due to mixture instability. In fact, the MOF particles were not effectively immobilized and tend to leach from the PUF matrix during the reaction, leading to reduced catalytic efficiency and poor reusability owing to aggregation.

In contrast, the as-prepared PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite exhibited superior catalytic performance, shorter reaction times, and excellent stability, confirming the synergistic effect between the PUF framework and the MOF active sites.

A systematic investigation of reaction parameters, including temperature and solvent, was carried out to identify the optimal conditions. Various solvents, including water, methanol, ethanol, and DMF were examined. No significant reaction progress was observed in water or methanol. Influence of substituents on reaction yield

The catalytic activity of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite in the condensation of aromatic aldehydes with malononitrile was systematically investigated using a diverse set of substrates bearing both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, as summarized in Table 1. The Knoevenagel condensation reactions proceeded efficiently under mild conditions, generally affording high yields. A detailed analysis of the results reveals a clear trend based on the electronic nature of the substituents on the aromatic ring. Aldehydes containing electron-withdrawing groups—such as nitro, cyano, and halogen substituents—consistently exhibited higher conversions and faster reaction rates. This behavior can be attributed to the increased electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon, which facilitates nucleophilic attack by malononitrile and accelerates the condensation process. In contrast, substrates bearing electron-donating groups, including methoxy and methyl, showed slightly lower yields and longer reaction times under identical conditions. These substituents reduce the electrophilic character of the aldehyde, thereby slowing the reaction rate.

A similar trend was observed in the synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives, where electron-withdrawing substituents led to higher yields and shorter reaction times, while electron-donating groups resulted in comparatively moderate conversions. Overall, these findings demonstrate the broad applicability and efficiency of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ microreactor, particularly for substrates containing strong electron-withdrawing groups, where the highest catalytic performance was achieved.

Catalyst recycling and reusability assessment

After each reaction, the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite catalyst was easily separated from the reaction mixture by filtration or decantation, depending on the experimental setup. The recovered catalyst was thoroughly washed with ethanol to remove residual organic species and then dried at 60 °C under vacuum for 2 h prior to reuse. To evaluate catalyst stability and reusability, the Knoevenagel condensation and the synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives were carried out under identical conditions over multiple cycles using the recovered catalyst. The catalytic performance remained high, with product yields retaining over 80% of their initial values after four cycles for the Knoevenagel reaction and three cycles for the 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran synthesis. These results indicate that the nanocomposite preserves both its catalytic activity and structural integrity upon repeated use. The structural stability of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite after multiple catalytic cycles was further evaluated using FE-SEM, XRD, and elemental mapping analyses. FE-SEM images (Fig. 10) show that the morphology and surface structure of the composite remain largely unchanged after reuse, with no evidence of agglomeration, cracking, or structural degradation.

FE-SEM images of the recycled PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite after catalytic cycles.

Elemental mapping analysis (Fig. 11) confirms a uniform distribution of Zr, C, N, and O elements across the catalyst surface, indicating negligible leaching and strong interfacial stability. Additionally, the XRD pattern of the reused catalyst (Figure 23 in Supplementary Information) shows no significant changes in peak positions or intensities compared to the fresh sample, confirming that the crystalline structure of UiO-66-NH₂ is well preserved during catalytic operation. Overall, these results demonstrate the excellent reusability, structural robustness, and long-term stability of the composite, highlighting its suitability for sustainable catalytic applications.

Mapping analysis of the reused PUF/UIO-66-NH₂ composite.

The TGA profile of the recovered catalyst showed only negligible changes compared to the fresh sample, indicating that the thermal stability of the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite was largely preserved after catalytic cycling. The minimal variation in decomposition behavior confirms the structural robustness of the microreactor during repeated use (Figure 24 in Supplementary Information).

Mechanism for the Knoevenagel condensation

The Knoevenagel condensation is proposed to proceed primarily through the basic amino functional groups on UiO-66-NH₂. Accordingly, the reaction is proposed to proceed predominantly via a basic pathway, in which the amino groups facilitate deprotonation of malononitrile or its tautomerism to generate a nucleophile and initiate the Knoevenagel condensation. Consequently, the nucleophile attacks the aldehyde carbonyl group, yielding the hydroxynitrile intermediate. Eventually, the elimination of water by dehydration reaction produces the corresponding benzylidene malononitrile product. In this mechanism, the –NH₂ groups facilitate both substrate activation and proton transfer, thereby accelerating the condensation process. The porous microreactor architecture further promotes efficient diffusion of reactants toward these accessible active sites, enhancing catalytic performance (Scheme 2).

A possible mechanism for the Knoevenagel condensation catalyzed by the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite catalyst.

The proposed catalytic mechanism is further supported by control experiments, where bare UiO-66 (without –NH₂ functional groups) showed no significant catalytic activity under identical conditions. This observation suggests that the –NH₂ groups play a crucial role in activating the substrate. Accordingly, the reaction is proposed to proceed predominantly through a basic pathway, in which the amino groups facilitate deprotonation of malononitrile and initiation of the Knoevenagel condensation process.

Comparison of the MOF-based catalysts

Compared to previously reported MOF-based catalysts for the Knoevenagel condensation, the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ Composite microreactor that was developed in this work demonstrates several clear advantages. The use of a three-dimensional polyurethane foam as a robust and processable support simplifies catalyst handling and enables its direct use in a microreactor setup. This eliminates the need for additional steps such as filtration or centrifugation, which are typically required with powder-based catalysts. The composite microreactor operates efficiently under mild and environmentally friendly conditions, specifically at 50 °C in ethanol, a green solvent. It shows high catalytic activity in the one-pot, three-component condensation involving various substituted benzaldehydes, malononitrile, and dimedone. This expands the substrate scope beyond the simpler two-component systems commonly reported in previous studies. The hybrid structure of the system ensures uniform dispersion of the MOF and stable phase interaction, contributing to consistent product yields and selectivity. Moreover, the catalyst is easy to recover and can be reused for at least three consecutive cycles with minimal loss in performance. Its monolithic design also facilitates scale-up and straightforward integration into continuous flow processes. Generally, the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ Composite microreactor offers a practical and reusable catalytic platform that outperforms traditional MOF powders in terms of operational ease, substrate compatibility, and potential for process intensification. Previous studies have demonstrated that various MOF-based catalysts can effectively promote the synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives under mild conditions, as seen in Table 4, generally achieving good yields (80–95%) and reasonable recyclability over multiple cycles. However, these systems typically rely on batch reactor configurations, requiring repeated catalyst separation and reloading, which limits scalability and continuous processing. In contrast, the PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ Composite microreactor developed in this study represents a significant advancement by integrating the MOF phase into a flexible, porous polyurethane foam suitable for flow applications. This system maintains high yield at 60 °C under green ethanol solvent, and demonstrates robust reusability over three cycles with minimal activity loss (≤ 10%). The microreactor design also facilitates easier catalyst handling and potential scale-up in continuous-flow settings, offering clear advantages for sustainable fine chemical synthesis.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel polyurethane (PUF) foam-based microreactor was successfully developed through the in-situ integration of the metal–organic framework UiO-66-NH₂. The resulting PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ foam nanocomposite combines the mechanical flexibility and processability of polyurethane with the high surface activity and thermal stability of the MOF. Comprehensive characterization, including SEM, XRD, FT-IR, BET, and TGA analyses, confirmed the successful incorporation of UiO-66-NH₂ into the polymer matrix while preserving its structural integrity and functional properties. The engineered microreactor exhibited enhanced thermal stability, as well as optimized porosity and surface characteristics. Its hierarchical structure facilitates efficient mass transport, leading to improved catalytic performance in Knoevenagel condensation reactions and the synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzo[b]pyran derivatives.

All reactions were carried out under mild and environmentally friendly conditions, yielding high product efficiencies (80–99%). Structural confirmation by NMR spectroscopy further verified the successful formation of the desired products, supporting the effectiveness of the catalytic system. Overall, this work presents a scalable and eco-friendly platform for heterogeneous catalysis. The synergistic combination of the polymer support and MOF functionality enables efficient transformation of organic substrates under environmentally friendly conditions. The PUF/UiO-66-NH₂ composite microreactor shows strong potential for broader applications in fine chemical synthesis, continuous-flow catalysis, and green chemical processes requiring robust, stable, and reusable catalytic systems.
