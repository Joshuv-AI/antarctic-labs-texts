Effects of board gender and foreign directorship on the financial sustainability of Saudi listed firms: Does family ownership matter?
Authors: Mohammed Naif Alshareef, Hamid Ghazi H Sulimany
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates how family ownership may influence the effects of board gender and foreign directorship on the financial sustainability. The panel data of 103 Saudi non-financial listed companies from 2013 to 2022 was analyzed using fixed effects, and the generalized method of moments (GMM) was used for robustness test. The research findings suggest a strong and positive effect of foreign directorship on financial sustainability, consistent with the resource dependency view and agency theory. However, board gender exerts a negative and significant influence, contradicting existing theories. Further, the moderation results show that family ownership positively moderates the effects of both board gender and foreign directorship on financial sustainability. This positive moderating result supports the agency theory's argument that robust and stringent monitoring of corporate ownership may complement boards' functions and boost organizational efficiency. The policy decision of the findings is that setting corporate boards with a considerable ratio of foreign directors may lead to sustainable performance. Also, it implies that family ownership seems to be an essential mechanism that can neutralize the adverse effects of boardroom diversity on outcomes.Keywords: Board diversity, Board gender, Foreign directorship, Financial sustainability, Saudi-listed firms

Introduction

This study explores how family ownership determines the relationships among board gender, foreign directorship, and financial sustainability. Corporate sustainability practices have received much attention because of their crucial link to organizational survival [[1], [2], [3]]. Financial sustainability is a business approach that focuses on long-term value creation to benefit diverse interests [4,5]. It is about generating substantial revenue to satisfy future financial commitments. Agency and resource dependency views serve as a theoretical base for understanding the connection between board diversity and financial sustainability. These frameworks argue that corporate boards with diverse personalities may make more informed decisions to ensure long-term value creation [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. These attributes include gender diversity and board nationality.

Accordingly, the empirical evidence suggests that women and foreign directors on board may bring an array of experiences and perspectives that can boost decision-making quality [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. However, some studies have advocated that foreign directorships and board gender may be detrimental to organizational outcomes. In particular, it is argued that boards with a mixture of women and men may be prone to conflicts and communication challenges [14,15]. Similarly, studies have shown that foreign directors’ advisory and monitoring roles may be ineffective due to communication barriers and lower board meeting attendance [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. These instances may negatively affect the board decision quality and reduce firm value.

Given the above review, it is evident that studies document inconsistent results regarding the effect of foreign directorship and board gender on organizational efficiency. Secondly, most studies in the literature focus on sustainability disclosure that covers environmental and social components and ignores the financial sustainability dimension [10,[20], [21], [22]]. Furthermore, a segment of the literature contends that a country's institutional structure may influence corporate board efficiency and function [[22], [23], [24]]. This suggests the need for a stronger firm governance mechanism, particularly in countries with weak institutional settings. Accordingly, the agency framework reveals the role of ownership structure in influencing a firm's internal governance [25,26]. In particular, the literature has argued that family ownership is a vital corporate governance mechanism [27,28]. These investors may be motivated to monitor management because of their skills and financial management strategies [29,30].

This study concentrates on Saudi because of the peculiar institutional structure of its corporate sector. The nation's corporate sector is facing some reforms that may encourage foreign investment. These transformations aimed at improving the country's revenue base to achieve Vision 2030 [13,31]. This plan may save Saudi Arabia from extensive dependence on crude oil income. Another feature of the Saudi corporate environment is the dominance of family shareholdings [13,32]. This significant family ownership may influence board diversity dynamics and its effects on long-term value creation. Specifically, the country's corporate governance framework has no explicit recommendations for board diversity [33]. Hence, this study explores how family ownership may influence the link between board gender, foreign directorship, and the financial sustainability of Saudi-listed firms.

This research adds to the body of knowledge by analyzing the link between board gender, foreign directorship and corporate sustainability. The analysis improves the literature on the influence of board diversity dynamics on organizational outcomes. The finding provides new ideas by which firms may attain sustainable performance through effective monitoring and resource provision from board gender diversity and nationality. Second, the moderating analysis offers a unique perspective by analyzing how family shareholding determines the link between board nationality, board gender and financial sustainability. Therefore, the finding offers new ideas on the potential of family ownership in neutralizing the adverse effect of boardroom diversity for sustainable growth. Third, the research outcome may encourage firms to take on more foreign directors to enrich their corporate board functions and maximize shareholder investment. Finally, the research findings may stimulate corporate organizations to support family ownership for stringent monitoring and ensure adherence to corporate governance rules, facilitating a sound financial system and resulting in sustainable performance.

The other parts of this paper include the literature review section and the description of the methodology. The next segment contains the main regression and robustness tests, followed by the conclusions.

Literature review

Theoretical review

This research employs agency theory as a framework to assess the nexus between foreign directorship, board gender and financial sustainability. This perspective argues that managers' actions should be monitored because of the separation between management and ownership in modern organizational structures [11,31,34]. Hence, this framework argues that a corporate board needs to be constituted to supervise management on behalf of shareholders [35,36]. Based on this philosophy, it has been argued that corporate boards may be more effective in monitoring when composed of female and foreign directors because of their stringent monitoring strategies [9,10,37]. Additionally, studies argue that foreign directorships provide different experiences and perspectives that can improve board's decision-making quality [10,11]. Similarly, gender diversity may shape board monitoring roles because of the greater participation of female directors in board activities [12,13]. This framework assumes that sophisticated and robust monitoring by female and foreign directors may motivate managers to come up with policies that maximize shareholder wealth, leading to financial sustainability [9,34]. From this perspective, board gender and foreign directorships are positively associated with financial sustainability due to the robust monitoring of these board members [10,38].

Moreover, resource dependency theory may underpin the link between board nationality, board gender and sustainable performance. This theory states that boards are set up to assist in providing essential resources to organizations [39,40]. These resources may include advice, financing, expertise, and legitimacy. According to this view, when corporate boards are composed of different individuals, there is a greater chance of drawing different resources from the external environment [41,42]. It is argued that foreign directors and women board members may serve as valuable sources of gaining essential resources for sustainable performance [3,20]. Specifically, the literature has shown that women on boards may bring new perspectives and innovative ideas because of their accommodative attitudes and other exceptional behaviors [43,44]. These peculiarities of women in the workplace may enable firms to gain a competitive advantage, leading to steady growth and a sound financial system. Based on this framework, foreign directors may perform a vital advisory role in board governance because of their cross-border experience and technical knowledge [45,46]. These directors may provide firms with greater access to financial resources through their connections with international capital markets, thereby enhancing their long-term growth and profitability [12,47]. Thus, the resource dependency view advocates a positive connection between foreign directorship, board gender diversity, and sustainable financial performance because of the expertise and counsel of these directors.

Upper echelons theory may also help understand the effect of foreign directorship and board gender on organizational outcomes. This theory argues that top managers’ personal traits and cognitive power influence their strategic choices [48,49]. Studies emphasize that female and foreign directors offer a wide range of opinions and skills to shape decision-making quality and boost firm value [50,51]. Also, it is argued that boards with more women and foreign directors attach greater concern to ethical behavior and sustainability management [20,43]. The exceptional attributes of female and foreign directors may aid the development of robust financial policies. Therefore, this theory believes that foreign and female board members may use their experiences to formulate strategic decisions that can improve firm value, leading to sustainable performance.

Hypotheses formulation

Board gender diversity and financial sustainability

There is increasing attention being paid to the role of women in organizations [44,52]. According to agency theory, board monitoring functions may be positively influenced because of their entrenched supervision [33,53]. In particular, it is reported that women directors regularly attend board meetings and evaluate issues from different perspectives when making decisions [9,53]. Likewise, one strand of the literature emphasizes that women on boards are more likely to develop relationships with various stakeholders [54,55]. It has also been suggested that female directors assist organizations in obtaining resources for sustainable growth because they can easily establish ties with the external environment [11].

From a different perspective, it has been argued that women possess unique cognitive frames that accommodate diverse interests when making decisions and judgments [10,43]. Hence, the peculiar qualities of women on boards may shape firms' decision-making abilities and provide better strategies for monitoring managers’ actions [33,56]. Effective monitoring may reduce agency conflict, enhance the chances of obtaining resources, and promote financial sustainability [10,20]. However, some studies have reported that board gender may be detrimental to financial sustainability. These studies argue that boards with a mixture of women and men are prone to conflict and communication challenges [14,15]. Effective communication may trigger agency conflicts, erode firm value, and adversely affect the attainment of sustainable growth [7]. Given these views, this study proposes the following hypotheses.

Gender diversity in boardrooms is connected to the financial sustainability of listed Saudi firms.

Foreign directorship and financial sustainability

The literature documents how foreign directors influence corporate governance practices. These directors may affect corporate boards’ monitoring capacity. Studies show that foreign directors on boards may bring greater skills, expertise, and new perspectives into the decision-making process [9,18]. It has also been argued that these directors may possess unique knowledge and understanding of foreign markets, enabling them to monitor managers effectively [19,51]. They can equally utilize their networking capacity to link firms to secure finances in favorable terms [17,33]. Thus, greater monitoring due to the presence of foreign directors may mitigate managerial entrenchment, help align divergent views between managers and shareholders, and lower agency conflicts [2,33]. Similarly, access to greater resources may pave the way for firms to finance their growth opportunities. This may improve wealth maximization for sustainable financial performance.

Conversely, prior empirical works have reported that foreign directors on boards may weaken board monitoring and advisory roles. In particular, it is shown that foreign directors may have lower board meeting attendance due to their distance from their home country [16,17]. Cultural and communication barriers may also weaken monitoring capacity [18,19]. Thus, firms with substantial foreign board members may suffer from agency conflicts, which may lower their ability to attain sustainable growth. Therefore, this study proposes that.

Foreign directorships are associated with the financial sustainability of listed Saudi firms.

Family ownership and financial sustainability

Family shareholding appears crucial to firm governance in both developed and emerging nations [29,57]. It has been argued that the major goal of family shareholders is to preserve their family heritage and maximize their wealth [58,59]. These shareholders may have an incentive to exercise greater monitoring by managers. Additionally, they can effectively evaluate policy decisions to achieve their goals. Studies suggest that managers' earnings management and underinvestment attitudes tend to be lower in family-controlled companies because of the stringent monitoring of family shareholders [60,61]. Family-owned firms may have lesser agency costs and superior performance that can facilitate financial sustainability [59,62]. By contrast, the literature shows that family companies usually appoint relations that do not possess the necessary knowledge and aptitude to hold such key positions [63]. Additionally, such companies may infringe on minority shareholders’ interests [63,64]. These cases may exacerbate agency conflicts in family-controlled firms, lower firm values, and constrain sustainable performance. Based on this discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed.

Family ownership is connected to the financial sustainability of Saudi firms.

Moderating effect of family shareholding

The literature considers ownership structure as an important control mechanism in firm governance [65,66]. According to this theory, shareholders can effectively monitor managers and ensure that their entrenchment attitude is neutralized [29,67]. Family shareholders' influence may affect board appointments and composition through voting power [27,28]. Prior studies suggest that family shareholders value companies with many foreign and female directors because of their unique perspectives [68]. More importantly, boards with many female and foreign directors may have diverse experiences and skills that can boost the quality of decision-making and improve firm value [10,11]. The association between family ownership and female and foreign directors may enhance the board monitoring and resource provision functions [61,69]. Thus, higher firm value may reduce agency costs, create long-term value, and ensure financial sustainability. Given this discussion, we formulated the following hypotheses.

Family ownership influences the effect of board gender on the financial sustainability of listed Saudi firms.

Family ownership influences the effect of foreign directorships on the financial sustainability of listed Saudi firms.

Research approach

Sample and data gathering

The research sample comprises 227 companies operating in the non-financial sector of Saudi Arabia from 2013 to 2022. The sample covers 103 firms, designed in the following manner: First, 44 insurance and banking institutions were exempted, given their exceptional operating requirements [70,71]. Additionally, 43 companies with inadequate data were removed when constructing the study sample. Moreover, firms that merged within the study timeframe and those listed after 2013 were stepped down equally, eliminating 37 companies from the study. The study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable is financial sustainability. This study adopted Higgins' (1977) growth framework to measure corporate financial sustainability. A sustainable growth rate is the yearly increase in companies’ turnover, consistent with their established financial policies, without distorting their capital structures [73,74]. This model emphasizes the need for synergy between financial policies and growth target. Based on this framework, corporate financial condition may worsen if there is no synergy between financial policies and growth target [72,75]. Main explanatory variables

Foreign directorship and board gender serve as the main explanatory variables. The study quantified board gender as a dummy variable. It takes the value of one when there is at least one female board member; otherwise, 0 [10,11]. Foreign directorship is quantified as the number of foreign board members over the total number of directors [2,9,33]. More importantly, following resource dependency view and agency perspective, diverse boards may be more capable of monitoring efficiently and giving better guidance to management [34,39,52,76]. Thus, enhanced supervision may compel management to formulate better strategies that guarantee sustainable growth.

Moderating variable

The moderating variable is family ownership structure. The reason for using family ownership as a moderator originates from agency theory arguments. This school of thought argues that family shareholders may positively affect companies' internal governance system, given their innovative ideas that can shape board decisions [28,60,67]. This paper measures family ownership as the number of equity shares families hold over the total ordinary shares [29,59]. Hence, we expect the influence of foreign and women directors on financial sustainability to become more robust as family shareholdings increase.

Control variables

This research uses some control variables to empower our models and equally minimize specification bias. The control variables were considered because prior studies have reported that these variables may affect firms' sustainable performance [[73], [74], [75],77]. The variables are board size (BS), calculated as the total number of directors [28,60]. We have board independence (BI), which is defined as the number of independent directors over the total number of directors. Also, we included firm size (FSIZE) and leverage (LEV). FSIZE was determined as the logarithms of the firms’ total assets value, whereas LEV was calculated as total debt over total assets [9,33].

Analytical method

The analytical framework utilized in this research is panel data methodology. The approach is more suitable because the study sample covers data from 103 companies over ten years. Panel data methodology is the examination of different firms over multiple periods [29,78]. The econometric literature has emphasized that panel data analysis has some advantages. The method reduces multicollinearity issues and generates more data points, thereby enhancing the efficacy of regression estimates [79]. The panel data techniques generally used in finance and accounting studies are the pooled OLS, random effects, and fixed effect methods [80,81]. However, the OLS estimator ignores cross-sectional differences across units and may be inconsistent and biased [44,78].

The Hausman test was used to identify the best model between random and fixed effects methods. Accordingly, the Hausman test supports the fixed effects method when the result is statistically significant at the 5 % level. However, the random effects framework may be suitable when its p-value is insignificant at 5 % [82,83]. Consequently, the Hauman test result appears significant, and this outcome reveals the appropriateness of the fixed effects method. The fixed effects analytical framework accounts for firm-specific effects and other cross-sectional variations [78,79]. Thus, the fixed effects technique accommodates variations across companies in its estimation procedure. The base-line format of a fixed-effects model is given below:

Descriptive results

Table 2 displays a summary description of the research variables. According to the analysis, financial sustainability (FS) has a mean value of 9.151. The variable exhibits a large variation among the companies. The ratio of female directors shows a mean value of 0.083, suggesting that 8.3 % of the directors are women within the period under review. On average, the foreign directorship ratio is 0.043, indicating that 4.3 % of the board members are foreigners. The average board size (BS) is approximately 6 members, with higher and lower values of 11 and 3 board members, respectively.

Moreover, the results disclose that independent board members represent 42.6 % of the total directors, with a slight variation across the companies. The firms appear to have different sizes, as displayed by the FSIZE variable. The leverage ratio (TD) has an average ratio of 0.213, signifying that, on average, the debt ratio is 21.3 % of the firms' total capital employed. The firm age (AGE) variable ranged from 1 to 23 years, demonstrating a substantial variation between the firms. Family ownership (FO) registered a mean of 0.081, while government ownership (GO) recorded an average of 0.062. This evidence implies that 8.1 % and 6.2 % of firms’ shareholdings represented family and government ownership, respectively, during the period under review.

Correlation results

The research employed the Pearson correlation to detect a potential multicollinearity issue in the stated models, and the results are reported in Table 3. Multicollinearity arises when two or more explanatory variables are highly linked in a specified model. Accordingly, a correlation of 0.8 is generally considered high [81]. The evidence in Table 3 reveals that none of the coefficients of the explanatory variables reached 80 percent. Thus, the outcome suggests that multicollinearity does not affect our empirical evidence.

Regression results

Some basic tests were carried out to diagnose the data generated. Such tests include the variance inflation test (VIF), heteroscedasticity and serial correlation tests. The outcome of the tests reveals that serial correlation and heteroscedasticity appeared in the specified models. This instance paved the way for the researchers to use a robust regression to generate more reliable empirical findings [92,93]. However, the VIF test revealed values from 1.07 to 1.32, indicating the nonappearance of multicollinearity among the predictor variables [83,94].

The regression results are shown in Table 4. The results were categorized into models (A) and (B). Model (A) shows a direct link between board gender, foreign directorship, and financial sustainability. Model B reports the moderating effects. According to the outcome of Model A, board gender significantly and negatively influences financial sustainability. This evidence supports H1 that board gender is associated with a firm's financial sustainability. This negative effect implies that increasing women directors on corporate boards may yield lower sustainable performance. This result supports the prior literature that argues that board gender may fuel higher agency conflicts because of cognitive differences and ineffective communication [14,15]. These instances can weaken sound corporate governance practices, lower firm value, and lead to sustainable growth. Also, the outcome did not support agency and resource dependency theories, which predict that board gender may improve monitoring and provide greater chances of securing diverse resources, thereby promoting financial sustainability [10,20].

In contrast, foreign directorships positively and significantly affect financial sustainability, supporting H2. This positive effect suggests that financial sustainability may rise as the proportion of foreign directors on a board increases. This evidence confirms the argument that foreign directors' monitoring capacity may mitigate managerial entrenchment and help align divergent views between managers and shareholders, which may lower agency conflict [2,19,33,51]. Moreover, this finding is consistent with the perspective that these directors may utilize their networking capacity to link firms to secure finances in favorable terms [17,33]. Thus, directors’ greater monitoring and networking capacities may promote firm performance and facilitate long-term value creation for sustainable growth.

Furthermore, the regression outcomes in Table 4 under Model A indicate that some control variables may strongly influence financial sustainability. Based on this evidence, board size significantly affects financial sustainability, while board independence positively influences it. These findings advocate that a smaller board with many independent directors may serve as robust mechanisms that yield higher sustainable performance [73,75]. Therefore, a well-structured board may guide firms in reducing operational risk and boosting profitability for sustainable performance. These results suggest that firm size and age are insignificant. However, leverage also negatively affects financial sustainability. This evidence indicates that financial sustainability decreases as borrowing increases. Thus, excessive leverage may lead to higher risks and operational costs, lowering firms’ sustainable financial performance [10,74]. However, government ownership had a significant positive effect. This finding suggests that effective ownership monitoring may yield higher value, leading to financial sustainability.

Model (B) focused on the moderating effect. In this analysis, the moderator variable, family ownership (FO), and the interaction terms (BG∗FO) and (FD∗FO) were included to examine their effects on the model. According to the outcome, the moderator variable (FO) has a significant positive coefficient that supports H3. This result implies that financial sustainability may increase with an increase in family ownership. The evidence supports the argument that family-owned firms may have lesser agency costs and higher profitability [59,62]. Importantly, the interaction terms (BG∗FO) and (FD∗FO) have positive and significant coefficients, supporting H4a and H4b. The results indicate that family shareholdings moderate the impact of board gender and foreign directorship on financial sustainability. They also show that the presence of family shareholders may minimize agency conflicts, shape firms' strategic decisions, and boost firm value for financial sustainability. The policy implication of this finding is that family ownership serves as a vital mechanism that can neutralize the adverse effects of boardroom diversity on outcomes and strengthen firms’ internal governance in the Saudi context.

Additional evidence

We conducted another analysis using the generalized method of moments (GMM) technique to evaluate the robustness of our regression results displayed in Table 4. This framework controls time effects and endogeneity issues, thereby generating more consistent findings [10,85,95]. We employ a two-step GMM system because of its consistency and efficiency in panel data analysis [9,47,87]. The results fulfil the basic conditions of valid GMM estimates. There is Hansen statistics, which check for a correlation between the error term and GMM instruments (Hansen, 1982). Therefore, an insignificant P-value of the Hansen test implies that a GMM instrument set is robust and valid [87]. Accordingly, the Hansen test results in our models are insignificant, confirming the validity of our GMM models. Another parameter that discloses the robustness of GMM results is the serial correlation in the differenced error term. This can be determined based on the outcome of the first-order correlation (AR1) and second-order serial correlation (AR2). The first-order correlation (AR1) can be significant because of the lagged dependent variable in the first differenced equation [96]. However, the P-value of AR2 should not be significant. A significant p-value of AR2 indicates some misspecification and implies that the specified GMM suffers from the second-order serial correlation. Thus, the GMM results in Table 5 satisfied the underlying assumptions of a valid GMM specification. The additional analysis is presented in Table 5. Like the evidence in Table 4, Model (C) reports the direct effect results, and Model (D) serves as a moderating effect analysis.

The additional analysis in Model (C) shows that gender diversity and foreign directorships maintain negative and positive effects, respectively. In the moderation analysis in Model (D), family ownership appears positive and significant. In addition, the interaction terms had a positive impact. Generally, the findings obtained by applying the fixed effects method and GMM technique appear robust and consistent.

Conclusion

This study investigates how female and foreign directors on boards influence financial sustainability. This study further explores the influence of family shareholdings on this relationship. The research utilizes 1030 observations of listed Saudi non-financial firms covering 2013 to 2022. The study focuses on an important issue in corporate sustainability. The results suggest a strong positive effect of foreign directorships on financial sustainability, whereas board gender exerts a negative and significant influence. This positive effect indicates that a higher proportion of foreign directorships leads to consistent wealth creation, leading to sustainable performance. The adverse effect of board gender on financial sustainability implies that employing more women on Saudi corporate boards may lead to higher agency conflicts, erode firm value, and yield lower sustainable performance. Furthermore, the moderation analysis shows that the effects of board gender and foreign directorships may be more robust in firms with a higher ratio of family shareholdings. This outcome suggests that the stringent monitoring of family shareholdings may neutralize the adverse effects of board diversity on organizational outcomes. It also indicates that the presence of family shareholders may minimize agency conflicts, shape firms’ strategic decisions, and boost firm value for financial sustainability. The evidence supports the arguments of agency and upper-echelon theories that effective monitoring may compel managers to pursue firm value maximization goals.

This study adds to the body of knowledge by addressing a vital issue in corporate sustainability literature that prior studies have ignored. The research provides a wider investigation by exploring how family ownership may shape the relationship between board gender, foreign directorship, and financial sustainability. Hence, the evidence reveals a new insight into the factors influencing financial sustainability, thereby unveiling new thoughts on maximizing shareholder wealth. In addition, our findings may motivate regulators and managers to support companies in designing more diverse boards to minimize agency costs and maximize profitability for sustainable performance.

Although this research adds to the body of knowledge, certain limitations are to be declared. It is essential to state that this work concentrates on non-financial firms. Future works should also focus on financial firms. Second, we examine two diversity attributes: board gender and nationality. Therefore, future research should analyze other diversity traits like age, educational background and ethnicity to gain a broader understanding of the subject matter. Third, upcoming studies should carry out similar work in other contexts or geographical settings to provide further empirical findings for comparison. Lastly, forthcoming studies may use other dimensions of corporate ownership, such as institutional and government ownership, as intervening variables for comparison.
