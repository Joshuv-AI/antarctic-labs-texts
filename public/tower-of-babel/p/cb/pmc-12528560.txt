Heparanase2 inhibits the growth of gallbladder cancer by regulating the VEGF/VEGFR pathway
Authors: Dengyi Cao, Jiawei Chen, Shaobo Zhou, Jie Tang, Zixiang Liu, Zixiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Heparanase 2 (Hpa2), a homolog of heparinase, lacks the conventional heparinase activity associated with heparin degradation. However, it exhibits a greater affinity for heparin sulfate (HS) compared to heparinase. In our study, overexpression of Hpa2 significantly inhibited the viability, migration, and invasion of gallbladder cancer cells, both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, this inhibitory effect of Hpa2 was reversed upon administration of agonists targeting the VEGF/VEGFR pathway. These findings suggest that Hpa2 overexpression may impede the growth of NOZ cells, potentially through modulation of the VEGF/VEGFR signaling pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03696-5.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GB cancer) is a rare but highly aggressive malignancy, often diagnosed at advanced stages, which contributes to its poor prognosis [1, 2]. Due to the lack of effective early detection methods and targeted therapies, the survival rate for patients with GB cancer remains low [3]. While surgical resection is the only potentially curative treatment [4], the high recurrence rate and metastatic potential of GB cancer underscore the need for the identification of novel therapeutic targets and improved molecular understanding of its pathogenesis.

Heparanase 2 (Hpa2) is a member of the heparanase family of enzymes [5], which are involved in the remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and the regulation of tumor progression [6]. Unlike Heparanase 1 (Hpa1), which is known to promote cancer metastasis through the cleavage of heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) in the ECM, Hpa2 has been less studied but is believed to exert distinct, potentially opposite effects. Emerging evidence suggests that Hpa2 may play a role in regulating the tumor microenvironment and cellular processes such as migration, invasion, and adhesion [7], though its precise role remains controversial.

Recent studies have reported aberrant expression of ECM-related proteins in GB cancer [8], highlighting the importance of ECM remodeling in tumor progression. In various cancer types, Hpa2 has been shown to interact with components of the ECM, influencing cell behavior. However, its role in GB cancer remains largely unexplored.

This study aims to explore the functional role of Hpa2 in GB cancer, focusing on its potential to inhibit key processes such as cell migration, invasion, and adhesion. By investigating the effects of Hpa2 on the ECM and associated signaling pathways, we hope to uncover new insights into its therapeutic potential and its role as a candidate target for GB cancer treatment.

Methods and materials

Main materials and reagents

Human NOZ and GBC-SD gallbladder cancer cells were purchased from MeisenCTCC (Zhejiang, China). Cells were grown in DMEM high-glucose medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics. BALB/c Nude mice were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co. VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 polyclonal rabbit antibodies were purchased from Affinity (Jiangsu, China); HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit antibodies and Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit fluorescent antibodies were purchased from Proteintech (Hubei, China). Hpa2 overexpression lentiviral vectors were purchased from Gikai Genetics (Shanghai, China).

Real-time PCR

Total cellular RNA was extracted with Trizol, reverse transcribed into cDNA, and then subjected to real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR using the SYBR Green method. Reaction conditions: denaturation at 95 °C for 40 s, annealing extension at 60 °C for 30 s, a total of 40 cycles.

Hpa2 primer sequence (from 5’ to 3’ end):

F-primer: CAGGGCATTGATGTCGTGATAC.

R-primer: GCCAGTAGTCTGGTAATGGGTTA.

β-actin primer sequence (from 5’ to 3’ end):

F-primer: TCACCCACACTGTGCCCATCT.

R-primer: CAGCGGAACCGCTCATTGCC.

Cell proliferation

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 method by inoculating 2000 cells per well into 96-well plates and replacing the old medium with 100 µL of serum-free medium containing 10% CCK-8 (Biosharp; Anhui, China) during the measurement period. The results were detected using an enzyme marker, and the absorbance (OD) value corresponding to each well was measured at 450 nm.

Transwell migration and invasion

We inoculated mixed serum-free medium and cells into the upper chamber of Transwell nested 24-well plate chambers with or without matrix gel (Corning, USA) and the lower chamber with medium containing 10% FBS, and incubated for 24 h before fixation and staining.

Wound healing assay

We inoculated cells into 6-well plates and allowed them to reach 100% confluence. A 10 µL pipette tip was used to create a scratch (wound) in the cell monolayer. Images of the wounds were captured immediately (0 h) and after 24 h to assess cell migration and wound closure.

Cell colony formation

Inoculated 600 cells per well in 6-well plates with medium containing 10% FBS and changed medium every 3–5 days. Two to three weeks later, fixation and staining were performed, and pictures were taken using a camera.

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After collecting the medium from cell culture, the supernatant was removed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by an enzyme marker after incubation with samples according to the instructions of the ELISA assay kit (Rexin Bio, Fujian, China).

Flow cytometry

After cells were separated with EDTA-free trypsin according to the instructions for the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis kit (Rexin Bio, Zhejiang, China), the cells were washed twice by centrifugation in PBS. 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL PI were added, incubated for 5 min at room temperature and protected from light, and analyzed using flow cytometry.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed and extracted with RIPA lysis solution (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) for protein extraction. BCA kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) was used for protein quantification, and mixed with SDS for protein denaturation at 100 °C. Electrophoresis was performed on 10% PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) by electroblotting. PVDF membranes were blocked with milk and incubated with antibodies (VEGFA, VEGFB, VEGFC, VEGFD, VEGFR1, VEGFR2; 1:1000), followed by overnight incubation at 4℃ with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit antibodies (1:5000) for 2 h. The use of ECL allowed the membrane to be imaged under a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA) by exposure, and the grayscale values of the bands were measured by ImageJ.

Cellular Immunofluorescence staining

Prepared cell slides were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, followed by overnight incubation with VEGFA or VEGFR2 antibodies (1:200) at 4 ℃. The slides were then incubated with a Cy3-conjugated anti-rabbit fluorescent antibody (1:200) for 2 h. After incubation, the slides were treated with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (Biosharp, Anhui, China) and imaged using a fluorescent inverted microscope. For the fluorescent staining of reactive oxygen species, the cells were stained according to the kit instructions (KGI Bio, Jiangsu, China) and photographed under a fluorescent microscope.

Tumorigenicity

Cells were separated by trypsin/EDTA digestion, matrix gel was added, and the cell concentration was adjusted to 5 × 10⁶/mL. 100 µL of cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into the lateral abdominal wall in front of the left hind limb, and tumor size was recorded every 5 days after tumor formation. After the experiment, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were extracted and photographed. The maximal tumor size/burden permitted by Bengbu Medical College Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Committee is as follows: The maximum tumor volume should not exceed 2000 mm³ for mice. The maximum tumor diameter in any dimension should not exceed 15 mm (1.5 cm) for mice. The tumor burden should not exceed 10% of the animal’s normal body weight for therapeutic studies. We confirm that the maximal tumor size/burden was not exceeded in any animal used in this study. Tumor dimensions and volumes were monitored regularly using calibrated calipers, and volumes were calculated using the formula V =(L×W2)/2 (where L is the longest diameter and W is the perpendicular shorter diameter). Animals were humanely euthanized promptly upon reaching any predefined humane endpoint to minimize suffering. All procedures were conducted in strict compliance with our approved animal protocol and institutional animal welfare guidelines. The animal facilities and experiments were approved by the Bengbu Medical College Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Committee (2021No.309).

Histopathology of tumor grafts

Tumor xenografts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) and sectioned by paraffin embedding. Sections were stained with a hematoxylin-eosin kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) and photographed and imaged under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections of tumor xenografts were dewaxed and hydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 0.01 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). The sections were then blocked with 5% goat serum (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies (VEGFC, VEGFR2, 1:200) at 4 °C. The sections were then incubated with a biotin-labeled anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:100) for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing with PBS, the sections were treated with a streptavidin-biotin complex (SABC, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. Color development was achieved using DAB reagent (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The sections were then mounted with neutral gum and imaged under a light microscope.

Statistics

The obtained experimental data were statistically analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.3, and all measurements were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical methods used were group t-test or one-way ANOVA, with P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001) considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated at least three times with similar results.

Results

Validation of transfection efficiency

We overexpressed Hpa2 by transfecting NOZ and GBC-SD cells with Hpa2 overexpression lentiviral vector, and Blank cells transfected with blank plasmid lentiviral vector and normal cells as controls. The results showed that both NOZ-Hpa2 and NOZ-Blank cells were successfully transfected by lentivirus (Fig. 1A), and the expression of Hpa2 mRNA and protein in NOZ-Hpa2 cells was significantly higher than that in NOZ-Blank cells and normal NOZ cells (P < 0.01; Fig. 1B-C), indicating the successful construction of Hpa2 overexpressing NOZ cells. Similarly, the expression of Hpa2 mRNA and protein in GBC-SD-Hpa2 cells was significantly higher than that in GBC-SD-Blank cells and normal GBC-SD cells (P < 0.01; Figure S1 A-B), indicating the successful construction of Hpa2 overexpressing GBC-SD cells.

Heparanase2 inhibits the growth of gallbladder cancer cells

We used CCK-8 assay to detect the effect of Hpa2 on the proliferation of NOZ cells and GBC-SD cells, and the results showed that the cell viability of NOZ-Hpa2 group cells at 24, 48 and 72 h was smaller than that of NOZ and NOZ-Blank groups, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A), as well as in GBC-SD cells (P < 0.01; Figure S1C). These results indicated that the proliferation of NOZ cells and GBC-SD cells was inhibited after Hpa2 stable overexpression. The wound healing assay and transwell migration and invasion assays were performed to examine the effect of Heparanase2 on the migration ability of NOZ cells and GBC-SD cells. The results showed that the scratch healing rate in the NOZ-Hpa2 group and GBC-SD-Hpa2 group was significantly lower than that in the NOZ and NOZ-Blank groups and GBC-SD and GBC-SD-Blank group after 24 h. Additionally, the number of cells migrating and invading in the transwell assay was significantly lower in the NOZ-Hpa2 group compared to the NOZ and NOZ-Blank groups, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.01; Fig. 2B-C, Figure S1 D-E). The results of colony formation assay showed that the number of colonies produced by the NOZ-Hpa2 group was significantly smaller than that of the NOZ and NOZ-Blank groups, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05; Fig. 2D). The above results indicated that Heparanase2 inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion ability of gallbladder cancer cells, and suppressed the growth of gallbladder cancer cells.

Heparanase2 inhibits VEGF expression and promotes the production of reactive oxygen species and apoptosis

To investigate the mechanism of Hpa2 inhibition of gallbladder cancer growth, we examined the effect of Heparanase2 on the expression of VEGF/VEGFR pathway-related proteins in NOZ cells by ELISA assay and Western Blot. The results showed that the content of secreted VEGF factors in the supernatant of NOZ-Hpa2 cell culture was lower than that in NOZ group and NOZ- Blank group. The VEGFA, VEGFB, VEGFC, VEGFD, VEGFR1, and VEGFR2 protein expression levels in OE-Hpa2 cells were also lower than those in the other two groups (P < 0.01; Fig. 3A, B, Figure S1F), indicating that Heparanase2 inhibited VEGF/VEGFR pathway protein expression in gallbladder cancer cells. Subsequently, we added the VEGF/VEGFR pathway agonist Mersalyl to NOZ-Hpa2 cells and verified that the agonist promoted VEGF expression by cellular immunofluorescence, and CCK8 as well as Transwell assays again examined the cell viability as well as migration invasion ability, and the results showed that the addition of Mersalyl The VEGFC expression content of NOZ-Hpa2 cells increased after the addition of Mersalyl, and Mersalyl also alleviated the inhibitory effect of Heparanase2 on NOZ cells (P < 0.01; Fig. 3C–E). We also examined apoptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels using flow cytometry and fluorescence staining, respectively. Our results showed that Hpa2 promoted both apoptosis and ROS production (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A, B). Additionally, we used the CHEA3 platform to predict 35 transcription factors potentially interacting between Hpa2 and VEGF (Table 1; Fig. 4C).

Heparanase2 inhibits the tumorigenesis of gallbladder cancer cells

In the tumorigenesis assay using nude mice, we observed that xenografts derived from NOZ-Hpa2 cells were smaller compared to those from normal NOZ cells. Additionally, the tumor size increased significantly in mice treated with Mersalyl (P < 0.01; see Fig. 5A), suggesting that Heparanase2 inhibits tumorigenic potential in NOZ gallbladder cancer cells. Furthermore, neovascularization within the tumors of the NOZ-Hpa2 group was reduced, with the blood vessels arranged in a monolayer of flat cells, compared to the normal NOZ cell group. Immunohistochemical analysis also revealed lower expression levels of CD31, VEGFC and VEGFR2 in the NOZ-Hpa2 tumors. The inhibitory effect of Heparanase2 overexpression on tumor growth and angiogenesis was diminished when Mersalyl was added. These results imply that Heparanase2 has an inhibitory effect on gallbladder cancer and may be mediated by affecting tumor angiogenesis through the VEGF/VEGFR pathway.

Discussion

Although heparanase has been reported in many tumors and its effects on tumors are mostly promotive [9, 10], Hpa2, a homolog of heparanase, does not act as clearly as heparanase. Heparanase is an enzyme that destroys degrades the extracellular matrix and degrades heparin sulfate (HS) and heparin sulfate proteoglycan side chains [11]. Tumor cells can release large amounts of heparanase, which degrades the vascular basement membrane and extracellular matrix, thus promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis [12]. It also induces angiogenesis by activating growth factors, inhibits activated T lymphocytes, and alters the tumor microenvironment [13]. Heparanase 2 can inhibit heparanase activity by competing with it, thereby weakening its pro-carcinogenic effects. Heparanase is known to have an oncogenic effect in gastric cancer [14], head and neck cancer [15], pancreatic cancer [6], bladder cancer [16], and smooth muscle sarcoma [17]. However, heparanase also promotes proliferation in colorectal cancer and is associated with prognosis [18]. Additionally, it has a pro-metastatic effect in metastatic thyroid cancer (PTC), which may be related to the methylation of the heparanase 2 gene promoter during transcription. Here, our study provides evidence that Hpa2 inhibits the growth of gallbladder cancer cells. Specifically, overexpression of Hpa2 leads to reduced proliferation, migration, and invasive abilities of these cells, resulting in smaller tumor xenografts.

The VEGF/VEGFR pathway is a well-known signaling pathway closely related to angiogenesis, and inhibition of the VEGF/VEGFR pathway can enhance the body’s anti-tumor immunity, and bevacizumab (Avastin), which targets the VEGF/VEGFR pathway, is currently available clinically for the adjuvant treatment of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer and metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [19]. VEGF/VEGFR is in turn widely involved in angiogenesis in various types of tumors, thus promoting tumor growth as well as metastasis [20]. Our study linked Hpa2 with the VEGF/VEGFR pathway in gallbladder cancer. Notably, although Hpa2 reduced the expression of VEGF as well as VEGFR, when we treated cells as well as mice with VEGF/VEGFR pathway agonists, the VEGF as well as VEGFR expression content was increased and the inhibitory effect of Hpa2 on gallbladder cancer was correspondingly diminished, and we also predicted common transcription factors between Hpa2 and VEGF. The presence of these transcription factors could help us to better explain how Hpa2 regulates VEGF, but we have not yet validated them experimentally and need to explore them further.

A key aspect of the relationship between VEGF and heparanase involves the regulation of heparanase activity through post-translational modifications (PTMs), particularly acetylation. Acetylation, a reversible modification of lysine residues, can significantly alter the structure and function of proteins, influencing their activity, stability, and interactions with other molecules. In the case of heparanase, acetylation may modulate its enzymatic activity, potentially enhancing or inhibiting its ability to cleave heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) in the extracellular matrix (ECM) [21]. This modification may influence the bioavailability of VEGF by regulating its release from ECM-bound storage. Moreover, acetylated heparanase might affect the interaction between VEGF and its receptors on endothelial cells, thereby influencing angiogenesis in the tumor microenvironment. The exact mechanism by which acetylation affects heparanase’s interactions with VEGF is still under investigation, but it is hypothesized that acetylation could either promote or inhibit the release of VEGF, ultimately impacting tumor vascularization and growth. Understanding this molecular regulation provides important insights into how heparanase, through PTMs like acetylation, may regulate VEGF availability and angiogenesis, which are critical processes in cancer progression and metastasis, including in gallbladder cancer.

It should also be noted that we also found that Hpa2 promotes apoptosis and increases reactive oxygen species levels in gallbladder cancer cells, which may be associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress, and this is in line with the findings of some researchers who support that Hpa2 enhances the endoplasmic reticulum stress response, resulting in increased apoptosis and diminished growth of cells [6]. However, we did not measure the expression levels of Hpa2 in different gallbladder cancer cell lines, nor did we investigate how varying levels of Hpa2 expression affect the proliferation and migration of these cells under physiological conditions. Additionally, we have not yet explored the impact of Hpa2 knockout on the biological functions of gallbladder cancer cells. Moving forward, we will also investigate the specific molecular mechanisms through which Hpa2 regulates the VEGF signaling pathway.

Conclusion

In summary, our results provide a series of evidence that Hpa2 inhibits the growth of gallbladder cancer, which may be mediated through the VEGF/VEGFR pathway, and that Hpa2 also promotes apoptosis and reactive oxygen species production in gallbladder cancer, which require further detailed studies to elucidate.
