The relationship between professional moral courage and patient safety silence among nurses
Authors: Vahid Yousofvand, Niloofar Sani, Salman Khazaei, Mohammad Torabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient safety silence—the deliberate withholding of concerns or observations related to patient safety—has emerged as a critical barrier to ensuring safe healthcare delivery. Among nurses, patient safety silence in the face of safety errors may result in adverse outcomes for patients. Given the ethical nature of speaking up, identifying psychological and moral determinants, such as professional moral courage, is essential. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the relationship between professional moral courage and patient safety silence among nurses.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive-correlational study was conducted from 2024 to 2025 in hospitals affiliated with Hamadan University of Medical Sciences. Using stratified sampling, 214 eligible nurses were selected for the study. Inclusion criteria were: at least one year of clinical experience, a bachelor’s degree or higher in nursing, and provision of written informed consent. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded. Data were collected using a demographic information form, as described by Sekerka et al. Professional Moral Courage Scale, and the Tangirala and Ramanujam Patient Safety Silence Questionnaire. Ethical approval was obtained, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.ResultsA significant negative correlation was found between professional moral courage and patient safety silence (r = -0.451, p < 0.001). In the initial regression model, professional moral courage significantly predicted lower levels of patient safety silence (β = -0.451, p < 0.001; Adjusted R² = 0.20). After incorporating demographic and occupational variables, the explained variance increased to 61.6% (Adjusted R² = 0.616), and professional moral courage remained a significant predictor (β = -0.270, p < 0.001). Factors associated with reduced patient safety silence included male gender, over 10 years of experience, and moderate to high job satisfaction. In contrast, insufficient ethics education, night shifts, high nurse-to-patient ratios, and burnout were associated with increased patient safety silence.ConclusionProfessional moral courage was found to have an inverse association with patient safety silence and remained a significant predictor even after adjusting for other factors. Male gender, longer experience, and job satisfaction reduced patient safety silence, while insufficient ethics education, night shifts, high workload, and burnout increased it. Therefore, strengthening professional moral courage through education and support may help reduce patient safety silence and improve safety culture in healthcare.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Nurses form a fundamental pillar of the healthcare system, responsible for delivering holistic care that encompasses patients’ physical, emotional, social, and spiritual well-being [1, 2]. Their critical contributions help advance patient health and uphold care standards [3–5]. However, evidence suggests that nurses frequently encounter complex ethical dilemmas in their daily professional practice, requiring them to make challenging decisions to maintain or enhance patient safety [6].

Given that one of the primary goals of healthcare organizations is to prevent patient harm and ensure safety during care delivery [7, 8], it is essential to view patient safety as a shared responsibility and to identify and effectively manage threats to it. Among these challenges is the phenomenon of “patient safety silence”—the tendency of healthcare staff, particularly nurses, to withhold concerns, ideas, or information about actual or potential threats to patient safety, even when they are aware of such risks [7].

Meanwhile, Multiple factors contribute to patient safety silence, ranging from individual-level issues such as apathy, lack of self-confidence, or prior experiences with punitive responses, to organizational and cultural influences like prevailing workplace norms, policies, and the broader societal context [8, 9]. Patient safety silence can delay the identification and resolution of system-related problems, potentially leading to serious patient care errors [10].

Recent studies suggest that factors such as ethical sensitivity, organizational ethical climate, and—most notably—professional moral courage can help mitigate patient safety silences. These attributes empower nurses to speak up about concerns and prevent adverse events. Cultivating a supportive, ethical environment where nurses feel psychologically safe to disclose errors is essential for reducing patient safety silence and promoting patient safety [7, 11].

Professional moral courage, a fundamental principle in nursing, serves as a coping mechanism in ethically challenging situations [12]. It reflects an individual’s ability to act in accordance with ethical principles, even when faced with negative consequences, social pressure, or risk [13]. Despite their clinical competence, nurses may experience moral hesitation in stressful or complex scenarios—not due to ignorance, but because of fear of consequences and a lack of support. In such situations, professional moral courage complements professional knowledge and skills, enabling ethical action [14].

Nurses with high levels of professional moral courage tend to make sound decisions in critical situations, thereby enhancing the quality of care and protecting patient safety. Research indicates that increased professional moral courage among nurses is associated with improved care outcomes, enhanced safety, and enhanced professional performance [11, 15, 16]. In contrast, its absence can result in burnout, moral distress, disengagement, and even attrition from the profession [15, 17].

Meanwhile, despite the growing attention to patient safety in recent decades, the phenomenon of Patient Safety Silence—in which nurses deliberately withhold concerns about safety threats—remains a hidden yet influential barrier to the delivery of safe care [18–21]. While organizational factors, such as leadership style and team communication, have been extensively examined, there is limited empirical evidence on the role of individual characteristics, especially professional moral courage, in reducing patient safety silence. Although professional moral courage has been linked to ethical decision-making, whistleblowing, and resilience in challenging situations, its direct impact on nurses’ willingness to speak up about safety threats—especially within local health systems—has not been systematically investigated [18, 21–24].

This research gap is especially relevant in the Iranian healthcare context, where cultural norms, hierarchical power structures, and fear of blame or punishment often hinder open communication about patient safety risks [25–27]. In such an environment, even nurses who are ethically knowledgeable and professionally competent may hesitate to report unsafe practices. Professional moral courage, in this context, could serve as a key enabler for ethical expression and proactive safety behavior [18, 19, 24]. However, there is a lack of methodologically sound evidence on the relationship between professional moral courage and patient safety silence among Iranian nurses.

Since professional moral courage is a skill that can be learned and nurtured, investigating its association with patient safety silence holds significant potential for improving patient safety culture. Understanding this link could inform the development of ethical leadership initiatives, resilience-focused training, and organizational policies aimed at enhancing nurses’ readiness to speak up about safety risks. As such, this study investigates the relationship between professional moral courage and patient safety silence, offering an innovative response to pressing challenges in high-stakes clinical environments.

Methods

Study setting

This study employed a descriptive-correlational cross-sectional design, conducted between October 23, 2024, and May 20, 2025. The research involved 214 nurses working at four hospitals affiliated with Hamadan University of Medical Sciences in Iran: Besat, Shahid Beheshti, Farshchian Heart Hospital, and Sina Farshchian Hospital.

Participants

A total of 214 nurses who met the inclusion criteria participated in the study. Eligibility criteria included providing written informed consent, having at least one year of clinical work experience, and holding a minimum of a bachelor’s degree in nursing. Incomplete questionnaire responses constituted the exclusion criterion.

Sample size determination

In the absence of comparable prior research, a pilot study was carried out with 10 qualified nurses to determine the necessary sample size. Using the obtained correlation coefficient of 0.255 between professional moral courage and patient safety silence, along with a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 95%, and an allowance for a 15% attrition rate, the calculated sample size required was 223 nurses.

Sampling method and data collection

Proportional stratified sampling was used in this study. First, the researcher obtained the total number of nurses employed at each hospital through their respective IT departments. Each hospital was treated as a separate stratum. Based on the estimated sample size and the number of nurses in each hospital, the required number of participants per hospital was calculated proportionally.

Within each hospital, five units were randomly selected. The researcher then compiled a list of all nurses in those units. After verifying eligibility, a revised list was prepared. A proportional number of participants from each unit was then randomly selected using a lottery method (nurses’ names were written on slips of paper and drawn from a container). Three nurses who submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis.

To ensure accuracy in data collection, one of the researchers was present in the selected hospitals during data gathering. After establishing rapport, the researcher explained the study’s objectives and provided participants with comprehensive information. Written informed consent was obtained, including consent for the anonymous publication of findings.

Participants were given clear written and verbal instructions on how to complete the self-report questionnaires. Each participant was asked to complete the forms within 20 to 30 min and was encouraged to reach out to the researcher with any questions or concerns. Additionally, participants were advised to postpone completion if they felt fatigued, were engaged in patient care, or if a physician was conducting rounds, and to resume when in a calm and stable state. The researcher coordinated data collection times to respect participants’ work schedules and comfort levels.

To minimize response bias, the researcher was not present during questionnaire completion, thus reducing any potential influence on responses. Questionnaires were completed in a quiet, private room within the hospital, typically during work breaks or immediately after shifts, depending on each nurse’s availability. This approach ensured privacy, reduced disruption to clinical duties, and allowed participants adequate time to complete the instruments without pressure.

The instruments used in this study have been widely applied in previous peer-reviewed studies in Iran, particularly with similar populations. Their use in the current research adhered to common academic and ethical practices in the region, with proper citation of the sources.

Data collection tools

The data in this study were collected using three instruments: a demographic questionnaire, the Patient Safety Silence Questionnaire, and the Professional Moral Courage Scale.

Demographic questionnaire

The demographic questionnaire included items on personal and professional characteristics, such as age, gender, marital status, level of education, years of clinical experience, and hospital ward. Based on a comprehensive review of relevant literature, the authors developed the demographic information questionnaire, which was then subjected to expert review, revision, and face validation by a panel of 10 nursing faculty members.

Patient safety silence questionnaire

The Patient Safety Silence Questionnaire used in this study was originally developed by Tangirala and Ramanujam [28] and later adapted and validated for use among clinical nurses by Han et al. [29]. The instrument consists of five items rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree). Higher scores indicate a greater level of patient safety issues related to silence. In a study by Hashemian et al., the reliability of this questionnaire was reported with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 [30]. In the current study, the internal consistency was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89.

Professional moral courage scale

The Professional Moral Courage Scale for nurses was developed by Sekerka et al. in 2009 [31]. It contains 15 items across five dimensions: moral agency, multiple values, threat endurance, going beyond compliance, and moral goal. The scale assesses nurses’ professional moral courage. Responses are scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with a total score range of 15 to 75. Scores between 15 and 35 are considered low, 36–55 moderate, and 56–75 high levels of professional moral courage. In a study by Rahimi et al., the Cronbach’s alpha for this instrument was reported as 0.85 [32]. In the present study, internal consistency was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were used to summarize the data. Inferential statistics included the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality, Pearson correlation coefficient, independent t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and multiple regression analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hamadan University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.UMSHA.REC.1403.541). Prior to data collection, participants received a clear explanation of the study’s objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from all individuals in accordance with institutional and national ethical standards, as well as the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. No names or personally identifiable information (e.g., contact details or ID numbers) were collected at any stage. To ensure anonymity and confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique identification code, preventing any link between responses and individual identities. All electronic data were stored on a password-protected computer, accessible only to the research team, while hard-copy materials were secured in a locked cabinet. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences. They were assured that all information would be kept confidential and used exclusively for research purposes.

Results

The study revealed that the largest group of participants was aged 25–30 years, comprising 36.9%. A majority identified as female (78%) and were married (76.6%). The majority of nurses had earned a bachelor’s degree in nursing (84.6%) and possessed 1 to 5 years of clinical experience (55.6%). A substantial number worked in non-critical care units (77.1%) and on rotating shifts (58.4%). Additionally, the nurse-to-patient ratio was most frequently reported as 3 to 6 patients per shift (65.9%).

Regarding occupational well-being, 44.9% reported moderate job satisfaction, 35% reported very low job satisfaction, and 27.6% experienced high levels of job burnout. Additionally, 48.1% rated perceived organizational support as low, and only 19.2% had received professional ethics training (Table 1).

To identify predictors of patient safety silence among nurses, a multiple linear regression analysis (Enter method) was performed in two models. In the first model, only professional moral courage was entered. The results indicated that for every one-point increase in professional moral courage—holding other variables constant—the patient safety silence score decreased by an average of 0.321 units (β = -0.451, p < 0.001), accounting for 20% of the variance in patient safety silence (Adjusted R² = 0.20).

In the second model, after including demographic and occupational variables, the model’s predictive power increased significantly, explaining 61.6% of the variance in patient safety silence (Adjusted R² = 0.616). Professional moral courage remained a significant predictor (β = -0.270, p < 0.001), with each one-point increase associated with a 0.187-unit decrease in the patient safety silence score, controlling for other factors. Several additional factors were identified as predictors of lower patient safety silence scores: male gender (β = -0.118, p = 0.008), more than 10 years of work experience (β = -0.200, p < 0.001), moderate job satisfaction (β = -0.326, p < 0.001), and high job satisfaction (β = -0.122, p = 0.013). In contrast, insufficient ethics education (β = 0.103, p = 0.022), working night shifts (β = 0.134, p = 0.005), a nurse-to-patient ratio greater than six per shift (β = 0.347, p < 0.001), high burnout (β = 0.127, p = 0.039), and very high burnout (β = 0.252, p < 0.001) were significant predictors of increased patient safety silence (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the relationship between professional moral courage and patient safety silence among nurses. The findings indicated a significant negative correlation between professional moral courage and patient safety silence, suggesting that nurses with higher levels of professional moral courage were less likely to remain silent when faced with safety concerns. This highlights the importance of professional moral courage as a crucial factor in reducing patient safety silence.

Previous studies have also confirmed this relationship. Kashani et al. [33] reported a significant positive relationship between safe care and professional moral courage. In a review by Arablarimi et al. [34], professional moral courage was linked to professional errors among nurses that compromise patient safety. Elhihi et al. [35] identified professional moral courage as a main predictor of error reporting and emphasized the importance of an ethical work environment that supports reporting without fear. Similarly, Mohammadi et al. [11] found that both moral sensitivity and professional moral courage are related to safe nursing behaviors. Furthermore, Afshari et al. [36] and Yılmaz et al. [37] reported significant associations between moral sensitivity, professional moral courage, and disclosure of safety concerns. Carlisle et al. [38] attributed improved care quality and safety to nurses with higher professional moral courage, and Jeong et al. [7] found a negative relationship between patient safety silence and nurses’ moral sensitivity.

Theoretical frameworks from organizational behavior and safety culture help contextualize these findings. For example, Han et al. [29] found that professional moral courage, as a psychological and ethical capacity, contributes to the establishment of psychological safety in the workplace, enabling staff to report concerns and errors without fear of negative consequences. In this regard, the concept of a Just Culture—which emphasizes accountability alongside support—is particularly relevant. Nurses with professional moral courage are more likely to tolerate environmental pressures and fear of punishment and to advocate for patients’ rights [38–41].

Beyond professional moral courage, this study identified several individual and organizational factors influencing patient safety silence. Male gender, more than ten years of work experience, and moderate to high job satisfaction were associated with reduced patient safety silence. In contrast, lack of ethics education, night shifts, high nurse-to-patient ratios, and job burnout were associated with increased patient safety silence. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported the influence of age, experience, working hours, and ethics training on safety attitudes among nurses [36]. Moreover, other research has shown that professional moral courage, gender, and work experience can explain up to 54% of the variance in safe nursing care [33].

Hashemian et al. [30] identified several organizational barriers to reporting, including limited nurse involvement in decision-making, concerns over care quality, physician-nurse communication, and job position. Similarly, Han et al. [29] emphasized the role of trust in supervisors, organizational culture, and interdisciplinary collaboration in reducing patient safety silence by strengthening safety motivation. A qualitative review by Zou et al. [42] found that fear of negative consequences, lack of managerial support, and a suppressive organizational culture were the main drivers of patient safety silence, leading to reduced job satisfaction and compromised patient safety.

The results highlight the multidimensional nature of patient safety silence, shaped by personal, institutional, and external factors. As a vital factor, professional moral courage empowers nurses to voice safety issues confidently and with a deep sense of accountability, particularly in situations involving ethical dilemmas [24]. This ability builds resilience against workplace challenges and fear of reprisal, while reinforcing commitment to professional ethical standards. Additionally, greater work experience may enhance nurses’ understanding of the consequences of unsafe care and strengthen their skills and confidence in high-pressure situations [43]. Job satisfaction, by improving organizational commitment, intrinsic motivation, and active participation in decision-making, also contributes to reducing patient safety silence [44, 45].

Notably, males were less likely to remain silent about patient safety issues. Some studies suggest that male nurses may demonstrate more assertive communication styles and be less influenced by fear of judgment in stressful environments [46]. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as only 22% of the sample were male, indicating a gender imbalance that may limit the generalizability of the result. Furthermore, cultural and organizational norms in Iran—particularly hierarchical workplace dynamics—may shape gendered communication patterns and perceptions of authority in distinct ways. Thus, future studies with balanced samples and attention to cultural context are needed to clarify these gender-related differences.

The present study also found that contextual factors, such as a lack of structured ethics education, night shifts, and heavy workloads, exacerbate patient safety silence. Without formal ethics training, may struggle to manage ethical dilemmas and hesitate to report safety concerns [47, 48]. Night shifts, often associated with understaffing, fatigue, and reduced supervision, have been found to decrease the motivation to report safety issues [49, 50]. A high nurse-to-patient ratio and job burnout further limited opportunities for incident reporting, contributing to patient safety silence [51–53].

In the present study, burnout was identified as a significant predictor; however, its interaction with professional moral courage was not explored. Future research is recommended to investigate this relationship to understand better how professional moral courage may influence the impact of burnout on patient safety silence. Such insights could inform targeted interventions to enhance professional moral courage and reduce burnout-related patient safety silence.

Based on these findings, targeted interventions such as interdisciplinary, scenario-based ethics education, improving night shift conditions through active supervision and psychological support, adjusting nurse-to-patient ratios, and burnout prevention are recommended. Utilizing experienced nurses as mentors and fostering a Just Culture that encourages error reporting without fear of blame are also crucial. The success of these strategies requires strong managerial commitment and interdisciplinary collaboration at the organizational level.

In conclusion, this study suggests that patient safety silence is not merely a result of difficult working conditions and organizational limitations—it may also be deeply rooted in individual characteristics, attitudes, and the psychological and ethical capacities of nurses. Addressing this phenomenon requires a comprehensive, systems-based approach that integrates individual empowerment, a supportive safety culture, effective organizational structures, and responsive policies. These findings can serve as a foundation for evidence-based interventions in the field of patient safety, playing a pivotal role in improving care quality, fostering clinical transparency, and preserving nurses’ professional dignity.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that may influence the validity and generalizability of its findings. First, the use of self-administered questionnaires may have introduced response bias, as answers could be affected by participants’ emotions or situational factors. Second, the possibility of self-selection bias should be acknowledged; nurses who agreed to participate may have inherently higher levels of moral courage, which could have influenced the observed associations. Third, restricting the sample to university-affiliated hospitals limits the applicability of the results to other healthcare contexts, such as private or military institutions. Fourth, the correlational design prevents causal interpretation of the findings. Finally, the result related to the male gender should be interpreted cautiously due to the unbalanced sample and the specific cultural context of Iran. Future research with more diverse settings, balanced samples, and longitudinal or experimental approaches is recommended to strengthen the validity and applicability of the results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that higher professional moral courage is related to reduced patient safety silence among nurses. Additionally, longer work experience, greater job satisfaction, and being male were associated with lower levels of patient safety silence. In contrast, inadequate ethics education, night shifts, excessive workloads, and occupational burnout contributed to an increase of patient safety silence. We recommend that administrators foster professional moral courage, improve work conditions, reduce stress, and create psychologically safe environments that encourage reporting. These findings provide a foundation for evidence-based interventions to improve care quality and reduce adverse outcomes. These interventions can inform policy and managerial efforts aimed at strengthening patient safety culture and supporting nurses’ professional integrity. Future research should use longitudinal or interventional designs to test training programs that strengthen moral courage and reduce patient safety silence.
