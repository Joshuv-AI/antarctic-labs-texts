SlJMJ12 overexpression confers improved cadmium tolerance in Arabidopsis and tomato
Authors: Geli Wang, Yexi Gao, Yanlong Dong, Yuanqiang Shi, Yuqi Wu, Yangcui Li, Chao Chen, Xiaoxia Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCadmium (Cd) is a toxic heavy metal threatening ecosystems and crop production. The functions of histone demethylases in plant Cd tolerance remain poorly understood. We functionally characterized the tomato histone demethylase SlJMJ12 using Arabidopsis and tomato. Cd rapidly induces leaf-expressed SlJMJ12, with peak expression at 3 h post-treatment. Compared with the wild type, SlJMJ12 loss-of-function mutants show Cd sensitivity in both Arabidopsis and tomato, with reduced biomass, higher plant Cd and ROS levels, and decreased antioxidant enzyme activities, whereas overexpression improves Cd resistance. Mechanistically, SlJMJ12 modulates genes governing metal uptake, translocation and chelation (AtZIP1, AtABCC1, AtNRAMP4, SlIRT1, SlNRAMP3, SlGSH1). Y2H screening further identified Cd-inducible SlKLCR1-Like as its putative interacting partner, which requires further functional verification. In summary, this study demonstrates the positive regulatory role of SlJMJ12 in plant Cd tolerance. These results enrich the regulatory mechanism underlying plant heavy metal tolerance and provide a valuable gene resource for breeding Cd-tolerant crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09362-4.Keywords: Cadmium tolerance, Histone demethylase, SlJMJ12, Cadmium stress, Reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Environmental contamination by heavy metals has become increasingly severe, with more than 20 million hectares of land affected worldwide [1]. Among these pollutants, cadmium (Cd) stands out due to its high mobility and toxicity. Its continual accumulation in the environment has drawn global concern because of its harmful effects on ecosystem stability and human health [2]. Industrial emissions and mining activities release cadmium into the environment, after which it infiltrates soils through seepage, runoff, and atmospheric deposition [3]. Cadmium’s environmental hazards extend beyond ecosystem damage; it also accumulates through the food chain, posing direct risks to human health. Long-term Cd exposure has been linked to developmental abnormalities, acute toxicity, and increased incidence of several cancers [4]. Although significant progress has been made in understanding Cd pollution sources and remediation strategies, substantial challenges remain in effectively addressing and managing cadmium contamination.

During their growth, plants are often exposed to elevated Cd levels in contaminated soils, where the metal imposes widespread toxicity on plant physiology and development. Cd exposure inhibits overall growth and leads to visible symptoms such as root browning, leaf wilting, chlorosis, and, in severe cases, tissue necrosis [5]. At the cellular and biochemical levels, Cd exerts its toxicity through several interconnected pathways. It compromises membrane integrity by competing with essential metal ions, thereby disrupting key metabolic processes, including photosynthesis and respiration [6]. Cd also triggers excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damages redox-sensitive enzymes, induces oxidative stress, and ultimately harms cellular DNA [7]. Additionally, Cd interferes with proper protein folding, disrupting protein homeostasis and causing endoplasmic reticulum stress, all of which further reduce cell viability [8]. Given the breadth of these effects, elucidating the mechanisms underlying plant responses to Cd stress is crucial. Such research deepens our understanding of the molecular regulatory networks that govern plant adaptation to heavy metal exposure and aids in identifying genes involved in Cd uptake, transport, and sequestration. Ultimately, these insights lay the groundwork for breeding Cd-tolerant crop varieties and developing effective strategies for mitigating environmental Cd contamination.

Epigenetics refers to heritable regulation of gene expression that occurs without changes to the underlying DNA sequence. This regulation is mediated through processes such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, RNA modifications, chromatin remodeling, and the activity of non-coding RNAs [9]. Accumulating studies, predominantly from Arabidopsis, confirm epigenetic modifications including DNA methylation and JMJ-mediated histone demethylation are vital for plant development and environmental stress adaptation [10, 11]. Recent studies have also demonstrated the indispensable roles of m6A RNA modification and INO80-mediated chromatin remodeling in regulating plant growth and stress adaptation [12, 13].

Notably, heavy metal cadmium can trigger widespread epigenetic reprogramming in plants via DNA methylation, histone modification and small RNAs [14]. The discovery of Lysine Specific Demethylase 1 (LSD1), the first histone demethylase, revealed histone demethylation as a crucial layer of epigenetic control [15]. Subsequently, Jumonji C (JmjC) proteins were identified as a second class of histone demethylases [16], and numerous studies have shown that they play important roles in plant responses to abiotic stresses. In Arabidopsis, for example, JMJ15 participates in salt stress responses, and jmj15 mutants display heightened salt sensitivity [17]. In rice, JMJ710 acts as a negative regulator of drought tolerance [18]. Besides model crops above, functional investigations in maize also indicated that these predicted genes of the ZmJMJ gene family were responsive to heat stress treatment, suggesting their potential roles in heat stress response, with representative members including ZmJMJ2, ZmJMJ3, ZmJMJ4 and ZmJMJ5 [19]. Similarly, JmjC family genes in G. hirsutum are responsive to cold and osmotic stresses and are speculated to exert regulatory functions under cold and drought conditions, among which GhJMJ3A, GhJMJ18A and GhJMJ18D serve as typical representative members [20].

With respect to Cd stress, several tomato JMJ genes have been implicated. Overexpression of the histone demethylase SlJMJ524 enhances Cd tolerance in Arabidopsis by modulating metal transporter gene expression and flavonoid biosynthesis [21]. Similarly, overexpression of SlJMJ18 and SlJMJ23 confers Cd tolerance by regulating metal transport and hormone homeostasis [22]. Notably, SlJMJ12 belongs to the conserved KDM4/JHDM3 subfamily, and relevant researches of this subfamily under Cd stress have not been reported in tomato so far. Given the global economic importance of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), elucidating its mechanisms of heavy metal detoxification holds both scientific and practical value. Therefore, we focused on SlJMJ12 to explore its function in Cd tolerance.

In this study, the SlJMJ12 gene from tomato was cloned, and both overexpression lines (SlJMJ12-OE) and loss-of-function (jmj12) mutants were generated in Arabidopsis and tomato. The results showed that SlJMJ12 overexpression significantly enhanced Cd tolerance in both species, whereas jmj12 mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to Cd stress.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growing conditions

The Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia was used in this study. Seeds of wild-type (WT), overexpression (OE) and mutant lines were vernalized for 3 d, sown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) solid medium, and cultured for 14 d prior to transplanting. This 14-day in-vitro cultivation duration was determined based on our cumulative laboratory data and the experimental protocol reported by Guo et al. [23], which is widely adopted for uniform seedling growth before soil transplantation in Arabidopsis cadmium-related research. Seedlings were then transplanted into pots filled with nutrient soil (peat soil: vermiculite = 2:1, v/v) and grown in a greenhouse at 25 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

Solanum lycopersicum Micro-Tom was employed. Plump seeds of WT, OE and mutant tomatoes were rinsed 3–4 times with sterile water, placed on moist filter paper, and incubated in the dark at 25℃ for 4 d to promote germination. When buds reached 1–2 cm in length, seedlings were transferred to pots containing the same nutrient soil formulation as used for Arabidopsis and maintained under the identical greenhouse environmental parameters described above.

RNA extraction and Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

To investigate tissue-specific expression patterns, total RNA was separately extracted from mature leaves, young leaves, primary roots, lateral roots, mature stems, young stems and flowers of tomato plants and reverse-transcribed into cDNA for subsequent qRT-PCR detection; for Cd stress treatments, whole-plant samples of wild-type (WT), SlJMJ12-OE and sljmj12 mutant Arabidopsis were collected under non-stress conditions and after 75 µM cadmium chloride (CdCl2) exposure for 0 h, 3 h and 6 h, while leaf tissues from WT, SlJMJ12-OE and sljmj12 tomato were harvested under non-stress and 6 h of 75 µM CdCl2 treatment. Total RNA from all above-mentioned samples was extracted using the Plant RNA Extraction Kit (FOREGENE, Chengdu, China) and reversely transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan), and the resultant cDNA was used as templates for qRT-PCR implemented with UltraSYBR Mixture (CWBIO, Taizhou, China). AtActin7 was applied as the internal reference gene for quantification of genes associated with metal transport and phytohormone metabolism in Arabidopsis samples, whereas SlActin11 served as the reference gene to quantify the expression of genes participating in antioxidant response, metal chelation, transport and uptake in tomato samples. Relative gene expression values were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, all primer sequences are provided in Table (Table S1-S3), and three independent biological replicates were arranged for every sample.

GUS transgenic plants stress

Tomato leaves were used as materials for genomic DNA extraction with the Plant DNA Isolation Kit (FOREGENE, Chengdu, China). Based on the 2000 bp promoter sequence upstream of the CDS region of the SlJMJ12 gene retrieved from the Phytozome database, the target fragment was cloned and ligated into the pCAMBIA3301 vector to generate the recombinant plasmid. The constructed plasmid was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and then transformed into Arabidopsis following the floral dip method [24]. Transgenic Arabidopsis plants were subjected to Cd stress at 75 µM for 0, 3, 6, and 12 h, followed by β-glucuronidase (GUS) staining. Briefly, the samples were incubated in GUS staining solution at 37℃ for 12–16 h in darkness [25]. Whole transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings were decolorized to remove endogenous plant pigments, which could cause background interference and obscure GUS staining signals. Stereomicroscope observation was required to precisely identify the tissue-localized distribution of GUS staining. After decolorization, the samples were observed under a microscope to characterize the spatial expression pattern of the SlJMJ12 promoter under cadmium stress.

Construction of SlJMJ12-pCAMBIA2300 recombinant expression vector

The full-length SlJMJ12 fragment amplified by PCR and plasmid pCAMBIA2300 were separately double-digested with corresponding restriction endonucleases in a 50 µL system (1.5 µL restriction enzymes, 5 µL 10×buffer, 20 µL template DNA, supplemented with ddH2O to 50 µL). After incubation at 37℃ for 4 h, digested products were separated via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified by gel extraction, and the recovered fragments were stored at -20 °C. The purified target insert and linearized vector were ligated using T4 DNA ligase in a 10 µL system containing 1 µL T4 ligase, 1 µL 10×ligation buffer, 5 µL insert fragment and 2 µL linear vector, with ddH2O to a final volume of 10 µL, followed by overnight ligation at 16 °C to construct the recombinant SlJMJ12-pCAMBIA2300 plasmid.

Construction of CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids for SlJMJ12 knockout

The two sgRNA target sites of SlJMJ12 were designed using the online tool CRISPR-P v2.0 (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/). To achieve effective gene knockout, target loci were selected within the CDS region close to the N-terminus or critical functional domains of the encoded protein. The sequences of the two targets were listed as follows: Target 1: ACCCAATTCCTGATGTCGGGTGG; Target 2: CATACTCGACGTAGATAGGGTGG. Subsequently, the CRISPR/Cas9 knockout vector of the SlJMJ12 gene was designed and constructed by Jiangsu Weimi Biotechnology Co (Changzhou, China).

Genetic transformation of Arabidopsis and tomato

The recombinant plasmid was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and then transformed into Arabidopsis following the floral dip method [24]. The Arabidopsis ortholog of tomato SlJMJ12 is AT4G20400.1, and its T-DNA insertion mutant was obtained from Fuzhou AraShare Technology Co (Fujian, China). Tomato cotyledons were transformed via A. tumefaciens-mediated infiltration [26].

Identification of positive transgenic seedlings in Arabidopsis and tomato

Putative transformants were verified via PCR and qRT-PCR, with primer sequences listed in the corresponding table (Table S4). Harvested T0 seeds of SlJMJ12-overexpressing Arabidopsis were screened on 1/2 MS medium containing 25 mg/L kanamycin and 150 mg/L timentin with WT as control. Viable seedlings were preliminarily selected as transformants. RNA isolated from T2 seedlings was reverse transcribed to cDNA, followed by target-specific PCR; lines with target amplicons were identified as overexpression lines (Fig. S1). Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissues of jmj12 mutant Arabidopsis, followed by PCR amplification using the three-primer screening method. Plants producing an amplicon with the jmj12-LP/jmj12-RP primer pair were identified as wild type. Homozygous mutant plants yielded a specific band using the jmj12-BP/jmj12-LP primer pair, whereas no PCR product was obtained from the jmj12-LP/jmj12-RP combination in homozygous mutants (Fig. S2).

qRT-PCR was conducted on cDNA reverse-transcribed from RNA of SlJMJ12-OE tomatoes, lines with high gene expression were preliminarily positive. Leaf genomic DNA was amplified by PCR with primers targeting the junction of 35 S promoter and SlJMJ12, and amplicons of correct size indicated positive plants (Fig. S3). Leaf DNA of SlJMJ12 CRISPR knockout tomatoes served as PCR template. Cas9-F/R vector primers were used for primary PCR screening, and lines with specific bands were preliminarily positive. Primers flanking 250 bp of each target site were synthesized for PCR of WT and mutant DNA. Purified PCR fragments were sequenced commercially, and lines with frameshift mutations were retained for further experiments (Fig. S4-5).

Transgenic Arabidopsis stress treatment

Wild-type (WT), SlJMJ12-overexpressing (OE) and mutant Arabidopsis seeds were surface-sterilized and vernalized before use. The gradient CdCl2 concentrations (10, 20, 30, 50 and 75 µM) used in this study were determined according to our accumulated laboratory data and published findings from Li et al. [21]. For root phenotype assay, sterilized seeds were placed on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 10, 20 or 30 µM CdCl2, and primary root length was measured after 7 days of vertical cultivation. For germination analysis, seeds were cultured on 1/2 MS medium with 50 or 75 µM CdCl2, and seed germination status was assessed at 7 days after sowing. For soil culture, all seeds were sown in nutrient soil and incubated at 22℃ under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. Four-week-old seedlings were selected for Cd treatment based on our long-term laboratory experience and previous studies by Dguimi and Alzahrani [27], as this developmental stage is widely applied for cadmium-related physiological analyses in Arabidopsis. Subsequently, the seedlings were subjected to 75 µM CdCl2 hydroponic treatment, followed by the measurement of POD, SOD, CAT, MDA and chlorophyll contents.

Transgenic tomato stress treatment

Seeds of wild-type (WT) tomato, T2 generation of SlJMJ12-overexpressing (OE) lines and homozygous T2 mutant lines were sown in the aforementioned nutrient soil and cultivated at 22 °C with a 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod. At the four-leaf-one-heart stage, seedlings were transferred into 75 µM CdCl₂ solution for hydroponic Cd stress treatment for 96 h according to previous laboratory experimental data. After treatment, root and leaf tissues were sampled instantly, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Cd concentration determination

After cadmium stress treatment, tomato seedlings were oven-dried to constant weight at 65℃. Approximately 1 g dry weight of each sample was delivered to Ainiwei Biotechnology Co (Heilongjiang, China) for Cd concentration quantification. Following sample digestion, cadmium content was determined via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Element identification was performed according to the characteristic mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of target element. The internal standard method was applied for quantitative analysis, where the ratio of mass spectral signal intensity between target analyte and internal standard was proportional to the element concentration.

Determination of physiological indexes

Relevant physiological indices were determined in transgenic Arabidopsis and tomato plants after stress treatment. Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were measured following the method described by Lichtenthaler [22]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction method [28], and peroxidase (POD) activity was determined via the guaiacol method [29]. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined based on the ammonium molybdate colorimetric method; the residual H₂O₂ reacts with ammonium molybdate to form a stable yellow complex, and absorbance was detected at 405 nm with a spectrophotometer [30], while ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was assayed using the ascorbate oxidation method [31]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method, with absorbance values measured at 450 nm, 532 nm, and 600 nm using a spectrophotometer [32].

Histochemical detection of reactive oxygen species

3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining was used to visualize hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation, following the method described by Rehman [33]. Nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) staining was employed to detect superoxide anion (O₂·⁻) accumulation, according to the protocol of Yang et al. [34]. O₂·⁻ content in plant leaves was determined using the Superoxide Anion Activity Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), with detailed procedures following the manufacturer’s instructions. H₂O₂ content in leaves was measured via the potassium iodide (KI) method [35]: briefly, 0.1 g of leaf tissue was homogenized in 1.5 mL of pre-cooled trichloroacetic acid (TCA), and centrifuged at 8500 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. Subsequently, 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 0.5 mL of 10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 1 mL of 1 M KI, followed by incubation at 28℃ for 1 h. The absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 390 nm.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay

The purified target fragments with restriction enzyme sites and empty vectors were double-digested with corresponding restriction endonucleases in 10×K Buffer at 37℃ for 12 h. After digestion, the insert fragments and linearized vectors were separately purified via agarose gel recovery. Ligation was conducted using Thermo Scientific T4 DNA Ligase following the manufacturer’s instructions. The full-length CDS of SlJMJ12 was inserted into pGBKT7 to generate the bait construct SlJMJ12-pGBKT7 (Fig. S6), while the full coding sequence of SlKLCR1-Like was cloned into pGADT7 to obtain the prey construct SlKLCR1-Like-pGADT7 (Fig. S7). Subsequent yeast cotransformation and spot culture assays were performed according to the method described by Zhang et al. [36]. Briefly, the bait and prey plasmids were co-transformed into yeast strain AH109 using a conventional yeast transformation kit (Coolaber, Beijing). Single yeast colonies were cultured in SD/-Trp liquid medium for activation. After centrifugation and supernatant removal, cell pellets were resuspended in 0.9% NaCl to an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.4. The suspension was serially diluted to 100, 10− 1, 10− 2 and 10− 3, and 2 µL of each dilution was spotted onto SD/-Trp/-Leu, SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade and SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade supplemented with X-α-gal solid plates, followed by incubation at 30℃ for 3–4 days.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed by IBM SPSS Statistics 20 (IBM China Company Ltd., Beijing, China), and all results are presented as the standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Gene expression levels were statistically evaluated by Student’s t-test. Other data were statistically evaluated via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for pairwise comparisons. Significant differences were defined at P < 0.05. All figures were generated with GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results

Expression analysis of SlJMJ12 and its contribution to Cd tolerance in Arabidopsis seedlings

qRT-PCR analysis showed that SlJMJ12 was expressed in leaves, roots, stems, and flowers, with the highest expression in young leaves, approximately 24-fold higher than in flowers (Fig. 1B). To examine its response to Cd stress, five-leaf-stage tomato plants were treated with Cd, and leaf samples were collected at 0, 3, 6, and 12 h. SlJMJ12 expression increased rapidly, peaking at 3 h at a level 1.56 times higher than that of the untreated control before gradually declining (Fig. 1C). GUS staining was performed on whole seedlings of transgenic Arabidopsis carrying the SlJMJ12 promoter, and the strongest staining signal was observed in leaves at 3 h under Cd stress (Fig. 1A), consistent with the qRT-PCR results. These results suggest that SlJMJ12 is transcriptionally responsive to Cd stress, implying its potential involvement in plant Cd responses.

To further evaluate this function, T2 generation seeds of two SlJMJ12 overexpression Arabidopsis lines (JMJ12-5 and JMJ12-10), a T-DNA insertion line (jmj12), and wild type (WT) seeds were germinated on 1/2 MS medium containing 0, 50, or 75 µM CdCl₂ for one week. The germination rate was calculated on each day. Under control conditions, no significant differences in germination rates were observed among the genotypes (Fig. 1D-E). At 50 µM CdCl₂, the SlJMJ12 overexpression lines reached maximum germination by day 2 (90 and 88%, respectively), whereas the WT reached 79% and the jmj12 mutant showed the lowest germination rate (62%). A similar pattern was observed at 75 µM CdCl₂, although overall germination was reduced. On day 2, germination rates were 83 and 87% for the overexpression lines, 70% for WT, and 50% for the loss-of-function mutant (Fig. 1F-G).

To characterize the role of SlJMJ12 during early seedling development under Cd stress, WT, overexpression, and loss-of-function Arabidopsis plants were grown in 1/2 MS medium supplemented with increasing concentrations of CdCl2. No significant phenotypic differences were detected at 0 µM. Under Cd stress, however, overexpressed lines exhibited significantly longer roots than WT, whereas the jmj12 mutant displayed markedly shorter roots (Fig. 1H-I). The degree of root growth inhibition intensified with increasing Cd concentrations, and fresh weight followed the same trend (Fig. 1J). These results collectively demonstrated that SlJMJ12 overexpression enhanced Cd tolerance during Arabidopsis germination and seedling growth.

Physiological responses of transgenic Arabidopsis to Cd stress

To evaluate oxidative stress levels, O₂·⁻ accumulation was assessed by NBT staining in WT, SlJMJ12 overexpression, and jmj12 mutant plants (Fig. 2A). Under non-stress conditions, all genotypes showed similarly light staining. Following Cd exposure, staining intensity increased in a concentration-dependent manner, with the jmj12 mutant exhibiting the darkest staining and the overexpression lines displaying the weakest signal.

F Chlorophyll content. Data in (B–F): means ± SEM from three independent biological replicates (n = 3). * and ** indicate significant differences at P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively, versus wild-type (WT)

Consistent with these observations, antioxidant enzyme activities showed clear genotype-dependent differences under Cd stress (Fig. 2B-F). SOD activity increased significantly in the overexpression lines and remained consistently higher than in WT plants, whereas the jmj12 mutant showed the lowest SOD activity. POD and CAT activities followed a similar pattern: POD activity in JMJ12-10 was 1.32-fold higher than in WT, and CAT activity in JMJ12-5 was 1.28-fold higher than in WT.

In contrast, MDA content—an indicator of lipid peroxidation and membrane damage—displayed the opposite trend. Under the highest Cd concentration, the jmj12 mutant accumulated 1.5-fold more MDA than WT, whereas the overexpression lines (OE-5 and OE-10) showed significantly lower levels (0.44- and 0.56-fold of WT, respectively), indicating reduced Cd-induced membrane damage (Fig. 2E).

Chlorophyll content also differed significantly among genotypes after Cd treatment. The OE-5 ine exhibited 1.46-fold higher chlorophyll content than WT, whereas WT maintained 1.58-fold higher levels compared with the jmj12 mutant (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that SlJMJ12 overexpression enhances antioxidant capacity, maintains chlorophyll content, and mitigates oxidative and membrane damage under cadmium stress.

SlJMJ12 regulates stress-related gene expression in Arabidopsis under Cd stress

To investigate whether SlJMJ12 enhances Cd tolerance through modulation of stress-responsive pathways, the expression of ABA (abscisic acid), EBR (24-epibrassinolide), and SL (strigolactone) -related genes (ABF2, ABI1, ABI5; BES1, BIN2, BAK1; D14, BRC1, CCD7) was examined, along with key metal transporter protein marker genes (ZIP1, ABCC1, NRAMP4). As shown in Fig. 3, hormone-related genes were consistently upregulated in the SlJMJ12 overexpression lines compared with WT, whereas the jmj12 mutant displayed the lowest transcript levels. The ABA- and SL-related genes exhibited a rapid induction peaking at 3 h following Cd exposure before declining, while the EBR-related gene expression increased more gradually, reaching maximum expression at 6 h (Fig. 3A-I).

Expression of metal transporter genes also differed significantly among genotypes: ZIP1 transcript levels were elevated in the overexpression lines and reduced in the jmj12 mutant. ZIP1 and NRAMP4 expression in the overexpression lines peaked at 3 h and declined by 6 h, yet remained higher than in WT plants. In contrast, ABCC1 gene expression reached its highest level at 6 h (Fig. 3).

These results suggest that SlJMJ12 enhances Cd tolerance by regulating the expression of hormone-related genes (SL, ABA, EBR) and metal transporters, thereby contributing to a coordinated transcriptional response under Cd stress.

Overexpression of SlJMJ12 enhances Cd tolerance in tomato

WT tomato plants, SlJMJ12-overexpressing T2 homozygous lines (OE13 and OE15), and jmj12 mutant T2 lines (jmj12-4 and jmj12-10) were subjected to Cd stress (Fig. 4). All seedlings displayed comparable phenotypes under control conditions. Under Cd exposure, growth was inhibited across genotypes (Fig. 4A). However, OE13 and OE15 exhibited greater plant height and root length than WT, whereas the mutants jmj12-4 and jmj12-10 showed the most severe growth suppression (Fig. 4B-C). Measurement of Cd accumulation revealed patterns consistent with the phenotypes: mutants accumulated the highest Cd levels, while overexpression lines accumulated substantially less Cd than WT (Fig. 4D). These findings indicate that SlJMJ12 overexpression enhances Cd tolerance in tomato.

Photosynthetic pigment levels, which reflect plant performance under stress, were also quantified. Under Cd stress, chlorophyll and carotenoid contents decreased in all genotypes relative to control conditions; the decline was significantly milder in overexpression lines, whereas mutants exhibited distinctly lower pigment levels than WT (Fig. 4). In particular, OE15 exhibited 1.18-fold higher carotenoid content, 1.26-fold higher chlorophyll a, and 1.20-fold higher chlorophyll b relative to WT, and substantially higher values than the jmj12-4 mutant (1.42-, 1.50-, and 1.34-fold, respectively) (Fig. 4E-H). These findings suggest that SlJMJ12 alleviates Cd-induced pigment degradation in tomato under cadmium stress, which may help preserve the structural basis for normal photosynthesis and thereby improve plant Cd tolerance.

SlJMJ12 enhances antioxidant enzyme activity in tomato

SOD, CAT, POD, and APX onstitute key components of the plant antioxidant defense system, and higher activities of these enzymes are closely associated with improved tolerance to Cd stress. As shown in Fig. 5, antioxidant enzyme activities did not differ significantly among genotypes under non-stress conditions. However, following Cd exposure, OE13 and OE15 exhibited markedly higher SOD, CAT, POD, and APX activities than WT plants, whereas the jmj12-4 and jmj12-10 mutants showed significantly reduced activities (Fig. 5A-D). For example, in the OE15 line, SOD activity was 1.40-fold higher than WT and 2.28-fold higher than the jmj12-10 knockout. Similarly, POD, CAT, and APX activities in OE15 increased by 1.55- and 2.12-fold, 1.19- and 1.55-fold, and 1.25- and 1.49-fold relative to WT and jmj12-10, respectively. These findings suggest that SlJMJ12 overexpression may strengthen the antioxidant enzyme system under Cd stress.

To further evaluate oxidative damage, NBT and DAB staining were used to assess O₂·⁻ and H₂O₂ accumulation. As shown in Fig. 5, no obvious differences were observed among genotypes under control conditions. Following Cd treatment, however, mutant leaves displayed the darkest staining, indicative of increased ROS accumulation, whereas OE plants showed the lightest staining (Fig. 5E). Quantification confirmed that both H2O2 and O₂·⁻ levels increased after Cd exposure, with mutants accumulating significantly more hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anion than WT and OE lines; OE plants had the lowest levels (Fig. 5F-G). Consistently, MDA content was highest in mutants, approximately twice that of WT and OE plants (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results suggest that SlJMJ12 overexpression alleviates Cd-triggered oxidative damage, which may contribute to enhanced cadmium tolerance in tomato, possibly via modulation of antioxidant capacity.

SlJMJ12 modulates the expression of stress-responsive genes in tomato under Cd stress

To gain preliminary insight into the molecular mechanisms through which SlJMJ12 contributes to Cd tolerance, referring to previous research by Li and Kanwal [21, 37], we selected a series of marker genes for qRT‑PCR detection, including antioxidant enzyme‑related marker genes (SlSOD, SlPOD, SlCAT1, SlAPX1, and SlGST), chelation‑process‑related genes (SlGSH1 and SlGSH2), and metal uptake and transporter protein‑related marker genes (SlZIP, SlNRAMP3, SlIRT1, SlABCC2, SlABCC3). All these genes were quantified via qRT‑PCR in WT, OE, and mutant tomato lines. The results revealed that SlJMJ12 overexpression led to the downregulation of SlGST, SlZIP, and SlIRT1, whereas the remaining stress‑related genes were upregulated relative to WT plants (Fig. 6). These expression patterns suggest that SlJMJ12 enhances tomato tolerance to Cd stress by modulating a regulatory gene network involved in antioxidation, metal chelation, and metal transport.

However, only the positive control grew on SD/-Trp-His medium, indicating that SlJMJ12 lacks self-activation activity and was suitable for subsequent yeast library screening.

Discussion

Tomato is one of the most important economic crops worldwide and serves as a key model species for studies on genetics, fruit development, and stress tolerance [38]. Understanding cadmium stress-responsive genes in tomato has significant implications for breeding low-Cd-accumulating cultivars. The JmjC structural domain-containing JMJ genes play essential roles in plant growth and development through epigenetic regulation, including histone modification, DNA methylation, and transcriptional control [39]. To date, most studies on JMJs have focused on Arabidopsis, in which 21 JmjC-domain proteins have been identified [40]. These include JMJ27, which regulates pathogen defense and flowering time [41]; JMJ15, which participates in salt stress responses [17]; and JMJ17, which modulates dehydration responses [42]. In tomato, 20 proteins belonging to the JMJ family were identified [43], with research primarily emphasizing their roles in fruit ripening. For example, overexpression of SlJMJ3 accelerates fruit ripening [44], while SlJMJ7 acts as a key negative regulator of this process [45]. In contrast, the involvement of JMJ genes in tomato responses to abiotic stresses is not well understood. In this study, the role of SlJMJ12 was investigated, identifying its importance in mitigating Cd stress in plants.

Gene expression patterns provide critical clues to functional specialization. qPCR analysis revealed that SlJMJ12 is expressed in mature leaves, young leaves, primary roots, lateral roots, mature stems, young stems, and flowers, with the highest transcript levels occurring in young leaves, followed by mature leaves, indicating tissue-specific expression (Fig. 1B) -a tissue-specific pattern that aligns with the role of young leaves as both photosynthetic hubs and Cd-sensitive sites. Examination of SlJMJ12 expression in tomato leaves under Cd stress showed an initial increase followed by a decline (Fig. 1C), and GUS staining in Arabidopsis yielded consistent results, with peak staining intensity detected at 3 h (Fig. 1A). This temporal pattern coincides with the early phase of Cd stress response, during which plants perceive Cd signals and initiate primary defense cascades, strongly suggesting that SlJMJ12 acts as an early responder to Cd exposure [46]. Collectively, the tissue- and stage-specific expression of SlJMJ12 provided the initial evidence for its involvement in Cd stress regulation.

Cadmium toxicity severely impacts plant growth, damages root systems, and causes leaf chlorosis [5]. Therefore, growth indices provide insight into plant Cd tolerance. Our phenotypic data from SlJMJ12-transgenic Arabidopsis and tomato demonstrated that heterologous overexpression of SlJMJ12 rescued Cd-inhibited seed germination, root elongation and biomass accumulation, while loss-of-function mutants were hypersensitive to Cd stress (Figs. 1D-J and 4A-C and A-C). Combined with elemental quantification showing less Cd enrichment in overexpression tomato lines (Fig. 4D), we speculate that SlJMJ12-mediated alleviation of Cd phytotoxicity is partially attributed to restricted cadmium accumulation in plant tissues. This observation is consistent with previous studies, in which reduced cadmium accumulation effectively alleviates Cd-induced growth retardation in plants [37].

Photosynthesis is highly sensitive to heavy metal stress, and Cd disrupts photosystem components, impairing photosynthetic efficiency [47]. The photosynthetic pigments were examined in transgenic Arabidopsis and tomato subjected to Cd stress (Figs. 2F and 4F-H). Chlorophyll content in SlJMJ12-overexpressing plants was significantly higher than in WT, whereas mutants exhibited significantly reduced chlorophyll levels. This may result from the displacement of magnesium ions in chlorophyll or interference with proteins of the chloroplast electron transport chain due to Cd exposure [48]. Carotenoid content in tomato followed a similar pattern, with SlJMJ12-overexpressing lines showing higher carotenoid levels under Cd stress, and mutants displaying lower levels relative to WT (Fig. 4E). This protective effect is likely a direct consequence of reduced Cd accumulation. Cd competitively displaces Mg²⁺ in chlorophyll [49] and impairs chloroplast electron transport [50], so limiting Cd accumulation preserves photosynthetic apparatus integrity. As reported previously, carotenoids participate in photosynthetic light harvesting and meanwhile alleviate photooxidative injury by eliminating excess reactive oxygen species [51]. These results imply that SlJMJ12 may contribute to improved photoprotection and enhanced oxidative stress tolerance in tomato.

Changes in physiological indices were further examined in transgenic Arabidopsis and tomato. Cd exposure triggers excessive accumulation of ROS, including O₂·⁻ and H2O2 [52], which can be detected using NBT and DAB staining [53]. Staining intensity (Figs. 2A and 5E) and quantitative measurements (Fig. 5F-G) indicated that SlJMJ12 overexpression significantly reduced ROS accumulation in both species under Cd stress. Cd also induces lipid peroxidation, leading to elevated MDA levels. Consistently, SlJMJ12-overexpressing Arabidopsis and tomato displayed the lowest MDA content (Figs. 2E and 5H), indicating reduced membrane oxidative damage [54, 55]. Antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, CAT, and APX play key roles in ROS scavenging [56]. Under Cd stress, the activities of these enzymes were significantly higher in overexpression lines and substantially reduced in mutants (Figs. 2B-D and 5A-D). These observations suggest that SlJMJ12 may be associated with ROS homeostasis, and activated antioxidant enzymes possibly contribute to SlJMJ12-improved Cd tolerance.

Transcriptomic expression analysis was performed to explore the potential association between SlJMJ12 and Cd stress response. In Arabidopsis, overexpression of SlJMJ12 significantly altered the expression of ABA (ABF2, ABI1, ABI5)-, EBR (BES1, BIN2, BAK1)-, SL (D14, BRC1, CCD7)-related genes, as well as metal transporter genes (ZIP1, ABCC1, NRAMP4). ZIP, NRAMP and ABCC belong to common metal transporter families with no exclusive Cd transport specificity. Existing literature confirms these proteins primarily transport essential metals including Zn, Fe, Mn and Cu and incidentally mediate Cd uptake, translocation and intracellular detoxification under Cd stress [57]. Specifically, ABA signaling positively regulates plant antioxidant defense and metal detoxification ability by activating SOD, CAT and APX antioxidant systems to scavenge excess Cd-triggered reactive oxygen species (ROS) in tomato and multiple crops [58], while EBR enhances plant stress resistance by alleviating heavy metal-induced photosystem damage and lipid peroxidation via reinforcing non-enzymatic antioxidant pools and glyoxalase metabolism [59]. As for strigolactone, exogenous SL application improves Cd tolerance by restraining root Cd uptake and upregulating a series of antioxidant coding genes to relieve oxidative injury triggered by excess cadmium [60]. Consistently higher transcript abundances of these hormone-related genes were detected in SlJMJ12-OE lines compared with wild type and mutant (Fig. 3). In transgenic tomato, SlJMJ12 overexpression downregulated SlGST, SlZIP, and SlIRT1, whereas it upregulated antioxidant genes (SlSOD, SlPOD, SlCAT1, SlAPX1), chelation-related genes (SlGSH1, SlGSH2), and metal transporter genes (SlNRAMP3, SlABCC2, SlABCC3) (Fig. 6). These results suggest that SlJMJ12 coordinates hormone signaling, antioxidant defense and metal homeostasis pathways at the transcriptional level to modulate Cd tolerance. This multifaceted regulatory characteristic conforms to the pleiotropic property of epigenetic regulators [61]. Specifically, altered transcription of SlZIP/SlIRT1 (major Cd uptake transporters) and SlNRAMP3/SlABCCs (key mediators of Cd vacuolar sequestration) in SlJMJ12-overexpressing plants may be one of the important reasons for reduced Cd accumulation.

Using Y2H assays preliminarily screened SlKLCR1-Like as a potential interacting candidate of SlJMJ12 (Fig. 7A-B). Expression analysis of SlKLCR1-Like across tomato tissues revealed the highest expression in young leaves. Under normal conditions, SlKLCR1-Like expression was relatively low. However, Cd stress markedly upregulated its expression in both roots and leaves (Fig. 7C-E). Collectively, these transcriptional data imply that SlKLCR1-Like is likely involved in plant response to Cd stress at the transcriptional level, and the tentative interaction detected via Y2H provides a preliminary clue for exploring the potential link between SlJMJ12 and SlKLCR1-Like in the SlJMJ12-associated Cd-responsive regulatory network.

In summary, we put forward a regulatory model explaining how SlJMJ12 mediates tomato Cd stress tolerance (Fig. 8). Cd exposure triggers the upregulated transcription of SlJMJ12, which initiates multiple protective cascades: this gene restricts Cd deposition by regulating metal transporters, preserves photosynthetic structural integrity, and improves ROS elimination via activating antioxidant enzymes. These protective processes rely on transcriptional modulation of stress-related genes and phytohormone signaling. Moreover, in vitro biochemical assays suggest a physical interaction between SlJMJ12 and Cd-responsive SlKLCR1-Like protein, raising the possibility that they may form a synergistic regulatory module contributing to Cd resistance.

A mechanistic diagram of the function of SlJMJ12 in tomatoes, illustrating its role in responding to cadmium stress. Overexpression of SlJMJ12 enhances photosynthesis in tomatoes and improves their tolerance to cadmium stress by increasing their ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS). SlJMJ12 also increases the expression levels of antioxidant enzyme-encoding genes (SlAPX1, SlCAT1, SlSOD, and SlPOD) and chelation-related genes (SlGSH1 and SlGSH2). Additionally, SlJMJ12 gene overexpression alters the expression levels of ion absorption and transport-related genes (SlZIP, SlNRAMP3, SlIRT1, SlABCC2, and SlABCC3), thereby reducing cadmium accumulation in tomato plants. Nonetheless, this study has evident shortcomings. We lack direct in vivo proof for the epigenetic enzymatic activity of SlJMJ12, including its specific histone modification targets; the physiological interaction between SlJMJ12 and SlKLCR1-Like also remains unconfirmed. Future work will clarify the function of SlKLCR1-Like in Cd tolerance and screen downstream histone targets of SlJMJ12. Relevant research will elaborate epigenetic mechanisms underlying tomato Cd adaptation and facilitate molecular breeding of low-Cd, Cd-resistant tomato materials.

Conclusions

This study identified a histone demethylase gene, SlJMJ12, that is highly expressed in tomato leaves. Its expression was induced by Cd. To explore its role in Cd stress responses, SlJMJ12-overexpression lines and knockout mutants of both Arabidopsis and tomato were generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Overexpression of SlJMJ12 reduced Cd accumulation in plants, alleviated Cd-induced damage to the photosynthetic system, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity and ROS scavenging capacity, lowered MDA levels, and activated stress-responsive genes. In contrast, SlJMJ12 knockout mutants showed the opposite effects, exhibiting reduced tolerance to Cd. SlJMJ12 also interacts with SlKLCR1-Like, whose expression is upregulated in tomato roots and leaves under Cd stress. These findings suggest that SlJMJ12 may regulate SlKLCR1-Like to mediate plant responses to abiotic stress. Collectively, SlJMJ12 confers Cd tolerance via integrating reduced Cd accumulation, photosynthetic protection, and antioxidant defense, filling gaps in understanding tomato JMJ genes’ roles in abiotic stress and providing a target for breeding low-Cd-accumulating, Cd-tolerant tomatoes to support phytoremediation and sustainable agriculture.
