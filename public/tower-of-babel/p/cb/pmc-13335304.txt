Integrative multi-omics analysis uncovers the regulatory network of wheat grain development during grain filling
Authors: Xuqin Wang, Chen Chen, Yuanyuan Zuo, Guofei Jiang, Hanxue Li, Yuming Xie, Jianlong Zhou, Huanyao Xia, Chuanli Zhang, Peng Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWheat grain development is the core biological process that determines the formation of grain yield and quality. However, as a heterohexaploid crop, wheat has not been systematically analyzed for its molecular regulatory network governing grain development.MethodsIn this study, the wheat cultivar 'Dianmai 24' was used as the experimental material to identify the phenotypic characteristics of grains at three key grain filling stages, namely 7 days (d), 14 d, and 21 d after anthesis, and to integrate transcriptomic, non-targeted metabolomic, and proteomic analyses.ResultsPhenotypic analysis showed that there were significant differences in grain width and biomass among the three stages, which exhibited the typical growth characteristics during the grain filling period. A total of 2665 metabolites were identified in the metabolomic analysis, among which flavonoids, amino acids and their derivatives, and lipid metabolites were the main differential metabolic components across different developmental stages. Transcriptomic analysis yielded a total of 85.13 Gb of clean data. A total of 10,445, 16,306, and 8,437 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened out in the three comparison groups, which were significantly enriched in metabolic pathways, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, starch and sucrose metabolism, and carbon metabolism. A total of 17,729 proteins were identified by proteomic analysis, among which α-amylase/trypsin inhibitor CM3, β-amylase, and globulin-3A accumulated continuously across the three stages, and these proteins may play a core role in the process of grain development.ConclusionsThrough multi-omics analysis, the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway was identified as the core regulatory pathway, and 15 key genes, 17 core proteins, and 11 characteristic metabolites were screened to participate in the molecular regulatory network of grain filling. This study systematically analyzed the molecular regulatory mechanism of the wheat grain filling stage and provided important candidate genes and metabolic targets for the breeding of high-yield and high-quality wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09073-w.Keywords: Grain development, Grain filling stage, Multiomics, Regulatory network, Wheat

Introduction

As one of the three major food crops in the world, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a core source of human staple food. It can provide essential starch and proteins, and at the same time, due to its unique gluten properties, it has a wide adaptability to flour processing, thus being widely cultivated worldwide [1]. The yield and quality of wheat grains are largely determined by physiological events during the growth and development process. Therefore, in-depth analysis of the biological processes of grain development and their regulatory mechanisms is of great significance for improving wheat yield and quality traits [2]. Wheat grain development is a complex process. The wheat grain is composed of the embryo, starchy endosperm, aleurone layer, and pericarp, and its development can be divided into the cellular, effective filling, and mature stages [3]. At the early developmental stage, cells undergo division, expansion, and differentiation to form the unique structure of the endosperm. Approximately 80% of the final grain size is determined by the number of endosperm cells involved in cell division and proliferation during this stage [3, 4]. After the basic structure of the grain is formed, it enters the filling stage, during which storage compounds such as starch and proteins begin to synthesize and accumulate in large quantities [5, 6], accompanied by significant changes in physiological processes, genetic characteristics, and regulatory networks [7–9]. Therefore, the key to understanding grain development lies in analyzing the molecular regulatory mechanism during the grain filling stage. In recent years, the development of molecular technology tools such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics has provided a powerful means for characterizing the genes, proteins, and metabolites involved in wheat grain development [10–12]. However, as a heterohexaploid species, wheat has a large and complex genome, consisting of three subgenomes (A, B, and D). Compared with diploid cereals such as rice and maize, the development of genomic resources and genetic analysis of traits in wheat are relatively lagging [13, 14]. Existing studies have mostly focused on a single omics level, such as using transcriptomics to analyze the gene expression characteristics during the early grain development and grain filling stages [15–17], or using proteomics and metabolomics to analyze specific grain components and responses to abiotic stress [18–22]. In the study of wheat grain development, existing studies have used transcriptomic technology to analyze the gene expression characteristics during wheat grain development [23–25]. RNA-seq was employed to identify the candidate genes responsible for starchy endosperm cell wall development in the developing starchy endosperm [26]. Transcriptomic analysis identified the differentially expressed genes and pathways that may affect wheat pericarp cell size and grain weight [27]. By performing RNA-seq on 35 wheat genotypes at 14 and 30 DAA, Rangan et al. uncovered critical differentially expressed transcripts participating in various metabolic pathways throughout wheat grain filling [28]. Compared with model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana and rice, wheat has a larger genome and a more complex proteome [29]. However, single-omics analysis is difficult to fully reveal the complex regulatory network among genes, proteins, and metabolites, making it urgent to systematically integrate multi-omics data to analyze the molecular mechanism of wheat grain development. Currently, most available studies focus merely on single-omics analysis of wheat grain development, while systematic dynamic multi-omics investigation across continuous grain-filling stages remains insufficient. Moreover, the synergistic regulatory relationships among genes, proteins and metabolites underlying grain development are still poorly characterized. To fill this research gap, this study selected the wheat cultivar Dianmai 24 as experimental material and chose 7, 14, and 21 days after anthesis (DAA) as three representative time points. These three stages precisely cover the initial grain-filling stage, vigorous rapid-filling stage and late grain-filling maturation stage, which can fully capture the dynamic physiological and molecular changes throughout the whole grain-filling process and avoid the limitation of single time-point sampling in previous studies.Considering the inherent defects of single-omics analysis, we combined phenotypic evaluation with transcriptomics, proteomics and non-targeted metabolomics to systematically characterize molecular dynamics during grain development. The aims of this study were to identify core regulatory pathways, screen key candidate genes, proteins and metabolites, and provide a theoretical basis and molecular targets for elucidating grain development mechanisms and supporting high-yield and high-quality wheat breeding.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The wheat Breeding varieties Dianmai 24, which was bred by Yunnan Agricultural University by crossing Shumai 580 with Xiangmai 46, was used as the experimental material in this study. It was planted in October 2024 at the Modern Education and Scientific Research Base of Yunnan Agricultural University, Xundian County, Kunming, Yunnan Province, China (102°41′ E, 25°20′ N). The experimental field has moderate soil fertility and sound drainage and irrigation conditions. All wheat plants were cultivated under conventional field management practices. Specifically, compound fertilizer (N:P₂O₅:K₂O = 15:15:15) was applied at 750 kg/hm2 as basal fertilizer before sowing, and urea was top-dressed at 150–225 kg/hm2 during the jointing stage. During the entire growth period, unified water regulation, manual weeding, and eco-friendly pest and disease management were implemented to maintain consistent field cultivation conditions. At the mid-anthesis stage, uniform spikes were labelled for subsequent sampling. On days 7, 14, and 21 after anthesis (DAA), healthy wheat plants with consistent growth and free from pests and diseases were selected. The six central spikelets of each labelled spike were collected as experimental samples. Sampled grains were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, fully mixed, and preserved in an ultra-low temperature freezer at − 80 °C. Transcriptomic, metabolomic and proteomic analyses were conducted by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; https://www.metware.cn). Three developmental stages were coded as A1 (7 days after anthesis, DAA), A2 (14 DAA), and A3 (21 DAA), respectively.

Determination of morphological indexes

Samples were collected at 7, 14, and 21 days after flowering (DAA) during the grain-filling stage of wheat. Grain length (L), width (W) and thickness (T) were measured using a digital caliper (precision 0.01 mm, model CD-6CSX, Mitutoyo Corp., Kawasaki, Japan). Grain biomass (fresh weight and dry weight) was determined using an electronic analytical balance. Three biological replicates were set for each sample, with 10 grains selected per replicate.To eliminate the influence of position on grain development, only the spikelets in the middle of the main stem spike (3–5 spikelets per spike) were chosen as experimental materials, and the average value was used as the final phenotypic data for a single grain.

Metabolomic analysis

Biological samples were freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze dryer (Scientz-100F) and fully ground into fine powder with a tissue grinder (MM 400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. Briefly, 30 mg of sample powder was accurately weighed using an electronic balance (MS105D). A total of 1500 μL of pre‑cooled 70% aqueous methanol extraction solution containing internal standard (− 20 °C) was added to each sample, with a fixed extraction ratio of 1500 μL extractant per 30 mg sample. For samples with a weight less than 30 mg, the extractant volume was adjusted proportionally. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s every 30 min, repeated six times in total. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and transferred to injection vials for subsequent UPLC‑MS/MS detection.Both known and unknown metabolites were identified and quantified via ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC‑MS/MS). UPLC separation was performed on a Shimadzu UFLC CBM30A system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an Agilent SB‑C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid (Phase A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (Phase B). The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 5% Phase B at 0.00 min; a linear increase to 95% Phase B within 9.00 min and maintained for 1.00 min; decreased to 5% Phase B from 10.00 to 11.10 min, followed by column equilibration at 5% Phase B for 14.00 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 4 μL. Mass spectrometry data were acquired under positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. The spray voltages were set at 5500 V (positive) and 4500 V (negative). The ion source gas I, ion source gas II and curtain gas were set to 50 psi, 60 psi and 25 psi, respectively. Fragment collision energy was set to high, and multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) ion pairs were monitored separately according to metabolite elution characteristics.

Metabolite quantification was conducted using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Waltham, MA, USA) with MRM mode. Metabolite qualitative annotation was based on in‑house databases of MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; www.metware.cn) and public metabolic databases. Redundant signals, including isotopic peaks, ion adducts (K⁺, Na⁺, NH₄⁺) and repeated fragment ions, were manually removed. All mass spectral raw data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software, including baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction and peak alignment, to finally obtain a standardized data matrix of retention time, m/z value and peak intensity.

Multivariate statistical analysis was performed for metabolomic data. Principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out via the prcomp function in R with scale = TRUE. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‑DA) was implemented using the MetaboAnalystR package.During the analysis, representative components were extracted from the independent variable matrix X and the dependent variable matrix Y, and the correlations among these components were calculated. The algorithm integrated orthogonal signal correction (OSC) with the PLS-DA approach, which decomposes the variation contained in matrix X into two parts: one correlated with matrix Y and one uncorrelated with Y. Through removing irrelevant variation, efficient screening of differential variables was achieved. Prior to model establishment, raw data were first subjected to Log₂ transformation followed by centering processing. Here, matrix X represents the quantitative information matrix of samples, and matrix Y represents the grouping information matrix of samples.A heatmap was plotted using the ComplexHeatmap package in R software, and hierarchical cluster analysis was employed to analyze the accumulation patterns of metabolites among different samples. The following parameters were selected to identify differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) for subsequent analysis: variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or fold change ≤ 0.5, and P-value < 0.05 were used as the screening criteria. Metabolic pathways of the differentially accumulated metabolites were constructed based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Dianmai 24 seeds using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method combined with PBiozol reagent, and the extracted RNA was dissolved in diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. Subsequently, a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was employed for accurate RNA quantification, and the RNA quality number (RQN) was determined using a Qsep400 high-throughput biological fragment analyzer (BiOptic, New Taipei City, Taiwan, China) to ensure that the sample quality met the criteria for library construction. Subsequently, cDNA library construction was performed. Based on the structural characteristic that most eukaryotic mRNAs possess a poly(A) tail, mRNA with poly(A) tails was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The purified mRNA was fragmented into small segments using fragmentation buffer at an appropriate temperature. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by the synthesis of second-strand cDNA, which was accompanied by terminal repair and dA-tailing. After adapter ligation, the products were purified using DNA magnetic beads, and fragment size selection was performed to obtain a library with insert fragments of 250–350 bp. The ligation products were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and the amplified products were purified again using DNA magnetic beads. Finally, the purified products were dissolved in nuclease-free water. Following library construction, the library concentration was detected using a Qubit fluorometer, and the fragment size was determined using a Qsep400 high-throughput biological fragment analyzer (BiOptic, New Taipei City, Taiwan, China). After the library passed quality control, different libraries were pooled according to their effective concentrations and the target offline data volume, followed by Illumina sequencing, which generated 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were filtered using Fastp software, mainly to remove adapter-containing reads. Specifically, paired-end reads were discarded if the N content in either read exceeded 10% of the total number of bases in that read; paired-end reads were also discarded if the number of low-quality bases (Q ≤ 20) in either read exceeded 50% of the total number of bases in that read. All subsequent analyses were based on the obtained clean reads. The wheat reference genome and its corresponding annotation files were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. An index of the reference genome was constructed using HISAT2 software, and the clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome using the constructed index with default parameters unless otherwise specified. StringTie software was used for novel gene prediction. Specifically, StringTie employed a network flow algorithm and optional de novo assembly to splice transcripts. FeatureCounts software was used to count gene alignments, and the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) value of each gene was then calculated based on gene length. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the DESeq2 package in R software, and the P-value was adjusted using the Benjamini & Hochberg method [30]. The adjusted P-value and |log₂(fold change, FC)| were used as the thresholds to define significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes with |log₂FC|≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered as significant DEGs.The false discovery rate (FDR) threshold for identifying DEGs was set at < 0.05. For enrichment analysis of DEGs, the goseq package in R software [31] was used to perform enrichment analysis based on the hypergeometric test. For Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis, the hypergeometric distribution test was conducted with pathways as the unit; for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, the analysis was performed based on GO terms. Moreover, multiple testing correction with FDR was conducted to optimize the enrichment results of GO and KEGG analyses. The raw RNA sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1417462.

Proteome analysis

Samples were taken out from the −80 °C refrigerator and approximately 100 mg of tissue was ground into fine powder under liquid nitrogen. An appropriate amount of the ground powder was transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the immediate addition of SDT lysis buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and 2 mM EDTA). The mixture was thoroughly vortexed, boiled at 95 °C for 15 min, and then subjected to ultrasonic lysis on ice for 10 min. The supernatant protein solution was collected by centrifugation. Four volumes of cold acetone were added to the protein solution, and the mixture was precipitated overnight at −20 °C. After precipitation, centrifugation was performed at 4 °C to retain the precipitate, which was then washed three times with cold acetone. The washed precipitate was dissolved in 8 M urea, and the total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. Based on the measured protein concentration, 100 μg of the protein solution was taken, and its volume was adjusted to 200 μL with 8 M urea. The protein solution was then reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT) at a final concentration of 5 mM at 37 °C for 45 min, followed by alkylation with iodoacetamide in a dark room at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 800 μL of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution and 2 μL of trypsin were added to the mixture, which was then digested overnight at 37 °C. The pH of the digested peptides was adjusted to 2–3 using 20% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), followed by desalting with C18 resin (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Finally, the peptide concentration was determined using the Pierce™ Quantitative Peptide Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).The peptide samples were separated using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in 100% acetonitrile. Sample injection was performed using the trap-elute two-column method. A PepMap Neo trap column (300 μm × 5 mm, 5 μm) was used as the trap column, and an Easy-Spray™ PepMap™ Neo UHPLC column (150 μm × 15 cm, 2 μm) was employed as the analytical column. The column temperature was maintained at 55 °C, with 200 ng peptide per injection, a flow rate of 2.5 μL/min, an effective gradient of 22 min, and a total run time of 24 min. For data-independent acquisition (DIA) analysis, chromatographic separation was performed using a Vanquish Neo system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The samples separated by nanoflow high-performance liquid chromatography (nanoHPLC) were subjected to DIA mass spectrometry analysis using an Orbitrap Astral high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The detection mode was set to positive ion mode. The precursor ion scanning range was 380–980 m/z, with a primary mass resolution of 240,000 at 200 m/z, a normalized AGC target of 500% (corresponding to 5 × 10⁶ ions), and a maximum ion injection time of 5 ms. For MS2 analysis, the DIA data acquisition mode was adopted with 299 variable-width scanning windows covering 380–980 m/z, a higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) collision energy of 25%, a normalized AGC target of 500% (corresponding to 5 × 10⁶ ions), and a maximum ion injection time of 3 ms. The raw MS data were analyzed using DIA-NN software (version 1.8.1) with a database-free approach. The match between runs (MBR) option was used to generate a spectral library from the DIA data, which was then employed for reanalysis of the data. At both the protein and precursor ion levels, the false discovery rate (FDR) of the search results was adjusted to < 1%, and the remaining qualified results were used for subsequent quantitative analysis. In this study, three biological replicates were set for each group of samples, and significantly differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined based on false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.01, fold change (FC) > 1.5, FC < 0.6667, and P-value < 0.05. The identified proteins and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were subjected to functional annotation and enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation, euKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG) functional classification, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, protein domain analysis, subcellular localization, and signal peptide prediction. The STRING database (http://string-db.org/) was used for protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis. Protein sequences were imported into Cytoscape software [32] (version 3.9.1) for PPI network visualization and editing. R software (version 4.3.0) was used to draw the corresponding network diagrams.

RT-qPCR

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed to verify the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing results of Dianmai 24, The ATP-dependent 26S proteasome regulatory subunit was used as the internal reference gene. Gene primers were designed using Primer 5 (Table S1). Core genes and transcriptome-derived differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. The system used was 20 µ L, the reagent was perfect start TM SYBR qPCR supermax (transgen biotechnology, Beijing, China). The amplification instrument was an ABI Prism 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method [33].

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 was used for data organization. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on all experimental data via SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Duncan’s new multiple range test was applied for statistical analysis of phenotypic data [34], and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and analysis

Morphological analysis of Dianmai 24 at different growth periods

According to the phenotypic map of Dianmai 24 at three different stages (Fig. 1A), the morphological development of grains showed significant differences. With respect to grain length and thickness (Fig. 1B, D), there was no significant difference in the length and width of grains among the three different periods. In terms of grain width (Fig. 1C), the grain width at 21 days after anthesis (DAA) was significantly higher than that at 14 DAA and 7 DAA. Regarding grain biomass (Fig. 1E, F), both the dry weight and fresh weight of Dianmai 24 grains at 21 DAA were significantly higher than those at 14 DAA and 7 DAA, with the lowest biomass observed at 7 DAA and the highest at 21 DAA. With the progression of grain filling, the thousand-grain weight increased gradually and peaked at 21 days after anthesis. The grain filling rate reached its lowest level at 7–14 DAA, showing a dynamic trend of initial rise, subsequent decline, and final increase throughout the filling period.In conclusion, the grain size and biomass of Dianmai 24 varied among different developmental stages, which provides a basic phenotypic foundation for the subsequent analysis of the regulatory mechanism underlying grain development in Dianmai 24.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of metabolites of Dianmai 24 at different developmental stages

Non-targeted metabolomics technology based on UPLC-MS/MS was used to detect metabolites in nine samples of Dianmai 24 collected at 7, 14, and 21 days after flowering. Metabolite quantification was performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Fig S1A,B). This method achieved accurate quantification and excellent repeatability. The total ion chromatograms (TICs) of different quality control (QC) samples were highly overlapping, with consistent retention times and peak intensities, demonstrating the reliability of the dataset (Fig. S1C, D). In addition, principal component analysis (Fig. 2A), sample correlation analysis (Fig. 2B), and hierarchical clustering heatmap (Fig. 2C) collectively demonstrated good biological repeatability within groups and significant differences in related metabolites between groups. In this study, qualitative and quantitative metabolite analyses were performed on nine samples of Dianmai 24 at three different post-anthesis stages.A total of 2665 metabolites were identified and classified into 13 categories (Fig. 2D), including 376 flavonoids, 360 amino acids and derivatives, 355 unclassified metabolites, 322 lipids, 308 terpenes, 291 alkaloids, 278 phenolic acids, 119 lignans and coumarins, 114 organic acids, 103 nucleotides and derivatives, 15 steroids, 18 quinones, and 6 tannins (Table S2). This study mainly analyzed three sample groups, A2 vs A1, A3 vs A1 and A3 vs A2, and detected and quantified 2656, 2654 and 2606 metabolites respectively.

Analysis of differential metabolites in Dianmai 24 at different developmental stages

Principal component analysis (PCA) could preliminarily verify the differences in wheat varieties at different periods (Fig. S2A–C). The results showed clear separation between groups, significant differences among groups, and good biological repeatability of samples within each group. Validation analysis of the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model for the three comparison groups (Fig. S2D–F) revealed that both R2Y and Q2 values were greater than 0.9 (p < 0.05), indicating that the established model was stable and reliable, and thus suitable for this study. The variable importance in projection (VIP) values derived from the OPLS-DA model could preliminarily screen out metabolites with differences between different groups. Meanwhile, metabolites with a p-value < 0.5, or a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 in univariate analysis were combined to further screen out differential metabolites among the three different periods. The cluster heatmap of metabolites across the three different periods (Fig. 3A–C) showed significant differences in metabolites among the three groups. For the three pairwise comparison groups (A2 vs A1, A3 vs A1, and A3 vs A2), there were 846 (622 upregulated, 224 downregulated), 1321 (1083 upregulated, 238 downregulated), and 820 (694 upregulated, 126 downregulated) significantly differential metabolites, respectively (Table S3). Meanwhile, amino acids and their derivatives, lipids, nucleotides and their derivatives, phenolic acids, organic acids, and flavonoids are closely associated with wheat grain development during the grain filling stage (Table 1). The VIP values of differential metabolites (Fig. S2G–I) and the bar charts of differential metabolites (Fig. 3D–F) indicated that the differential metabolites with significant differences among the three groups were flavonoids, terpenes, alkaloids, amino acids and their derivatives, and phenolic acids. In particular, 6 out of 9 metabolites in the A3 vs A2 group were alkaloids. K-means cluster analysis was performed on the differential metabolites across the three different periods of wheat (Fig. 3G and Table S4) to explore the variation trend of the relative content of wheat grain metabolites at different periods. These 1513 substances included 260 flavonoids, 150 alkaloids, 197 amino acids and their derivatives, 187 terpenes, 165 phenolic acids, and 182 lipids. Specifically, the relative contents of 107 flavonoids and 67 terpenes in Subclass 1 showed the same variation trend; Subclass 2 was mainly composed of lipids and terpenes; the relative contents of 89 amino acids and their derivatives and 62 lipids in Subclass 3 exhibited the same trend; lipids were the main components in Subclasses 6 and 7, while flavonoids and alkaloids were the main components in Subclass 5. The relationship between different metabolites in each group is shown in the form of Venn diagram. A total of 342 differential metabolites were identified across the three comparative groups, with 731, 399 and 686 unique differential metabolites respectively detected in the A3 vs A1_A2 vs A1, A2 vs A1_A3 vs A2 and A3 vs A1_A3 vs A2 paired comparisons (Fig. S3). Differential metabolites interact with each other in organisms to form various biosynthetic pathways. The KEGG classification diagrams of differential metabolites (Fig. S4A–C) showed that, among the three comparison groups, metabolic pathways and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites accounted for a large proportion of the differential metabolites.The KEGG enrichment results of differential metabolites are presented in (Fig.S4 D–F). In the A2 vs A1 comparison, differential metabolites were significantly enriched in the pathways of tricin O-glycosides biosynthesis, biosynthesis of other tricin derivatives, flavonoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of caffeic acid derivatives. For both A3 vs A1 and A3 vs A2 comparisons, differential metabolites were co-enriched in linoleic acid metabolism and α-linolenic acid metabolism, as well as the tricin O-glycosides biosynthesis pathway and its derivative synthesis pathway. Notably, tricin O-glycosides biosynthesis and biosynthesis of other tricin derivatives were significantly enriched across all three comparison groups, suggesting that these two metabolic pathways may play critical regulatory roles in wheat grain growth and development during the grain-filling stage.

Transcriptome analysis at different developmental stages

In this study, transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed on seeds of Dianmai 24 wheat at different developmental stages, with three biological replicates per stage. A total of 85.13 GB of clean data were obtained. The clean data for each sample reached 6 GB, and the Q30 base percentage was ≥ 95% (Table S5). The mapping efficiency of transcriptome data to the reference genome was high (> 85%), indicating good quality of the reference genome assembly. Wheat transcriptome sequencing data showed high consistency with the reference genome, and the sequencing results were accurate and reliable, thus suitable for subsequent analyses. The PCA plot (Fig. 4A) and sample correlation heatmap (Fig. 4C) of each group showed tight clustering within groups, indicating stable experimental methods and high data quality. The Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) among the three biological replicates of Dianmai 24 samples at different stages were all > 0.8, indicating strong correlations between biological replicates. Meanwhile, the three sample groups exhibited an obvious separation trend and good biological repeatability. These results suggest that changes in metabolite accumulation are strictly regulated by the expression of differential genes. The gene expression density distribution plot (Fig. 4B) shows the gene expression trends in the three groups, reflecting the distribution range of gene expression in the samples. The gene expression level, measured as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM), ranged from log₁₀⁻3 to log₁₀2. After Z‑score normalization (data centralization and standardization) of differentially expressed genes, FPKM expression values were extracted for subsequent cluster analysis. Gene expression differences among the three groups were then analyzed, hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted, and clustering heatmaps were generated for each comparison group (Fig. 4D–F). The results showed obvious differentiation among the differentially expressed genes at the three different stages, indicating high reliability of the sequencing data. In conclusion, the above data meet the requirements for screening differentially expressed genes. In this study, a total of 66,898, 80,500, 118,789, 53,256, 118,059, 90,611, and 94,173 genes were annotated using the KEGG, Swissprot, NR, KOG, TrEMBL, GO, and Pfam databases, respectively. A total of 10,445 (4950 up-regulated and 5495 down-regulated), 16,306 (7699 up-regulated and 8607 down-regulated), and 8437 (3687 up-regulated and 4750 down-regulated) differentially expressed genes were identified in the three pairwise comparison groups A2 vs A1, A3 vs A1, and A3 vs A2, respectively (Table S6).

Differentially expressed genes were identified according to the screening criteria used in this study. Differential gene analysis was performed using DESeq2. There are 1491 differentially expressed genes, which may play a key role in grain development. Notably, members of the NAC, MYB, and bZIP families showed the most dramatic expression fluctuations and formed distinct stage-associated clusters, consistent with their known roles as key regulators of cereal grain development. These expression patterns suggest that these TF families may exert specific regulatory functions during different phases of grain filling. Meanwhile, the three groups were also clearly distinguished in the cluster analysis (Fig. S7A–C). Analysis of proteome differences at different developmental stages

Proteomic analysis was performed on wheat at three developmental stages, as shown in Fig. S8A. A total of 130,674 protein sequences were selected from the database, among which 17,609 were identified as known sequences and all were quantified; additionally, 17,729 proteins were identified. A total of 128,514 peptides were identified, most of which were distributed within the range of 7–20 amino acids. This is consistent with the general rules of enzymatic hydrolysis and mass spectrometry fragmentation, and the distribution of peptide lengths identified by mass spectrometry met the quality control requirements (Fig. S8B). These results indicate that there is a clear distinction among different developmental stages, and the experimental procedure is stable and reliable. The results showed that the samples were well differentiated with good inter-group repeatability (Fig. S8C). The abundance value distribution plot of each sample (Fig. S8D) demonstrated good consistency among samples within the three groups, indicating that the experimental data of this study were reliable. Significantly differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined as those with a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or FC ≤ 0.6667 and a p-value ≤ 0.05. Statistical analysis of DEPs among the three comparison groups (Fig. 6A) revealed 4274 (1545 upregulated, 2729 downregulated), 6375 (1814 upregulated, 4561 downregulated), and 2037 (706 upregulated, 1331 downregulated) DEPs in the A2 vs A1, A3 vs A1, and A3 vs A2 groups, respectively. Discussion

Wheat is an important food crop that provides humans with energy, proteins, dietary fiber, and other nutrients [35]. In China, wheat is the main food source for residents in northern regions, more than 85% of which is used to produce various flour products such as bread, biscuits, and noodles [36, 37]. Therefore, studying wheat grain development is of great significance for improving wheat quality. We integrated proteomics, transcriptomics, non-targeted metabolomics, and morphological data analysis to identify the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Dianmai 24 at three key stages, namely 7, 14, and 21 days after flowering (DAA). On this basis, we further analyzed the expression differences of DEPs, DEMs, and DEGs across the three stages, and discussed the potential functions of key differential molecules through different metabolic networks, as well as their roles in regulating the grain development of Dianmai 24.

Metabolomics is a discipline that enables simultaneous qualitative and quantitative analysis of all low-molecular-weight metabolites in specific species, developmental stages, organs, tissues, or cells. It can characterize metabolic alterations and identify differential metabolites, thereby facilitating the dissection of plant metabolic pathways or networks. This approach plays a vital role in elucidating the molecular mechanisms relevant to crop breeding [38, 39]. In this study, metabolomic analysis of Dianmai 24 at three different stages identified 13 types of metabolites, among which flavonoids, amino acids and their derivatives, and lipids accounted for a high proportion. Studies have shown that as an important component in wheat grains, flavonoids play a crucial role in the composition and function of wheat grains, enhance the antioxidant capacity of wheat grains, and their content affects wheat grain quality [40]. Huang et al. profiled metabolite diversity across nine cereal crops and identified a total of 311 flavonoid compounds, revealing substantial variation in flavonoid composition and abundance among cereal species [41]. Moreover, flavonoids are well documented to benefit human and animal health; colored cereal grains can be utilized as whole-grain raw materials for developing fortified functional foods and preparing antioxidant dietary supplements [42]. Consistent with these findings, our metabolomic analysis detected widespread accumulation of flavonoids in developing wheat grains, with tricin glycosides exhibiting prominent differential accumulation across all three grain-filling stages. Tricin glycosides have been reported to act as effective ROS scavengers and enhance cellular antioxidant capacity [43], which provides a mechanistic explanation for the extensive flavonoid enrichment observed in our metabolomic dataset. Collectively, these results highlight the vital involvement of flavonoids in wheat grain development and suggest that their accumulation contributes to improved antioxidant potential in Dianmai 24 during grain filling.Our metabolomic study showed that flavonoids accumulated in large quantities, indicating that flavonoids play a role in wheat grain development and can enhance the antioxidant capacity of Dianmai 24. For instance, previous studies have demonstrated that the flavonoid content in wheat grains can improve wheat grain quality by enhancing antioxidant capacity. Shi et al. [44] performed transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on the low protein content of Xinchun 26 at 7, 14, and 21 days after anthesis (DAA). The metabolites were mainly classified into 12 categories, with lipid and lipid-like molecules, phenylpropanoids, and polyketides being the most abundant—similar to the metabolites with the highest proportion in the wheat grains in this study. Studies also show that lipid metabolism plays an important role in wheat grain development. The accumulation and decomposition of lipids in wheat grains are affected by planting conditions, nutrient supply, and climate change. For example, under a high CO₂ environment, the accumulation of lipids in wheat grains increases, which may affect the quality and nutritional value of wheat grains [45]. This also confirms the importance of α-linolenic acid metabolism (a key part of lipid metabolism) in the grain development of Dianmai 24. Meanwhile, D’Almeida et al. investigated the dynamic changes in phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity during sorghum grain maturation. Their results demonstrated that phenolic content gradually increased with grain development, and the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway was significantly activated, which accounts for the higher antioxidant activity observed in mature grains [46].Transcriptomic analysis showed that the differential genes in the three groups were enriched in the following pathways: Photosynthesis-antenna proteins, Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, Starch and sucrose metabolism, and Carbon metabolism. These pathways play an important role in the wheat grain filling stage. Buttar et al. \* MERGEFORMAT [47] employed transcriptomic analysis to explore the potential mechanism of the wheat variety "Shannong 33" and its sister line control (CK) at the critical stage of grain development. They identified and classified 94,972 genes into four groups, which were mainly involved in translation, ribosomal structure and biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, and starch and sucrose metabolism pathways. Lin et al. performed integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of sweet potato tuber development. They found that both DEGs and differentially regulated metabolites (DRMs) were markedly enriched in starch and sucrose metabolism as well as flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. Genes associated with flavonoid biosynthesis displayed progressively elevated expression during tuber development, peaking at 130 days after growth [48].At present, proteomic methods have been used to identify the molecular processes during grain development in different wheat varieties [49] and at different grain development stages [9, 50–52]. Proteomic analysis showed that the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) involved in starch biosynthesis, storage proteins, and defense/stress-related proteins were significantly accumulated, while the DEPs related to photosynthesis exhibited an opposite expression trend at the late stage of grain development [53]. Du et al. [54]found that DEPs at 6 DAA, 12 DAA, and 20 DAAwere mainly involved in carbon metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and metabolic pathways, respectively. Li et al. combined cytological observation with proteomic and metabolomic analyses to investigate wheat grain filling at 15, 20, 25 and 30 days after anthesis. A total of 1541 differentially expressed proteins and 406 differential metabolites were identified. These molecules were mainly involved in protein metabolism, stress and defense responses, energy metabolism, and amino acid metabolism, whereas the later grain-filling stage was dominated by the metabolism of storage proteins and carbohydrates [55]. In this study, the three groups were significantly enriched in metabolic processes and cellular processes, and were mainly involved in photosynthesis, carbon metabolism, and ribosome pathways at the late flowering stage. Consistent with previous studies, these pathways play an important role in wheat grain development. In the joint analysis of transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, we found that the key genes and their encoded proteins in Dianmai 24, such as SPLA2, LOX2S, CYP74A, HPL, CEQORH, AOC, OPR, and ACOX1, exhibited their own unique up-regulated or down-regulated expression patterns at the three developmental stages. These dynamic expression characteristics indicate that the aforementioned genes and proteins play an important regulatory role in the process of wheat grain filling and development. To sum up, this study systematically analyzed the differential changes in grain development of Dianmai 24 during the grain filling stage through multi-omics integration analysis. This not only provides an important theoretical basis for elucidating the molecular regulatory mechanism of wheat grain development, but also offers potential targets for screening genes and proteins related to wheat grain development.

Conclusion

Using an integrated multi-omics approach, this study systematically investigated the dynamic changes and underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms during three key post-anthesis developmental stages of grains in Dianmai 24. Phenotypic characterization revealed extremely significant differences in grain width and biomass across the three developmental stages, reflecting the stage-specific developmental patterns of grain filling and expansion. Metabolomic profiling demonstrated that flavonoids, amino acids and their derivatives, and lipid metabolites displayed significant differential accumulation patterns across developmental stages, thereby contributing to and sustaining grain development and nutrient accumulation. Proteomic analysis revealed that differentially accumulated proteins exhibited obvious stage-specific characteristics. Among these proteins, α-amylase/trypsin inhibitor CM3, β-amylase, and globulin-3A maintained continuously high abundance throughout grain development, exerting crucial regulatory functions in starch metabolism, storage protein accumulation, and grain maturation. Integrated analysis of the transcriptome, proteome and metabolome revealed that the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway was significantly enriched at all three omics levels. Based on the synergistic alterations of key factors within this pathway, 15 core genes, 17 differentially accumulated proteins and 11 pivotal metabolites were identified and screened. This study refined the coordinated regulatory molecular network comprising α-linolenic acid metabolism, starch and storage protein biosynthesis, as well as flavonoid, amino acid and lipid metabolism during wheat grain development, and clarified the dynamic regulatory patterns at multi-omics levels. Future studies could expand the panel of wheat genotypes assessed, extend the grain-filling sampling period, and further elucidate the regulatory networks governing grain filling. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis and candidate resources for dissecting the molecular mechanisms underlying wheat grain development and improving grain-related traits.
