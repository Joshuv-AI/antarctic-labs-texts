Generalized Euler method to study the vaccination effects on dynamics of measles infection model under non-singular kernel
Authors: L. K. Yadav, M. M. Gour, S. D. Purohit, Qasem Al-Mdallal, D. L. Suthar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Measles is a highly contagious and potentially fatal viral disease that spreads primarily through direct contact with infected individuals. In this study, we develop a fractional measles infection model using the Atangana-Baleanu fractional derivative with a nonlocal and non-singular kernel. To analyze the considered model, we apply the generalized Euler method (GEM), a semi-analytical numerical approach tailored for fractional-order nonlinear systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). The proposed method approximates solutions as a series of polynomials, ensuring both accuracy and stability through iterative computations. We also examine the uniqueness and convergence of the obtained solutions using fixed-point theory. In addition, the model equilibria are determined, and the novel Jacobian determinant method recently constructed in the literature of epidemiological modeling of infectious diseases is applied to determine the threshold quantity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R_0}$$\end{document}. Furthermore, we construct appropriate Lyapunov functions to establish the global asymptotic stability of the disease-free and endemic equilibrium points. Numerical simulations are carried out in MATLAB 2021a for various integer and non-integer orders within the interval (0, 1] along with sensitivity analysis of key model parameters. The simulation results demonstrate that interventions such as quarantine, vaccination, and treatment significantly reduce the exposed and infectious populations, thereby contributing to effective disease control. Based on these findings, we recommend that governments allocate financial support to enhance vaccine distribution programs.

Introduction

Measles is an infectious disease caused by the measles virus, a member of the Paramyxoviridae family. It presents with symptoms such as a characteristic rash, high fever, coryza, cough, and conjunctivitis. The disease is extremely contagious and is primarily transmitted through respiratory droplets or by contact with contaminated surfaces. The incubation period from exposure to the onset of prodromal symptoms typically ranges from 11 to 12 days, with initial presentation often marked by a high fever lasting 10-12 days following exposure to an infected individual or contaminated environment1. Despite the availability of safe and cost-effective vaccines, measles remains a major global public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries2. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that in 2020, approximately 23.5 million children missed their routine childhood vaccinations, marking the highest figure since 2009 and nearly 3.8 million more than in 2019. In addition, the number of measles cases worldwide increased by almost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$79\%$$\end{document} during the first two months of 2022 compared to the same period in 20213. In Ghana, the Northern Region recorded around 120 measles cases by February 2023, a situation mainly linked to shortages of vital vaccines, according to the Paediatric Society of Ghana. The Ghana Health Service (GHS) explained that the decline in the value of the Ghana Cedi disrupted the supply of vaccines used in routine childhood immunization programs, including those for tetanus, Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), whooping cough, tuberculosis (TB), and oral polio (OPV)4.

Mathematical modeling is a valuable approach to understand the patterns of how infectious diseases spread. Utilizing mathematical tools allows for a deeper understanding of various infectious diseases. Throughout the existing body of the literature, interdisciplinary efforts involving epidemiologists, mathematicians, ecologists, and statisticians have been undertaken to elucidate the spread of diseases such as the spread of the COVID-19 virus5–10, HBV11,12, HIV/HCV co-infection model13, human T lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-I)14,15, the Marburg virus16, the Brusselator chemical reaction model17, Ebola virus with vaccination control18,19, Lassa fever with dual-dosage vaccination20, Chikungunya virus dynamics21, fractionalized investigation of tuberculosis disease22, and other related topics23–26.

Wallinga et al.27 showed that even in highly vaccinated populations, the persistence of susceptible children can sustain measles outbreaks, emphasizing the need for consistently high vaccination coverage. Rehman et al.28 analyzed an SIQR measles model incorporating natural delays and demonstrated that time-delay effects significantly influence disease dynamics, while James et al.29 reported that combining vaccination with hospitalization is more effective in reducing peak infection levels than applying either intervention alone. Fakhruddin et al.30 further highlighted that improving access to treatment during outbreaks can be more effective than relying solely on vaccination. To account for memory and nonlocal effects in disease transmission, Abboubakar et al.31 and Ogunmiloro et al.32 developed fractional-order measles models using Caputo and Atangana-Baleanu-Caputo derivatives, respectively, showing improved dynamical realism compared to classical formulations. Almeida and Qureshi33 demonstrated that fractional models outperform integer-order counterparts when applied to epidemiological data with persistent trends, while Qureshi and Jan34 confirmed that vaccination remains the most reliable control strategy within a fractional modeling framework. Nazir et al.35 extended these findings by providing analytical solutions for a measles model with multiple vaccination rounds using the Caputo-Fabrizio derivative. More recently, Yunus and Olayiwola36 proposed a novel measles dynamics model and showed through numerical simulations that enhanced vaccination strategies significantly reduce infection prevalence and accelerate disease eradication.

Akuka et. al37 develop a SEVIQR model, which is based on the following key simplifying assumptions: (i) constant total population size without migration or age structure; (ii) homogeneous mixing among compartments, neglecting spatial heterogeneity; (iii) fixed rates for transmission, vaccination, and quarantine, assuming time-invariant intervention efficacy; and (iv) permanent recovery with lifelong immunity after infection. Fractional calculus has gained considerable attention in recent decades for its wide applications in science and engineering38–40. Unlike classical integer-order models, which assume memoryless dynamics and often oversimplify complex epidemiological processes, fractional derivatives naturally incorporate nonlocal and memory effects. This makes them particularly suitable for infectious disease modeling, where past states influence future transmission through mechanisms such as waning immunity, delayed intervention responses, and repeated exposure risks in under-vaccinated populations, leading to improved predictive accuracy in scenarios with long-term herd immunity effects. Key challenges in this modeling approach include parameter uncertainty arising from empirical data variability, the high computational demands of fractional operators (due to non-local kernels increasing simulation time and complexity), difficulties in accurate estimation of the fractional order and other parameters from noisy or sparse real-world data, and the need for specialized numerical methods to ensure stability and convergence while handling memory effects.

Motivated by these limitations and challenges, we present a novel fractional SEVIQR measles model based on the Atangana-Baleanu-Caputo derivative, which provides a more realistic representation of memory-dependent transmission dynamics. To solve the resulting system, we employ the generalized Euler method (GEM), which offers a balanced combination of computational efficiency, numerical accuracy, and stability for fractional nonlinear systems. The novelty is evident in our comparative analysis, which highlights GEM’s superiority in accuracy and stability for fractional systems, making it ideal for predicting vaccination impacts in real-world scenarios with memory effects. Through comparative analysis, the importance of selecting an appropriate numerical technique is emphasized. While the Euler method is valued for its simplicity and computational efficiency, the predictor-corrector method demonstrates superior accuracy and stability, making it more suitable for intricate and precise applications. This analysis serves as a valuable resource for researchers seeking to refine their numerical simulations, ensuring reliable and efficient solutions tailored to specific requirements.

Conclusion

This study investigates the mathematical modeling and analysis of measles infection dynamics using the Atangana-Baleanu fractional-order derivative, incorporating quarantine and vaccination effects. Through appropriate qualitative techniques, we establish the existence and uniqueness of solutions, determine model equilibria, compute the threshold quantity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {R}}_0$$\end{document} via the Jacobian determinant method, and prove global asymptotic stability of the disease-free and endemic equilibria using suitable Lyapunov functions. Numerical simulations in MATLAB 2021a using the generalized Euler method (GEM) adapted for fractional-order nonlinear systems reveal the influence of key parameters and fractional order on compartment trajectories, demonstrating that vaccination and quarantine significantly reduce infected and exposed populations, with stronger effects at lower fractional orders due to captured memory phenomena. Compared to classical schemes like the standard Euler method, GEM provides superior accuracy, stability, and preservation of memory effects through polynomial approximations and iterative computations, making it particularly effective for realistic epidemiological predictions. The results highlight that sustained vaccination coverage and timely isolation are biologically crucial for controlling measles outbreaks, while the fractional order serves as a meaningful indicator of population memory, reflecting persistent behavioral and immunological factors. Despite limitations such as parameter uncertainty and computational demands, our approach aligns with international measles modeling efforts and underscores the long-term benefits of preventive interventions in high-prevalence settings. Future work will extend the framework to fractal-fractional derivatives (Caputo and Atangana-Baleanu types) and integrate them into optimal control strategies for enhanced disease management.
