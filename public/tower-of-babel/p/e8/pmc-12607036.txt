Higher viremia and pathogenesis of human adenovirus 7 is likely associated to enhanced disruption of junction integrity
Authors: Jing Zhou, Qigao Chen, Bolin Cheng, Lianzhong Li, Yihao Yang, Yongping Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Distinct Viral Trafficking Patterns: HAdV-7 exhibits unrestricted bidirectional entry and release in endothelial cells, unlike HAdV-3, which may enhance systemic dissemination. This contrasts with epithelial cells, where both viruses release apically despite bidirectional entry.•Superior Replication Efficiency: HAdV-7 replicates more robustly than HAdV-3 in both cell types, correlating with its clinical association with higher viremia and severe pediatric outcomes.•Epithelial Barrier Disruption: HAdV-7 infection significantly compromises tight junction integrity, reducing transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and increasing paracellular flux.•Novel Pathogenic Mechanism: The unrestricted endothelial trafficking and junctional damage by HAdV-7 provide a mechanistic link to its heightened pathogenicity, offering therapeutic targets to mitigate barrier dysfunction.

Importance

Epidemiology has shown that HAdV-7 infection is associated with more severe and fatal adenoviral infections in children. A higher ocurrence of HAdV-7 in blood (viremia) has been linked to its spread to multiple organs and with severe clinical complications. Therefore, the mechanism by which high viremia in HAdV-7 is linked to pathogenesis deserves further investigation. Herein, we used polarized lung epithelial and endothelial cells to investigate the polarity of entry and egress of HAdV-3 and HAdV-7. We also comparing their effect on the disruption of epithelial barriers and tight junctions. Our study suggests that HAdV-7 strong replicative ability and more severe tight junction damage may explain the higher viremia observed.

Introduction

Human adenoviruses (HAdV) cause a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations. In acute respiratory infections (ARIs) (Mao et al., 2022), HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 are the most commonly causative agent among identified HadV types. Of the two, HAdV-7 is associated with more severe clinical outcomes in children (Mao et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022), and can cause severe pneumonia, multiple organ dysfunction and also death. Although recent studies have advanced the understanding of HAdV-7 pathogenic mechanisms, the development of targeted therapeutic strategies continues to be hindered by its intricate pathological cascades and systemic multi-organ involvement.

Viremia refers to the pathological state in which viruses enter the bloodstream from localized infection sites, leading to systemic dissemination and infection. Studies demonstrate that HAdV-7 infection can induce viremia, a critical factor contributing to severe disease progression (Chen et al., 2021). Viremia not only elevates the risk of multi-organ involvement but may also exacerbate clinical outcomes by triggering systemic inflammatory responses through immune hyperactivation (Echavarria et al., 2001; Gu et al., 2016). Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms underlying HAdV-7-associated viremia is essential for advancing novel therapeutic strategies.

Destruction of the alveolar-capillary barrier by the virus is a possible cause of viremia. The alveolar epithelium, composed of tightly adherent type I and type II pneumocytes, forms a selective permeability barrier alongside the adjacent capillary endothelium—a bilayer separated by a shared basement membrane (Enenbaum-Katan et al., 2018). The alveolar-capillary barrier exhibits strong polarization. Cells are divided into apical (close to the alveolar space or vessel lumen) and basolateral (close to basement membrane) surfaces (Nova et al., 2019). The integrity of this barrier is maintained through tight junctions (TJ) and the polarized distribution of membrane proteins (Chan et al., 2009; Markov and Amasheh, 2014 Jun 24). Viruses infected alveolar-capillary barrier and release are also directional. The release of viruses at apical surfaces leads to local infection, while bidirectional or basolateral release may lead to viremia and further spread to other organs (Cong and Ren, 2014; Tseng et al., 2005; Tao et al., 2013). In addition, the virus can cause viremia by disrupting TJ, which can impair the function of the alveolar capillary barrier (Chai et al., 2021; Ramirez et al., 2018).

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the high viremia observed in HAdV-7 infection, we selected two complementary cellular models that mimic key physiological barriers encountered during viral dissemination: NCI-H441 cells, representing the pulmonary epithelial barrier, and primary human lung microvascular endothelial cells (HPMECs), representing the vascular endothelium. The two types of cells were used to compare the polarity of HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 entry and egress, and the effect of these two viruses on TJ disruption.

NCl-H441 cells represent an excellent model for studying the permeability barrier due to their ability to form polarized monolayers with high transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) values, low paracellular flux, and expression of key tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin (Salomon et al., 2014).

Methods

Culture and polarization of epithelial and endothelial cells

NCl-H441 cells, exhibiting morphological traits of type II pneumocytes and club cells, were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HPMECs were obtained from Sciencell (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Both types of cells were independently plated in the apical compartment of a 0.4 μm pore Transwell (Corning) at a density of 5 × 104 cells/cm2. NC1-H441 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Gibco) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), whereas HPMEC cells were cultivated in endothelial cell growth medium (Sciencell). From day 3 of culture, the medium was refreshed daily and dexamethasone (1 μM, Sigma) was added to the medium in the upper compartment until a dense and complete monolayer of cells was formed.

Co-culture model of alveolar epithelial-endothelial barrier

HTS 24-Transwell® filters (polycarbonate, 0.4 μm pore size; Costar, Wiesbaden, Germany) were coated with rat tail collagen type-I (12.12 μg/cm2, BD Biosciences, Heidelberg, Germany). HPMECs cells (5 × 104/cm2) were seeded on the lower surface of the inverted filter membrane. After 2 h of adhesion at 37 °C and 5 % CO2, H441 cells (2 × 104/cm2) were placed on the top side of the membrane. The cells were cultured for about 10 days in RPMI 1640 medium with l-glutamine supplemented with 5 % FCS and Pen/Strep (100 U/100 μg/ml). From day 3 of cultivation, H441 cells were treated with 1 μM dexamethasone.

Monolayer permeability and integrity

The permeability of epithelial and endothelial monolayers was evaluated using the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran assay, and was confirmed via TEER measurements (EVOM2; World Precision Instruments, Herts, UK). To assess permeability, FITC-labeled 20 kDa dextran (FD20) was added to the medium in the upper chamber of a Transwell filter (5 µg/µL). After allowing FD20 to equilibrate for 3 h at 37 °C, a 100 µL sample from the lower chamber was collected and analyzed with 488 nm excitation using a fluorescent plate reader (Victor, Perkin Elmer). Permeability ( %) was determined from the ratio of fluorescence intensities in lower and upper chambers, multiplied by 100.

Viral strains and titrations

Viral strains were extracted from children infected with HAdV. Samples were obtained from the Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA), and extracted isolates HAdV-3 (GZ/1609–18) and HAdV-7 (GZ/1706–198) were then propagated in A549 cells. For virus titration assays, confluent A549 cell cultures in 96-well plates were inoculated with the viruses and were daily monitored for cytopathic effects (CPE) over six days. TCID50 values were determined using the Spearman-Karber method.

Infection of polarized cells with viruses

Cells were infected with either HAdV-7 or HAdV-3 at the apical or basolateral side, at 10 TCID50. For apical infection, the virus-containing solution (200 μL) was introduced into the apical compartment of the Transwell. For basolateral infection, the Transwell insert was inverted to expose the basolateral surface of the membrane, and 40 µL of virus-containing solution was applied directly onto it. After 2 h of incubation, the insert was returned to its original orientation, followed by washing. In both cases, after 2 h of incubation, the insert was repositioned to its original orientation and washed. Supernatants and cell samples were collected separately from the basolateral and apical chambers at 0, 1, 2 and 3 days post-infection (d.p.i.).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were rinsed twice with PBS, followed by fixing with paraformaldehyde (4 %) for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were rinsed thrice with PBS and permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature. The monolayers were then rinsed with PBS, blocked with 5 % BSA for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-adenovirus IgG2a (Abcam,1:400), anti-claudin 4 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam,1:300), anti-occludin rabbit monoclonal antibody [EPR20992] (Abcam,1:300) and anti-junctional adhesion molecule 1 mouse monoclonal antibody [J10.4] (Abcam,1:300). They were then rinsed thrice with PBS, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C with the anti-mouse AlexaFluor 488 (1:400) and anti-rabbit AlexaFluor 488 (1:400). Transwell membranes were subsequently rinsed three times with PBS, excised, placed on a glass slide and fixed with a DAPI-containing mounting medium. Images were obtained using an inverted fluorescence microscope (FV1000; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analyses

Each experimental group included three technical replicates, and the experiment was independently repeated twice on separate days using freshly prepared cells and reagents. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from the combined technical replicates. Fluorescence intensity was measured using Image J software. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 5. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study compares the polarity of infection in HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 in the contexts of alveolar epithelium and endothelium, and their effects on TJ integrity. In epithelial cells, both viruses can infect via basolateral or apical surfaces. This bipolar mode of entry is consistent with infection of a new host established via the apical side of the airway epithelial cells, whereas basolateral entry into these cells during viremia would cause virus shedding at mucosal surfaces.

Viral release from epithelial cells was observed primarily at the apical surface. While apical release facilitates lateral spread within the airway lumen, it does not directly enable systemic dissemination. In contrast, basolateral release aids in the vertical transmission from epithelial cells to the bloodstream and lymph vessels, enabling a systemic infection. However, the fact that we observe that HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 infections disrupt the integrity of the polarized epithelial monolayer, more so in HAdV-7, allows these viruses pass through the alveolar epithelial cells, infecting the endothelial cells. In polarized lung microvascular endothelium, both HAdV-3 and HAdV-7 could initiate productive replication from either the apical or basolateral surface, and viral release occurred from both sides. Structurally, the apical side of endothelial cells faces the vascular lumen, while the basolateral side faces the basement membrane, and is adjacent to alveolar epithelial cells. Therefore, viruses released from epithelial cells can infect endothelial cells via their basolateral surface; subsequent apical release may result in viral particles entering the bloodstream.

This process likely involves both epithelial and endothelial cells. To address the limitation of monolayer cultures, we established a physiologically relevant alveolar epithelium-endothelium co-culture model. Results from this stratified system robustly confirmed that HAdV-7 infection induces a more severe impairment of barrier integrity (TEER) and exhibits higher replication titers than HAdV-3. In co-culture models, viral egress occurred predominantly from the apical side of the epithelial barrier, with no basolateral release, most likely due to the limited number of virus particles that come into contact with endothelial cells, or that the duration of the infection was too short.

Our monolayer culture data demonstrate that, in both cell types, HAdV-7 exhibits higher replication efficiency and greater apical release than HAdV-3. In particular, apical release from endothelial cells was significantly higher after basolateral infection by HAdV-7, suggesting that enhanced trans-barrier transmission may be a key factor contributing to its higher viremia. While the precise molecular mechanism remains to be fully elucidated, our findings indicate that both cell types participate in this process, and that viral polarity of infection and release may be critical determinants of systemic dissemination.

Our study reveals that HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 induce alveolar epithelial barrier dysfunction by disrupting TJ between alveolar epithelial cells. Viruses induce alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction by disrupting TJ, triggering a cascade of pulmonary diseases. This mechanism has been extensively validated in studies of multiple respiratory viruses. Studies demonstrate that IAV infection leads to significant reductions in tight junction proteins, e.g., ZO-1, ZO-2, ZO-3, Occludin, and Claudin-4. The loss of these proteins directly impairs alveolar barrier integrity, resulting in edema and fluid accumulation in alveolar spaces (Short et al., 2016; Ruan et al., 2022). SARS-CoV-2 infection similarly disrupts the alveolar-capillary barrier by impairing TJ in alveolar epithelial cells and inducing endothelial cell dysfunction (Gonzalez-Rubio et al., 2023). This disruption may result from direct viral infection or cytokine-mediated degradation of TJ complexes (Walters et al., 2002; Deinhardt-Emmer et al., 2021). Based on the polarity of HAdV-7 infection and egress, we propose that the key mechanism enabling viral entry into the vasculature is not direct basolateral budding, but rather, virus-induced disruption of tight junctions—a hallmark of HAdV-7 infection. By generating paracellular leaks, HAdV-7 facilitates its transit across the compromised epithelial layer to infect the underlying endothelium. Once inside endothelial cells, as observed in our HPMEC studies, the efficient replication of HAdV-7 and its subsequent apical release into the vascular lumen directly seeds the bloodstream with viral particles. Thus, the synergy between enhanced replicative capacity and potent barrier breakdown offers a compelling explanation for the high viremia characteristic of severe HAdV-7 infections.

In this study, we focused on evaluating the expression of three key tight junction proteins, occludin, claudin-4, and JAM-A, in the context of HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 infection. These specific proteins were selected based on their well-established roles in maintaining alveolar epithelial barrier integrity and their susceptibility to disruption by various respiratory viruses. Occludin and claudin-4 are critical for paracellular sealing and barrier selectivity, while JAM-A is involved in cell-cell adhesion and inflammatory signaling. The ZO proteins (such as ZO-1, ZO-2, or ZO-3) were not included in the current analysis because of our experimental design. We prioritized the evaluation of the transmembrane junctional components, which are more directly exposed to viral and host-derived proteases, and are frequently the initial targets of viral-induced barrier disruption.

In our study, comparative analysis of polarized alveolar epithelial cells infected with inactivated versus non-inactivated HAdV-7 revealed marked reductions in TEER and increased FITC-dextran permeability, only with the replication-competent virus (Supplemental file 1). These findings indicate that active viral replication directly compromises TJ protein expression and barrier integrity. However, given the precedent from other viral infections, we cannot exclude the possibility that HAdV-7 also induces cytokine-mediated TJ degradation. To elucidate this potential mechanism, future studies could quantify pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) in HAdV-7-infected cultures, and assess the effect of conditioned medium on TJ expression. This approach would help differentiate between direct viral damage and secondary cytokine effects on epithelial barrier dysfunction.

HAdV-7 induced more pronounced reduction in TJ protein levels, particularly claudin-4. Therefore, claudin 4 protein may play a key role in HAdV-7 crossing the alveolar epithelial cell barrier. Claudin-4 is one of four predominant claudin proteins expressed in alveolar epithelial cells (Overgaard et al., 2012). Cohen et al. observed significant impairment of tight junction barrier function in the alveolar epithelium of a septic rat model, correlating with reduced claudin-4 levels (Lee et al., 2018). Wray et al. further confirmed that diminished claudin-4 expression induces tight junction leakage, reduces alveolar fluid clearance and exacerbates pulmonary edema during mechanical ventilation. Augmenting claudin-4 expression may optimize alveolar fluid clearance efficiency (Wray et al., 2009). These findings suggest that HAdV-7-induced downregulation of claudin-4 may facilitate its crossing of the alveolar epithelial barrier and subsequent entry into the circulation.

Claudin-4 has also been proposed as a therapeutic target in lung injury. In preclinical models, pharmacologic or genetic upregulation of claudin-4 has been shown to enhance alveolar fluid clearance and restore barrier function, thereby improving outcomes in acute lung injury (Wray et al., 2009). Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms driving claudin-4 degradation in HAdV-7 versus HAdV-3 infections may help identify strategies to preserve epithelial integrity and mitigate viral dissemination.

This study has certain limitations. First, this reductionist approach is somewhat limited by the fact that it is impossible for an in vitro model to fully capture the complex interactions in the lung during HAdV infection. Indeed, this model reproduces the events early in the infection, when leukocytes and cytokines (which may also have an important impact on barrier integrity) are almost absent in the lung. Second, our analysis was confined to three key tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin-4, and JAM-A), whereas the alveolar barrier integrity is maintained by a more complex network of junctional molecules. Future studies should broaden the scope to include other critical tight junction and adherens junction components, in order to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying HAdV-induced barrier dysfunction.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate that both HAdV-7 and HAdV-3 disrupt alveolar epithelial barrier integrity by degrading tight junction proteins, facilitate their passage across the epithelial barrier, and infect the basolateral side of capillary endothelial cells. Subsequent apical release of viruses into the vasculature drives systemic viremia. Claudin-4 downregulation appears to be a key feature of HAdV-7 infection, and may contribute to its more severe epithelial damage and greater systemic spread. Although the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be clarified, this study highlights the importance of epithelial-endothelial interactions and TJ modulation in adenoviral pathogenesis.
