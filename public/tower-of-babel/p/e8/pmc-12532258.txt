Expanding Horizons: Transesophageal Ultrasound across Organ Systems in Prone Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome Patients
Authors: Amol Trimbakrao Kothekar, Neeraj P Singh
Published: 2025

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains a major challenge in intensive care units, with a mortality rate ranging from 30 to 45% despite advances in ventilatory support.1 Among the available therapeutic strategies, prone positioning has become a cornerstone intervention in moderate-to-severe ARDS, particularly following the proning severe ARDS patients (PROSEVA) trial, which demonstrated significant reductions in mortality.2 While the oxygenation benefits of prone positioning are well established, it poses challenges for clinical ultrasound monitoring techniques, such as transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and lung and abdominal ultrasound, because of restricted access to the anterior thoracic and abdominal regions.3,4

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), on the other hand, enables safe and reliable cardiac assessment during prone ventilation.5 Ultrasound performed using a TEE probe, referred to as transesophageal ultrasound (TEUS), is emerging as a promising modality for evaluating the lungs and abdominal structures. Transesophageal ultrasound provides high-resolution imaging of dependent lung regions that are often inaccessible via transthoracic windows, facilitating the detection of consolidation, atelectasis, and pleural effusions.6 Additionally, Doppler assessment of hepatic, portal, and renal venous flow enables evaluation of systemic and splanchnic congestion, thereby guiding fluid management.7,8 The importance of conservative fluid management in ARDS was underscored by the fluid and catheter treatment trial (FACTT), which demonstrated a significant reduction in the duration of mechanical ventilation and improved lung function, without increasing the risk of non-pulmonary organ failure.9 Incorporating TEUS with the venous excess ultrasound score (VExUS) and lung ultrasound (LUS) into bedside monitoring may enable intensivists to identify venous congestion more accurately and individualize fluid restriction. Several studies have demonstrated that such examinations can be performed safely, with only minor adverse events such as nasogastric tube displacement.6–8 Collectively, these findings support the integration of TEUS into routine practice for comprehensive monitoring in patients with ARDS.

The retrospective observational study by Isa et al.,10 published in this issue of the Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine, provides valuable insights into the clinical feasibility and utility of TEUS in this challenging setting. The study included 109 patients with moderate-to-severe ARDS, who underwent prone ventilation, the majority of whom had COVID-19-related ARDS. The protocol incorporated cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, portal, and renal venous assessments without the need for patient repositioning or interruption of mechanical ventilation. Key findings included high prevalence of right ventricular dysfunction (36.7%), acute cor pulmonale (27.5%), and significant venous congestion evidenced by portal vein pulsatility (32%), hepatic vein abnormalities (18%), and impaired renal vein flow (25.7%). Lung ultrasound demonstrated interstitial-alveolar syndrome in 98%, consolidation in 38%, and only rare instances of pleural effusions. The procedure was safe and associated with minimal adverse events. Importantly, the TEUS assessments facilitated individualized fluid management and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) adjustments based on real-time findings.

This study underscores the value of comprehensive TEUS and TEE assessments in moderate-to-severe ARDS for tailoring management strategies and may catalyze further prospective investigations. Although the cohort primarily consisted of patients with COVID-19-related ARDS, future studies can explore these outcomes in larger and more heterogeneous ARDS populations. Prospective trials may also evaluate the impact on clinical endpoints such as ventilator-free days, ICU length of stay, and survival. The study was conducted in specialized tertiary care centers with intensivists trained in critical care ultrasound. Other ICUs may need to overcome practical challenges related to equipment availability, operator expertise, and structured training in TEUS, particularly in low-resource settings.

Incorporating serial TEUS assessments may also facilitate tracking the evolution of cardiopulmonary and abdominal congestion, thereby guiding individualized fluid and ventilatory strategies. Transesophageal ultrasound represents a valuable opportunity to strengthen ICU care delivery, democratize advanced monitoring, and ultimately transform patient management.
