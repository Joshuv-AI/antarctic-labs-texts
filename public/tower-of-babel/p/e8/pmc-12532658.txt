Impact of multi-generational laboratory rearing on the development and viability of Cochliomyia hominivorax
Authors: Giuliano Pereira de Barros, Laura Lívia Arias Avilés, Marceli Carvalho da Silva, Patrizia Ana Bricarello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe screwworm fly, Cochliomyia hominivorax, is a significant pest in Brazil, posing threats to agriculture and public health. Laboratory rearing is crucial for developing control strategies like the sterile insect technique (SIT); but the effects of multi-generational rearing on the fly’s biological parameters are not fully understood. This study investigated the impact of rearing C. hominivorax for 12 inbred generations on its morphometrics, reproductive capacity, and life expectancy. Specimens were collected at distinct developmental stages across 12 generations for morphometric analysis, and key viability traits were assessed through biological assays. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. The results revealed that morphometric parameters and key reproductive traits (oviposition, egg weight, hatchability, and larval survival) remained stable across all generations (p > 0.05). However, a significant decline in adult life expectancy was observed (log-rank test, χ2 = 338.257, p < 0.001), becoming pronounced from the fifth generation onwards. Significant sexual dimorphism was also confirmed: males were larger and heavier (Mann–Whitney U = 9884.5, p < 0.001), while females exhibited significantly greater longevity (log-rank test, χ2 = 44.538, p < 0.001). Continuous laboratory rearing of C. hominivorax appears robust for maintaining physical and reproductive traits, but negatively impacts fitness-related traits like longevity, likely due to inbreeding. These findings provide a critical quantitative baseline for quality control in mass-rearing programs and highlight the need for strategies to manage genetic diversity in laboratory colonies to ensure their effectiveness for research and SIT applications. Introduction

The blowfly Cochliomyia hominivorax (Diptera: Calliphoridae), also known as the New World screwworm (Coquerel 1858), is a species that causes myiasis in both humans and a wide range of animals, including livestock, wild, and companion animals (Patton 1921; Singh and Banerjee 2022) and has reemerged in Central America since 2021 (Zaldivar-Gomez et al. 2025). In Brazil, this myiasis is considered a neglected zoonosis (Barros and Bricarello 2020) that harms the agricultural sector (Costa-Júnior et al. 2019) and has become relevant in the context of emerging One Health concerns (Colwell and Wall 2018; Altuna et al. 2021).

Laboratory rearing of this blowfly is a crucial technique for research aimed at developing control strategies and advancing the understanding of its biology (Mastrangelo 2011; Mastrangelo et al. 2011; Chen et al. 2014; Nogueira et al. 2020). Mass rearing is essential for producing sterile insects for the sterile insect technique (SIT), an effective population control method (Dyck et al. 2005; Vargas-Terán et al. 2005; Concha et al. 2016), and for supporting studies prospecting new insecticidal substances (Bricarello et al. 2021; Nogueira et al. 2020). Such studies require large quantities of insects reared under standardized methods to ensure reliable results (Wang et al. 2022).

However, the process of laboratory rearing itself can affect the natural development of insects (Crystal 1967; DeVaney and Garcia 1975), potentially compromising the validity of experimental outcomes (Isman 2020; Lavoir et al. 2022). Several factors, such as artificial diets (Taylor and Mangan 1987), population density, and inadvertent artificial selection, that can lead to a loss of genetic diversity (Bush and Neck 1976; Peterson and Candido 1987), can alter the colony’s biology. Therefore, understanding the extent to which multi-generational rearing affects key biological parameters—many of which are used to evaluate new control products (Dharmagadda et al. 2005; Pavela 2009; Seugling et al. 2019; Bricarello et al. 2021)—is crucial for refining rearing techniques (Mastrangelo 2011) and ensuring the reliability of research findings.

Thus, this study aims to investigate the effect that laboratory rearing over 12 inbred generations has on the development, reproductive capacity, hatchability, larval survival, and life expectancy of C. hominivorax specimens.

Materials and methods

Artificial rearing of insects

The insect colony that formed the basis of this study was created from the collection of third-stage larvae of naturally occurring myiasis in the sheep of the Agroecology Research and Extension Center at the Ressacada Experimental Farm, Center for Agricultural Sciences of the Federal University of Santa Catarina. The basic methodology for rearing these blowflies is described in Mastrangelo (2011) and Bricarello et al. (2021). Larvae from six different myiasis occurring between January and March 2020 were used. The larvae were collected directly from the tissues of parasitized animals and transported in sanitized Falcon® 50-mL tubes at ambient temperature immediately after collection to the Animal Parasitology Laboratory of the Federal University of Santa Catarina. The wild larvae were housed in 500 mL glass jars containing a layer of 3 cm of sterile vermiculite. The jars with the larvae were then placed in a Newlab® Biochemical Oxygen Demand (B.O.D.) incubator set to maintain a temperature of 26.5 ± 0.5 °C and 50% relative humidity, with a standard photoperiod of 12 h light and 12 h dark. This temperature was chosen to provide optimal environmental conditions for the pupation stage, whereas the 36.5 °C temperature used for subsequent generations was intended to mimic the host’s body heat during the larval feeding stage. In this environment, the larvae underwent metamorphosis, and from the ninth day after their deposition in the incubator, the emergence of adult flies from the pupae occurred. After the emergence of the flies from the pupae, the adult insects were transferred to a sanitized rearing cage maintained under controlled environmental conditions. The adult insects remained in rearing cages measuring 35 × 35 cm2 in a room with controlled temperature at 27 °C, ambient humidity at 60%, and a photoperiod of 12-h light and 12-h dark.

The diet of the adult insects consisted of grated sugarcane and drinking water. Grated sugarcane was specifically chosen over granulated sugar because, under the rearing room temperature (27 °C), granulated sugar tends to liquefy, creating a sticky trap that causes fly mortality. The grated sugarcane provides a stable, solid carbohydrate source, allowing for safe and efficient feeding by the flies. The adult insects were kept under these conditions until the end of their life cycle, when their death occurred naturally. In Brazil, insects are not considered laboratory animals, and thus, this study was not evaluated by an Animal Ethics Committee. However, all recommendations of Directive No. 86/609/EEC on the protection of invertebrate animals used for experimental and other scientific purposes were followed in the planning and development of the experimental activities.

Seven days after the emergence of the adult insects from their pupae, the artificial oviposition process of the insects was carried out, aiming to continue the colony in a new endogamous generation derived from wild larvae. The artificial oviposition process was performed by inserting a Petri dish containing portions of 5 g of ground beef brushed with 2 mL of fresh ovine blood inside the cage containing the adult insects. Ovine blood was used specifically due to its practical availability and ease of access for our research team. The meat and blood substrate was preheated to approximately 37 °C in an incubator immediately before being offered to the females. The blowflies laid eggs on the prepared meat portions. The egg masses were then collected and directed into a 150 mL plastic container containing approximately 50 g of a mixture of fresh ground bovine meat and artificial liquid diet. The artificial liquid diet was prepared by dissolving 60 g of powdered whole blood, 20 g of powdered bovine milk, and 20 g of powdered chicken egg in drinking water to a final volume of 1 L, to which 1 mL of formalin was then added. This mixture was prepared at a ratio of approximately 2:1 (w/v), combining 2 kg of meat for every 1 L of liquid diet. After 48 h of staying in this initial container, the developing larvae were transferred to a second plastic container with a capacity of 500 mL containing around 250 g of the same mixture of ground bovine meat and the previously described artificial liquid diet. This container with C. hominivorax eggs, fresh meat, and artificial liquid diet was then placed in a B.O.D. incubator set to maintain a temperature of 36.5 ± 0.5 °C and 60% relative humidity (h0). The hatching of first-stage larvae (L1) occurred approximately 24 h after the egg incubation process (h24). After 48 h of staying in this initial container, the developing larvae were transferred to a second plastic container with a capacity of 500 mL containing around 250 g of the mixture of ground beef and artificial liquid diet. At this moment (h48), the larvae were transitioning to their second larval development stage (L2). This container was again placed in a B.O.D. incubator set to maintain a temperature of 36.5 ± 0.5 °C and 60% relative humidity. This container accommodated the larvae until they reached the third larval development stage (L3), which occurred approximately 24 h after transfer to this second container (h72). The L3 remained in this container, feeding on the artificial substrate and increasing their size and weight for another 48 h (h96 and h120). Larval rearing density was carefully managed throughout development to ensure adequate food availability and minimize competition. Initially, approximately 500 eggs were incubated in each plastic container. Upon hatching, the first-instar larvae (L1) were transferred to a second plastic container, and the density was equalized to approximately 1000 L1 per plastic container. Finally, upon reaching the third instar (L3), the larvae were transferred to a final plastic container where the density was reduced to approximately 300 L3 per container. This final reduction in density accounted for the increased substrate consumption by the larger L3 larvae, ensuring that competition was not a confounding factor in their morphometric development. Upon reaching the pre-pupal stage (h120), the pre-pupal larvae were then removed from the container and placed in 500 mL glass jars containing a layer of 3 cm of sterile vermiculite. The openings of the jars were covered with voile fabric fragments held by elastics. These jars were then placed in a B.O.D. incubator set to maintain a temperature of 26.5 ± 0.5 °C and 50% relative humidity. The pupation period lasted approximately 9 days. After emergence, the adult insects were transferred to a sanitized rearing cage maintained under the previously described environmental conditions and containing the same diet as reported earlier.

Experimental design

This study consists of a set of different experiments that analyzed the variation of characteristics related to the development, reproductive capacity, hatchability, larval survival, and life expectancy of C. hominivorax specimens reared in the laboratory over the course of 12 endogamous generations. The same experiments were repeated over the generations with the aim of capturing differences related to the increase in endogamy in the genetic composition of the insect colony and its adaptation to captivity.

The assays to evaluate reproductive capacity, egg hatchability, and larval survival used the methods described in Bricarello et al. (2021), and were performed in the F2, F5, F7, F10, and F12 generations. In each assay, one group was exposed to a negative control (water) to ensure the insects maintained their viability under laboratory conditions. Ten repetitions were performed for each treatment in every assay.

Development of specimens

For the experiment that analyzed the development of the specimens, 30 larvae were collected at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h after egg incubation (h0); 30 pupae with 5 days of pupation, and 30 adult flies (15 males and 15 females) in each of the 12 generations of the colony were followed in the study. The morphometric evaluation of the specimens was performed using standard laboratory procedures. This study establishes the morphological patterns of the different developmental stages of medically and veterinarily important dipterans, including C. hominivorax. The measured variables were weight in grams (g), length and width in millimeters (mm), and body area in square millimeters (mm2). Weight was measured on an analytical precision balance with four zeros after the decimal point. Length and width were measured using A4-sized millimeter paper (210 × 297 mm) while being observed under a direct light stereomicroscope. The area was calculated by multiplying the length and width variables in MS Excel® v.2019 software. Gender discrimination was performed for the group of adult specimens.

Reproductive capacity

The assay consisted of exposing a group of 15 fertilized adult flies to indirect contact with water and observing its effect on the ability of fertilized blowflies to lay eggs.

To carry out this experiment, experimental apparatuses were manually fabricated by our research team using Falcon® tubes with a capacity of 50 mL containing a wad of cotton wrapped in gauze, fixed at the bottom of the tube. This device served as a substrate for fixing the substances tested. At the opposite end, a portion of 2 g of beef was fixed and brushed with fresh ovine blood. After the construction of this equipment, a quantity of 1 mL of the tested substance was dispensed over the cotton wad, and then a group of 15 healthy female flies, 7 days old, were confined in this apparatus. This age was chosen because it represents the peak fertility of C. hominivorax under laboratory conditions (Hightower et al. 1972). The devices were then closed with a piece of voile fabric secured by elastic at the opening and were placed in a B.O.D. incubator adjusted to maintain a temperature of 36.5 ± 0.5 °C and 60% relative humidity. The flies remained in this condition for 1 h, after which the assay result was evaluated by counting the number of egg masses (n) that occurred in each tube and by weighing (mg) the total egg masses collected from each apparatus. The experimental unit considered was the set of 15 flies in one apparatus.

Hatchability

This assay consisted of subjecting a group of 15 C. hominivorax eggs to direct contact with water and observing its effect on the hatching rate of first-stage larvae 24 h after this contact.

To perform this experiment, cylindrical glass bottles without lids, designed specifically for this purpose (9 cm in height × 4 cm in diameter), were used. Initially, a Whatman® 125 mm diameter filter paper disk was deposited at the bottom of each bottle. A group of 15 C. hominivorax eggs from flies 7 days old were fixed on each of the filter paper disks with the aid of blunt-tipped forceps and a binocular stereomicroscope. One milliliter of the tested substances (water or trichlorfon) was sprayed onto each disk. Subsequently, these devices were placed in a B.O.D. incubator adjusted to maintain a temperature of 36.5 ± 0.5 °C and 60% relative humidity. The devices remained in this condition for 24 h. After this period, the hatching rate of the eggs in each device was measured using a binocular stereoscope.

The mean mortality rate of each treatment was calculated by averaging the sum of the individual mortality rates of each bottle, weighted by the number of repetitions of the treatment. Each device containing a group of 15 eggs after the application of one of the tested products was considered an experimental unit.

Larval survival

This assay involved subjecting a group of 15 third-stage larvae, 120 h after the incubation of C. hominivorax eggs, to direct contact with water and directly observing its effect on the emergence rate of adult flies nine days after this contact.

To conduct this assay, a cylindrical glass bottle without a lid, specially developed for this purpose (9 cm in height × 4 cm in diameter), was used. Initially, a Whatman® 125 mm diameter filter paper disk was placed at the bottom of the bottle. Then, a group of 15 third-stage larvae of C. hominivorax was deposited onto this filter paper, and 1 mL of the tested substance was sprayed directly onto the larvae. After the treatment application, the larvae were covered with a portion of 4 g of sterile vermiculite. Each bottle was covered with voile fabric secured with elastic at the opening of the bottle. The bottles were then placed in B.O.D. incubators adjusted to maintain a temperature of 26.5 ± 0.5 °C and 60% relative humidity for 9 days. After this period, the emergence rate of adult insects was evaluated. Each bottle containing a group of 15 larvae after the application of one of the substances was considered an experimental unit. The mean mortality rate of each treatment was calculated by averaging the sum of the individual mortality rates of each experimental unit, weighted by the number of repetitions of each treatment.

Adult insect life expectation

The life expectancy of adult specimens was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. This method is based on the analysis of the conditional probability that the dipteran is alive on the day of its death, which is the probability of surviving until the known moment of its death. For this analysis, we considered that each adult specimen began its life expectancy on the day it emerged from the puparium. The deaths of adult flies were recorded daily in spreadsheets as part of the laboratory routine and served as the basis for survival analyses.

Statistical analysis

The variation in all datasets collected during the experiments was statistically analyzed as follows: Initially, descriptive statistics were used to summarize and organize the data. To assess the impact of multi-generational rearing on the development and viability of C. hominivorax, different statistical models were employed depending on the nature of the data.

The morphometric variables (weight, length, width, and body area) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). This approach was chosen to properly account for the longitudinal nature of the data across generations and the non-independence of measurements. In these models, “developmental stage,” “generation,” and their interaction (“stage*generation”) were treated as fixed effects, while “replicate” was included as a random effect. To account for allometric effects, body length was used as a covariate when analyzing other size variables. Additionally, to address the sexual dimorphism in adults, as suggested by the reviewers, an overall comparison of weight and body area between males and females was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

For the biological assay data (reproductive capacity, egg hatchability, and larval survival), which now only include the negative control group, variations across the F2, F5, F7, F10, and F12 generations were also analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. In this case, “generation” was the fixed effect, and “replicate” was the random effect. Egg hatchability and larval survival rates were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

The life expectancy of adult specimens was analyzed using survival analysis. Survival curves were constructed for each of the 12 generations (with discrimination by sex) using the Kaplan–Meier method. To compare the overall survival distributions among all generations, the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test was employed. This test was also used to assess the overall difference in survival curves between males and females across all generations. To specifically identify from which generation the life expectancy began to significantly decrease, pairwise comparisons between the survival curves of different generations were conducted using the log-rank test with a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

All analyses were performed using IBM® SPSS v.25 software, and a statistical significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted for all tests.

Results

Development of insects reared in laboratory

The linear mixed-effects model applied to the morphometric data confirmed a highly significant effect of the developmental stage (F = 4323.5, p < 0.001) and the interaction between stage and generation (F = 1.6, p < 0.001) on insect weight, with similar significant results for all other morphometric variables (length, width, and area). This indicates that the growth pattern across developmental stages was not uniform across all generations. However, the isolated effect of generation was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting an overall stability in size for any given stage across the 12 generations.

Comparison of the survival curves revealed a statistically significant decline in the longevity of adult insects across the generations. Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate the growth and development dynamics of the different larval stages/instars of C. hominivorax over 12 inbred generations, detailing the variations in morphological variables both intragenerationally (comparing the variation of a variable within the same generation, e.g., weight variation according to different developmental stages) and intergenerationally (comparing the behavior of this variable among different generations, e.g., weight variation of the same developmental stage over the different generations analyzed).

The variation in size and weight of the instars within the same generation reveals distinct growth patterns that provide important insights into the biology of this insect (Davidowitz et al. 2003; Yurk and Powell 2010; Gentile et al. 2021), corresponding to what is expected for the growth of holometabolous insects (Yurk and Powell 2010; Hoffmann and Ross 2018). The data show that growth from L1 to L2 is relatively slight, indicating a smooth transition between these initial stages. However, the transition from L2 to L3 is marked by much more pronounced growth. Specifically, larval weight increases from 3 mg in L2 to 33 mg in L3, while body area grows from 6 to 36 mm2. This substantial growth suggests that the L3 stage is a critical period of development where larvae accumulate significant energy reserves in preparation for pupation. Larvae at the L3 stage continue to grow significantly over time (Hoffmann and Ross 2018). The data indicate that, in the pre-pupal L3 stage (h 120), larval weight can reach 65 mg and body area can reach 50 mm2. This continuous growth during the L3 stage is crucial for accumulating the energy needed for metamorphosis and subsequent development into pupae and adults (Crystal 1970; Davidowitz et al. 2003). As larvae transition from the L3 stage to pupae, they experience a decrease in weight and size. This phenomenon is likely related to the loss of water from larval tissues, a common process during pupation in many insects. The loss of water may be necessary for the internal reorganization of tissues as larvae transform into pupae, a crucial stage in complete metamorphosis (Crystal 1970; Spates and Hightower 1970; Davidowitz et al. 2003).

Another relevant finding of the study is the significant difference in size and weight between male and female adults of C. hominivorax. Males are, on average, significantly larger and heavier than females. This sexual dimorphism can be attributed to evolutionary and natural selection factors, where traits such as larger size may confer competitive advantages to males in terms of reproduction and survival (Crystal 1970; Spates and Hightower 1970; Davidowitz et al. 2003).

The growth patterns observed across the different instars of C. hominivorax have important and actionable implications for its control. The pronounced biomass gain during the transition from L2 to L3, highlighting this stage's importance for energy reserve accumulation (Crystal 1970; Hoffmann and Ross 2018), identifies L3 as a prime target. Control interventions, such as larvicides or insect growth regulators, could be timed to coincide with the peak L3 population to maximize impact before pupation. Furthermore, the continued growth capacity of L3 larvae until the pre-pupal stage underscores the need for management strategies that are robust to temporal variations in larval development, perhaps by utilizing agents with longer residual activity or by implementing careful surveillance to guide application timing.

The physiological changes during the transition to pupation, including weight and size loss potentially linked to water management (Crystal 1970), also offer avenues for control. While environmental manipulation of pupation sites (e.g., altering soil moisture) presents challenges for widespread field application, understanding these sensitivities can inform strategies for more localized control or for enhancing the production of high-quality insects in laboratory settings for research or SIT programs.

The significant differences in size and weight between adult males and females, with males typically being larger, directly pertain to the efficacy of control methods like the sterile insect technique (SIT). The competitiveness of sterile males is a cornerstone of SIT success, and male size can be a determinant of mating success. Rearing protocols for SIT should therefore aim to produce males of optimal size and vigor, for which our morphometric data provide a useful baseline. Monitoring these characteristics in mass-reared insects is crucial for quality control in SIT programs.

The data indicate that the height, width, length, weight, and body area of the different larval developmental stages remained stable over the 12 inbred generations analyzed. This finding is corroborated by the Supplementary Tables accompanying the article (Supplementary Material Files), which provide the mean values of weight, height, width, and body area for all the different instars of the insect, separated by generation. The observed stability suggests that C. hominivorax has a robust ability to adapt to laboratory rearing conditions, maintaining consistent physical development even under inbreeding conditions.

Despite the stability in physical development, there was a significant decrease in the life expectancy of adult flies after the fifth inbred generation. Statistical analysis using the Log-Rank test indicated that this difference was statistically significant (χ2 = 338.257, df = 11, p < 0.001). This finding suggests that although laboratory rearing conditions can support the physical development of the insects, prolonged inbreeding may negatively impact other aspects of insect biology, such as longevity. This effect is similar to that already described in other studies (Peterson et al. 1983; Cunha-e-Silva and Milward-de-Azevedo 1996). To mitigate the negative effects of inbreeding, it is essential to periodically introduce new genetic material into the population. This can be achieved through the incorporation of wild-caught individuals or by mixing individuals from different laboratory colonies. Maintaining genetic diversity within laboratory populations is not only crucial for the health and viability of the insects but also for the reliability and applicability of experimental results (Sørensen et al. 2012). Ensuring a genetically diverse population can help produce more consistent and generalizable findings, particularly when studying traits related to fitness, behavior, and physiological responses.

Another interesting finding was the difference in adult insect survival related to sex, as already described in other studies (Peterson et al. 1983). On average, males lived 6 days less than females across all generations analyzed. This difference may be related to intrinsic biological and physiological factors, where females may possess more effective cellular repair mechanisms or resource allocations that favor greater longevity compared to males. This pattern of greater female longevity relative to males is observed in several insect species. From a control perspective, this differential longevity has several implications. For instance, in programs utilizing the sterile insect technique (SIT), the shorter lifespan of males—the sex typically sterilized and released—means that the effective period for these sterile males to mate with wild females is inherently limited. This could influence the required frequency and intensity of sterile male releases needed to achieve adequate suppression of the wild population. Furthermore, accurate demographic parameters, including sex-specific lifespans, are crucial for developing robust population dynamics models. These models are often employed to predict pest population growth, assess the potential impact of various interventions, and optimize the timing and scale of control efforts. A shorter male lifespan, compared to the egg-laying female, impacts the operational sex ratio over time and the overall reproductive output of a population, factors that must be accurately parameterized in such models to guide effective pest management strategies.

Regarding reproductive capacity, larval survival, and egg hatchability, the results indicate that laboratory rearing does not significantly affect any of these variables. Analyses showed that the adult emergence rate, egg hatchability, and larval survival remained consistent across generations. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of insecticide efficacy testing, which aims to develop innovative products for pest control (Isman 2017), especially in the specific context of the blowfly C. hominivorax (Nogueira et al. 2020). The stability of these variables suggests that the laboratory rearing methods employed are adequate for maintaining healthy colonies, providing a reliable basis for efficacy experiments of new products.

The results of this study are highly relevant for developing pest control strategies. Laboratory rearing of insects is a fundamental technique for producing sterile insects that are released in infested areas to control wild fly populations through the sterile insect technique (SIT) (Crystal and Whitten 1976; Mastrangelo et al. 2011; Concha et al. 2016). The observed stability in insect development over inbred generations suggests that it is possible to maintain healthy and effective colonies for pest control programs. However, the decrease in adult life expectancy after the fifth inbred generation and the difference in survival between males and females highlight the need to monitor and possibly mitigate the effects of prolonged inbreeding on the colonies. In addition to the implications for pest control, the findings of this study contribute to a broader understanding of the biology and ecology of C. hominivorax. The maintenance of stability in the physical development of insects under laboratory rearing conditions highlights the species’ resilience and the effectiveness of the rearing methods employed. However, the decrease in adult life expectancy and the difference in survival between males and females underscore the importance of considering longevity as a critical factor in management and conservation practices, especially in public health and agriculture contexts. The stability in reproductive capacity, larval survival, and egg hatchability suggests that the rearing methods are adequate and do not compromise the effectiveness of insects in laboratory experiments.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the continuous laboratory rearing of C. hominivorax over multiple generations does not significantly affect the physical development of specimens, reproductive capacity, larval survival, or egg hatchability, but it results in a significant decrease in adult life expectancy after the fifth inbred generation. Additionally, a consistent difference in survival between males and females was observed, with males living shorter lives than females. These results provide valuable insights that can improve laboratory rearing practices and contribute to the development of more effective and sustainable pest control strategies. The continuation of this line of research is essential for optimizing management techniques and ensuring that insects reared in the laboratory maintain their effectiveness when released into natural environments.
