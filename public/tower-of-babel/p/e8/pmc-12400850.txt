Percutaneous CT-guided Onyx embolization of CSF-venous fistula: illustrative case

Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is an important cause of headache and may lead to a range of symptoms with variable severity, including orthostatic headaches that worsen with standing, tinnitus, vertigo, vision changes, cognitive impairment, or even coma.1–3 CSF-venous fistulas (CVFs) are a direct pathological connection between a spinal nerve root and the epidural venous plexus and an increasingly recognized cause of SIH.4,5

In patients with SIH radiographic findings on brain MRI may include pachymeningeal enhancement, brain sag with effacement of the suprasellar and prepontine cisterns, narrowing of the mamillopontine distance, venous engorgement, and subdural collections.6 Spinal MRI may demonstrate an epidural fluid collection or other findings that may suggest CSF leakage, such as meningeal diverticula, or osteodiscogenic microspurs; however, CVFs do not typically cause extrathecal fluid collections and are often occult on spinal MRI.7 Conventional CT myelography, MR myelography with intrathecal gadolinium, and heavily T2-weighted MRI sequences8 may improve the diagnostic yield of spinal MRI for SIH but have limited diagnostic yield in identifying CVFs.9 The “hyperdense paraspinal vein” sign on conventional CT myelography may be a marker of CVF.10 If a longitudinal extradural CSF collection is seen on spinal MRI, this may help guide subsequent diagnostic imaging.11 Definitive diagnosis of a CVF and localization of the fistulous site require direct visualization.9,12 Digital subtraction myelography, dynamic myelography, or dynamic CT myelography with a tilt table in the lateral decubitus position and thin-cut cross-sectional images may improve the diagnostic yield.9,12

Treatment options for CVFs include surgical ligation,13 epidural blood patch,14 CT-guided fibrin glue occlusion,15 and transvenous embolization.16 Transvenous embolization of CVFs using Onyx (Medtronic Inc.) has gained increasing popularity since it was first described in 2021 by Brinjikji et al.,17 due to its high rate of efficacy compared with epidural blood patch and fibrin glue, with up to 95% of patients experiencing substantial or complete symptom relief after a single treatment.18 Both epidural blood patch and fibrin glue are thought to work by compressing the fistula, while transvenous embolization may directly obliterate the CVF.19 Furthermore, transvenous embolization has a low complication rate and is well tolerated.18,19 The use of Onyx, a liquid embolic agent, offers several advantages in treating CVFs. The nonadhesive nature of Onyx and gradual solidification allow for controlled delivery and penetration of the fistulous network, and its radiopacity enables real-time visualization during injection.20

Although transvenous access to the paraspinal veins and the CVF is typically uncomplicated, there may be some cases in which transvenous access is difficult and a previously identified CVF may not be reached with a microcatheter that would support embolization. In those cases, direct CT-guided puncture of the epidural veins and subsequent liquid embolic injection to obliterate the CVF may be a feasible option with similar efficacy and safety to transvenous embolization. However, direct percutaneous access to the CVF followed by Onyx embolization is a technique that has not been previously described in the literature. Here, we present a novel case of CT-guided puncture of the epidural veins involved in a CVF with liquid embolic injection to obliterate a CVF in a paraspinal vein that was difficult to navigate transvenously.

Illustrative Case

A 67-year-old male Jehovah’s Witness presented with several months of severe positional headaches following minor head trauma and whiplash injury. The patient described the pain as 9 of 10 in intensity, worse with standing upright, walking, or shaking his head, and resolved when lying flat. He noted that driving was painful and that he felt wobbly and/or dizzy when standing up quickly. He reported drinking a cup of coffee daily, but this did not affect his symptoms. MRI of the brain demonstrated smooth pachymeningeal enhancement, subdural FLAIR hyperintensity, a chiasm-sellar distance of 3 mm, a prepontine distance of 3 mm, venous distension, and a mamillopontine distance of 7 mm consistent with intracranial hypotension, and the patient’s Bern score was 7, suggesting a high probability of a CSF leak. Complete spinal MRI did not demonstrate any overt CSF leak or extrathecal CSF collection, but some evidence of degenerative spine disease was noted.

The patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position and underwent fluoroscopy-guided injection of 10 mL of omnipaque (iohexol) 300 mg/mL into the intrathecal space using a 22-gauge × 3.5-inch Quincke spinal needle at the L2–3 spinal level. The opening pressure was 9 cm H2O. A dynamic CT whole-spine myelogram was subsequently obtained using a tilt table. The patient remained in the right lateral decubitus position and was transported from fluoroscopy to CT, with CT imaging performed on a photon counting CT scanner. Imaging was performed with three phases of respiration: resistive inspiration, end inspiration, and valsalva.

Several perineural cysts were identified in the lower cervical and thoracic spine with mild bone spurring. A CVF was identified at the right T4 nerve root with associated tubular enhancement extending out of the right T4 neural foramen into the paraspinal vein, with anterior and posterior venous communications (Fig. 1). No other definite fistula was identified.

The risks and benefits of different treatment options, including conservative management, epidural blood patch, CT-guided fibrin glue occlusion, surgical ligation, and endovascular transvenous Onyx embolization were discussed with the patient. The patient declined epidural blood patch due to his religious beliefs, and informed consent for transvenous embolization was obtained. The patient was brought to the interventional neuroradiology suite and positioned supine under general anesthesia for diagnostic spinal venography with planned transvenous embolization with Onyx. Venous access was obtained through the right common femoral vein, and the catheter system was carefully navigated into the azygous vein. An attempt was made to identify the right superior intercostal vein, which emerges from the superior aspect of the arch of the azygous vein, but this strategy was unsuccessful. Next, the catheters were navigated into the right T6 paraspinal vein in order to approach the fistula from below via the intersegmental veins and the lateral epidural venous plexus. However, multiple attempts to navigate the spinal venous system were unsuccessful. Therefore, the procedure was aborted.

Treatment options were again discussed with the patient. A percutaneous approach to the right T4 epidural venous plexus with direct CT-guided puncture and transvenous Onyx embolization of the CVF was discussed with the patient, and informed consent was obtained for the procedure. The patient was brought back to the interventional neuroradiology suite and positioned prone on the fluoroscopy table under general anesthesia. A rotational 3D syngo DynaCT (Siemens Medical) was obtained on the Siemens C-arm without contrast injection to identify the right T4 neural foramen. Three-dimensional guidance software was used to determine the optimal trajectory and identify the insertion site. An 18-gauge Chiba needle was advanced along this trajectory into the right T4 neural foramen until backfilling of blood was observed (Fig. 1). Contrast injection into the right T4 epidural venous plexus confirmed venous access. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)–compatible extension tubing was connected to the needle, and the system was flushed with 0.15 mL of DMSO. A 1-mL medallion syringe containing Onyx liquid embolic material was connected to the extension tubing, and Onyx 34 was then carefully injected into the right T4 epidural venous plexus under continuous fluoroscopic monitoring. Postprocedure imaging showed an extensive Onyx cast within the neural foramen (Fig. 2).

The patient tolerated the procedure well with no neurological deficits or complications noted. He was discharged home the following day with mild but significantly improved headaches. At 2-week and 8-month follow-ups, he reported complete resolution of his positional headaches.

Discussion

Here, we describe a novel approach to transvenous embolization of a CVF using CT-guided direct puncture of the epidural venous plexus and direct transvenous Onyx embolization of the CVF in a paraspinal vein that was difficult to access through the typical transazygous approach. This approach may offer an additional treatment option for cases that are difficult to access endovascularly while still offering the benefits of transvenous embolization of the CVF.

Observations

Previous studies have described the utility of CT-guided fibrin glue occlusion of CVFs.15 However, this strategy relies primarily on injecting fibrin glue into the neural foramen and compressing the CVF.19 Several groups have demonstrated the efficacy of transvenous embolization using Onyx liquid embolisate to obliterate the CVF.18,19,21 Brinjikji et al. reported significant improvement in headaches in more than 90% of patients undergoing transvenous embolization with Onyx in the largest single-center study to date.18,19 In a recent meta-analysis of 77 patients across 9 studies, Orscelik et al. found that 84.4% of patients undergoing transvenous embolization for CVF reported significant improvement or complete resolution of symptoms.22 Epidural blood patch is often an important first step in the management of patients with SIH.14 Even in patients without epidural collections on spinal MRI and evidence of CVF, epidural blood patches offer a favorable risk-benefit profile and are often pursued as a first interventional procedure, but many patients may require multiple blood patches, and a higher number of patients with CVF may not get relief even from repeated epidural blood patches.14 A recent study of fibrin glue injection for CVF found high rates of improvement in a cohort of 10 symptomatic patients; however, larger prior studies of SIH found complete resolution in only 59.7%, partial improvement in 34.5%, and no improvement in 5.9% of patients.23 Therefore, transvenous Onyx embolization remains a promising treatment option.

In the present case study, direct percutaneous puncture of the CVF was confirmed with CT-guided needle puncture, biplane fluoroscopy, and aspiration of dark venous blood. Onyx embolization was performed under continuous fluoroscopic guidance; however, there is a possibility that some, if not a majority, of the embolisate was injected epidurally and that obliteration of the CVF may have occurred due to extradural compression of the CSF leak and elimination of the potential space. Therefore, the procedure may work similarly to an epidural blood patch or fibrin glue occlusion compared with the transvenous Onyx embolization. In either case, the embolization appears durable, and the Onyx is a more permanent and robust embolisate compared with the fibrin glue, which typically is resorbed after several weeks.24,25 Further investigation is required to determine whether the intervention is both an effective and durable solution.

The indication for direct CT-guided percutaneous access to the CVF for Onyx embolization in this case was the difficult venous anatomy and inability to access the foraminal vein at the level of interest. Indeed, Borg et al. reported that 16.7% of CVFs in their series were found between T1 and T4 and were particularly difficult to embolize due to the anatomical orientation of the origin of the superior intercostal vein on either side.26 When cannulation of the superior intercostal vein is not possible, alternate strategies to approach these CVFs include approaching the fistula through the lateral epidural venous plexus via the cervical veins from above or through the intersegmental veins via the azygous (or accessory hemiazygous vein) from below.26 Here, approaching the fistula via the azygous vein and the lateral venous plexus above and below the fistula was unsuccessful. Thus, we opted to pursue the direct puncture described in this case. Additional indications may include patients with persistent symptoms after previous transvenous Onyx embolization for treatment of their CVF, which may prohibit transvenous access for subsequent interventions. There is a theoretical risk of nerve root injury, neurological deficit, or pain associated with CVF embolization that may be higher with direct puncture and with stiffer and more durable embolic material such as Onyx; however, none of these complications have been reported with transvenous Onyx18,19 embolization or fibrin glue occlusion.15 There is a small risk of transvenous embolization to the lung with direct percutaneous Onyx embolization similarly to the transvenous Onyx embolization route. Pulmonary embolism with Onyx after transvenous embolization was reported in 7.5% of 40 cases in the study by Brinjikji et al., all of which were clinically silent.19

Lessons

In cases of SIH with an underlying CVF identified on imaging that may be difficult or impossible to access transvenously, percutaneous Onyx embolization may be an option for intervention that offers similar efficacy, safety, and durability compared with transvenous Onyx embolization. This case demonstrates the utility of percutaneous Onyx embolization as an alternative treatment when traditional transvenous access is not feasible.
