Repurposing licensed viral vaccines as anti-cancer therapeutics: Turning cold tumors hot
Authors: Dorine A de Bont, Maud Reugebrink, Nadine van Montfoort, Vera Kemp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRising cancer incidences worldwide underline the need for effective and globally accessible therapies. Oncolytic viruses (OV), targeting tumor cells and stimulating anti-tumor immunity, are attractive anti-cancer agents but face challenges like stringent regulatory hurdles and excessive costs, which decelerate their global clinical implementation. Licensed viral vaccines for cancer therapy have emerged as a promising alternative to OVs, leveraging their well-established safety profiles and potential cost benefits. This review discusses the current understanding on their anti-cancer effects. Preclinical experiments, case studies and clinical trials highlight their anti-cancer potential and reveal underlying mechanisms. Similar to OVs, viral vaccines can transform “cold” tumor immune microenvironments into “hot,” synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors, and are not necessarily hampered by pre-existing immunity. Collectively, they represent a promising and readily available addition to cancer therapy. Further studies into optimizing efficacy, delivery, and synergy, as well as clinical evaluation are warranted to fully realize their anti-cancer potential.KEYWORDS: Viral vaccines, drug repurposing, cancer immunotherapy, oncolytic virotherapy, tumor immune microenvironment

Introduction

Cancer remains a critical global health challenge, ranking second as the leading cause of death worldwide according to the World Health Organization.1 The rising incidence of cancer underscores the urgent need for cost-effective and globally accessible therapeutic approaches to improve treatment outcomes.

In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer treatment by harnessing the patient’s immune system to target tumor cells.2 Immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has advanced cancer therapy by showing impressive clinical responses in a number of malignancies including melanoma and lung cancer. However, efficacy can be hampered by a lack of tumor-specific T-cells, low immune cell infiltration, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.3

Among various immunotherapeutic approaches, oncolytic virus (OV) therapy has shown considerable potential. OVs have the unique capacity to selectively infect and kill cancer cells while simultaneously stimulating the immune system to mount a robust anti-tumor response. This dual mechanism of action – specific oncolysis and immune stimulation – positions OV therapy as a compelling option for treating malignancies. Notably, in 2015, Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) became the first OV approved by the Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency for the treatment of advanced melanoma.4 Moreover, T-VEC and other OVs appear to have a promising synergistic effect in combination with ICI, by reshaping the “cold” tumor immune environment from a suppressive and desolate state toward an active (“hot”) state and enhancing systemic anti-tumor immunity.5,6

Despite its promise and favorable safety records, the clinical application of OVs faces significant challenges, including lengthy development times, high costs, and stringent regulatory hurdles associated with administering live-replicating viruses. Together, these challenges decelerate the clinical application of OVs.7 In response to these challenges, researchers are exploring alternative strategies that can deliver similar immunotherapeutic benefits without the complexities associated with OV development. One such strategy is the repurposing of approved viral vaccines for cancer therapy.8 Certain licensed vaccines contain viral strains with intrinsic oncolytic capacity and may therefore be directly repurposed as anti-cancer therapeutics without requiring further engineering into conventional OV products. Moreover, the success of prophylactic vaccines treating virus-associated cancers, such as HPV-induced malignancies, highlights the broader potential of viral vaccines in oncology and raises the question whether these licensed vaccines may also exert therapeutic benefit in tumors that are not virally induced.9 Originally designed to prevent infectious diseases, these vaccines have well-established safety profiles and can be produced at scale at low cost, making them attractive alternatives to OVs.

This review aims to explore the potential of leveraging approved viral vaccines to mimic the immunotherapeutic effects of OVs, with a focus on their ability to induce remodeling of the tumor immune landscape and to enhance cellular anti-tumor immunity. By examining both preclinical and clinical evidence supporting the use of these vaccines in cancer therapy, we investigate whether licensed vaccines can be repurposed as a cost-effective and accessible approach to improving cancer treatment outcomes.

Comparable mechanisms of action between OVs & viral vaccines

Key elements underlying the immunotherapeutic effect of OVs are their capacity to specifically infect and lyse malignant cells, to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and to enhance cellular anti-tumor immunity.10 Together, these mechanisms are able to convert immunologically ‘cold’ tumors into immune-active ‘hot’ tumors, which renders them more amenable to additional types of immunotherapy including ICI therapy. In the following sections, we will review the studies supporting that viral vaccines can mimic these anti-tumoral effects.

Direct oncolysis

OV therapy is based on the capacity of OVs to preferentially infect and lyse cancer cells while sparing normal cells.10 Infection and oncolysis have also been demonstrated in vitro across various human cancer cell lines for several marketed vaccines, including vaccines for MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), rotavirus, yellow fever (YF), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), Zika virus (ZIKV), and a live-attenuated dengue virus strain (DV).11–18 A commonality between these vaccines is that all of them are based on the live-attenuated platform, which are in principle still replication-competent to some degree,19 insinuating that these vaccines also partially retain their ability to infect and lyse cancer cells. For the live-attenuated YF vaccine, YF 17D, in vivo studies have confirmed that its direct oncolytic effect is dependent on the virus replication after repeated intratumoral injections, as UV-inactivated YF 17D did not delay the tumor growth.13 In clinical trial context, tumor infection and oncolytic effects have been described upon intratumoral injection of the attenuated measles vaccine strain, MV-EZ, in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma20 and recurrent breast cancer.21 Leveraging its in vitro established tropism for the CD46 receptor abundantly expressed on tumor cells,22 the vaccine strain can enter lymphoma cells inducing the formation of syncytia leading to syncytial apoptosis.20 Thus, it seems that direct oncolysis can play a role in the anti-tumor effects seen with some vaccines, in particular live-attenuated vaccines, resembling OV therapy.

Reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME)

Additional factors besides direct oncolysis appear to play key roles in the anti-tumor effects of viral vaccines, as even inactivated viruses elicit anti-tumor responses despite their incapacity to productively infect tumor cells. A major mechanism contributing to the success of OVs in cancer treatment seems to be their capacity to remodel the tumor immune landscape, leading to an environment favorable for anti-cancer immunity. Transforming the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) from an immunologically “cold” state – where there is little immune cell infiltration – into a “hot” state that favors anti-tumor immunity is one of the key elements underlying the mechanism of action of OVs. In vivo studies involving multiple OVs have demonstrated a transition from immunologically “cold” to “hot” tumors, characterized by the induction of an interferon stimulated-genes (ISG) signature, upregulation of PD-L1 and infiltration as well as activation of dendritic cells (DCs) and CD8+ T cells following treatment.23–26

Mechanistically, the RNA and DNA genomic structures of tumor-infecting viruses activate innate immune responses by triggering toll-like receptors (TLRs) and other pattern recognition receptors (PRRs).27 This activation induces ISGs in the tumor, eventually leading to the recruitment of immune cells. In addition, the oncolysis process triggers immunogenic cell death (ICD), release of tumor antigens and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). The viral genomic structures and the DAMPs together are sensed by freshly recruited conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) that are critical for the initiation of anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. DCs are specialized in capturing tumor antigens, processing them, and presenting them to CD8+ T cells in the local lymph nodes, through a process called cross-priming.7,10,13 After clonal expansion, tumor-specific T-cells migrate back to the tumor to eliminate the cancer cells.

This section explores the reported mechanisms of TIME remodeling by viral vaccines.

Viral vaccines have been shown capable of activating innate immune responses in the tumor. Aleynick et al. demonstrated that many common pathogen vaccines contain natural agonists of PRRs that activate murine and human cDCs via distinct mechanisms.28 Inactivated rotavirus vaccines can initiate type I interferon (IFN) signaling through RIG-I, a cytosolic PRR that senses double-stranded RNA.11 Intratumoral injection of inactivated modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA), a vaccine against smallpox and mpox, in murine melanoma and colon cancer models can induce a type I IFN response and anti-tumor immunity via STING.29 Intracerebral injection of the Zika virus vaccine in a mouse model of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) also induced strong innate immune activation, including the IFN, NF-κB, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathways. Moreover, they observed upregulated levels of specific cytokines, notably CXCL10, a chemokine that attracts CD8+ T cells and inhibits tumoral angiogenesis.16 Similarly, seasonal unadjuvanted influenza vaccines in vivo robustly stimulate TLR7, leading to reduction of tumor progression.30

Viral vaccines have also been shown to modulate the composition of the immune cells in the tumor in favor of anti-tumor immunity. In animal models of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colorectal cancer (CRC), intratumoral injection of the MMR vaccine led to an increase in CD8+ GranzymeB+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and a decrease in macrophage populations.31 For the rotavirus vaccine, no significant increase in CD8+ T cells has been observed but increases in CD8+ T-cell activation and pro-inflammatory myeloid cells were found within the TIME.11 For the YF and JEV vaccine, enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration was also observed in murine cancer models after intratumoral or intracerebral injection, respectively.13,17,32 The JEV vaccine specifically triggered a decrease in immune suppressive M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells, while the YF vaccine enhanced the infiltration of type 1 cDCs.13,17 Increased numbers of cDCs and CD8+ T cells in the tumor were also reported after intratumoral injection of influenza vaccines.33 Importantly, there was a difference between unadjuvanted vaccines and a vaccine that contained the squalene-based adjuvant, AddaVax. This adjuvant abrogated the anti-tumor effect of the influenza vaccine and changed the immune composition in the TIME in favor of regulatory B cells.

Building further on this preclinical work, a phase 1/2 clinical trial with intratumoral injection of an unadjuvanted seasonal influenza vaccine was conducted in patients with sigmoidal or rectal carcinoma scheduled for surgery. Besides safety, this study demonstrated enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in the tumors following intratumoral injection. Furthermore, upregulation of cytotoxicity associated genes like GZMA and CXCR4 and downregulation of anti-inflammatory associated genes like TGFB1 were noted. Additionally, spatial analysis of protein expression comparing pre- and post- intratumoral treatment indicated a decrease in FOXP3 levels, a marker for regulatory T cells prevalent in an immunosuppressive TIME.34 Taken together, as has been demonstrated for OVs, licensed vaccines can reshape the TIME toward a more immune-active phenotype that favors anti-tumor immunity.

Activation of cellular anti-tumor immunity

Viral vaccines have also been shown to contribute to enhanced cellular anti-tumor immune responses. Tumor-specific cellular immune responses, where T-cells specifically recognize the (neo)antigens of the tumor, are favorable because they can lead to immune memory and longlasting protection against recurrent tumors. Aleynick et al. demonstrated that a rationally designed mix of PRR agonists present in common pathogen vaccines can activate cDCs, thereby promoting intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration and cross-priming of tumor-neoantigen-specific T cells in vitro and in a murine lymphoma model.28 Furthermore, preclinical studies with YF and influenza vaccines have also demonstrated the priming of tumor-specific T cells after intratumoral administration.5,12

The induction of systemic cellular immunity is important to attack tumor cells and micrometastases at distant sites. A strong indication for the induction of systemic anti-tumor immunity is the regression of distant, non-injected tumors, as demonstrated for several OVs in preclinical and clinical studies.4,23,35–37 This phenomenon has also been observed for a number of approved viral vaccines, including the rotavirus vaccine, YF vaccine, and heat-inactivated MVA (hiMVA).11,13,29 HiMVA also protected against a tumor re-challenge, suggesting the presence of a durable tumor-specific immunity. Mechanistically, in the case of the YF, hiMVA and influenza vaccines, both CD8+ T cells and cDCs were found to be essential for tumor regression, highlighting the critical role of cellular immunity and cross-priming by DCs in the anti-tumor effect of these viral vaccines.13,29,33

Together, there is a growing body of evidence that intratumoral injection of viral vaccines can make cold tumors hot and promote the induction of systemic anti-tumor immunity, suggesting a potential synergy with immunotherapy, including ICIs.

Combinatorial therapeutic strategies

Based on the mechanisms of action discussed above, viral vaccines are attractive candidates to integrate with systemic immunotherapeutic approaches. Likewise, the combined administration of viral vaccines with ICIs is an appealing strategy, as was previously demonstrated for OVs.5,6 Preclinical models have demonstrated the potential of this approach, as shown in studies involving the influenza vaccine, live-attenuated MMR vaccine, rotavirus vaccine, JEV vaccine, and YF vaccine.11,17,33

Several clinical trials are now exploring the synergistic potential of intratumoral influenza vaccination combined with ICIs. A phase 2 study (NCT04591379) in 10 proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) sigmoid or rectal cancer patients demonstrated that intratumoral injection with an inactivated influenza vaccine increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression.34 A follow-up trial (NCT05909423) combines this vaccine with pembrolizumab in early pMMR colorectal cancer to assess enhanced cancer cell death.38 Furthermore, a phase 1 study (NCT04697576) investigates the combination of intralesional injection of an inactivated influenza vaccine with ICIs in melanoma.39,40

The recently published results from a phase 2 trial (NCT05116917) combining intramuscular flu vaccination with ICIs and radiation in pancreatic cancer indicated that this combination did not lead to objective responses.41 It is important to note that encouraging results from preclinical and early phase clinical trials does not always translate into clinical success. In particular, demonstrating superior clinical efficacy of ICI combination strategies over ICI monotherapy in a randomized clinical trial can be challenging, as was demonstrated for example in the MASTERKEY-265 study, evaluating talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) combined with pembrolizumab vs. placebo + pembrolizumab in 692 patients with advanced melanoma. Despite encouraging results in the early phases and a median survival of 14.3 of the combination vs. 8.5 months for the control group, the randomized controlled trial failed to meet its primary endpoints, defined as superior progression-free survival or overall survival. One of the explanations was the high efficacy of the monotherapy arm in this particular study. In anticipation of results of the other ongoing trials, it remains exciting whether the combination approaches with viral vaccines will demonstrate clinical benefit in patients with cancer.

Mode of administration

In most studies discussed above, viral vaccines were injected directly into tumors, which is not always possible in patients, depending on the location of the tumor. This raises the question of whether other routes, such as intramuscular, subcutaneous, or systemic intravenous injections, could also result in anti-cancer effects. Here, we discuss data from preclinical animal studies and human case studies that shed light on this.

Intramuscular injection of the MeV vaccine prevented tumor formation and eradicated established MeV-hemagglutinin protein-expressing tumors in vivo. This effect was linked to a reduction in myeloid derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells, alongside an increase in IFNγ, TNFα, and granzyme B-producing CD8+ T cells.42 For the influenza vaccine, perioperative intramuscular administration in mice following surgical resection of solid tumors to prevent postoperative metastasis demonstrated enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity against the tumor.43

Intramuscular and subcutaneous administration of the rabies vaccine has been associated with tumor regression in melanoma, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.44–46 Existing literature suggests that the rabies vaccine may enhance current immunotherapies by promoting a shift from “cold” to “hot” tumors, fostering a more immunologically active TIME. However, no robust clinical trials or direct evidence supporting this mechanism have been reported to date.46

Several case studies from SARS-COV2 vaccines; Moderna, AstraZeneca and the Chinese Sinovac vaccines, have demonstrated a beneficial association between intramuscular and subcutaneous vaccination and tumor regression.47–49 In addition, for the Moderna vaccine, histological and immunophenotypical analyses of tumor samples pre- and post-vaccination of the patient revealed augmented immune cell infiltration, in particular CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, DCs, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and B cells. Concurrently, reductions were observed in immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells, anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, and cancer-associated fibroblasts.49 Similarly, subcutaneous injection of the AstraZeneca vaccine resulted in increased tumor infiltrating CD8+ T cells as well.47 These reports are further substantiated by a recent study showing that SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines administered via intramuscular injection can sensitize anti-tumor immunity. Mechanistically, experiments in mice showed that intramuscular injection of mRNA vaccine resulted in elevated IFNα levels, generation of tumor-antigen-specific T cells in the spleen and upregulation of PD-L1 on tumor cells and circulating immune cells. Elevated levels of IFNα, increased PD-L1 expression on myeloid cells and activated T cells were also found in blood of 5 humans after SARS-CoV2 mRNA vaccination. In retrospective cohorts of human subjects, receipt of SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine within 100 d prior to start of immune checkpoint therapy was associated with prolonged median survival. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that viral vaccines administered via intramuscular, intravenous or subcutaneous routes can exert anti-cancer effects via promoting systemic anti-cancer immunity.26

Contrastingly, as discussed above, intramuscular injection of an influenza vaccine, as part of a combinatorial approach with ICI and radiotherapy in patients with pancreatic cancer, did not show clinical benefit.41 However, it is difficult to attribute the lack of clinical benefit only to the route of administration, since this was not systematically investigated in this clinical trial. Interestingly, a preclinical study with the seasonal unadjuvanted influenza vaccine demonstrated that intratumoral but not intramuscular administration reduced tumor growth in a melanoma mouse model.33 Similarly, systemic administration of a rotavirus vaccine, in combination with anti-CTLA-4 therapy, failed to achieve the same tumor-reducing effects as intratumoral delivery of the rotavirus vaccine.11 These findings suggest that intratumoral delivery is likely more effective in tumor reduction than systemic administration which may be explained by improved local tumor microenvironment remodeling and oncolysis vs. systemic activation of immune cells that need to migrate to the tumor site.

Taken together, while for most vaccines, intratumoral delivery of the viral vaccines has been demonstrated to induce optimal tumor-reducing effects, it has also been demonstrated that other routes of administration which are routinely used in prophylactic vaccination practice have the potency to induce systemic anti-tumor immune responses. As research continues, determining the most optimal route of vaccine administration will be central in optimizing this type of cancer treatment, taking into account the mode of action and practical considerations.

Discussion

In this review, we critically explored the use of approved viral vaccines for cancer treatment. The rationale was based on the therapeutic effect induced by clinically implemented OV therapy. To date, in vitro and in vivo preclinical studies have demonstrated the beneficial therapeutic effects of nine licensed viral vaccines (Table 1). Clinical trials and case reports with several vaccines are ongoing and have further highlighted the anti-tumor potential of influenza, MeV and rabies vaccines, emphasizing the broad applicability of these vaccines in oncology (Table 2). Furthermore, vaccines for MMR, YF, JEV, ZIKV, and rotavirus have shown strong potential for clinical translation due to their ability to reduce tumor growth in murine cancer models. These studies also provide critical mechanistic insights that will aid in refining the repurposing of viral vaccines within cancer treatment regimens.

Mechanisms of anti-tumor activity

As summarized in this work, viral vaccines can resemble oncolytic viruses in their main mechanisms of action as anti-cancer agents. Remodeling the tumor-microenvironment from cold to hot, as demonstrated by increased infiltration and activation of CD8+ T cells following vaccine administration, is one of the key mechanisms of action irrespective of the mode of administration. This raises the question whether the infiltrating CD8+ T cells are virus- or tumor-specific. Virus-specific T-cell responses frequently dominate over tumor-specific T-cell responses, since viruses are more immunogenic.51 Should the infiltrating CD8+ T cells demonstrate virus specificity, it would be favorable if the tumor cells present viral antigens and become target cells for the virus-specific T cells. Local high viral titers are needed to accomplish this, advocating for intratumoral administration as the preferred delivery route. Conversely, if the infiltrating CD8+ T cells exhibit tumor specificity, systemic or intramuscular delivery may already suffice for achieving a beneficial therapeutic effect as has been demonstrated for the SARS-CoV2 vaccines (Figure 1).

Preexisting immunity

One of the proposed determinants for effective OV therapy is the presence or absence of preexisting immune responses, either virus-specific neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) or virus-specific cellular responses. As recently reviewed, preexisting immunity against OVs has been shown to impede or enhance their therapeutic effects, depending on the virus and the mechanism of action.52 On the one hand, pre-existing nAbs may prevent infection of the tumor cells and circulating CD8+ T cells may recognize and eliminate infected cells before the virus completes its replication and oncolytic cycle.52 On the other hand, preexisting immunity may sustain or even enhance the immunotherapeutic effect of some OVs.53–55 A recent clinical study with the modified oncolytic HSV-1 CAN-3110 reported better survival in patients with preexisting virus-specific nAbs.56 For licensed viral vaccines in national vaccination programs, pre-existing immunity is prevalent. Importantly, studies have shown that pre-existing immunity does not affect the therapeutic effects of influenza, MMR, and rotavirus vaccines,11,50,57 and for YF, it even enhances tumor clearance through CD8+ T cell activity.38 Moreover, mice studies demonstrated that pre-existing immunity induced by the childhood polio vaccine enhanced the antitumor efficacy of subsequent virotherapy by intratumoral presentation of recall antigens.58 Furthermore, recently it has been demonstrated that pre-existing T cell immunity against the subunit HPV vaccine and mRNA SARS-CoV vaccine following intratumoral delivery of MHC-1 presented peptides.59 In conclusion, current data suggests that preexisting immunity, which is often abundant for childhood vaccines, does not necessarily impede therapeutic anticancer efficacy and may even enhance it. Nevertheless, this could be circumvented by using vaccines that are not in routine vaccination programs, like rabies, as only a small proportion of the population will possess nAbs.

Implementation

The strength of this approach lies in the direct repurposing of already licensed vaccines, leveraging their established safety profiles and existing manufacturing infrastructure. In contrast, conventional OV therapy often requires further engineering of viral strains with oncolytic capacity into novel cancer therapeutics before clinical application. Viral vaccines with oncolytic capacity described in this review, such as measles-based vaccines,12,14,20,21 may instead be directly repurposed as anti-cancer therapeutics, while benefiting from their extensive prior safety and clinical testing. Importantly, the well-established safety profiles of these vaccines may also support their use not only in advanced malignancies but also in pre-malignant and neoadjuvant settings, where low treatment-associated toxicities are critical.

Certain vaccines, such as seasonal influenza and SARS-CoV2 vaccines, are annually reformulated due to antigenic drift, which may partially compromise their “off-the-shelf” and cost-effective applicability. Nevertheless, since the anti-tumor effects of these vaccines appear to rely on broader immune activation and tumor immune microenvironment remodeling rather than strict virus-specific antigen recognition, previously licensed vaccine formulations may still retain anti-cancer potential.

Conclusion and future prospects

The integration of licensed viral vaccines into cancer therapy is promising. These vaccines have the potential to remodel the tumor immune landscape, promoting robust and systemic anti-tumor responses and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapies like ICIs. Preexisting immunity against these vaccines is not a barrier and may be leveraged for improved therapeutic outcomes. However, there is a significant gap in randomized clinical trials to validate these observations. Future trials should confirm the efficacy of viral vaccines across a range of cancers, fine-tune delivery methods, and explore combination treatment approaches. Repurposing licensed viral vaccines could offer a more accessible, cost-effective, and safe alternative to traditional oncolytic virus therapies.
