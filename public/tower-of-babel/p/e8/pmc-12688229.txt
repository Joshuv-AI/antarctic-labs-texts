Unmet needs and emerging therapeutics in low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma: from chemoresistance to precision medicine
Authors: Brian M Slomovitz, Vivek Podder, Erin Crane, Jubilee Brown
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLow-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC) is a rare and clinically distinct subtype of ovarian cancer, which presents unique challenges in diagnosis and treatment. LGSOC is characterized by low proliferation rates, high expression of hormone receptors, and frequent alterations in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. Initial standard treatment includes cytoreductive surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy and endocrine therapy. However, high recurrence rates (~80%) and poor responses to chemotherapy underscore the urgent need for new treatment strategies. Endocrine therapy is being investigated in the upfront setting, and recent advances in targeted therapies, including MAPK pathway inhibitors and investigational agents such as cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors, are shaping the evolving LGSOC treatment landscape. Notably, the recent US Food and Drug Administration approval of the avutometinib/defactinib combination for KRAS-mutated recurrent LGSOC marks a significant milestone in targeted therapy for this disease. Despite these advances, challenges remain in optimizing sequencing strategies and overcoming acquired resistance. This review addresses the importance of understanding the distinct pathophysiology of LGSOC, diagnostic challenges, limitations of conventional treatments, and evolving therapeutic approaches in LGSOC.KEYWORDS: Low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, MAPK pathway, CDK4/6 pathway, KRAS mutation, avutometinib, defactinib

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the leading cause of gynecologic cancer – related mortality worldwide [1]. In 2022, an estimated 324,603 women were diagnosed with ovarian cancer worldwide, and by 2050, that number is projected to approach half a million, an increase of 55%, with deaths expected to rise nearly 70%, underscoring the growing global burden of this disease [1]. In the United States, it is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women, accounting for approximately 2% of all cancer deaths [2,3]. In 2025, it is projected to affect 20,890 women and cause 12,730 deaths in the United States alone [3]. High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) is the most common epithelial ovarian cancer subtype, accounting for 70% to 80% of cases, followed by endometrioid carcinoma (10%) and clear cell carcinoma (10%) [4]. Rare subtypes include low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC; < 5%), mucinous carcinoma (3%), and ovarian carcinosarcoma (3%) [4,5]. LGSOC is a molecularly distinct entity, with an estimated incidence of 1,000 to 2,000 cases annually in the United States [6].

Due to its asymptomatic nature in early stages, most ovarian cancers are diagnosed at an advanced stage (III or IV), leading to a poor prognosis. LGSOC primarily affects younger women (median age, approximately 48 years) and is characterized by slow growth, high recurrence rates ( > 80%), and poor responsiveness to platinum-based chemotherapy [7–10]. The 5-year relative survival rate for all ovarian cancer is 51.6%, with stage-specific survival rates of 91.7% for early-stage, 70.7% for stage III, and 31.8% for stage IV disease [2,3]. In LGSOC, survival outcomes are more favorable in early-stage disease (88%–91%) but decline significantly in advanced stages, with a 5-year survival of 49% in stage III and 25% in stage IV disease [11]. The decline in survival outcomes for LGSOC in advanced stages compared with HGSOC suggests that advanced LGSOC should not be considered an indolent cancer and should be actively managed.

Surgical outcomes strongly affect prognosis in LGSOC, with complete gross resection at primary cytoreductive surgery associated with markedly improved survival (multivariable hazard ratio: 0.27) compared with macroscopic residual disease [12]. Patients with no residual disease show a 5-year overall survival (OS) of 73%, compared to 22% to 47% in those with residual disease; notably, minimal residuals ( < 10 mm) are still associated with better progression-free survival (PFS) and OS than suboptimal resection [11,13].

Secondary cytoreductive surgery also offers survival benefits. Median PFS was 60.3 months for patients achieving no gross residual disease after secondary cytoreductive surgery, compared with 10.7 months for those with residual disease (P=0.008). Median OS from diagnosis was 167.5 months versus 88.9 months, respectively (P = 0.10) [14].

Despite these clinical and biological differences, some current treatment strategies for LGSOC are largely adapted from those developed for HGSOC [6,15]. Molecular profiling has identified key pathways driving LGSOC, particularly alterations in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) pathways, which offer opportunities for targeted therapeutic approaches [9,16]. Agents initially developed for other malignancies – such as MAPK kinase (MEK) inhibitors for melanoma and CDK4/6 inhibitors for hormone receptor – positive breast cancer – are now under investigation in LGSOC, supporting a shift toward precision oncology [16–23]. Some therapies approved for other tumor types by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), such as MEK inhibitors and hormonal therapies, are part of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) treatment guidelines for recurrent LGSOC [15]. The MEKi trametinib is the only agent to date with positive late-phase (Phase 2/3) results, as demonstrated in the GOG 281/LOGS randomized controlled trial in LGSOC [15,19]. More recently, the combination of avutometinib and defactinib received accelerated approval by the FDA for the treatment of KRAS-mutated recurrent LGSOC [24].

This review outlines the unmet clinical need in LGSOC management and highlights molecularly driven strategies under investigation. It integrates the most current data, including recent FDA approvals, emerging targeted treatments such as MAPK and CDK4/6 inhibitors, and the expanding role of precision medicine and biomarkers, offering a timely and comprehensive synthesis that advances the current understanding of LGSOC.

Molecular landscape and pathophysiology of LGSOC

Building on its distinct clinical behavior and treatment resistance, LGSOC is characterized by a unique molecular profile that differentiates it from HGSOC. Often arising from serous borderline tumors, LGSOC exhibits limited responsiveness to cytotoxic chemotherapy in contrast to HGSOC [4,9]. Recent research suggests that LGSOC may originate from ovarian epithelial inclusions derived from fallopian tube epithelium, which may serve as a cell of origin, supporting a stepwise progression model that includes benign cystadenomas and borderline tumors, further distinguishing its pathogenesis [25,26].

Hormone receptor expression is a common feature of LGSOC. LGSOC shows estrogen receptor (ER) expression in the majority of cases (58%–96%) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression in approximately 50% of cases [9,27–29]. Although ER (80%–95%) and PR (20%–60%) are also expressed in HGSOC, the expression pattern in LGSOC, combined with observed clinical benefit, offers a stronger biological and therapeutic rationale for endocrine-based treatment of LGSOC [9,27–29]. The clinical efficacy observed with these agents has led to the use of endocrine therapy in the first-line maintenance setting [15].

The MAPK signaling pathway is another key driver in LGSOC pathogenesis [30]. Activating mutations in KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF are identified in 47% to 60% of LGSOC tumors, whereas these alterations are rare in HGSOC [9,17,31,32]. These mutations promote uncontrolled cell growth, proliferation, and survival, making MAPK inhibition a rational therapeutic approach [30,33]. Surprisingly, these oncogenic mutations, when present, confer an improved prognosis compared to patients whose tumors are wild-type for these oncogenes. In a retrospective cohort study of 79 patients with LGSOC, the presence of KRAS or BRAF mutations was associated with significantly improved OS compared to wild-type tumors (median OS: 106.8 vs 66.8 months; P = .018), with mutation status remaining independently significant on multivariable analysis [34]. These findings highlight the prognostic significance of MAPK pathway alterations in LGSOC [35].

Another distinguishing feature of LGSOC is the low frequency of TP53 mutations, which play a crucial role in DNA damage sensing and preservation of genomic stability. While TP53 mutations are present in over 95% of HGSOC cases, they are rare in LGSOC. Earlier reports suggesting rates up to 8% likely reflect an overestimation due to misclassification of HGSOC. In well-curated LGSOC cohorts, TP53 mutations are rare ( < 5%), and the p53 expression pattern in LGSOC is typically wild-type TP53, occasionally showing aberrancy in a small subset [6,26,36,37]. This distinct genomic profile supports the diagnostic utility of wild-type p53 expression as a hallmark of LGSOC.

Unlike HGSOC, which is characterized by genomic instability and high proliferative activity, LGSOC demonstrates higher genomic stability and a lower proliferation rate [7,8]. The clinical consequences of this indolent behavior include a generally limited response to conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy. These molecular differences underscore the necessity of differentiated therapeutic strategies for LGSOC.

Diagnosis and evolving molecular testing strategies in LGSOC

LGSOC typically presents with the absence of symptoms in its early stages, resulting in delayed diagnosis and frequent presentation at advanced stages (III or IV) [10,15,38]. In advanced disease, patients may present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal and pelvic discomfort, bloating, urinary urgency, or ascites [15]. Its rarity, slower progression, and histologic similarity to other epithelial ovarian cancers further complicate recognition and contribute to frequent misclassification [7,39,40]. One survey reported an average diagnostic delay of nearly 3 years among the 81% of patients with LGSOC experiencing symptoms, with 68% of the same patients reporting an initial misdiagnosis [41].

The NCCN clinical practice guidelines outline key diagnostic steps for LGSOC, including histopathologic evaluation and imaging modalities such as transvaginal ultrasound, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging [15]. Given its unique molecular profile, genetic testing and molecular profiling, including immunohistochemistry, are critical in differentiating LGSOC from HGSOC and in guiding treatment selection. While hereditary ovarian cancers account for approximately 5% to 24% of cases, most commonly involving BRCA1/2 mutations and Lynch syndrome genes, LGSOC is less frequently associated with inherited mutations or strong family histories and is not typically linked to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndromes [15,26,42–46]. In addition, somatic tumor testing is advised to detect mutations in genes such as KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, TP53, and BRCA, aiding in distinguishing LGSOC from HGSOC and informing optimal treatment selection [15,42,43].

Although circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a promising noninvasive tool for molecular profiling in patients with cancer, two recent studies illustrate limitations of detecting KRAS mutations in blood in patients with LGSOC. A prospective study from the Royal Marsden Foundation Trust evaluated 40 patients with LGSOC and identified MAPK pathway mutations – including KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, and NF1—in 45% of tumor samples via tissue-based sequencing [39]. Among patients with a KRAS mutation in tissue and ctDNA available (n = 12), a KRAS mutation was detected in ctDNA in 41% of them at the time of radiographic progression [47].

In the RAMP-201 trial cohort (n = 65), only 32% of patients had ctDNA tumor fractions above the limit of detection. Among those with KRAS mutations in tumor tissue, ctDNA testing detected the mutation in only 44%, while 56% yielded false-negative results [48]. These results highlight the low-shedding nature of LGSOC and the current limitations of blood-based ctDNA assays for reliably detecting KRAS mutations [48].

These data suggest caution in relying on ctDNA to rule out KRAS mutations and highlight the importance of LGSOC-specific testing and validation for clinical assays. Both studies indicate that LGSOC is a low-shedding tumor and that ctDNA has limited utility in detecting KRAS mutations in LGSOC. With currently available assays, false positives are uncommon, while false negatives are common.

Standard-of-care treatment and NCCN guideline recommendations in LGSOC

The initial standard-of-care treatment for LGSOC involves cytoreductive surgery aiming for complete gross resection given its significant survival advantage, followed by adjuvant treatment with platinum-based chemotherapy and/or endocrine therapy maintenance [12,15]. The extent of residual disease following debulking surgery remains the primary prognostic factor, particularly in predicting outcomes following first-line chemotherapy. The response to cytotoxic chemotherapy is generally poor in LGSOC [5,13]. Endocrine maintenance therapy with aromatase inhibitors is recommended to reduce the risk of disease progression and improve OS [15,49,50]. Although data supporting a survival benefit are limited, the NCCN guidelines endorse the addition of bevacizumab to adjuvant first-line platinum-based chemotherapy, followed by bevacizumab maintenance, for eligible patients with stage II – IV LGSOC (category 2A recommendation) [15].

In the recurrent setting, secondary cytoreductive surgery is recommended in cases where complete gross resection is achievable, given its potential to offer a benefit in both PFS and OS, along with MEK- and BRAF-targeted therapies based on molecular profiling, such as the avutometinib and defactinib combination, trametinib, dabrafenib (category 2A recommendations), and binimetinib (category 2B recommendation) [14,15,51]. The combination of avutometinib (rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma [RAF]/MEK clamp) and defactinib (focal adhesion kinase [FAK] inhibitor) was approved by the FDA on 8 May 2025, as treatment of recurrent LGSOC with known KRAS mutations and is the only treatment included in the NCCN guideline recommendations with an FDA-approved indication in LGSOC [15,24].

Limited role of chemotherapy

Adjuvant chemotherapy following primary cytoreductive surgery offers limited effectiveness in LGSOC [49,50,52]. Two retrospective cohort analyses found that adjuvant chemotherapy did not improve OS compared to surgery alone, even after adjusting for clinical confounders such as stage and residual disease after primary cytoreductive surgery [50,53]. Consistent with these findings, a separate retrospective analysis showed significantly lower response rates to platinum-based chemotherapy in LGSOC versus HGSOC (23.1% vs 90.1%; P < .001) in patients with residual disease after primary cytoreductive surgery [47]. Further research indicates that chemotherapy has limited efficacy in the recurrent setting as well, with overall response rates (ORRs) ranging from 0% to 13% and a median PFS of approximately 10.6 months [18,19,54]. These findings highlight the intrinsic chemoresistance of LGSOC, which is further supported by results from clinical trials such as GOG-281 and MILO. Both trials suggest that targeted therapies, particularly MEK inhibitors, offer more promising efficacy in the recurrent setting. Collectively, this body of evidence challenges the conventional use of cytotoxic chemotherapy for LGSOC and supports the development of more personalized therapeutic approaches.

Bevacizumab in first-line therapy

Despite limited evidence of survival benefit, bevacizumab has been recommended for first-line treatment of LGSOC, administered alongside platinum-based chemotherapy and/or continued as maintenance monotherapy [15]. This recommendation is largely based on data from broader ovarian cancer populations that included both HGSOC and LGSOC [15,55–57]. Bevacizumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets vascular endothelial growth factor, a key mediator of angiogenesis and tumor progression in epithelial ovarian cancer. Two pivotal Phase 3 trials – ICON7 and GOG-0218—evaluated the addition of bevacizumab to carboplatin – paclitaxel chemotherapy in patients with newly diagnosed advanced ovarian cancer [56,57]. Importantly, these trials did not stratify patients by histologic subtype. ICON7 demonstrated a modest improvement in median PFS, while GOG-0218 reported improved PFS with longer-duration bevacizumab (throughout treatment versus initiation-only) [55,56]. However, OS results were mixed: ICON7 showed improved OS in high-risk patients receiving bevacizumab, whereas GOG-0218 found no significant OS difference between arms [55,56].

More recently, retrospective studies have investigated bevacizumab in the upfront setting, specifically in advanced LGSOC. The multicenter MITO 22 study (n = 128; 46 patients with LGSOC) demonstrated significantly improved median PFS with first-line bevacizumab/chemotherapy compared to chemotherapy alone (47.9 months vs 22.6 months; P = .0392) [57]. In a smaller cohort of 10 patients with LGSOC from the same institution, the response rate to bevacizumab-based chemotherapy was 55%, substantially higher than the historical 4% response rate with chemotherapy alone [58]. In another single-institution study assessing women with recurrent LGSOC (n = 40), bevacizumab-containing regimens had an ORR of 47.5% and achieved a clinical benefit in 77.5% of patients [59].

Despite these promising signals, bevacizumab use is associated with increased toxicity, including hypertension requiring medical intervention, thromboembolic events, and gastrointestinal perforation [55,56,59]. The GOG-0218 study reported treatment-related mortality rates of 2.3% in patients receiving combination bevacizumab plus chemotherapy, compared to 1.0% in those treated with chemotherapy alone [56]. Furthermore, in one study assessing bevacizumab-containing regimens in recurrent LGSOC, 37.5% of patients discontinued bevacizumab due to treatment-related adverse events (AEs) [59]. Taken together, while bevacizumab may enhance response rates in selected patients with LGSOC, its survival benefit remains uncertain. Moreover, no prospective trials have directly compared bevacizumab to endocrine therapy in this population. Given the toxicity profile and lack of definitive survival advantage, there is a clear need for LGSOC-specific studies evaluating less toxic and more effective options.

Endocrine therapy in first-line and recurrent settings

LGSOC is characterized by high ER (58%–96%) and PR (40%–50%) expression, providing a strong rationale for adjuvant endocrine therapy with aromatase inhibitors [8,9,28]. The ORR to endocrine therapy in recurrent LGSOC is 9.0%; however, response rates are strongly associated with chemotherapy sensitivity, with an ORR of 13.5% in patients with platinum-sensitive disease and 2.7% in those with platinum-resistant disease [60]. A retrospective cohort study among patients with newly diagnosed primary stage III or IV LGSOC showed improved survival, with a 5-year OS of 75% in patients receiving adjuvant endocrine therapy compared to 65% in those not receiving endocrine therapy [61]. Longer PFS with endocrine maintenance therapy compared to observation alone (median, 64.9 months vs 26.4 months; P < .001) in patients with recurrent stage II – IV LGSOC following primary cytoreductive surgery and chemotherapy has been reported in another retrospective cohort study [62]. ER-positive/PR-positive expression was associated with a longer median time to progression than ER-positive/PR-negative expression [62]. However, no significant difference in OS was observed between treatment groups (median, 102.7 months vs 115.7 months; P = .42) [62]. Additionally, endocrine therapy with aromatase inhibitors displays a tolerable safety profile, making it a preferred treatment option for maintenance after adjuvant therapy [61]. However, robust late-phase clinical trial data are still lacking.

MAPK pathway inhibitors

Given the distinct molecular landscape of LGSOC, including frequent alterations in the MAPK signaling pathway, targeted therapies are now established as a cornerstone of disease management. The MAPK pathway plays a key role in tumor proliferation, survival, and resistance to therapy [30,33]. Up to 70% of LGSOC cases harbor mutations in RAS pathway – associated genes, most commonly KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, and ERBB2 [17,31,63]. A systematic review of MAPK pathway inhibitors in patients with ovarian cancer indicated greater efficacy in LGSOC compared to other subtypes, with combined dabrafenib and trametinib treatment in BRAFv600-mutated LGSOC (n = 6) demonstrating the greatest efficacy (ORR, 83%) [64].

Various MEK-only inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in Phase 2/3 trials for recurrent LGSOC, though with varying degrees of tolerability. The MILO/ENGOT-ov11 Phase 3 trial compared binimetinib with the physician’s choice of chemotherapy in patients with recurrent LGSOC following first-line chemotherapy. Binimetinib achieved an ORR of 16%, median PFS of 9.1 months, and median OS of 25.3 months, compared to an ORR of 13%, median PFS of 10.6 months, and median OS of 20.8 months with the physician’s choice of chemotherapy [18]. The lack of a difference between treatment arms and the study’s failure to meet the primary efficacy endpoint led to early closure of the study. The most common grade ≥3 AEs were elevated blood creatine phosphokinase (26%), vomiting (10%), dermatitis acneiform (6%), and diarrhea (6%), with drug discontinuations due to AEs being 31% [18]. Notably, KRAS mutations were associated with better response rates; while not a primary endpoint of the MILO study, this association highlights the importance of further studies identifying target populations for specific therapies [18].

The GOG-281 (NRG-GY004) Phase 2/3 trial compared trametinib with standard-of-care treatments (including chemotherapy and endocrine therapy) in patients with recurrent LGSOC following first-line chemotherapy. Trametinib demonstrated superior efficacy, with an ORR of 26%, median PFS of 13.0 months, and OS of 37.6 months, compared to an ORR of 6%, median PFS of 7.2 months, and OS of 29.2 months in the standard-of-care group [19]. Notably, while the clinical benefit of trametinib was observed irrespective of KRAS, BRAF, or NRAS mutation status, both PFS and ORR were markedly higher for patients with mutations in these genes compared with wild-type patients. ORRs were substantially more favorable for trametinib than standard-of-care therapy in mutation-positive patients (50% vs 9%) compared to mutation-negative patients (8% vs 7%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. The study concluded that, as discussed in previous studies, mutations of KRAS, BRAF, or NRAS may be predictive of ORR regardless of the therapy chosen [19]. The most common grade ≥3 AEs were skin rash (13%), anemia (13%), hypertension (12%), diarrhea (10%), nausea (9%), and fatigue (8%), with drug discontinuations due to AEs being 36% [19].

Dual MAPK pathway and FAK inhibitors

Although MEK inhibitors have shown signs of efficacy in LGSOC and are included in NCCN guidelines, their effectiveness is often limited by treatment-related toxicity and acquired resistance [15,18,19]. One major resistance mechanism involves compensatory reactivation of MEK signaling via upstream RAF activation, allowing tumor cells to bypass inhibition [33,65]. While RAF/MEK inhibition addresses the limitation of MEK inhibition alone, it also leads to compensatory activation of FAK [66]. Adding a FAK inhibitor to a dual RAF/MEK inhibitor addresses this key adaptive resistance mechanism.

The combination of the first-in-class RAF/MEK clamp avutometinib with the FAK inhibitor defactinib has newly been approved by the FDA for the treatment of recurrent LGSOC with known KRAS mutations, and it is of continued investigational interest [24]. RAMP-201/ENGOT-ov60/GOG-3052, a Phase 2 open-label study, evaluated the combination of avutometinib and defactinib in patients with recurrent LGSOC following chemotherapy [20]. A total of 115 patients received combination treatment, 58 with a KRAS mutation and 57 without [20]. Patients had received a median of 3 prior therapies, including bevacizumab (51%) and MEK inhibitors (22%) [20]. In patients with KRAS-mutated LGSOC, ORR by blinded independent central review was 44%, and median PFS was 22.0 months [20]. Patients with KRAS–wild-type LGSOC had an ORR of 17% and median PFS of 12.8 months [20]. In the overall population of patients, the ORR was 31% [20]. The most common grade ≥3 treatment-related AEs were elevated blood creatine phosphokinase (24%), diarrhea (8%), anemia (5%), and dermatitis acneiform (4%) [20]. Based on the Phase 2 trial data, avutometinib/defactinib received FDA breakthrough therapy designation, and the combination has now been approved by the FDA as treatment for recurrent KRAS-mutated LGSOC [20,24,70]. The ongoing RAMP 301/GOG-3097/ENGOT-ov81 (NCT06072781) Phase 3 open-label study compares the avutometinib/defactinib combination with the investigator’s choice of treatment in patients with recurrent LGSOC following progression on chemotherapy [67]. The study includes patients with and without KRAS mutations [67].

Ongoing research in LGSOC

Areas of research

The high recurrence rate and poor outcomes associated with conventional therapies underscore the urgent need for novel treatment strategies that improve patient outcomes while minimizing toxicity [8]. Approaches focused on endocrine therapy, CDK4/6 inhibitors, and combination regimens are being explored to improve outcomes in both first-line and recurrent settings. Endocrine therapy

Several ongoing Phase 3 trials are evaluating a potentially expanded role of endocrine therapy in LGSOC. The LEPRE (NCT05601700) and NRG GY-019 (NCT04095364) trials compare adjuvant letrozole therapy to carboplatin/paclitaxel in patients with stage III or IV ER+ LGSOC following primary cytoreductive surgery [73,74]. Another Phase 3 trial, MATAO (NCT04111978), is evaluating letrozole maintenance therapy in patients with ER+ stage II – IV LGSOC following primary or secondary debulking surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy [75]. These studies will clarify endocrine therapy efficacy in both adjuvant and maintenance settings for LGSOC management and potentially allow for chemotherapy de-escalation following initial surgery.

Resistance to endocrine therapy has important implications. For example, ESR1 gene alterations are recognized as a major driver of resistance in breast cancer and as promising prognostic and predictive biomarkers for response to endocrine therapy [76]. Recent studies suggest that ESR1 mutations may similarly contribute to resistance in LGSOC, warranting further investigation [77,78]. In addition to first-line settings, endocrine therapy is being explored in the recurrent setting, particularly for patients with platinum-resistant disease. A Phase 2 trial (FUCHSia) recently demonstrated that selective ER downregulator (SERD) therapy with fulvestrant achieved stable disease in 100% of patients with recurrent gynecologic cancers, including 4 patients with LGSOC, highlighting the potential for endocrine-based strategies beyond initial therapy [79]. Thus, switching to SERDs or selective ER modulators after progression on endocrine therapy with aromatase inhibitors may offer clinical benefit.

CDK4/6 inhibitors

CDK4/6 plays a critical role in cell cycle progression and inactivates the tumor suppressor function of the retinoblastoma protein [16]. Consequently, dysregulated CDK4/6 activity in cancer promotes proliferation and is associated with tumorigenesis [16]. Up to 20% of LGSOCs express alterations in the gene-encoding tumor suppressor p16 (CDKN2A), which inhibits the G1 cell cycle phase via CDK4/6, providing a strong rationale for CDK4/6 blockade in LGSOC treatment [80,81]. Furthermore, emerging ctDNA methods are under investigation, with the goal of detecting CDK4/6 alterations as predictive biomarkers for CDK4/6 inhibition in LGSOC [82].

Several CDK4/6 inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in Phase 2 trials for recurrent LGSOC. LEE011, a Phase 2 study, evaluated the CDK4/6 inhibitor ribociclib combined with letrozole in patients with recurrent ER+ ovarian cancer, including 3 patients with LGSOC who had not previously received an aromatase inhibitor [21]. Ribociclib combination treatment with letrozole demonstrated an ORR of 15%, median PFS of 5.4 months, and median OS of 18.9 months in patients with ovarian cancer, and the greatest benefit was observed in the 3 patients with LGSOC, for whom both ORR and 2-year PFS were 100% [21]. The most common grade ≥3 AEs were leukopenia (23%), lymphocytopenia (23%), neutropenia (15%), fatigue (13%), dehydration (8%), abnormal liver function tests (8%), and acute kidney injury (8%) [21]. Discontinuations due to AEs were 19% [21].

The GOG-3026 Phase 2 study evaluated ribociclib combined with letrozole in patients with recurrent LGSOC who had not received an aromatase inhibitor following first-line chemotherapy [22]. The results showed an ORR of 30.6% and clinical benefit rate of 84% with the ribociclib combination treatment in 49 patients evaluable for response [22]. Two patients discontinued due to toxicity, but no other safety data were reported [22]. Another Phase 2 study evaluated neoadjuvant fulvestrant combined with abemaciclib in patients with unresectable, untreated stage III or IV LGSOC. Preliminary results in 15 evaluable patients showed an ORR of 47% and a complete gross resection rate of 80% at the time of interval cytoreductive surgery. The most common grade ≥3 AEs were acute kidney injury (6.7%) and neutropenia (6.7%), although the discontinuation rate was not reported [23].

Because many patients now receive aromatase inhibitors as adjuvant or maintenance treatment, the potential role for CDK4/6 inhibitors with aromatase inhibitors is unclear in LGSOC, but it may be appropriate for study in the adjuvant setting in future trials.

Conclusion

LGSOC remains a uniquely challenging malignancy, marked by intrinsic chemoresistance and high recurrence rates [9,49,50]. Although standard chemotherapy continues to be used in the frontline setting, its limited effectiveness has shifted attention toward endocrine-based and targeted therapies. Even so, options such as MEK-only inhibitors offer only modest efficacy and are frequently limited by poor tolerability and resistance [15]. These realities highlight the urgent need to move beyond conventional treatment frameworks and toward precision-based, biomarker-driven care.

Several recent clinical trials have shaped the evolving therapeutic landscape in LGSOC. The MILO/ENGOT-OV11 trial (binimetinib vs physician’s-choice chemotherapy) was terminated early for futility, likely reflecting an unexpectedly strong control arm, eligibility restriction to ≤ 3 prior chemotherapy lines, and a heterogeneous patient population [18]. In contrast, GOG-281/LOGS demonstrated a significant PFS benefit with trametinib over standard care in a more heavily pretreated population [19]. These discordant results may reflect both trial design and agent-specific differences. Preclinical data indicate that trametinib achieves more potent MAPK pathway inhibition and stronger antiproliferative effects than binimetinib, although no head-to-head clinical comparison has been conducted [83]. The recent FDA approval of avutometinib plus defactinib for KRAS-mutated recurrent LGSOC illustrates a paradigm shift in LGSOC management, highlighting the importance of targeting multiple resistance pathways simultaneously [17,19,26,72]. Additional studies have explored combinations with endocrine therapy, CDK4/6 inhibitors, and alternative targeted approaches [21–23]. Because LGSOC shares the endocrine sensitivity and CDK4/6 pathway dysregulation observed in breast cancer, emerging therapies in LGSOC include treatments used in breast cancer, such as CDK4/6 inhibitors, and may be interesting to explore in combination with aromatase inhibitors at their first usage [15,16,21,22].

These trials highlight the need to consider patient characteristics (eg, KRAS mutation status, prior therapies), control arms, and mechanisms of action when interpreting outcomes. Although cross-trial comparisons must be interpreted with caution, as they are not head-to-head studies and vary in design, collectively they inform a future in which rationally designed, biomarker-driven combinations may offer more durable and personalized treatment options for patients with LGSOC. Future trials should consider adaptive designs to allow for modifications, inclusion of real-world data to enhance generalizability, and international collaboration to address the challenges associated with rare cancers.

Another important frontier is the creation of new liquid biopsy approaches, such as ctDNA, for clinical management in LGSOC. While future assays may enable noninvasive monitoring of tumor evolution and treatment response, currently available technologies do not appear to be well-suited for LGSOC. Larger studies are needed to validate its sensitivity and concordance with traditional tissue-based diagnostics. Although ctDNA-based surveillance could eventually support real-time therapy adjustments and reduce invasive biopsies, its clinical utility and standardized decision-making algorithms remain to be established [47,48].

Beyond therapeutic limitations, several structural and clinical gaps persist. The field still relies on retrospective data and basket trials, with few prospective, randomized studies conducted in LGSOC. Validated predictive biomarkers for treatment selection are also lacking. Emerging preclinical drug screening with LGSOC-specific in vitro models is helping identify predictive biomarkers, new therapies, and resistance mechanisms to guide future trials [84–86]. Developing robust laboratory platforms, such as cell lines, organoids, and murine systems, is essential for advancing mechanistic insights and validating therapies in LGSOC. Quality-of-life outcomes, especially for younger women, also remain underreported.

Despite these promising avenues, patient access to innovation remains a concern. Clinical trial enrollment is essential to advancing LGSOC care, yet participation is hindered by restrictive eligibility criteria, lack of awareness, logistical burdens, and persistent disparities in representation [26]. Future research must prioritize inclusive trial designs, expand access through decentralized models, and improve outreach to both clinicians and patients [26,87,88].
