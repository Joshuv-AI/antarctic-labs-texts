Diagnosing and managing small bowel neuroendocrine tumors presenting as acute obstruction in an elderly patient: A case report and comprehensive management overview
Authors: Hager Behi, Ahmed Omry, Rania Dallagi, Amel Changuel, Dholkefl Troudi, Med Bachir Khalifa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceNeuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of the small bowel are rare but clinically significant due to their challenging diagnostic pathways and potential for insidious progression. Early identification is critical for effective management and improved prognosis in these cases.Clinical presentationHere, we present a case of a 75-year-old patient with no significant medical history who presented acutely with diffuse abdominal pain, vomiting, and signs of bowel obstruction. Diagnostic workup, including CT imaging, revealed a distal ileal neuroendocrine mass with mesenteric lymphadenopathy, necessitating urgent surgical intervention.DiscussionThis case underscores the diagnostic complexities and therapeutic challenges associated with small bowel NETs. Surgical resection with meticulous lymph node dissection remains the cornerstone of treatment, aimed at achieving complete tumor excision and optimal disease control. The role of imaging modalities and biochemical markers in guiding clinical decisions and postoperative management strategies is discussed considering the patient's clinical course.ConclusionTimely recognition and intervention are crucial in the management of small bowel NETs, given their potential for late presentation and nonspecific symptoms. Despite diagnostic and procedural challenges highlighted in this case, early surgical intervention and comprehensive follow-up are essential for achieving favorable outcomes and minimizing recurrence risks in patients with small bowel NETs.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of the small bowel are rare but increasingly recognized malignancies, often presenting with nonspecific symptoms that delay diagnosis [1,2]. The complexity of these tumors lies in their variable clinical manifestations and the challenge of early detection [2]. This case report details the presentation and management of a 75-year-old patient with an acute small bowel obstruction caused by an ileal neuroendocrine tumor (iNET). The aim is to highlight the diagnostic hurdles and emphasize the importance of considering NETs in similar clinical scenarios to enhance early detection and intervention.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2023 criteria [3].

Case presentation

A 75-year-old patient with no significant medical or surgical history presented to the emergency department with diffuse abdominal pain that had been progressing for 12 h, accompanied by early postprandial vomiting and cessation of bowel movements and gas. The patient denied any history of melena, hematemesis, or recent changes in bowel habits such as constipation or alternating diarrhea and constipation. He also denied smoking or alcohol use and had no family history of gastrointestinal neoplasms.

Physical examination revealed a patient in generally good condition, hemodynamically and respiratorily stable, with a blood pressure of 133/76 mmHg, heart rate of 87 bpm, respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute, and normal oxygen saturation in ambient air. There were no signs of dehydration. Abdominal examination showed generalized tenderness, distension, and tympanism, with no palpable masses and solid hernial orifices. Rectal examination revealed an empty rectal vault.

Laboratory results indicated a biological inflammatory syndrome with a white blood cell count of 12 × 10^3/mm^3 and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 50 mg/L. Hemoglobin was 11 g/dL. Renal function, electrolyte levels, and coagulation profile were within normal limits. An upright abdominal X-ray revealed multiple air-fluid levels typical of small bowel obstruction. A contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic CT scan demonstrated distension of the jejunal and distal ileal loops, with maximum distension measuring 35 mm in diameter upstream of a transition zone characterized by a collapsed loop and an irregular, stenosing, thickened wall of a distal ileal loop (Fig. 1). The patient was admitted to the surgical intensive care unit where he received intravenous fluids and was placed on nasogastric suction. Due to the lack of improvement, an emergency laparotomy was performed via a midline incision. Intraoperative findings included a small amount of serous effusion and distended small bowel with preserved vitality upstream of a distal ileal obstruction caused by an ileo-ileal intussusception involving a neoplastic-appearing lesion located 80 cm from the ileocecal valve and 240 cm from the ligament of Treitz (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The mesentery adjacent to the affected bowel contained several suspicious, indurated lymph nodes measuring approximately 1 cm. Palpation of the remaining small intestine and the colonic frame did not reveal any other suspicious lesions. An oncological resection of the small bowel was performed, with 10 cm margins on either side of the lesion, followed by a hand-sewn, side-to-side isoperistaltic ileo-ileal anastomosis. The postoperative course was uneventful.

Pathology revealed an invasive, well-differentiated, Grade 1 neuroendocrine tumor, penetrating through the muscularis propria into the submucosa without breaching the overlying serosa. All surgical margins were clear of tumor involvement. Metastatic disease was identified in four of the eight examined mesenteric lymph nodes. Immunohistochemical staining showed the tumor was positive for chromogranin, synaptophysin, CD56, CDX2, and had a Ki-67 index of approximately 1 %. It was negative for CK7, CK20, PAX8, and TTF-1. Laboratory results showed a serum chromogranin A level of 213 (normal <317), serum serotonin level of 257 ng/mL (reference range 55–245 ng/mL), and a 24-hour urine 5-hydroxyindolacetic acid (5-HIAA) level of 2.6 mg/24 h (reference range ≤ 5.0 mg/24 h).

The patient was discharged on the fourth postoperative day with outpatient follow-up in oncology, surgery, and gastroenterology. During follow-up, both an octreoscan and a PET scan with 18FDG were conducted to detect any additional tumor sites. These imaging studies did not reveal any other suspicious findings.

Discussion

Small bowel cancers are uncommon malignancies within the gastrointestinal tract [4]. The incidence of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) overall, and specifically those of the small bowel, has been steadily increasing since the 1970s [5]. According to the National Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program registry, the incidence of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) increased 6.4-fold from 1973 to 2012, rising from 1.09 per 100,000 to 6.98 per 100,000 [6]. The increasing incidence of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) is likely attributable to the expanded use of imaging and endoscopy, as well as enhanced awareness and understanding by physicians, though this remains uncertain [[5], [6], [7]]. Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) typically present in the sixth decade of life, with a median age at diagnosis of 63 years [6]. Among 35,618 NET patients in the SEER database, the prevalence is 52 % in women and 48 % in men [6].

Carcinoid syndrome was first characterized by Thorson et al. in 1954 and is classically described by symptoms such as flushing, diarrhea, valvular heart disease, and bronchospasm [5]. However, in the absence of hepatic metastasis, most locoregional small bowel neuroendocrine tumors (SBNETs) do not exhibit typical carcinoid syndrome because excess hormones are metabolized and inactivated by the liver [6]. Consequently, classical carcinoid syndrome is rarely observed in the absence of metastatic disease, aligning with our patient's clinical presentation [4,5].

Because of the length and complex structure of the small intestine, tumors in this area frequently manifest symptoms late, which are predominantly nonspecific [6,7]. Consequently, diagnosis is often challenging, resulting in delays in identification [7]. This study seeks to highlight the diagnostic complexities associated with small bowel tumors and to increase awareness of this clinical challenge. Small bowel neuroendocrine tumors (SBNETs) are frequently advanced with local nodal metastasis at the time of diagnosis [4,8]. Local invasion often leads to mesenteric fibrosis, resulting in complications such as bowel obstruction, ischemia, perforation, or bleeding, which may necessitate emergency surgery [6]. In our patient, this led to a bowel obstruction requiring urgent surgical intervention.

The diagnostic approach for small bowel neuroendocrine tumors (SBNETs) should be tailored to each patient's clinical presentation [6,9]. For instance, patients exhibiting carcinoid symptoms such as flushing and diarrhea may benefit from timely evaluation of biochemical markers specific to SBNETs [5,6]. Neuroendocrine cells secrete hormones or amines that serve as potential serum biochemical markers for screening purposes [6]. These include chromogranin A (CgA), chromogranin B (CgB), bradykinin, substance P, neurotensin, human chorionic gonadotropin, neuropeptide K, and neuropeptide PP [5,6,10]. Among these biomarkers, chromogranin A (CgA) is considered the most effective overall screening marker because it is produced by a broad spectrum of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), irrespective of their primary site, including nonfunctional tumors [6]. Diagnosing serotonin-secreting neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) can be achieved through measuring the biomarker 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), a breakdown product of serotonin [6]. Unlike serum serotonin levels, which fluctuate with daily activity and stress, urinary 5-HIAA provides a more reliable assessment with an 88 % specificity [5,6].

Imaging modalities for detecting ileal neuroendocrine tumors (iNETs) include anatomical and functional approaches [9]. CT scans are pivotal for staging and surgical planning, with sensitivity ranging from 7 % to 38 %, potentially increasing to 82 % with signs of mesenteric lymphadenopathy or fibrosis indicating a primary small bowel tumor [6,8]. CT enteroclysis offers higher sensitivity, between 50 % and 85 % [6]. Abdominal CT can reveal mesenteric tumors attached to the bowel, lymphadenopathies, and liver metastases, though these masses may not always be visible due to their location outside the bowel lumen [2,8]. However, these imaging techniques occasionally provide limited preoperative visualization, as seen in the current clinical case [6,8].

MRI offers several advantages over CT imaging. It boasts a sensitivity of 93 % and specificity of 86 % in detecting neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) [6]. Furthermore, MRI eliminates the risk of ionizing radiation exposure and enhances the detection of liver metastases [6,10].

Functional imaging techniques leverage somatostatin receptor (SSTR) expression in neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) [6,8]. Indium-111 pentetreotide or lanreotide (octreoscan) binds to SSTR2, 3, and 5, facilitating specific tumor localization with a sensitivity of 52–78 % and specificity of 98 % [5,6]. Gallium-68-based imaging, including 68Ga-DOTATATE, 68Ga-DOTATOC, and 68Ga-DOTANOC, is preferred over indium-111 due to higher sensitivity (88–93 %) and specificity (92–93 %), along with reduced radiation exposure and improved imaging quality [6]. These advancements have enhanced the detection and management of small bowel NETs [1,2].

In our patient, who did not present with carcinoid syndrome, the diagnostic approach was streamlined due to the urgent clinical scenario. Only a CT scan was performed urgently to rapidly assess the patient's condition and plan further management.

Surgery, whether curative or palliative, is the primary treatment for intestinal neuroendocrine tumors (iNET) [8,9]. Laparotomy is preferred due to its ability to thoroughly explore the small intestine and ensure adequate lymph node resection [6,9]. This case combined laparotomy with video assistance for effective tumor removal [1,9]. Key surgical principles include complete tumor resection, radical lymphadenectomy of at least seven nodes, and consideration of metastasis resection for liver involvement [9]. Successful outcomes depend on tumor location, disease extent, tumor functionality, and the ability to achieve complete resection [6]. Timely diagnosis and treatment are crucial for favorable results [2,9].

Although complete tumor resection appears to be curative, long-term recurrence rates of approximately 50 % have been reported [6]. Therefore, close surveillance for 10 years post-resection of neuroendocrine tumors (NET) is advised, with follow-up intervals of six to 12 months utilizing imaging, biochemical markers, or endoscopy [6,7]. Presently, surveillance strategies vary in clinical practice and should be individualized based on the patient's specific presentation [6].

In relation to our case, the patient with small bowel neuroendocrine tumors (SBNETs) underwent regional lymph node resection along with the segmental bowel resection [11]. This procedure involved resecting up to the origin of the segmental vascular branches from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) or vein (SMV) [11]. Given the presence of lymph node metastases involving the root of the mesentery and the proximal SMA/SMV, we recommend considering referral to a specialized neuroendocrine tumor center [11]. To prevent peritoneal implants in small bowel neuroendocrine tumors (SBNETs), early surgery is essential [11]. For advanced cases, it is important to remove as much tumor as possible while minimizing risk [11]. Limited seeding can be resected, and smaller lesions treated with electrocautery or argon beam [11]. Currently, HIPEC has no proven benefit as an adjunct to these local treatments for peritoneal metastases [11].

In conclusion, this case report highlights both advantages and disadvantages. The nonspecific presentation of the patient emphasizes the need for increased awareness among practitioners about iNETs, which often present with vague symptoms. This case also underscores the importance of recognizing and managing small bowel obstructions caused by iNETs. However, there are notable limitations: the follow-up period was only six months, no histopathology images were provided, and the quality of the CT scan images was suboptimal. These factors should be considered when evaluating the case and its broader implications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of small bowel neuroendocrine tumors (SBNETs). The patient's acute abdominal symptoms required urgent surgery, revealing an underlying iNET. This underscores the importance of considering neuroendocrine tumors in differential diagnoses of small bowel obstructions. Comprehensive management, including timely surgical resection and vigilant postoperative surveillance, is crucial for favorable outcomes. Early recognition and intervention are essential to improving patient prognosis.
