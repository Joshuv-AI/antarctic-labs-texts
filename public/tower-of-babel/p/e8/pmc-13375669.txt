PSMC2 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression through interaction with EGFR
Authors: Yecheng Li, Qunxue Su, Zhenyu Feng, Hui Xu, Tengfei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Growing evidence highlights the critical involvement of the ubiquitin-proteasome system in cancer development. As an essential component of the 26 S proteasome, Proteasome 26 S Subunit ATPase 2 (PSMC2) has been implicated in various malignancies, but its role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression remains poorly understood. We analyzed PSMC2 expression using The Cancer Genome Atlas database (TCGA) database, and validated findings in clinical HCC specimens and cell lines through immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Western blot. Functional assays (CCK-8, colony formation, Scratch test and transwell invasion assay) were performed to assess the oncogenic properties of PSMC2 in the progression of HCC. Mechanistic studies employed co-immunoprecipitation, Western blot, immunofluorescence and in vivo xenograft models to investigate PSMC2-EGFR interactions and downstream signaling. PSMC2 was significantly overexpressed in HCC tissues and correlated with poor patient prognosis. Genetic knockdown of PSMC2 inhibited HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, while suppressing tumor growth in vivo. Conversely, PSMC2 overexpression enhanced malignant phenotypes. Mechanistically, PSMC2 physically interacted with EGFR, stabilizing EGFR protein levels and enhancing phosphorylation of downstream AKT and ERK1/2 pathways. Our study identifies PSMC2 as a novel regulator of HCC progression through EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 signaling axis activation. These findings position PSMC2 as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for HCC intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02178-4.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy, ranking sixth globally in incidence and third in cancer-related deaths [1]. HCC is characterized by aggressive progression, high recurrence rates, and limited treatment options in advanced stages. Despite advancements in surveillance, diagnosis, and therapies—including surgical resection, liver transplantation, and targeted therapies—the prognosis remains poor for many patients, especially those diagnosed at late stages [2, 3]. Understanding its molecular mechanisms, risk factors, and novel therapeutic targets is critical for improving outcomes.

The ubiquitin/26S proteasome pathway is a critical protein degradation system in eukaryotic cells, selectively regulating key cellular processes such as cell cycle progression, DNA repair, and apoptosis [4]. As an essential component of the 26 S proteasome, Proteasome 26 S subunit ATPase 2 (PSMC2) plays a pivotal role in substrate recognition, unfolding, and translocation into the 20 S catalytic core [5]. Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulation of PSMC2 plays a critical role in tumorigenesis and cancer progression. Multiple studies have demonstrated that PSMC2 overexpression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in various malignancies, including colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and gallbladder carcinoma etc. Notably, experimental evidence from PSMC2 knockdown studies has confirmed that suppression of PSMC2 expression effectively inhibits tumor cell proliferation and migration while inducing apoptosis [6–8]. Emerging evidence indicates that PSMC2 drives HCC progression through the p21/cyclin D1 signaling axis [9]. Another study establishes that PSMC2 exerts its tumor-promoting effects via direct interaction with ITGA6 [10]. However, the complete mechanistic landscape of PSMC2 in HCC pathogenesis remains to be fully elucidated. The clarification of PSMC2’s regulatory role and precise molecular mechanisms in HCC progression is of critical importance for the diagnosis and therapeutic management of HCC.

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a key member of the ErbB receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) family, is a well-established oncogenic driver that critically regulates cell proliferation, survival, and metastatic dissemination [11]. Although EGFR-targeted therapies—such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and monoclonal antibodies—have shown substantial clinical benefits in cancers like non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [12], their effectiveness in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains limited [13]. Evidence has demonstrated that epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exhibits significant overexpression in more than 60% of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cases [14]. Importantly, the dysregulation of complex signaling networks mediated by EGFR may serve as one of the key molecular mechanisms driving HCC malignant progression [15, 16]. A deeper understanding of the precise regulatory networks controlling EGFR signaling could facilitate the discovery of novel HCC biomarkers and therapeutic approaches, thereby enhancing patient prognosis.

Here we investigated the role of PSMC2 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and its interaction with EGFR. We demonstrate that PSMC2 is highly expressed in HCC and functionally promotes tumor progression. Knockdown of PSMC2 significantly inhibits HCC cell proliferation, migration and invasion, while its overexpression has the opposite effects. Importantly, we found that PSMC2 physically interacts with EGFR protein and regulates its expression levels as well as the phosphorylation of downstream AKT and ERK1/2 signaling pathways. These findings indicate that PSMC2 modulates HCC progression both in vitro and in vivo through its interaction with the EGFR signaling cascade, suggesting PSMC2 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for HCC treatment.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens and public data sources

Sixty-one pairs of pathologically confirmed HCC tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues and another 29 HCC tissues without matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were randomly selected from all those undergoing radical HCC resection between 2022 and 2025 at the Department of Pathology of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Suzhou, China). None had received chemotherapy or radiation therapy before surgery. HCC diagnoses were confirmed via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining after surgical resection. This study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital. And a written informed consent form was obtained from each patient prior to their enrollment in the study, and this study was approved by The Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. JD-LK2024047). The component of this study involving human samples complies with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. RNA sequencing data of 384 HCC cancer tissues were downloaded from TCGA HCC dataset.

Cell culture and cell lines

HCC cell lines (Li-7, Huh7, 97 H, Hep3B and HepG2) and HEK293T cell were purchased from Fuheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Li-7, Huh7, 97 H, HepG2 and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA). Hep3B cells were cultured in modified Eagle’s medium (MEM) (Gibco, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco).

Plasmid transfection, virus production and infection

Plasmid and lentivirus shRNA used in the article were synthesized from GenePharma Co. (Suzhou, China). The human PSMC2 gene fused with 3 × Flag at the C-terminal was cloned in pcDNA3.1vector at AflII-XhoI cut sites. Empty vector with 3 × Flag was used as the control vector. The human EGFR gene was cloned in pcDNA3.1 vector at AflII-XhoI cut sites and the pcDNA3.1 was used as the control vector. The lentivirus shRNA sequences for PSMC2 were GCCAGGGAGATTGGATAGAAA (1#) and CAACGTAAAGCAGTTTGCCAA (2#), and shNC lentivirus shRNA sequences was TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT. When the cells plated in a six-well plate reached appropriate density (70–80%), plasmids were transiently transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 48 h after transfection, the cells were used for subsequent experiments. The HEK293T cells were used to produce lentivirus and the procedure was performed as preciously described [17]. The HCC cells were infected with lentivirus supernatant for 72 h and selected with puromycin (2 µg/ml) for 15 days to get the stable pooled clones.

Western blot

The prepared cell samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors mentioned before [17]. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA (Solarbio) assay, with 25 µg protein/lane separated by SDS-PAGE gel (Epizyme) and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature, 1 h). For all detections involving phosphorylated and total proteins, we first probed for the phosphorylated proteins. After imaging, the membranes were washed with stripping buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #P0025B) for 15 min, and then blocked again with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h. Subsequently, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies against the corresponding total proteins and loading controls to detect their expression levels. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent. Primary antibodies against PSMC2 (1:200, Santa cruz, sc-166972), GAPDH (1:20000, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), β-Actin (1:100000, Abclonal, AC026), p-EGFR (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, 3777 S), EGFR (1:200, Santa cruz, sc-373746), p-AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4060 S), AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4691 S), p-ERK1/2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4370 S) and ERK1/2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4695 S) were used in this study.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells from different experimental groups were plated in 96-well plates at densities of 2000 cells/well. After allowing cell attachment for 6 h, CCK-8 reagent (10 µL) was added to each well containing 90 µL of fresh medium at specified time points (6, 24, 48, and 72 h). The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

Huh7 and Hep3B cells infected with shNC, shPSMC2-1# or shPSMC2-2# lentivirus or transfected with informed plasmid were collected after digested by trypsin (Beyotime Biotechnology). Cell of different groups were resuspended and seeded in a 6-well plate (600 cells/well) and further cultured for 12 days. After fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Service-bio, ) and stained with crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich), all colonies were photographed with a digital camera and colony number was counted.

Wound healing assay

Cells of informed groups were seed in 6-well plates and grown achieved 80% density. A standardized wound was created in the cell plate using a 200 µL pipette tip. Following wound induction, cells were maintained in serum-free medium to minimize proliferation-dependent migration. Wound closure was documented at two critical time points: baseline (0 h) and 48 h post-wounding. Quantitative analysis of migration kinetics was performed by measuring the wound closure using ImageJ software. Five random fields were counted under a microscope in each group, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis. All experiments were performed independently in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. To avoid subjective bias, quantification was performed by two independent researchers in a blinded manner.

Transwell assay

Cell invasion capacity was evaluated using matrigel-coated (Corning) transwell inserts (8-µm pore size, Corning). Briefly, 5 × 10⁵ cells in 200 µL serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained 500 µL complete medium with 10% FBS. After 48 h incubation, non-invading cells were removed from the upper membrane surface by cotton swab abrasion. Invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% PFA, stained with 0.2% crystal violet. Five random fields were counted under a microscope in each group, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis. All experiments were performed independently in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. To avoid subjective bias, quantification was performed by two independent researchers in a blinded manner.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the glioma cells with the use of Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse transcribed by using the HiScript IV RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme). The cDNA was detected by quantitative polymerase chain reaction by using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The primers used in this study were listed below: β-Actin: Forward (F) 5’-CCTGGCACCCAGCACAAT-3’, Reverse(R) 5’-GGGCCGGACTCGTCATAC-3’; PSMC2: F: 5’- CAGCACTCTGGGATTTGGCT-3’, R: 5’-TTTCTATCCACGCCCACTCTC-3’; EGFR: F: 5’- CCCACTCATGCTCTACAACCC-3’, R: 5’-TCGCACTTCTTACACTTGCGG-3’.

Immunoprecipitation

Huh7 cells were lysed in NP-40 buffer (Beyotime) containing PMSF. For each reaction, 1 mg protein lysate was immunoprecipitated with 5 µg/ml specific antibodies anti-EGFR antibody (1:40, Santa cruz, sc-373746), anti-PSMC2 antibody (1:40, Santa cruz, sc-166972), or normal mouse IgG antibody (1:200, Millipore, 12–371) at 4 °C overnight. Antibody-protein complexes were captured using Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen, 10003D) for 4 h at 4 °C. The whole cell lysate was used as input sample. Precipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed by immunoblotting with indicated antibodies.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Huh7 cells were seeded into 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) with pre-cleared glass slides. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Solarbio) for 15 min at 4°C. The permeabilized cells were blocked with PBS containing 3% goat serum (Beyotime). The cells were then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 60 min. The images were observed using Zeiss confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss). The primary and secondary antibodies used were mouse anti-PSMC2 (1:50, Santa cruz, sc-166972), rabbit anti-EGFR (1:200, Proteintech, 18986-1-AP), and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (H + L)/Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 8890 S/4412S). Finally, cells were incubated with 4′, 6- diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, sigma) for no more than 10 min.

Protein half-life assay

HCC cells were treated with 20 µg/mL CHX for 0, 4, 8 and 16 h. The cells were collected and EGFR protein expression was detected by Western blot to examine the effect of PSMC2 on EGFR protein half-life. The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ, and the protein half-life was calculated. The following normalization approach was used: The band intensity of EGFR at each time point was first normalized to its corresponding loading control (GAPDH), and then further normalized to the normalized value at 0 h. The final result is expressed as the “percentage of remaining EGFR protein relative to 0 hours.”

Xenograft model and IHC staining

All animal experimental procedures conformed to ethical principles and guidelines approved by The Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Soochow University (approval No. 202406A0101). The animal study followed the ARRIVE guidelines and the ARRIVE Checklist is available [18]. For the subcutaneous model, 5 × 106 Huh7-shNC/Huh7-shPSMC2 cells were diluted in 150 µL matrigel (Corning) and injected subcutaneously into 5–6 weeks old female athymic nude mice (n = 5). The tumor volume was calculated by the formula V = [1/2] ab2 (a and b represent the long and short diameters of the tumor, respectively). All animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Blinded analysis was conducted for tumor measurement and quantification to eliminate potential subjective bias. Mice were sacrificed by anesthesia when tumor reached 1000 mm3. All tumors were harvested and fixed with 4% PFA, and the paraffin tissue sectioned for IHC staining. IHC staining was performed with antibodies against PSMC2 (1:100, Santa cruz, sc-166972), EGFR (1:100, Santa cruz, sc-373746), cleaved-Caspase 3 (1:400, Cell Signaling Technology, 9661 S) and Ki67 (1:800, GB111499, Service-bio).

Statistical analysis

All experiments in the study were carried out at least 3 times and analyzed using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Results were displayed as Mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test for tumor volume analysis or one-way analysis of variance for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ (version 1.53t) with the JACoP plugin. The Costes automatic thresholding algorithm was adopted for Pearson correlation coefficient calculation to eliminate background noise, and manual thresholding was simultaneously applied as a control. The threshold was defined as two standard deviations above the mean background fluorescence intensity for each channel. Representative scatter plots of colocalization analysis were performed using ImageJ: representative cells with moderate and uniform fluorescence intensity were selected, and a linear region along the long axis of individual cells was drawn. Fluorescence intensity along the defined line was quantified via ImageJ. In the scatter plot, red dots indicate PSMC2 fluorescence intensity, green dots represent EGFR fluorescence intensity, and each dot corresponds to a single pixel.

Result

PSMC2 is upregulated in HCC and correlates with poor prognosis

We first determined PSMC2 protein expression in HCC patient samples by IHC assay. IHC staining showed elevated PSMC2 levels in HCC tissues compared to para carcinoma tissues (Fig. 1A). Quantification of immunoreactivity scores (IRS, n = 61) further validated the upregulation of PSMC2 in HCC (Fig. 1B, ***P < 0.001). We statistically analyzed the association between PSMC2 expression and clinical characteristics in HCC patients. The results showed that positive PSMC2 expression was significantly and positively correlated with tumor size, histological differentiation, vascular invasion, and Child-Pugh stage, whereas no significant correlation was observed with age, gender, or liver cirrhosis status (Table 1). Furthermore, we detected PSMC2 protein expression in HCC cell lines by Western blot. The result of Western blot revealed that PSMC2 protein expression was significantly elevated in HCC cell lines (Li-7, Hep3B, Huh7, and 97 H) compared to transformed human liver epithelial-2 cell line (THLE2) (Fig. 1C). Given the consistent upregulation of PSMC2 in both clinical specimens and cellular models, PSMC2 expression level may play an important role in the survival of HCC patients. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that HCC patients with high PSMC2 expression had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) than those with low expression (Fig. 1D, P = 0.033). Based on PSMC2 protein expression levels shown in Fig. 1C, Huh7 and Hep3B cell lines were selected for subsequent investigations.

PSMC2 knockdown suppresses HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Given PSMC2 is upregulated in HCC cells, we employed lentiviral shRNA to suppress PSMC2 expression in both Huh7 and Hep3B cell lines. To examine the consequences of PSMC2 knockdown on tumor cell behavior, we first confirmed PSMC2 expression downregulation through Western blot and qPCR analysis (Fig. 2A-B, Fig. S1A). Subsequent functional assays revealed that PSMC2 depletion significantly inhibited HCC cell proliferation, as evidenced by reduced viability in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 2C-D, ***P < 0.001). The colony formation assay serves as a crucial indicator for evaluating both proliferative capacity and tumorigenic potential in HCC. Following PSMC2 knockdown, both the Huh7 and Hep3B cells showed significant decrease in plating colony formation efficiency (Fig. 2E-F, ***P < 0.001). To further characterize the phenotypic impact of PSMC2 knockdown, we performed wound healing and transwell assays. These experiments demonstrated marked reductions in both migratory and invasive capabilities upon PSMC2 silencing (Fig. 2G-K, ***P < 0.001). Specifically, PSMC2-deficient cells showed approximately 20% decreased wound closure and 60% fewer invading cells compared to shNC controls in both Huh7 and Hep3B cells, indicating PSMC2’s crucial role in maintaining the metastatic potential of HCC cells. Additionally, we have added proliferation data under serum-free conditions as a control to confirm that the observed wound closure was predominantly caused by cell migration rather than proliferation. (Fig. S3A-D, ***P < 0.001, ns, not significant)

PSMC2 overexpression enhances HCC malignant phenotypes

Conversely, we then assessed the biological function of PSMC2 in HCC using gain-of-function approaches. We utilized Flag-tagged overexpression plasmids to enhance PSMC2 expression in both Huh7 and Hep3B cell lines. To investigate the effects of PSMC2 overexpression on malignant phenotypes, we first confirmed successful protein induction through Western blot analysis (Fig. 3A-B). Subsequent functional characterization demonstrated that PSMC2 overexpression markedly promoted HCC cell proliferation, as shown by enhanced viability in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 3C-D, ***P < 0.001). The colony formation assay, a gold standard for evaluating oncogenic potential, revealed that PSMC2-overexpressing cells formed significantly more and larger colonies compared to vector controls (Fig. 3E-F, ***P < 0.001). Then, we conducted wound healing and transwell assays to investigate the influence of PSMC2 overexpression in HCC cells. These experiments showed substantial enhancements in both migratory and invasive capacities following PSMC2 overexpression (Fig. 3G-K, ***P < 0.001). Specifically, PSMC2-overexpressing Huh7 cells exhibited approximately 15% increased wound closure and 75% more invading cells relative to NC-Flag controls. Similarly, Hep3B cells showed higher increases of 25% in wound closure and 65% in invading cells compared to NC-Flag controls. These results underscore PSMC2’s critical function in driving HCC aggressiveness. Overall, these findings establish PSMC2 as a key regulator of hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation and motility, suggesting its importance in HCC pathogenesis.

PSMC2 regulates AKT and ERK1/2 phosphorylation via interaction with EGFR

Previous studies established PSMC2 as an AKT pathway activator in several cancers [7, 19–22]. Here, we investigated its mechanism in HCC, focusing on EGFR-mediated regulation of AKT/ERK signaling. We first examined the effect of PSMC2 knockdown and overexpression on EGFR, AKT, and ERK1/2 phosphorylation in Huh7 and Hep3B cells. Western blot analysis revealed that PSMC2 knockdown significantly reduced the levels of phosphorylated EGFR (p-EGFR), AKT (p-AKT), and ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2), and total EGFR protein level was significantly downregulated (Fig. 4A). Conversely, PSMC2 overexpression enhanced the expression of p-EGFR, p-AKT, p-ERK1/2 and total EGFR (Fig. 4B), suggesting that PSMC2 positively regulates EGFR-mediated downstream signaling. However, bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA dataset demonstrated a weak correlation between PSMC2 and EGFR mRNA expression (Fig. S2, R2 = 0.27, P < 0.001). Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of HCC patient tissues revealed higher EGFR expression in PSMC2-high tumors compared to PSMC2-low tumors (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that PSMC2 likely regulates EGFR expression at the protein level rather than through transcriptional regulation of mRNA (Fig. S1B). To determine whether PSMC2 directly interacts with EGFR, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in Huh7 cell lysates. Immunoprecipitation with either anti-PSMC2 or anti-EGFR antibodies followed by immunoblotting confirmed a physical interaction between PSMC2 and EGFR (Fig. 4D). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated co-localization of PSMC2 (green) and EGFR (red) in the cytoplasm of Huh7 cells (Fig. 4E and Fig. S2C, Pearson correlation coefficient (R) higher than 0.6), further supporting their interaction. Additionally, we performed protein half-life assays by treating hepatocellular carcinoma cells with cycloheximide (CHX) for different durations and monitored EGFR protein degradation via Western blot analysis. The results demonstrated that PSMC2 knockdown not only suppressed EGFR protein expression but also resulted in a shorter EGFR protein half-life compared to the control (shNC) group (Fig. 4F-G). PSMC2 silence promoted the ubiquitination level of EGFR protein in Huh7 cells (Fig. S2B). Taken together, these findings suggest that PSMC2 regulates AKT and ERK1/2 phosphorylation by interacting with and modulating EGFR protein stability in HCC.

EGFR overexpression reverses PSMC2 knockdown-induced phenotypes in HCC cells

To investigate whether EGFR overexpression could rescue the effects of PSMC2 knockdown in HCC cells, we first examined EGFR-related signaling pathways. Western blot analysis confirmed that EGFR overexpression in PSMC2-depleted Huh7 cells restored the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, AKT, and ERK1/2 (Fig. 5A), suggesting reactivation of downstream oncogenic signaling. Functional assays further demonstrated that EGFR overexpression reversed the inhibitory effects of PSMC2 knockdown on HCC cells. CCK-8 assays revealed that EGFR restoration significantly rescued the reduced viability of PSMC2-silenced Huh7 and Hep3B cells (Fig. 5B-C, ***P < 0.001). Similarly, colony formation assays showed that EGFR overexpression partially restored the impaired clonogenic ability of PSMC2-silenced HCC cells (Fig. 5D-E, ***P < 0.001). Moreover, EGFR overexpression mitigated the suppression of migration and invasion caused by PSMC2 knockdown. Wound healing assays demonstrated that EGFR-reconstituted cells exhibited significantly enhanced wound closure compared to PSMC2-silenced group (Fig. 5F-H, ***P < 0.001). Transwell invasion assays further confirmed that EGFR overexpression increased the number of invading cells in both Huh7 and Hep3B PSMC2-knockdown models (Fig. 5I-J, ***P < 0.001). Collectively, these results indicate that EGFR overexpression counteracts the tumor-suppressive effects of PSMC2 knockdown by reactivating proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities in HCC cells.

PSMC2 knockdown suppresses HCC tumor growth in vivo

To assess the role of PSMC2 in HCC progression in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using Huh7 cells stably transfected with shPSMC2 or shNC. Tumors derived from shPSMC2-transfected cells were significantly smaller than those in the shNC group (Fig. 6A, n = 5). Tumor growth curves further confirmed that PSMC2 knockdown markedly reduced tumor volume over time (Fig. 6B, n = 5, mean volumes of shNC group 889.9 mm3, mean volumes of shPSMC2 group 536.2 mm3, ***P < 0.001). Consistently, the excised tumors in the shPSMC2 group exhibited significantly lower weight compared to the shNC group (Fig. 6C, n = 5, ***P < 0.01). IHC analysis of tumor tissues revealed that PSMC2 knockdown downregulated EGFR expression and reduced the proliferation marker Ki67, while increasing the apoptotic marker caspase-3 (Fig. 6D-E). These findings indicate that PSMC2 depletion inhibits HCC tumor growth in vivo by suppressing proliferation and promoting apoptosis, further supporting its critical role in HCC progression.

Discussion

The present study elucidates the critical role of PSMC2 in HCC progression, revealing its oncogenic function through interaction with EGFR. Our analysis of TCGA data and clinical samples revealed that PSMC2 is overexpressed in HCC, correlating with poor patient prognosis, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. Functionally, PSMC2 depletion suppressed HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, while its overexpression promoted these malignant phenotypes. Mechanistically, PSMC2 was found to physically interact with EGFR, stabilizing its protein levels and enhancing phosphorylation of downstream AKT and ERK1/2 pathways, thereby promoting tumor aggressiveness. Rescue experiments confirmed that EGFR overexpression reversed the effects of PSMC2 knockdown, highlighting the critical role of the PSMC2-EGFR axis in HCC progression. These findings position PSMC2 as a potential therapeutic target for HCC intervention.

Previous studies have demonstrated PSMC2 overexpression to be associated with poor prognosis and aggressive tumor behaviors. The critical role of PSMC2 in tumor development was first reported in 2012, loss-of-function demonstrating its essentiality for tumor cell survival [23]. Subsequently, multiple studies have revealed that PSMC2 promotes malignant progression across various cancers through distinct molecular mechanisms in addition to the above mentioned. For instance, in breast cancer, PSMC2 drives tumor invasion and metastasis by upregulating PLAU (urokinase-type plasminogen activator) [24]; in skin cutaneous melanoma, it facilitates tumor progression by modulating the GSK3β-β-catenin signaling pathway [25]; and in gastric cancer, PSMC2 regulates both in vitro and in vivo oncogenic functions via the RPS15A/mTOR pathway [26]. Notably, a synthesis of existing research indicates that PSMC2 participates in regulating the AKT signaling pathway in multiple malignancies-such as ovarian cancer, prostate cancer, oral squamous cell carcinoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and glioma—thereby influencing key cellular behaviors including proliferation, apoptosis, and migration [18–22]. Aberrant activation of AKT signaling plays a pivotal role in the initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance of numerous human cancers [27, 28]. However, although PSMC2 has been consistently implicated in AKT-mediated tumor promotion across cancer types, the precise mechanism underlying PSMC2’s regulation of AKT remains unclear. Classical upstream regulators of AKT include EGFR, PTEN, and PI3K [27]. In this study, we uncovered for the first time that PSMC2 modulates EGFR expression at the protein level (rather than transcriptional level). Experimental results demonstrated that PSMC2 knockdown significantly reduced both total and phosphorylated EGFR levels, accompanied by suppressed phosphorylation of downstream AKT and ERK1/2. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays further confirmed a direct physical interaction between PSMC2 and EGFR. These findings suggest that PSMC2 may stabilize EGFR protein and enhance its signal transduction, thereby activating the AKT and ERK1/2 pathways to ultimately drive tumor progression.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the EGFR signaling pathway plays a central regulatory role in malignant biological behaviors of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), including metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance [15, 29, 30]. As an important intracellular protein degradation system, the 26 S proteasome has been confirmed to participate in EGFR signal regulation through multiple mechanisms. For instance, the PSMD9 subunit can maintain sustained activation of EGFR signaling by interacting with c-Cbl to block EGFR degradation [31]. In prostate cancer, the PSMC4 subunit promotes tumor progression by mediating the CBX3-EGFR-PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway [32]. Our research team has discovered that PSMC2, as a crucial regulatory subunit of the 26 S proteasome, is also involved in regulating the EGFR signaling pathway. Through establishing a rescue experimental system combining PSMC2 knockdown with EGFR overexpression, we observed that EGFR overexpression significantly restored the decreased phosphorylation levels of EGFR, AKT, and ERK1/2 caused by PSMC2 deficiency, and effectively rescued the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of HCC cells. These results not only confirm the critical role of EGFR in the PSMC2 regulatory network but also suggest that PSMC2 may be an important regulatory molecule for EGFR signal activation in HCC. Notably, abnormal activation of the EGFR signaling pathway has been confirmed as a major cause of treatment resistance in liver cancer. Emerging evidence indicates that lenvatinib treatment, while effectively suppressing FGFR signaling, paradoxically triggers compensatory activation of the EGFR-PAK2-ERK5 axis in HCC, thereby contributing to drug resistance. Genetic ablation of EGFR expression has been shown to markedly restore lenvatinib sensitivity in HCC models [30]. Similarly, PSMD9 knockout has been shown to inhibit HCC progression and increase sensitivity to erlotinib in both in vitro and in vivo experiments by suppressing EGFR signaling [31]. Another study has confirmed that PSMC2 can promote temozolomide resistance in glioblastoma by inhibiting JNK-mediated autophagy [33]. The mechanism of PSMC2 in HCC treatment sensitivity has not been reported. The findings of this study demonstrate that PSMC2 plays a crucial role in regulating EGFR and its downstream signaling pathways, suggesting that PSMC2 may serve as an important regulatory factor of EGFR. Fan et al. have demonstrated that GOLM1 forms a positive feedback loop via the EGFR‑STAT3‑CSN5 axis and inhibits the proteasomal degradation pathway, thereby mediating lenvatinib resistance in HCC. Targeting GOLM1 can effectively synergize with lenvatinib to exert anti‑tumor effects [34]. Notably, the proteasome has emerged as an important therapeutic target for various cancers, with proteasome inhibitors demonstrating remarkable potential in overcoming tumor drug resistance [35].

PSMC2, as an important ATPase subunit of the 19 S regulatory particle of the 26 S proteasome, participates in tumor progression mainly by maintaining intracellular protein homeostasis and regulating multiple oncogenic signaling pathways. The proteasome serves as the central machinery for MHC class I–mediated antigen processing and presentation, which determines the peptide repertoire and immunogenicity of hepatocytes and HCC cells. Ubiquitination directs protein substrates for proteasomal degradation, and alterations in proteasome regulatory subunits can disrupt proteasome activity, reshaping the antigen landscape and impairing CD8 + T cell recognition. Dysregulation of this pathway facilitates immune evasion in HCC [36]. PSMC2 also plays a key driving role in tumor immune escape [37]. In addition, PSMC2 significantly upregulates the expression of immune checkpoint molecules activating NF-κB, PI3K/AKT and other signaling pathways, directly leading to T-cell exhaustion and functional suppression [22]. On the other hand, PSMC2 downregulates proteins involved in antigen processing and presentation, weakening the recognition of tumor cells by effector T cells and further facilitating tumor immune escape [38]. Targeted inhibition of PSMC2 is expected to reverse tumor immune escape and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy [39].

Therefore, the malignant phenotypes of hepatocellular carcinoma regulated by the PSMC2–EGFR axis reveal a newly identified signaling cascade involved in HCC progression, which may serve as a promising potential therapeutic target for hepatocellular carcinoma.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrated that PSMC2 upregulation was associated with a poor prognosis in HCC patients. Our results demonstrate, to our knowledge, for the first time that PSMC2 promotes HCC cell proliferation and metastasis via interaction with EGFR activation of the downstream signaling. Our data highlight the molecular mechanism of PSMC2 in HCC progression and provide potential targets for HCC prognosis evaluation and treatment.
