Plasma leukotriene B4 decreases after bariatric–metabolic surgery in severe obesity: A prospective observational study
Authors: Seiji Nishikage, Yushi Hirota, Yasushi Nakagawa, Masakazu Shinohara, Yusei Hosokawa, Shinsuke Nakajima, Kai Yoshimura, Akane Yamamoto, Tomofumi Takayoshi, Seiichi Kitahama, Wataru Ogawa
Published: 2025

BACKGROUND

Obesity increases the risk of diabetes and other metabolic disorders. 1 Bariatric–metabolic surgery (BMS) achieves durable weight loss and glycaemic improvement, 2 yet the biological mediators of these benefits remain incompletely defined. Low‐grade inflammation is a key feature of obesity. 3 Among inflammatory pathways, lipid mediators (LMs) integrate immune and metabolic signalling: leukotriene B4 (LTB4) is a potent pro‐inflammatory LM generated by 5‐lipoxygenase (5‐LOX) and signalling via its high‐affinity receptor BLT1, whereas specialised pro‐resolving mediators (resolvins, protectins and maresins) counter‐regulate inflammation. 4 , 5 Animal studies implicate LM imbalance in obesity‐related metabolic dysfunction, 6 , 7 but human data are limited, particularly for plasma measurements and postoperative changes. Because LM levels are susceptible to ex vivo coagulation and cell activation, 8 serum measurements can be artefactually altered; standardised plasma assays are preferable yet remain less common in human studies.

We therefore performed a prospective, exploratory study in severe obesity, comprehensively profiling plasma LMs before and 12 months after BMS using targeted LC–MS/MS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This prospective observational study was designed to investigate changes in plasma LM levels between before and after BMS and their association with clinical parameters. The clinical cohort was single‐centre at Chibune General Hospital; plasma LM assays were performed centrally at Kobe University. The protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Review Board of Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine (approval no. B190120; approval date, 24 September 2019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study period was from 24 September 2019 to 31 March 2025.

Participants and sample size

Adults (≥20 years) scheduled for BMS at Chibune General Hospital were eligible; exclusions were severe comorbidities or investigator judgement. This was a pilot/exploratory study; with no prior human effect‐size estimates for plasma LMs, no formal power calculation was feasible. Limited power, especially for small to moderate correlations, is acknowledged.

Data collection

Assessments at baseline and 1, 3, 6 and 12 months; the primary comparison was baseline versus 12 months. Body composition (InBody 270, InBody Japan) and CT‐derived adiposity metrics and liver‐to‐spleen ratio (L/S) ratio were obtained per standardised procedures (Figure S1). Plasma for LMs, type IV collagen 7S and free fatty acids (FFAs) was stored at −80°C and analysed centrally; other labs were measured locally. Participant flow is shown in Figure S2.

Measurement of LMs

Targeted LC–MS/MS lipidomics was performed as described with deuterated internal standards, 8 automated C18 SPE and QTRAP 6500. A panel of 40 LMs was assayed; eight were consistently below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) in paired samples and not tested, leaving 32 quantifiable mediators.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in plasma LM levels between baseline and 12 months after surgery. Secondary outcomes included a restriction analysis in participants without antidiabetic medications throughout follow‐up. Exploratory analyses assessed associations between changes in LM levels and changes in clinical measures.

Statistical analysis

LMs are summarised as median [interquartile range]. Pre–post comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (two‐sided), reporting the Hodges–Lehmann median difference (Δ = post−pre) with 95% confidence intervals. For the 32‐mediator family, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 5% using Benjamini–Hochberg; q values are shown alongside unadjusted p. Correlations used Spearman ρ (two‐sided). Changes in medication use were analysed with McNemar test. Software: SPSS v29.0 and EZR. Significance thresholds followed the above specification (q < 0.05 for FDR‐controlled families; otherwise two‐sided p < 0.05).

RESULTS

Participants

Forty individuals were enrolled between 11 January and 18 August 2020 (Figure S2). Follow‐up was completed on 17 September 2021. Four participants discontinued postoperative visits and one had missing samples; thus, 35 participants (mean age 41.7 ± 11.0 years; 57.1% female; mean BMI 41.2 ± 7.3 kg/m2) were included in the analysis. No severe adverse events occurred.

Changes in clinical parameters

The clinical characteristics of participants at baseline and 12 months after surgery are summarised in Table 1. Body weight, BMI, body fat mass and visceral fat area decreased; the L/S increased. Indices of insulin resistance and HbA1c improved; HDL‐C increased. Use of antidiabetic and antihypertensive medications declined (Table 1).

Medication‐free restriction analysis

Findings were directionally consistent in participants without antidiabetic medications (n = 21): LTB4 decreased with nominal significance (p = 0.002) but did not survive FDR across 32 mediators (q = 0.064) (Table S1).

Correlation between plasma LTB 4 levels and clinical parameters

In exploratory Spearman analyses, no correlation between changes in LTB4 and changes in clinical measures reached statistical significance (Table S2).

CONCLUSIONS

We performed a comprehensive analysis of plasma LM profiles before and after substantial weight loss achieved by BMS in individuals with severe obesity. Among the 32 quantifiable LMs, only LTB4 showed a statistically significant change after FDR control, with a pronounced postoperative decrease. To address potential confounding by antidiabetic therapy, 9 we conducted a restriction analysis in participants not receiving antidiabetic medications; the direction and magnitude of the LTB4 change were similar, although multiplicity‐adjusted significance was not retained in this smaller subgroup—findings consistent with limited statistical power.

In exploratory Spearman analyses, changes in LTB4 were not significantly correlated with changes in clinical measures. Given the modest sample, type‐II error remains possible, and trends involving weight‐loss indices (e.g., percentage excess weight loss), body fat percentage, and FFAs warrant re‐evaluation in adequately powered cohorts.

Preclinical work has linked LTB4–BLT1 signalling to adipose‐tissue inflammation and systemic insulin resistance, and pharmacologic or genetic interruption of the 5‐LOX–LTB4–BLT1 axis improves metabolic parameters in obese models. 7 , 10 , 11 Our results extend this literature by providing human, plasma‐based evidence that LTB4 declines after surgical weight loss. Prior clinical studies using serum did not observe a significant change in LTB4 after bariatric surgery because eicosanoids are sensitive to coagulation and cell activation; serum measurements can be artefactually altered. 12 The use of standardised plasma assays here addresses this limitation and may explain the discrepant findings.

There are several limitations to our study. First, it was an exploratory, single‐centre surgical cohort and no formal power calculation; precision is limited, particularly for correlations. Second, the primary analysis focused on plasma LM profiles, and tissue LM measurements were not obtained, precluding mechanistic inference about anatomic sources of circulating changes. Third, although the medication‐free analysis mitigates confounding by antidiabetic drugs, residual confounding from other clinical changes cannot be excluded. Finally, generalisability to non‐surgical weight‐loss settings (e.g., lifestyle or pharmacotherapy) remains to be established.

In conclusion, BMS was associated with a robust, FDR‐controlled reduction in plasma LTB4 over 12 months. Although correlations with clinical improvements were not significant in this exploratory analysis, the consistency and biological plausibility of the LTB4 signal suggest it may serve as a potential candidate biomarker, pending validation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Figure S1. Study design and data collection schedule. The study included five visits: baseline (visit 1) as well as 1 month (visit 2), 3 months (visit 3), 6 months (visit 4) and 12 months (visit 5) after surgery. At each visit, various clinical and laboratory data were collected. Anthropometric measurements included body weight, BMI, muscle mass, body fat mass, body fat percentage, skeletal muscle mass, skeletal muscle index (SMI). CT scans were performed to evaluate visceral, subcutaneous and total fat areas as well as the liver‐to‐spleen (L/S) ratio as a measure of liver fat content. Laboratory assessments included glycaemic parameters (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, fasting insulin, fasting C‐peptide, HOMA‐IR and Adipo‐IR), lipid parameters (LDL‐C, HDL‐C, TG and FFAs), liver function tests (AST, ALT, γ‐GTP, type IV collagen 7S, Fib‐4 index and NAFLD fibrosis score), kidney function markers (creatinine, eGFR and uric acid), haematologic parameters (including platelet count) and inflammatory markers (CRP and albumin). The adipokines leptin and adiponectin were also measured. Medication use—including for diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia treatment—was documented at each visit. The plasma concentrations of 40 LMs, including pro‐inflammatory and pro‐resolving mediators, were measured.

Figure S2. Flow diagram of participant enrollment and analysis. All 40 individuals assessed for eligibility provided informed consent and were enrolled. Five participants were subsequently excluded: four as a result of loss to follow‐up during the study period and one as a result of missing samples. The final dataset for analysis therefore included 35 individuals.
