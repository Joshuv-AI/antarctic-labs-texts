Introduction

Pain is the most common reason for consultation in paediatric emergency departments (PED) yet remains frequently undertreated.1 This is especially true for severe traumatic pain, where timely and effective analgesia is crucial. Intravenous opioid administration, the standard treatment for severe pain,2 is often delayed in children due to the technical challenges and emotional distress associated with venous access. Thus, there is a need for alternative, rapid, non-invasive analgesic strategies. Intranasal (IN) delivery offers a fast and needle-free route for administering analgesics. The nasal mucosa is highly vascularised and allows rapid systemic absorption, avoiding first-pass metabolism. In emergency paediatric settings, IN analgesia has demonstrated both efficacy and acceptability among healthcare providers, patients, and caregivers.3,5 Ketamine, a dissociative anaesthetic with analgesic and sedative properties, is increasingly used intranasally for procedural sedation and pain management in children. It provides effective pain relief without significant hypotension and has a favourable safety profile, particularly in short-term emergency use.6,8 Sufentanil, a potent µ-opioid receptor agonist, is 7–10 times more potent than fentanyl. Compared with ketamine, IN opioids such as fentanyl or sufentanil provide rapid and potent analgesia with fewer psychodysphoric effects. While commonly used in adult emergency medicine9,12 and paediatric anaesthesia,13 its IN use in children for acute pain has not been adequately studied. Previous trials have compared IN ketamine with IN fentanyl in children with limb fractures, demonstrating feasibility and analgesic efficacy.14 To date, only one paediatric study has assessed the analgesic effect of an IN ketamine–sufentanil combination.15 However, no randomised controlled trial has yet compared IN ketamine with IN sufentanil in this context. Evidence is thus needed to provide the best care and to establish safe, effective analgesic protocols suitable for paediatric emergencies under real-life emergency conditions. Inhaled equimolar nitrous oxide-oxygen mixture (MEOPA) is widely used as a first-line analgesic and anxiolytic for children presenting with acute traumatic pain and constitutes standard of care in many settings. As a result, IN analgesics are most often administered in addition to MEOPA rather than as standalone treatments.

Thus, the objective of this study is to compare the incremental analgesic effect of IN sufentanil versus IN ketamine administered in addition to standard care including MEOPA, in children presenting to our PED with severe traumatic limb pain. Secondary objectives include the time required to achieve analgesia, the level of sedation, the occurrence of adverse events, the use of rescue analgesia, and the satisfaction reported by children and their parents.

Methods and analysis

Study aim

To determine whether IN sufentanil provides greater analgesia than IN ketamine at 30 min when administered in addition to standard care in children with severe traumatic limb pain.

Study design

This trial is designed as a phase II, single-centre, randomised controlled superiority study with two parallel arms. Recruitment will take place at the PED of Lenval Children’s Hospital in Nice, France, over a 24-month period. Study procedure

Patients participating in the study will be recruited from our PED. On admission, children aged 6 to 17 years weighing over 15 kg and presenting with severe limb trauma pain are initially managed according to standard department procedures. Pain is assessed by the triage nurse using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). If no analgesia has yet been given, oral paracetamol (15 mg/kg) and/or ibuprofen may be administered according to weight and routine clinical practice. The child is then transferred to a designated care area and placed in a supine position under cardiorespiratory monitoring.

All the investigators involved in patient enrolment and study procedures will receive standardised training prior to study initiation covering eligibility criteria, IN drug administration using a MAD, pain and sedation assessment, and adverse event reporting.

Eligibility and informed consent

Before starting any other treatment, the investigator assesses eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. If the child meets these requirements, the investigator provides both the parents or legal guardians and the child with clear written and verbal information regarding the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and anticipated benefits. A period of reflection is allowed while the child is monitored and continues to receive routine analgesia, ensuring that consent is given freely and without pressure. No study-specific procedures are initiated before consent has been obtained. Once written informed consent from the parent(s) or legal guardian and assent from the child, when appropriate, have been secured, randomisation is performed through a secure electronic system.

Randomisation and blinding

After obtaining signed consent and confirming eligibility, the child is randomised using the REDCap electronic system to receive either IN sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg) or IN ketamine (1 mg/kg). Randomisation is available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week and is implemented in a 1:1 ratio using block randomisation stratified by age group (6–11 and 12–17 years) and sex. The allocation sequence is automated and is supervised by the Department of Clinical Research and Innovation at the Nice University Hospitals (France).

Parents and patients will remain blinded to the allocated treatment. Investigators will not be blinded due to differences in drug preparation and administration; however, pain assessment is based on patient-reported outcomes at predefined time points and rescue analgesia criteria are strictly protocolised to minimise performance bias.

Study treatment administration

Whatever the group allocation generated by the randomisation, all children systematically receive inhaled MEOPA to reduce pain and anxiety prior to the administration of the medication. MEOPA is delivered according to institutional protocols, ensuring light sedation while preserving the child’s ability to cooperate.

The study medications—sufentanil and ketamine—are classified as controlled substances and stored in a locked cabinet accessible only to authorised staff. In the sufentanil group, the child receives IN sufentanil at a dose of 0.5 µg/kg, undiluted, using a MAD. Nasal secretions are aspirated beforehand if necessary. The total drug volume is divided equally between both nostrils, including a dead space allowance of 0.1 mL per side. The volumes reported in tables2 3 correspond to the active drug volume and do not include the dead space volume. The final delivered volume per nostril therefore includes an additional 0.1 mL dead space allowance. The syringe is held at a 45° angle to ensure atomisation onto the nasal mucosa.

Postadministration monitoring (60-minute follow-up)

Pain is assessed using the VAS at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 min and continued up to 60 min. Treatment failure is noted if the pain remains severe 30 min after IN administration; defined as a reduction in the VAS score of less than 20 mm or less than 2 points. This 2-point threshold was selected based on previous evidence suggesting that a decrease of at least 2 points on the VAS represents clinically meaningful pain reduction in children.19

In such cases, IV analgesia will be initiated as rescue treatment according to institutional protocols (see table 4), and the study intervention will be discontinued. MEOPA is not considered a rescue analgesic and is not readministered after study drug administration. The patient will remain under clinical monitoring, but further outcome assessments for the trial will be censored beyond this point.

Physiological monitoring

Physiological parameters are closely monitored to ensure patient safety throughout the 60 min of follow-up. Sedation is assessed using the Ramsay Sedation Scale at the same time points as the VAS assessments. Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate and blood pressure are recorded at baseline (T0), every 5 min up to 30 min, and subsequently every 10 min until the end of the 60-minute follow-up.

In case of respiratory depression following administration of sufentanil, naloxone will be administered intravenously at a dose of 0.01 mg/kg (up to a maximum of 0.1 mg/kg per dose, not exceeding 2 mg). The dose may be repeated every 2 min if clinically required.

Safety assessment

Adverse events are prospectively collected throughout the study, and serious adverse events are defined and reported in accordance with regulatory requirements. Events of special interest include excessive sedation, respiratory depression (desaturation, bradypnoea), bradycardia, hypotension, nausea, vomiting and discomfort during IN administration. Adverse event severity will be graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), from grade 1 (mild) to grade 5 (death). This grading is used to standardise severity reporting and does not modify predefined criteria for protocol discontinuation or withdrawal, which remain based on clinical judgement and patient safety.

Study completion and follow-up

Final assessment of pain and sedation is performed at 60 min. Parent and child satisfaction is measured using age-adapted questionnaires. Study participation ends at that point and the child resumes standard clinical care based on their condition which may include discharge, further investigations or hospital admission. For the purposes of the study, the 60-minute follow-up period was chosen to focus on immediate analgesic efficacy and short-term safety after IN administration in the emergency setting.

Medical device

A MAD will be used for IN drug administration in all patients. This single-use, non-invasive device allows for the atomisation of liquid medication into a fine mist, enhancing absorption through the highly vascularised nasal mucosa and ensuring rapid systemic effect.

Prior to administration, the device will be primed with 0.1 mL of sterile saline to ensure proper atomisation. The medications (sufentanil or ketamine) are delivered into each nostril in divided volumes as per predefined dosing tables based on patient weight. The atomiser is positioned at a 45° angle to optimise mucosal coverage and absorption.

The use of a MAD contributes to the study’s aim of evaluating rapid, needle-free analgesia in a paediatric emergency setting, minimising discomfort and procedural delays commonly associated with intravenous access. All the nurses involved in patient care will receive standardised training in the use of the MAD device.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome will be the change in pain intensity 30 min after administration of the investigational treatment. Pain intensity will be assessed using a validated 100 mm vertical VAS, appropriate for use in children aged 6 years and older. The baseline score (T0) will be recorded immediately before IN administration of the study medication (sufentanil or ketamine), and the follow-up score will be recorded at 30 min (T30). The primary endpoint will be defined as the mean change in VAS score between T0 and T30.

Sample size

The sample size is determined based on previous studies indicating a mean reduction in pain intensity (VAS) at 30 min of 5.2 points (SD 2.5) following IN sufentanil,11 and 3.1 points (SD 1.8) following IN ketamine.20 Although the sufentanil data are derived from adult populations, these values represent the best available estimates in the absence of robust paediatric efficacy data. This study is therefore designed as a phase II exploratory trial to estimate treatment effects and inform the design of future multicentre paediatric studies.

The margin of superiority retained corresponds to 1.0 point (10 mm) in VAS reduction, a choice based on methodological literature in emergency medicine, which identifies the minimum clinically significant difference as 13 mm, with CIs ranging from 9 mm to 17–18 mm.21 22

Assuming this difference of 1.0 point in VAS reduction between groups, with a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 and 80% power, we have calculated that 52 patients per group will be required. Anticipating a 10% dropout rate, we plan to enrol a total of 116 children (58 per group).

This sample size is deemed sufficient to detect a meaningful difference in analgesic efficacy while accounting for the real-world constraints of paediatric emergency recruitment. No interim analysis is planned.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be performed by the statistician from the Department of Clinical Research and Innovation at the Nice University Hospitals using SAS Enterprise Guide V.7.1 (SAS Institute). A two-sided p<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Prior to analysis, the assumptions for each test will be assessed.

The primary analysis will be conducted on a modified intention-to-treat population, which will include all randomised patients who receive at least one dose of the study medication. A per-protocol analysis will be also performed, excluding major protocol deviations, for sensitivity purposes.

The primary outcome—the reduction in pain intensity between baseline (T0) and 30 min (T30), measured by a 100 mm VAS—will be assessed using an analysis of covariance model. The model will include the treatment group as the main fixed effect and will be adjusted for age, sex and baseline VAS score.

Concomitant treatments such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) administration and limb immobilisation will be recorded descriptively as part of standard care; no stratified or subgroup analyses based on these variables are planned.

Data management

All study data will be collected using a dedicated electronic case report form (e-CRF) built with the RedCap software. The design, setup and deployment of the e-CRF, including user access and training, will be managed by the Data Management Unit of the Department of the Clinical Research and Innovation at the Nice University Hospitals. Investigators will enter clinical data directly into the system, supported by a clinical research associate (CRA) to ensure completeness and accuracy.

Data access will be restricted through role-based permissions to guarantee confidentiality and data integrity. Regular data monitoring visits will be conducted by the sponsor’s CRA team to check the accuracy of data entries and protocol compliance. Data quality control procedures will include automated consistency checks and manual review of entered data.

Once data entry is complete, a comprehensive data validation process will be performed by the data manager. All data changes will be tracked via an audit trail. Following resolution of any data queries, a final database lock will be conducted and signed off by the principal investigator, data manager and study sponsor. The locked database will be then transferred to the study statistician for final analysis.

Discussion

Effective and timely management of acute pain in children remains a critical challenge in emergency medicine. Despite the availability of potent analgesics, paediatric patients frequently experience delays in adequate pain control, often due to barriers to intravenous access and concerns regarding opioid use. IN administration presents an attractive alternative, offering rapid onset, ease of use and improved patient acceptability.

Ketamine is widely used intranasally in paediatric emergency settings, supported by a growing body of literature demonstrating its effectiveness and safety. Sufentanil, although well-established in adult anaesthesia and critical care, has been less extensively studied via the IN route in children, particularly in direct comparison with ketamine. Its high potency, rapid onset and short half-life nevertheless make it a promising candidate for the management of severe paediatric pain.

This randomised controlled trial is designed to directly compare IN sufentanil and IN ketamine in children presenting with severe traumatic pain in a PED. By evaluating pain relief, sedation, adverse events, patient and parent satisfaction and the need for rescue analgesia, the study aims to generate robust data on both the efficacy and short-term safety under real-world emergency conditions.

The pragmatic design of the trial, conducted in a high-volume PED and incorporating standard care practices, is expected to enhance the external validity of the findings.

The results of this study may help inform clinical practice and contribute to the development of evidence-based IN analgesic strategies for children, including potential applications in prehospital care and other acute care settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The systematic administration of inhaled MEOPA, required by national regulations and reflecting standard care in French PEDs, may influence baseline pain intensity. However, as MEOPA is administered identically in both groups, the study is designed to assess the incremental analgesic effect of IN sufentanil versus IN ketamine under real-world conditions rather than their efficacy as a monotherapy. In addition, the single-blind design, with unblinded investigators, may introduce performance bias despite predefined outcome measures and protocolised rescue analgesia criteria.

Furthermore, the larger IN volume required for ketamine, related to formulation and dosing requirements, represents an inherent limitation of this pragmatic comparison, although administration procedures and delivered volumes are strictly standardised in both groups. Sedation is assessed using the Ramsay Sedation Scale, which is the standard tool used in our institution and embedded in local emergency department protocols. While pragmatic and widely used in French emergency settings, it may not capture all aspects of ketamine-induced dissociative states.

The 60-minute follow-up was designed to evaluate immediate analgesic efficacy and short-term safety after IN administration in the emergency setting. Delayed pain recurrence and late adverse events will not be assessed. In addition, the single-centre setting and the short follow-up period may limit the generalisability of the findings and preclude assessment of longer-term outcomes. Further studies conducted in different emergency department settings, including multicentre designs, would help to confirm the generalisability of the findings.

Ethics and dissemination

Consent or assent

Parents or legal guardians will receive a parent/guardian information sheet and will be given verbal explanations about the study by a member of the clinical team in the PED. They will be informed that refusal to participate will not affect the child’s medical care.

Written informed consent will be obtained from both legal guardians whenever possible. Examples of the participant information sheets and consent/assent forms adapted for parents, children and adolescents are provided as onlinesupplemental files 13. If only one guardian is present, verbal consent from the second guardian may be obtained by telephone prior to inclusion, and written confirmation will be collected as soon as feasible. Assent from the child will also be sought in accordance with their age and level of understanding.

The study was approved by the French Ethics Committee CPP Sud Méditerranée V and authorised by the French National Agency for Medicines and Health Products Safety, IDRCB n°2025-521075-31-00.

Confidentiality

Personal data of participants will be collected and stored in a secure, pseudonymised manner. Each participant will be assigned a unique study identification number, and the correspondence table linking identifiers to personal data will be stored separately on a secure hospital server with restricted access. Data entered into the e-CRF (REDCap) will not contain directly identifiable information. Access to study data will be restricted via role-based permission and all data modifications will be documented through audit trails.

All data handling will comply with applicable French data protection regulations and the General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679). Data will be archived securely in accordance with regulatory requirements.

Dissemination policy

The results of the study will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed scientific journals and presentation at national and international scientific conferences. The findings may also be communicated within academic and clinical networks involved in paediatric emergency care.

Trial status

The current protocol version is V0.2 (September 2025).

The study is registered in the EU Clinical Trials Information System under the identifier: 2025-521075-31-00 and on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT06968546.

At the time of manuscript submission, recruitment has not yet started. Participant enrolment is planned to begin in January 2026, with an anticipated completion in December 2027.

All study procedures will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable regulatory requirements.
