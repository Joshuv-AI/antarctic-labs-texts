CASE PRESENTATION

A 67-year-old man presented to the emergency department with right thigh and lower back pain for 2 months, which had worsened over the past week. He denied preceding trauma, radiculopathy or other neurological symptoms. He also noticed abdominal distension for 1 week but did not experience abdominal pain, vomiting or fever. His past medical history was significant for poorly differentiated appendiceal adenocarcinoma that was initially complicated by intestinal obstruction, for which he underwent a right hemicolectomy 6 years before. He had known peritoneal metastases and was on active chemotherapy.

On examination, the patient was afebrile, with blood pressure of 82/61 mmHg, heart rate of 107 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 32 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. He appeared lethargic. His abdomen was distended but soft and nontender, and examination of the right lower limb was unremarkable with no obvious swelling and an intact neurovascular status. Laboratory results at presentation showed white blood cell count of 3.41 (normal range: 3.37–11.03) ×109/L, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein of 188 (≤5.0) mg/L and venous lactate of 7.3 (0.5–2.2) mmol/L.

An abdominal radiograph was performed to assess the abdominal distension, and multiphasic computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis was also requested to exclude ischaemic bowel as a cause for the raised lactate. IMAGE INTERPRETATION

The abdominal radiographs [Figure 1] show mottled linear lucencies in the right paravertebral region at the T12–L1 vertebral level, extending inferiorly and adjacent to the right lesser trochanter (arrows). In view of these findings, gas within the psoas muscle should be suspected, of which infection is an important cause. The colonic loops are dilated, which raises the suspicion for large bowel obstruction. No pneumoperitoneum is seen.

The CT images [Figure 2] show features of large bowel obstruction, secondary to a mass-like transition point at the rectosigmoid (not shown). Figure 2a shows dilated colonic loops and discontinuity of a right-sided colonic loop along its posterior wall (curved arrows in 2a), in communication with an abscess (Ab) containing feculent material. The abscess, in turn, is inseparable from the right psoas muscle (Ps), which contains gas. Pneumoretroperitoneum is also seen (arrow in 2a). Figure 2b shows intramuscular gas along the course of the psoas muscle (Ps), some of which has dissected into the subcutaneous plane (arrow in 2b).

DIAGNOSIS

Gas-containing right psoas abscess from colonic perforation.

CLINICAL COURSE

The patient received fluid resuscitation, vasopressors and intravenous antibiotics and was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). He was also urgently referred to General Surgery and Interventional Radiology for consideration of source control, but this was deemed to be futile, given the extent of infection, the patient’s unstable clinical condition, and the low likelihood of achieving definitive bowel repair even after temporising measures with percutaneous drainage. A blood culture later yielded polymicrobial gram-negative bacteraemia (Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae). Unfortunately, the patient continued to deteriorate and became increasingly obtunded overnight. After discussions with the patient’s family, the ICU team decided to pursue comfort-focused measures, and the patient passed away the next morning.

DISCUSSION

Psoas abscess is a rare, potentially disastrous yet difficult to diagnose condition. It can be classified into primary and secondary aetiologies. In one review, secondary abscesses were found in 89% of cases, with causes such as gastrointestinal disease, spinal infections and postsurgical complications.[12] It is thought that the rich vascular supply of the muscle provides an ideal environment for abscess formation.

Most patients have a nonspecific clinical presentation, but common symptoms include abdominal pain and lower extremity pain. Inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein level are typically elevated. Common organisms include E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Psoas abscesses have been classically associated with tuberculous spondylodiscitis, which has declined in incidence[1] but is anecdotally still seen locally.

The psoas muscle arises from the T12 to L5 vertebral transverse processes and joins the iliacus muscle to form an iliopsoas tendon, which has tendinous and direct muscular attachments to the lesser trochanter of the femur. The combined iliopsoas muscle is the primary flexor of the hip and is located within the iliopsoas space, an extraperitoneal compartment.[3] It is intimately related to structures such as the retroperitoneal colon and appendix, which explains the direct spread of infection from these structures. In some individuals, the iliopsoas bursa (which lies deep to the iliopsoas tendon) communicates with the hip joint capsule, representing a potential route for spread into the joint.

Figure 3 shows the normal appearance of the psoas muscles on different imaging modalities. Radiographs are not sensitive for detecting psoas abscess. Nonetheless, the psoas muscles are usually visible on abdominal radiography and should be assessed. Abnormalities of the psoas, such as gas[4] and ill definition, can be seen on radiography — the latter is sometimes termed the ‘psoas sign’ and can suggest pathology in relation to the muscle.[5] However, some nonpathological confounding factors that can obscure the psoas shadow include scoliosis, sparse retroperitoneal fat, closely apposed kidney or bowel, or a globular shape of the muscle.[5] Gas strongly indicates infection but is not pathognomonic.[6] In our case, the large amount of gas within the psoas muscle could be contributed by fistulation from the bowel, in addition to gas-forming infection. Radiographs are also useful in showing features that suggest gastrointestinal pathology, such as dilated bowel loops or pneumoperitoneum.

(a) Radiograph, (b) oblique-coronal contrast-enhanced CT image and (c) T2-W MR image (acquired as part of an MR cholangiopancreatography study) show normal appearances of the psoas muscles.

Computed tomography is preferred for confirming the diagnosis and delineating the extent of the abscess, as collections and gas are easily demonstrated, with sensitivity close to 100%.[7] On CT, abscesses are seen as enlargement of the psoas muscle from low-density collections, which can also show rim enhancement after the administration of intravenous contrast. Other findings include increased attenuation of the surrounding fat with indistinct tissue planes (‘fat stranding’), gas and destruction of the adjacent bones.[3] An aetiology is sometimes suggested based on CT findings alone, which invariably influences management.

Gastrointestinal causes include inflammatory and infective aetiologies such as appendicitis, diverticulitis and Crohn’s disease. While reactive bowel wall inflammation can result from a psoas abscess, the presence of adjunct features, such as an inflamed appendix or diverticula at the centre of these changes, can point to the diagnosis. In Crohn’s disease, transmural inflammation can be complicated by perforation or fistulation. A high clinical suspicion is required, as lower extremity symptoms can mistakenly be ascribed to arthritis, an associated feature of inflammatory bowel disease.[8] Figure 4 shows a psoas abscess secondary to caecal Crohn’s disease.

Perforated colonic cancer is a known cause, but it is very rare, with an incidence of 0.3%–0.4%.[9] Features that suggest perforated colonic cancer as the aetiology include CT findings of irregular bowel wall thickening adjacent to the abscess, or nonresolving bowel wall thickening on follow-up scans in the context of resolving inflammatory changes elsewhere. Figure 5 shows a case of a psoas abscess secondary to perforated caecal adenocarcinoma. Microbiology can be instructive, as cultures growing gut flora should prompt consideration of a gastrointestinal source.[9]

A 69-year-old man presented to the emergency department with acute right inguinal pain and was found to have a focal inguinal swelling on examination. (a) Axial and (b) coronal contrast-enhanced CT images show an abscess within the right iliopsoas muscle (arrows) inseparable from mass-like thickening of the caecum. At surgery, a caecal mass was found to have perforated into the psoas muscle, and a right hemicolectomy was performed. This was confirmed at histology to be adenocarcinoma. C: caecum

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is probably more useful in the context of spondylitis, where it is the preferred modality to assess bone marrow signal, the epidural space and compression of neurological structures. Figure 6 demonstrates a case of tuberculous spondylodiscitis that was initially suspected on radiographs. Magnetic resonance imaging allowed superior visualisation of the disease extent, including psoas muscle involvement. If a gastrointestinal cause is suspected, however, MRI is seldom performed in the acute phase. Ultrasonography is less commonly used owing to its lower sensitivity and specificity, but it occupies an important role for certain populations such as children[10] and pregnant women, for whom ionising radiation should be minimised.

Gallium-67 radiotracer imaging can help localise infection to the psoas if there is no clinically apparent source. Single-photon emission computed tomography or CT is generally deemed superior to planar imaging for this application.[11]

On imaging, the main differential diagnoses for abscess include psoas muscle haematomas and neoplasm. Haematomas typically appear on CT as diffuse hyperdense enlargement of the psoas muscle in the acute phase, although a chronic haematoma can present as a focal hypodense collection and mimic abscess more closely. Magnetic resonance imaging can be useful in determining the age of the haematoma.[3] Neoplasia of the psoas muscle mostly comprises metastatic deposits from contiguous deposits (such as vertebral lesions, colorectal or genitourinary tract tumours, or lymphadenopathy) or haematogenous spread, and less commonly, primary neoplasms (which include liposarcoma, fibrosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma). The management of psoas abscesses typically includes broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy and drainage. In one study, small abscesses (<3.5 cm) responded well to antibiotic therapy alone.[1] Ultrasonography- or CT-guided percutaneous drainage is considered an effective and minimally invasive option, although the success rates reportedly vary from 40% to over 90%.[112] Additionally, drainage can allow identification of the offending organism and guide antibiotic choice. For abscesses caused by gastrointestinal causes,[1] some authors advocate surgical management, for example, aggressive surgical resection of affected organs in cases of perforated colorectal cancers.[13] Early surgical intervention is also thought to be preferable for gas-forming abscesses.[14]

In summary, psoas abscesses are a rare but established complication of gastrointestinal disease, among other causes. CT remains the key imaging modality to confirm the diagnosis, assess the extent and identify underlying aetiologies. Radiographs should be carefully evaluated for any abnormalities of the psoas muscle, as they are often obtained initially and can raise the possibility of a psoas pathology.
