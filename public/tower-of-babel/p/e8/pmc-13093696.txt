Paramagnetic Rim Lesions in Highly Active Multiple Sclerosis at the Time of Disease Diagnosis: Prevalence Data and Clinical Implications
Authors: Alessandra Cicia, Matteo Lucchini, Gianluca Di Fiore, Luca Ausili Cefaro, Assunta Bianco, Viviana Nociti, Vincenzo Carlomagno, Simona Gaudino, Massimiliano Mirabella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundParamagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) are emerging MRI biomarkers of smoldering inflammation in multiple sclerosis with prognostic relevance1. However, their prevalence in the early stages of the disease remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis prospective cohort study investigated the prevalence, anatomical distribution, and clinical significance of PRLs in newly diagnosed relapsing MS patients with high disease activity and explored their associations with conventional and non‐conventional MRI measures, serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL), and clinical outcomes.MethodsWe enrolled treatment‐naïve patients who had received an MS diagnosis in the previous 12 months. Patients underwent 3 T brain and spinal MRI including susceptibility‐weighted imaging at baseline, 6, and 12 months, along with neurological and cognitive assessments and sNfL sampling.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 21 patients, with a mean age of 31 and median disease duration of 9 months (IQR 4–17). Median EDSS score was 1.5. All patients presented with spinal cord lesions and 38% showed Gd + lesions. PRLs were detected in 76%, with a mean of 4.9 lesions per subject. Higher PRL burden correlated with greater T2 lesion volume and cortical lesion count, higher EDSS, reduced SDMT scores, and elevated baseline sNfL. PRLs remained stable over 12 months despite treatment; two new PRLs emerged at month 12. sNfL levels were higher in patients with ≥ 4 PRLs and decreased in all during follow‐up.ConclusionsParamagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) are detectable early in RMS and correlate with a more severe clinical and radiological profile. The combined assessment of PRLs and CLs may improve disease monitoring and guide personalized treatment.Keywords: cortical lesions, MRI, paramagnetic rim lesions, smoldering multiple sclerosis, sNfL

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disease [1] characterized by the presence of white matter lesions detectable on MRI as focal areas of hyperintensity on T2‐weighted images. To date, the assessment of gadolinium‐enhancing (Gd+) lesions, as well as new or enlarging lesions on T2‐weighted follow‐up images, represents the main radiological approach guiding disease monitoring [2, 3, 4]; however, it may fail to capture ongoing subclinical disease activity [5]. The recent definition of “smoldering MS [6]” has replaced the traditional dichotomy of relapsing and progressive MS, introducing the concept of a progressive, low‐grade inflammatory process that starts early in the disease history and contributes to long‐term neurodegeneration and disability accumulation, regardless of the occurrence of acute relapses [7]. Brain and spinal cord atrophy, microstructural alterations in normal‐appearing white matter (NAWM), and disrupted connectivity of WM fibers are closely correlated with clinical progression but are not routinely captured by standard MRI protocol [8]. Newer neuroimaging markers that may be integrated into clinical practice are being explored to identify this subtle disease activity and guide treatment strategies [9, 10].

Among them, paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), detectable on susceptibility‐based MRI, have emerged as potential markers of smoldering MS. They are characterized by an inactive demyelinated core with a hypointense border of iron‐laden microglia [8] that sustains chronic inflammation and neurodegeneration. PRLs are highly specific to MS [11] and have been included in the most recent revisions of the diagnostic criteria [12]. Initially described in patients with a progressive disease course, they are increasingly reported in relapsing MS, [5] representing a possible biomarker of early and severe disease progression [8] [10].

Despite their clinical relevance, PRLs are not yet part of routine imaging assessments because of non‐standardized detection methods and uncertain visibility on MRI below 3 Tesla (3 T). This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of PRLs on 3 T MRI in a cohort of newly diagnosed patients with highly active MS and investigate their correlation with conventional radiological measures, such as T2 lesions and black holes, as well as with unconventional markers like cortical lesions. In addition, it explores the clinical outcomes in patients with different PRLs burdens at diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria included previous treatment with disease‐modifying therapy (DMT), contraindications to MRI or contrast administration, and severe comorbidities interfering with study participation. Patients meeting the above criteria were consecutively enrolled based on the availability of eligible patients during the recruitment period.

Patients underwent 3 T brain and complete spinal MRI at the Radiology Department of Fondazione Policlinico Universitario “Agostino Gemelli” at baseline and 6 and 12 months after treatment initiation. Within 1 month of each MRI, patients also underwent a complete neurological evaluation, including a cognitive examination and serum sampling for sNfL analysis.

Clinical data at onset were obtained from medical records and included: age, sex, age at disease onset, disease duration, annualized relapse rate (ARR) in the year before enrollment. Neurological disability status was evaluated using the Expanded Disability Status Score (EDSS) [13] performed by certified neurologists (www.neurostatus.net). Cognitive evaluation was performed using the Brief International Cognitive Assessment for MS (BICAMS) [14]. Primary outcomes included the number of PRLs on MRI at baseline, changes in the number and features of PRLs at 6 and 12 months, and neurological disability at baseline, 6 and 12 months. Secondary outcomes included the occurrence of Confirmed Disability Worsening (CDW), evaluation of cognitive performance, and change in sNfL levels over the same time points in patients with different PRLs burdens. We defined CDW events by an increase in EDSS, either ≥ 1.0 points for patients with a baseline EDSS ≤ 5.5, or ≥ 0.5 points for patients with a baseline EDSS 5.5, confirmed by an EDSS assessment at least 3 months from the worsening [15, 16]. To minimize measurement bias, MRI readers were blinded to clinical and laboratory data.

MRI Imaging Protocol

MRI was performed following the MAGNIMS recommended protocol for MS [17], implemented with susceptibility‐weighted sequences to detect iron‐rim lesions. All MRI scans were performed using a 3 T GE scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA). MRI protocol specifications are described in Data S1.

Lesion segmentation was performed manually by two experienced neuroradiologists using the MIPAV software (Medical Image Processing, Analysis, and Visualization, version 11.2.0, https://mipav.cit.nih.gov/).

The imaging protocol was designed to assess both structural and paramagnetic rim lesions. The following types of lesions were identified and quantified: T2‐hyperintense, T1‐hypointense, gadolinium‐enhancing, and cortical lesions (CLs). The total number and volume of each lesion type were calculated. CLs were assessed following the MAGNIMS consensus recommendations for cortical lesions, as described by Guerts JJG et al. [18]. CLs were defined as focal abnormalities on double inversion recovery (DIR) images, hyperintense compared to adjacent normal‐appearing gray matter, and occupying at least 3 pixels, based on minimally 1 mm 2 in‐plane resolution.

Paramagnetic rim lesions were assessed using a phase map (PHIL‐PHA) derived from susceptibility imaging (3D‐SWAN), in accordance with the NAIMs recommendations [1], and were defined as T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions featuring a discrete rim with paramagnetic properties on a susceptibility‐sensitive MRI sequence. The rim had to show the following characteristics: continuous through at least two‐thirds of the outer edge of the white matter portion of the lesion, colocalized with the edge of all or part of a lesion core that was hyperintense on T2‐weighted images, and discernible on at least two consecutive or in two orthogonal planes. Lesions showing both contrast enhancement and a paramagnetic rim at baseline were classified as PRLs only if the rim persisted beyond the resolution of contrast enhancement on the 6‐month follow‐up MRI. In case of disagreement between the two primary raters, a third neuroradiologist independently reviewed the images to reach consensus.

To study the regional distribution of PRLs, we considered four brain areas: periventricular, juxtacortical, cortical, and infratentorial.

PRLs were assessed at baseline and at 6 and 12‐month follow‐ups and assigned as disappearing, newly appearing, or persisting at follow‐up.

Laboratory Assessment

sNfL levels were assessed using Ella Automated Immunoassay System at baseline and at 6 and 12‐month follow‐ups. sNfL concentrations were measured using an Ella microfluidic immunoassay system (ProteinSimple, Bio‐Techne, San Jose, CA, USA). Blood samples were collected by venipuncture, allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged to separate serum. Serum was divided into 1 mL aliquots and stored at −80°C until analysis. All samples were run in duplicate using the NfL Simple Plex Cartridge, and quality control procedures ensured that the intra‐assay and inter‐assay coefficients of variation remained below 10%.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise specified. The distribution of variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Dichotomous and categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages, respectively. Associations between the number of PRLs and other continuous radiological measures were evaluated using Pearson's correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables. The study cohort was stratified into three groups based on the number of PRLs (no PRLs; 1–3 PRLs; ≥ 4 PRLs). For comparisons between groups, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied when the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances (assessed by Levene's test) were met; otherwise, non‐parametric alternatives were considered. A repeated‐measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA) was performed to compare the longitudinal trends of serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) levels across the three groups over multiple time points. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Released 2019. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

Results

Demographic and Clinical Data

Over the 20‐month enrollment period, 80 newly diagnosed RMS patients were evaluated at our centre. Twenty‐two patients met the predefined inclusion criteria and were initially enrolled. One patient withdrew consent immediately after undergoing the first MRI and baseline assessments and was excluded. A total of 21 patients were ultimately enrolled and completed follow‐up. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The cohort consisted of 15 females (71%) and 6 males, with a mean age of 31 years (±6.9 SD) and a median disease duration of 1 year. The ARR in the year preceding the baseline MRI was 1.19 and the median EDSS score was 1.5. At baseline cognitive assessment, the following median scores were reported: SDMT: 44.5, CVLT‐II: 49, BVMT‐R: 41.5.

All patients received HET. Fourteen patients (67%) started anti‐CD20 monoclonal antibody therapy (nine ocrelizumab, five ofatumumab). Natalizumab was initiated in five patients (24%), whereas two received alemtuzumab and one started cladribine.

At the 1‐year follow‐up, no clinical relapses were reported. CDW events were observed in six patients (28%). The performance on the SDMT remained stable over the same period.

Radiological Results

At baseline, the mean number of T2‐hyperintense lesions was 31.4 (IQR 27), with a mean lesion load of 14.9 cm [3]. All patients showed periventricular (PV) and juxtacortical lesions (JC), while infratentorial involvement was observed in 17 of 21 participants. All patients showed at least one spinal cord lesion, with a mean number of 4 (DS 3.02, range 1–9). Gd + lesions were detected in eight patients (38%), with a mean number of 1.62 per patient. T1‐hypointense lesions were identified in 19 patients, with a mean count of 12.1 and an average total volume of 2.95 cm [3]. CLs on DIR sequences were present in 11 patients (52%), with a total of 66 lesions in the cohort. The mean number of cortical lesions per patient was 3.1, corresponding to a mean total volume of 0.86 cm [3].

Susceptibility‐weighted imaging revealed the presence of at least one PRL in 16 patients (76%), with a mean of 4.9 PRLs and a mean total volume of 0.94 cm [3] per patient.

PRLs were most frequently located in the periventricular white matter (71.4% of patients), followed by the juxtacortical (52.3%) and cortical (14.3%) areas. Infratentorial PRLs were identified in three of the patients. Ten patients exhibited both PRLs and cortical lesions. In this subgroup, the mean number of PRLs was 9.2 and the mean number of cortical lesions was 6.4. Baseline radiological findings are summarized in Table 2.

At baseline, 104 PRLs were detected in our cohort. Distribution analysis confirmed that PRLs were most commonly located in the PV region (53.8%), followed by the JC (35.5%), cortical (6.7%), and infratentorial regions (3.8%). Infratentorial PRLs accounted for 3.8% of all lesions. Although infratentorial PRLs are not considered a strict exclusion criterion according to NAIMS recommendations [1], we decided to exclude them from the analysis because of the risk of susceptibility artifacts in this region. Considering their overall low number, their exclusion did not impact the categorization of patients within the predefined cohort subgroups. Correlation Analysis

A correlation analysis was conducted between the radiological and clinical measures at baseline. We identified a significant positive correlation between the number of PRLs and other radiological findings, particularly with a higher T2 lesion load (r = 0.82, p = 0.001) and a greater number of cortical lesions detected on DIR scans (r = 0.81, p = 0.001) (Figure 2).

Correlation analysis with radiological measures. A. Patients with a higher number of T2 lesions showed a higher number of PRLs (r = 0.82, p = 0.001). B. Patients with a higher number of cortical lesions showed a higher number of PRLs (r = 0.81, p = 0.001).

Based on the literature, patients were categorized into three groups according to the number of PRLs: Group 1 (0 PRLs), Group 2 (1–3 PRLs), and Group 3 (≥ 4 PRLs) [19]. We found statistically significant differences in both EDSS scores and cognitive performance across these groups. In particular, Group 3 demonstrated higher EDSS scores and poorer cognitive performance on the SDMT than Group 1 and Group 2. The mean SDMT score in Group 3 was 38.3, which was significantly lower than the scores of 50.1 and 50.8 observed in groups 2 and 1 (p = 0.04) (Figure 3). Group 3 patients exhibited significantly higher baseline sNfL levels than those in the other groups (p < 0.01). As expected, sNfL concentrations significantly decreased following treatment initiation in all three groups. At the 6‐month follow‐up, the mean sNfL level was 14.6 pg/mL, and at 12 months, it further decreased to 10.7 pg/mL. Discussion

We reported radiological and clinical data in a cohort of newly diagnosed RMS patients to assess whether PRLs appearance is an early phenomenon in the natural history of MS. In the current literature, the prevalence of PRLs in patients with MS varies depending on the study population and imaging techniques used. According to recent studies, approximately 20%–60% of MS patients exhibit at least one PRL, and about 5%–15% of all MS brain lesions present a persistent paramagnetic rim [11] [20]. In our cohort, PRLs were present in a higher percentage of patients (76%), with a mean number of 4.9 lesions per patient and a total of 104 lesions detected.

We found that PRLs were more frequently located in the periventricular and juxtacortical areas of the brain. Few studies have reported the anatomical location of PRLs, indicating that the periventricular region is the most common site [21], as found in our cohort. Our results are also in line with a high‐field resolution MRI study on a cohort of 120 patients, analyzing 8502 lesions, where paramagnetic rims were observed in 22% of periventricular lesions, 20% of juxtacortical lesions, 14% of cortical lesions, and 6% of infratentorial lesions [22].

Cortical rims have rarely been reported. We identified a paramagnetic rim in 7 of 66 CLs. Their detection is currently not well validated, but preliminary data exist with unwrapped/filtered phase imaging and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) [23]. A recent study identified juxtacortical paramagnetic rims (JPRs) at the border between the cortex and WM in approximately 10% of patients using QSM and MP2RAGE [24]. In this study, histological examination revealed that JPRs were always associated with demyelination of the entire overlying cortical ribbon and that the presence of JPRs was associated with an increase in CL load. Consistent with these findings, we also observed in our cohort that patients presenting with cortical‐juxtacortical rims were also those with a higher CL load.

In our cohort, CLs were detected in 11 patients using DIR, highlighting the relevance of cortical pathology in early MS. CLs have been consistently associated with cognitive impairment and long‐term disability progression [25]. Nevertheless, their detection remains challenging with conventional MRI protocols, with estimates suggesting that up to 75% of lesions may go unnoticed [25]. The use of DIR sequences represents a valuable advancement, offering improved sensitivity and strong concordance with histopathological findings [25]. Radiologically detectable lesions may only represent the most superficial portion of a more widespread and clinically meaningful cortical involvement, the so‐called ‘tip‐of‐the‐iceberg’ phenomenon [26], which needs to be actively explored. This is further supported by ultra‐high‐field MRI studies, which have demonstrated that CLs are not only more frequent than PRLs but also tend to exhibit faster and more aggressive progression over time [27].

Regarding the correlations between PRLs and other available radiological measures, we observed that a higher number of PRLs was associated with a higher T2 lesion load and a higher number of cortical lesions. This is in line with the current literature on 3T [28, 29] and 7 T MRI [27].

To explore the clinical significance of the PRLs burden at baseline, we categorized our cohort into three subgroups according to the number of lesions detected and compared clinical measures across the three groups. We observed a consistent trend toward higher EDSS scores and lower SDMT scores in patients with a higher PRL burden (≥ 4) at baseline. This is in line with recent studies demonstrating that the presence of PRLs is associated with poor cognitive performance and higher disability [30, 31, 32] and that patients with more than four PRLs reach motor and cognitive disability significantly earlier than patients without PRLs [19]. During the observation period, no patients experienced clinical relapses, but in six (28%) confirmed disability worsening was observed at the one‐year follow‐up. Cognitive assessment scores did not differ significantly from baseline, probably because of the relatively short follow‐up period.

Regarding follow‐up data, radiological disease activity (new T2 lesions and/or Gd + lesions) was detected in five patients within the first 6 months of treatment and in one patient at month 12. Eleven new lesions were detected at month 6, and two of them exhibited a paramagnetic rim that persisted at the 12‐month follow‐up. In our cohort, the number and characteristics of PRLs detected at baseline remained unchanged after 1 year despite treatment initiation. While PRLs may appear macroscopically stable over time, recent longitudinal data suggest that subtle microstructural changes in PRLs may occur during treatment [33], suggesting the potential value of quantitative susceptibility‐based metrics for monitoring treatment effects in PRLs.

Regarding fluid biomarkers, we observed that baseline sNfL concentrations were significantly higher in patients with a greater number of PRLs. sNfL is a well‐established biomarker of neuroaxonal damage [34], and its serum levels have been consistently associated with both disease activity [35] and progression in MS [28] [36, 37]. Following the introduction of HET, sNfL levels declined markedly at the 6 month follow‐up and stabilized at 12 months, with a proportional reduction observed across all three groups. These findings are consistent with clinical trials and real‐world studies reporting significant reductions in sNfL concentrations after DMT initiation, with improved clinical and radiological outcomes [38], suggesting a potential role as a dynamic biomarker for treatment monitoring [39].

Longer‐term data will be essential to evaluate the trajectory of disease progression at the individual level and to assess whether, despite similar unfavorable prognostic factors at baseline, a higher PRL burden may identify distinct patient subgroups with different long‐term prognoses.

Limitations and Strengths of the Study

Despite its small sample size and limited follow‐up duration, this study provides some valuable insights. By including treatment‐naïve, newly diagnosed patients, it focuses on the natural history of the disease and underscores the presence of PRLs in the earliest stages of MS.

Furthermore, the longitudinal design allowed the assessment of treatment response using both radiological and laboratory markers and revealing a poor radiological response of PRLs despite a significant reduction in sNfL in the first 6 months.

Brain and spinal cord atrophy were not assessed, given the short observation period and the characteristics of the cohort, including highly active patients starting HET, with the potential occurrence of pseudoatrophy that could have limited interpretability of the volumetric data [40]. A future extension of our study, with a larger cohort and a longer follow‐up period, may incorporate atrophy measurements to improve the assessment of structural progression.

In addition to PRLs, we analyzed cortical lesions, which remain nearly absent from routine MS workup. In our cohort, we observed a high prevalence of CLs and a significant association between PRLs number and CL load. Previous studies have shown that these markers are both associated with worse clinical outcomes but show different evolution in patients with MS. Since they both persist over time and are related to disease progression, their combined assessment may contribute to identifying individual‐level profile risks of more severe disease progression.

Conclusions

Paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) are promising markers of MS progression in the absence of canonical signs of disease activity. Susceptibility‐weighted imaging on MRI is widely available and has proven to reliably identify PRLs. The high prevalence of PRLs in our cohort confirms that these types of lesions are indeed present from a very early stage of the disease. Cortical lesions could complement PRLs in providing a more comprehensive picture of disease evolution. As the concept of smoldering MS gains recognition, refining imaging strategies in clinical practice to detect chronic, low‐grade inflammation becomes increasingly relevant for optimizing long‐term disease management.
