ABSTRACT
Key Points•Shallow long-read WGS provides high-resolution copy number variation profiles for karyotyping in patients with AML.•LcWGS delivers highly reproducible, sensitive, and prognostic information within 72 hours.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a genetically diverse malignancy characterized by recurrent chromosomal abnormalities that not only drive leukemogenesis but also guide risk stratification and treatment decisions. Under the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, the presence of myelodysplasia-related cytogenetic changes (MRC), such as monosomy 5, monosomy 7, deletion of chromosome 17, complex karyotype, and other abnormalities, defined a distinct subgroup known as AML-MRC,1 for which the chemotherapy CPX-351 demonstrated efficacy and gained approval.2,3

The 2022 International Consensus Classification (ICC) and European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 recommendations updated this entity to “AML with myelodysplasia-related cytogenetic abnormalities” (AML-MRCA), and the WHO classification to “AML with myelodysplasia-related changes” (AML-MR), highlighting the continued prognostic and therapeutic significance of these genetic features.4, 5, 6 As novel targeted agents and intensified chemotherapeutic regimens such as CPX-351 are approved specifically for genetically defined AML subsets, there is a high need for comprehensive and timely cytogenetic profiling.

Conventional metaphase cytogenetic analysis remains the gold standard for detecting numerical and structural chromosomal abnormalities, but in routine practice it requires 7 to 21 days to report results, far exceeding the clinically acceptable window of 3 to 4 days for initiating treatment decisions.7 Consequently, many patients harboring AML-MR (ie, -MRC or -MRCA) are diagnosed too late to benefit from genotype-directed therapies such as CPX-351. A rapid karyotyping approach, capable of identifying all clinically actionable aberrations within days, would therefore fill a critical unmet need in AML management.8

Low-coverage whole-genome sequencing (lcWGS) has emerged as a promising alternative for cytogenetic screening, leveraging read-depth analysis to reveal copy number alterations across all chromosomes.9, 10, 11 Long-read sequencing-based Oxford Nanopore Technology sequencing (ONTseq), in particular, combines real-time nanopore sensing with streamlined library preparation to deliver lcWGS data within 24 to 36 hours.12 In ONTseq, individual DNA molecules translocate through nanopores embedded in a membrane; ionic current disruptions generated during passage are translated into base calls, and low-coverage mapping of these reads to a reference genome enables rapid detection of copy number changes and structural variants.13

Here, we evaluated lcWGS by ONTseq as a rapid, scalable method for AML karyotyping. We retrospectively selected 50 patients with AML meeting MRC-AML criteria and prospectively included 50 patients with de novo AML to estimate the frequency of MRCA karyotypes in individuals without previous myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or cytotoxic therapy. LcWGS was performed on diagnostic samples. We assessed the sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility of lcWGS in detecting monosomies, deletions, and complex karyotypes, and evaluated the turnaround time from sample receipt to cytogenetic report. Our goal was to determine whether lcWGS can reliably identify the defining aberrations of MRC-AML within a 3- to 4-day window, thereby enabling earlier therapeutic decision-making and broader implementation of precision-driven AML treatments.

Patients, materials, and methods

Patients and study design

One hundred adults with newly diagnosed AML were enrolled under 2 complementary study arms. For the retrospective cohort, eligibility required age ≥18 years, availability of conventional cytogenetics demonstrating an MRC-AML–associated aberration, and written informed consent to the AMLSG-BiO registry (German Austrian Acute Myeloid Leukemia Study Group, Biology Outcome (BiO) registry, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01252485).14 For the prospective cohort, patients were aged ≥60 years with de novo AML and provided the same informed consent. Patients receiving AML therapy before or at sampling, or (in the prospective arm) those with secondary AML arising from MDS, MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN), or therapy-related AML, were excluded. Diagnostic specimens consisted of peripheral blood or bone marrow mononuclear cells. Clinical data were extracted from electronic records at Hannover Medical School, Charité–Universitätsmedizin Berlin, and Ulm University Hospital. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of Hannover Medical School, Charité, and University of Ulm and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Conventional cytogenetic karyotyping

G- and R-banding analysis identified chromosomal aberrations at the time of diagnosis as part of routine diagnostics and was described according to the 2020 International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature.15

Myeloid panel sequencing

For myeloid panel sequencing, DNA was extracted using the Allprep DNA/RNA purification kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Diagnosis samples were analyzed using a custom TruSight or Nextera myeloid sequencing panel covering 46 and 48 AML-related entire genes or hot spots, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s protocol as described previously (Illumina, San Diego, CA).16,17 Samples were sequenced on a MiSeq sequencer, and sequencing data were analyzed as described previously using a variant allele frequency cutoff of 5%.16,17

ONTseq

Genomic DNA (1 μg) was processed without fragmentation and prepared for sequencing using the Ligation Sequencing kit (Flow Cell [R9] FLO-MIN106D, kit SQK-LSK109 [n = 95 samples], and kit SQK-LSK110 [n = 5 samples]; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, United Kingdom) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were loaded onto R9.4.1 flow cells and sequenced for 24 hours on a GridION device at 1 of 3 participating medical centers. Basecalling was performed using Guppy version 3.2 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) in high-accuracy mode concurrently to the sequencing, and reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using NGMLR.18 To assess reproducibility, 10 samples were independently sequenced in 2 different laboratories.

Optical genome mapping (OGM)

Ultrahigh molecular weight genomic DNA was isolated from frozen blood or bone marrow samples according to the manufacturer’s guidelines as described previously (catalog no. 30246; Bionano Genomics, San Diego, CA)19 and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA BR assay kit with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Genomic DNA (750 ng) was labeled according to the manufacturer’s guidelines by using the Bionano Prep Direct Label and Stain protocol. The labeled DNA was loaded on the Saphyr G2.3 chips and molecules were imaged by the Saphyr instrument with a maximum capacity of 2000 gigabases per sample.

Bioinformatics analysis

The bioinformatics analysis was performed using a custom pipeline incorporating open-source tools and proprietary scripts on an ×86 server with 32 cores at 3.2 GHz and 64 gigabytes (GB) of random access memory (RAM) or ×86 server with 48 cores at 3.2 GHz and 512 GB of RAM. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19, GRCh37) using the NGMLR aligner, followed by processing with integrative genomics viewer tools and visualization with integrative genomics viewer.20 To evaluate genome-wide coverage by lcWGS, the genome was divided into 100-kilobase (kb) intervals, and the average coverage was determined for each interval. Intervals falling within the lowest 10% of coverage were compared with those within the highest 10%. Copy number variants (CNVs) were inferred using the R package ACE using a segmentation approach with a bin size of 100 kb for absolute copy number estimation,21 and the resulting copy number models were manually translated into International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature karyotype formulas. CNVs were mapped to 290 human chromosome bands to enable direct comparison with conventional cytogenetic results. The cumulative ideogram was designed using Cytogenetic Data Analysis System (www.cydas.org).22

Karyotypes were classified based on 4 standard criteria: complex karyotype (≥3 chromosomal abnormalities), monosomal karyotype (as defined by Breems et al),23 myelodysplasia-related changes (MRC) karyotype per WHO 2016,1 and MRCA karyotype per ICC 2022.4 LcWGS-derived cellularity of aberrant clones was estimated based on the relative read depth of CNVs.

For validation, OGM data were analyzed using Bionano Access software (version 1.5.2), using de novo assembly and/or rare variant assembly and structural variant calling, as described previously, using reference genome hg19.19 Variants were filtered using default confidence settings, with thresholds set at 0.01 for interchromosomal translocations, a maximum of 0% in the control database (Bionano), and a minimum of 10 supporting molecules. Discrepant findings between lcWGS and conventional cytogenetics were further assessed by manual inspection of the corresponding OGM data.

Statistical analysis

Comparative analyses of variables were conducted for exploratory purposes using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Student t tests for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to compare data from replicate experiments. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve were calculated using R package pROC. The positive predictive value was calculated as the ratio of true positives to the sum of true and false positives. The negative predictive value was calculated as the ratio of true negatives to the sum of true and false negatives.

Treatment response and outcome measures were defined according to the 2022 ELN criteria.5 Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from achievement of complete remission (CR) or CR with incomplete count recovery (CRi) to either relapse or death. Patients without an event were censored at their last documented follow-up.

Median survival times were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, whereas the median follow-up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Survival between groups was compared using the log-rank test. A 2-sided P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2) and SPSS (version 29.0).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 100 patients were included: 50 were retrospectively selected from our hospital biobanks based on the presence of ≥1 MRC-defining cytogenetic aberration detected by conventional cytogenetics, and 50 were prospectively enrolled if they presented with newly diagnosed de novo AML and were aged ≥60 years (supplemental Figure 1). The median age of the total cohort was 65 years; 63 patients (63%) were male. According to the ELN 2017 classification, 72 patients (72%) were categorized as having adverse-risk disease, 49 (49%) had a complex karyotype, 69 (69%) received intensive chemotherapy, and 49 (49%) underwent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (Table 1). Overall, blast percentages ranged from 10% to 98%, with a median of 56%; blast percentage information was unavailable for 7 samples. Median blast percentages were comparable between peripheral blood and bone marrow, at 60% (range, 10%-98%) and 54.5% (range, 15%-98%), respectively. Compared with the retrospective cohort, patients in the prospective cohort were older by design, all had de novo AML, were numerically less frequently classified as having ELN 2017 adverse-risk disease, and had a lower incidence of complex karyotype. They were more often treated nonintensively (n = 19 [38%] vs n = 7 [14%]) and underwent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation less frequently (n = 15 [30%] vs n = 34 [68%]; Table 1). TET2 mutations were more commonly observed in the prospective cohort, whereas the distribution of other mutations was similar between the retrospective and prospective groups (supplemental Table 1).

Sequencing-based karyotyping

Sequencing was conducted using a standardized 24-hour run per sample, yielding a median of 1.681 million reads (range, 0.298-9.836) and a median of 13.089 billion bases (range, 6.226-24.707). This resulted in a median sequencing coverage of 4.51-fold across target regions (range, 2.11-8.00) and 4.20-fold genome wide (range, 2.00-8.00), enabling reliable detection of CNVs at a resolution of ≥0.1 megabase. The 10% of 100-kb intervals with the lowest coverage showed a median coverage of 3.25-fold (range, 0.99-6.77), compared with a median coverage of 5.54-fold (range, 2.60-9.72) in the 10% of intervals with the highest coverage, suggesting a relatively homogeneous coverage overall. The median read length was 8083 bases (range, 1322-25 196).

The primary FAST5 files had a median size of 158 GB (range, 75-299), which was reduced to 9.7 GB (range, 4.65-18.44) after conversion to FASTQ format; 92.9% of reads successfully mapped to the reference genome, indicating high analytical specificity.

Analytical and diagnostic performance of sequencing-based karyotyping

Among 71 patients with ≥1 cytogenetic aberration, lcWGS detected 68 abnormalities, resulting in a sensitivity of 93% (95% confidence interval [CI], 84-98), specificity of 93% (95% CI, 77-99), and overall accuracy of 93% (95% CI, 86-97) compared with conventional cytogenetics. Five patients were apparent false negatives and 2 were apparent false positives by lcWGS: 3 false negatives involved sole translocations that lcWGS in our setting cannot reliably resolve; 1 involved monosomy X; and 1 involved an add(22)(q22) present in only 2 of 3 metaphases (insufficient sample), whereas the 2 false positives were partial deletions of chromosomes 3 and 4 that were undetected by conventional cytogenetics (supplemental Table 3). Across the full cohort of 100 patients, the number of copy number gains and losses detected per patient by lcWGS showed strong correlation with those identified by conventional cytogenetics (Pearson correlation coefficient R = 0.77; Figure 1). To enable direct numerical comparison, we mapped CNVs from lcWGS to 290 chromosomal bands spanning all human chromosomes, and observed high correlations for gained bands (R = 0.85) and lost bands (R = 0.82; supplemental Figure 3A-B). Ideograms of all 23 chromosomes further illustrate highly concordant patterns of gains and losses between lcWGS and conventional cytogenetics (Figure 2), demonstrating the high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of lcWGS. To more directly compare concordance among the technologies, we list partial or full losses of chromosomes 5, 7, 17, and 20 and trisomy 8 in supplemental Table 4 and show high concordance between conventional cytogenetics and lcWGS. Importantly, in samples with abnormalities detected by conventional cytogenetics but not by lcWGS, blast percentages were ≥60%, indicating that low blast count was unlikely to account for the discrepancy. Given that lcWGS is performed on bulk cell populations, whereas cytogenetics analyzes individual cells, we assessed the ability of lcWGS to detect subclonal aberrations by comparing cytogenetic clone size estimates (based on metaphase counts) with lcWGS-derived estimates of aberrant clone cellularity. Among 50 evaluable samples, clone sizes showed a moderate correlation (R = 0.54; supplemental Figure 4).

Mapping to cytogenetic classifications

To assess reproducibility across established AML cytogenetic classifications, we compared lcWGS-based karyotyping against conventional cytogenetics as the gold standard in 100 patients. Not taking into account translocation data from other methods, a complex karyotype (≥3 aberrations) was correctly identified by lcWGS in 42 patients, missed in 7, and over-called in 2. Detection of monosomal karyotypes, which often relies on structural aberrations, yielded 19 true positives, 18 misses, and 5 false positives; these “false” positive calls are likely genuine aberrations undetected by metaphase analysis. The WHO-defined MRC karyotype (2016) and the ICC-defined MRCA classification (2022) were each correctly called in 57 patients, with 2 missed and 1 falsely identified in both classifications. In the prospective cohort of 50 patients with de novo AML, lcWGS and cytogenetics each identified ≥1 chromosomal gain or loss in nearly half of samples (24 [48%] vs 26 [52%], respectively). Complex karyotypes were found by lcWGS in 12 patients (24%) vs 14 (28%) by cytogenetics, whereas both technologies concordantly classified 17 patients (34%) as having an MRC karyotype according to WHO 2016 and an MRCA karyotype according to ICC 2022.

Reproducibility of lcWGS

Reproducibility of lcWGS was confirmed by independently sequencing and analyzing 10 samples in 2 laboratories. Across both sites, 110 CNVs were detected at identical chromosomal positions and with virtually identical lengths (R2 = 0.999; Figure 4). Only 8 CNVs (7%) from 3 patients showed substantial length discrepancies between replicates (median difference, 2.25 megabases [range, 0.4-17]; supplemental Table 4). To resolve these differences, 6 of the 8 discrepant CNVs (from 2 of the 3 samples) were re-evaluated by OGM. Four of these discordant calls were found to involve cryptic translocations, 1 reflected an overlapping amplification and deletion, and 1 corresponded to complex intrachromosomal rearrangements (supplemental Table 5). Thus, discrepant aberrations lengths seem to indicate more complex cytogenetic abnormalities than simple gains or losses. The high level of concordance underscores the excellent reproducibility of lcWGS.

Prognostic impact of chromosomal aberrations

The prognostic impact of chromosomal aberrations detected by conventional cytogenetics was evaluated for complex karyotype AML, defined by ≥3 aberrations. The median follow-up of all patients with survival information was 2.28 years. The subgroup of intensively treated patients with available outcome data comprised 69 patients with a median age of 61 years. ELN risk groups were favorable, intermediate, and adverse in 7 (10%), 14 (20%), and 48 (70%) patients, respectively, and 16 (23%) patients had a TP53 mutation (supplemental Table 6). Intensively treated patients with a complex karyotype had shorter OS and RFS compared with patients without, irrespective of whether conventional cytogenetics or lcWGS were used to define complex karyotype (supplemental Figure 5).

Processing time

For 25 prospectively analyzed patients, end-to-end processing times were recorded from library preparation through bioinformatic analysis. Median library preparation starting from extracted DNA required 3 hours 45 minutes, sequencing time was 24 hours, and downstream bioinformatic analysis took a median of 6 hours 3.0 minutes, but usually started on the next day, resulting in a median time from end of sequencing to end of bioinformatic analysis of 30 hours 30 minutes and a total median processing time of 57 hours 30 minutes (range, 31 hours 50 minutes to 105 hours 30 minutes; supplemental Table 7). Including sample processing and result interpretation, lcWGS enables delivery of clinical reports within 3 days following sample receipt, meeting the need for rapid genetic characterization in patients with AML.

Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated nanopore-based WGS as an alternative for cytogenetic profiling in AML. LcWGS demonstrated high analytical accuracy for detecting chromosomal aberrations in AML, with high overall concordance compared with conventional cytogenetics. LcWGS reliably identified clinically relevant karyotypes and prognostic subgroups, including complex and MRC karyotype classifications, while enabling delivery of actionable results within 3 days.

LcWGS has recently emerged as a rapid, versatile tool for cytogenomic analysis across oncology and genetic diagnostics, offering advantages of low DNA input, shorter turnaround times, and often higher resolution compared with standard karyotyping. For instance, Smolander et al benchmarked several read-depth algorithms on ultra-lcWGS data (<0.8×) in cell lines and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples, concluding that lcWGS provides a cost-effective, high-throughput alternative to conventional karyotyping for large CNV detection.24 Similarly, Kato et al applied lcWGS by ONTseq in pediatric leukemia, detecting clinically relevant CNVs within 48 hours.25 In reproductive diagnostics, it was demonstrated that shallow WGS on 300 products of conception identified chromosomal abnormalities in 67% of samples, matching or exceeding traditional cytogenetics while avoiding cell culture failure.26

In our study, lcWGS and conventional cytogenetics showed remarkable concordance in aberration detection and karyotype complexity, and lcWGS reproducibly detected CNVs across 2 laboratories. The few length discrepancies in CNV boundaries were clarified by OGM,19,27 which revealed complex rearrangements in those regions. Of note, although our lcWGS approach did not capture balanced translocations, it correlated strongly with cytogenetic aberration counts. LcWGS identified nearly all patients meeting the ≥3 aberration threshold for complex karyotype, missing only 4 patients mostly due to missed translocations. Prognostically, lcWGS-detected complex karyotypes mirrored the negative impact on OS and RFS seen with metaphase karyotyping both in all patients and in the subgroup of intensively treated patients, underscoring its clinical relevance. The higher resolution of lcWGS opens the possibility to reassess CNV-associated prognostic markers, including noncoding regions in the future.

Accurate assignment to diagnostic categories such as MRC/MR (WHO 2016/2022)1,6 and MRCA (ICC 2022)4 are crucial for CPX-351 treatment eligibility.2 This was achieved equally by both lcWGS and cytogenetics. Compared with alternative methods, fluorescent in situ hybridization offers rapid turnaround but covers only preselected loci; OGM delivers extensive structural variation and fusion gene detection but requires high DNA input and longer processing (∼7 days)28; high-coverage WGS remains costly and time-consuming; single nucleotide polymorphism arrays are largely obsolete in clinical settings. Crucially, lcWGS can be run per sample, without batching, achieving comprehensive analysis in a median of 34 hours and report delivery within 72 hours after receipt for a moderate cost of €233 to €367 per sample (dependent on flow cell type; supplemental Table 8), which is substantially faster and not much more expensive than conventional cytogenetics, which provides final karyotype information in 7 to 21 days.7,8 Nevertheless, metaphase karyotyping retains the exclusive advantage of single-cell resolution, detecting subclonal aberrations down to ∼10% to 15% prevalence. However, during cell culture, aberrant clones may preferentially proliferate and thereby distort the assessment of clonal composition. The moderate correlation between lcWGS-derived and metaphase-derived clone size underscores the need for further assessment of subclonal detection sensitivity, especially in low-burden samples in which our average 4.51× coverage may be insufficient. However, lcWGS may allow approximate estimation of clone size for copy number abnormalities, although this remains model dependent and is mainly restricted to copy number–defined subclonality, whereas robust reconstruction of clonal architecture typically requires additional allele-specific or mutation-level information and higher genome coverage.29,30 Importantly, emerging adaptive sampling approaches enable targeted fusion gene enrichment and balanced structural variation detection in AML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia in addition to CNV detection.25,31 Integrating such adaptive sampling into our lcWGS workflow represents a promising direction to overcome current limitations and provide even more comprehensive cytogenomic profiling.

Alongside measurable residual disease assessment,32 lcWGS may be used to reassess cytogenetic response after initial therapy, thereby confirming its prognostic relevance and clinical utility, as recently demonstrated.33

This study has several limitations. First, although lcWGS demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for detecting CNVs, it is inherently limited in its ability to detect balanced structural rearrangements such as translocations and inversions, which do not result in copy number changes and were missed in the respective samples. Second, the retrospective study cohort was selected for patients with CNVs rather than balanced rearrangements. Third, although the reproducibility of lcWGS was validated across 2 laboratories, the sample size for technical replicates was small, and performance in low leukemia burden samples remains to be explored. Fourth, the prognostic analyses related to complex karyotypes should be interpreted with caution, as the study population was biased toward high-risk cytogenetics and had a limited sample size.

In conclusion, nanopore-based lcWGS represents a promising and versatile diagnostic tool, which identified CNVs with high accuracy, showed good correlation with metaphase karyotyping, correct mapping to current cytogenetic classifications, high reproducibility, and the same prognostic impact as conventional cytogenetics, despite missing structural variants. Due to the flexible use, rapid turnaround time, high resolution, and excellent reproducibility, lcWGS is a promising technology for karyotyping in acute leukemias.
