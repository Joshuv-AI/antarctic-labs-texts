Ripple effects of a patient & health-system partnered engagement project: a co-developed protocol
Authors: Isabella Chana, Zeenat Ladak, Stella Medvedyuk, Abimbola Saka, Bikila Amenu, Howard Freedman, Tara Jeji, Vinesha Ramasamy, Jean-Claude Camus, Aisha Lofters, Annemarie Edwards, Christian Finley, Dayne Riche, Gary C. ABSTRACT
BackgroundEngaging knowledge users, including patient partners and health-system partners, in embedded health services research is increasingly recognized as essential for strengthening the relevance of research and improving the quality and equity of health services and is foundational to the success of team science. Existing engagement frameworks, however, often capture experiences at single time points and do not fully reflect relational dimensions over time such as trust, reciprocity, shared decision-making, and equity which can impact the outputs and outcomes of partnered research. There remains limited evidence describing how engagement unfolds or what conditions support or hinder meaningful, equitable partnerships in research. This study will address this gap by examining the ripple effects of a long-standing researcher–knowledge user partnership and identifying the relational and structural factors that sustain it.MethodsThis qualitative, participatory study will be guided by critical patient-oriented research (cPOR), an approach that centres equity, shares power, and structurally situates lived/living experiences throughout the research process. In alignment with cPOR principles, patient partners and health-system partners are co-researchers across all stages of the study from inception through to development of data collection tools and will partner in analysis and interpretation. The study will be conducted within the Lung Health Equity Advisory Committee, a partnership co-established to address inequities in lung health, where patient partners, clinicians, policymakers, program implementers, and researchers have worked together since 2020. Data collection will be informed by patient engagement tools, such as the Engaging with Purpose Patient Engagement Framework, to assess experiences across five pillars: Co-Build, Support, Mutual Respect, Inclusiveness, and Impact, using document analysis and annual surveys. Analysis will be guided by the theoretical concept of ripple effects, to explore how engagement processes, outputs, and outcomes accumulate and influence subsequent phases of work. Data will be analysed using combined deductive–inductive content analysis, with triangulation across all data sources. Preliminary findings will be synthesized with partners through Ripple Effects Mapping (REM), a participatory approach that supports collective interpretation, visualization of impact pathways, and opportunities for continuous improvement.DiscussionThis study offers a novel approach to understanding the experiences and impact of long-standing research-knowledge user partnerships that are not bound by a specific project or timeline. By exploring this relational approach to engagement, we will generate nuanced insights into how knowledge-user engagement is built, experienced, and adapted over time within an equity-oriented partnership. The exploration of ripple effects is expected to strengthen real-time learning and partnership dynamics while offering a transferable model for other research teams seeking to embed iterative, partner-guided improvement into engagement practices that are sustained over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00937-x.

Background & rationale

Engaging knowledge users as embedded research partners has been shown to strengthen healthcare systems and outcomes [1–3]. Knowledge users include people with lived/living experience (also referred to as patient/caregiver partners) and health-system partners such as clinicians, policymakers, and program implementers. Evidence indicates that meaningful, proactive engagement of patient and health-system partners within research teams, also known as team science, contributes to the design of more relevant studies and the implementation of health services that better promote effectiveness, efficiency, and equity in alignment with the quintuple aim [3–5].

In parallel, a growing body of literature has emerged to understand how engagement practices are experienced by different partners. Instrument-based tools such as the Public and Patient Engagement Evaluation Tool (PPEET) and the Patient Engagement in Research Scale (PEIRS) provide structured ways to assess the quality and perceived impact of engagement from participant perspectives [5, 6]. Conceptual frameworks, including the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) Patient Engagement Framework [7], the Engaging with Purpose Patient Engagement Framework (EPPEF) [8], and the Learning Together Evaluation Framework for Patient and Public Engagement (PPE) [9], further illuminate key principles for effective engagement such as respect, support, collaboration, inclusiveness, impact, relationships, transparency, and sustainability.

Despite the availability of multiple tools and frameworks, existing instruments do not adequately capture relational dimensions such as trust and reciprocity [10] - elements central to how partnerships between researchers and knowledge-users develop, evolve and function, especially in equity-oriented contexts where these relationships are sustained over time and not bound by a specific timeline or single project [2, 11, 12] Available engagement tools, frequently explore practices at single points in time, often near the end of projects, with a primary emphasis on documenting activities or demonstrating impact for accountability purposes [12]. Such episodic assessments offer little insight into how engagement evolves across the partnership life course, how trust and partnership synergy develop, or how the outcomes of one phase of work become the context for subsequent phases. Ripple effects are defined as the dynamic processes, outputs, or outcomes that build over time based on committed partnerships built on mechanisms of trust [13]. Ripple effects are useful to understand as they can contribute to partnership sustainability and innovation of new projects and initiatives, thus influencing systems-level change on a large scale [13].

Although patient engagement practices are frequently episodic and transactional, it is increasingly understood that sustained engagement is needed to build trusting conditions under which partners feel safe, valued, and able to contribute [2, 14, 15] particularly in contexts shaped by histories of exclusion and uneven power relations [2, 14, 15]. Understanding engagement through this lens requires attention to how trust is built, how decision-making is shared, how tensions are navigated, and how partners experience their roles across the lifespan of a partnership [5–8]. This orientation aligns with critical patient-oriented research (cPOR), which foregrounds engagement as a relational and political practice aimed at redistributing power and addressing structural inequities in health and social systems [2]. Situated within a long-standing equity-oriented lung health program where engagement has been sustained over time (since 2020) and generated multiple outputs and outcomes, this protocol paper outlines the proposed steps for a study co-designed to unpack mechanisms of trust-building over time and to explore how engagement practices generate ripples across individual, partnership, project organisation, and health-system levels.

Aim

The overarching aim of this research study is to explore the ripple effects of engagement processes, outcomes, and outputs experienced over time in a long-standing researcher-knowledge user partnership, and to identify partnership synergies and trust building conditions that sustain meaningful, equitable engagement. We will address this aim through the following co-designed research questions:

1. What ripple effects emerge from engagement activities, and how do they influence subsequent phases of work?

2. What mechanisms underpin trust-building and partnership synergy within the partnership to enable ripple effects?

Methods

Study approach

This study is an interdisciplinary, cPOR [2] project that uses qualitative participatory methods to centre equity, redistribute power, and build structural competence across all stages of the research process. Patient partners and health-system partners are actively engaged as co-researchers, contributing to the development of study aims, co-design of data collection tools, and will participate in interpretive analysis, and decisions about how findings will be shared and used to strengthen engagement practices more broadly. We will report our study using the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP2) and COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) Checklists [16, 17].

Study governance

The study is governed by the Lung Health Equity Advisory Committee (LHEAC), established in 2020 with a mandate to conduct research to support identifying inequitable care pathways, co-designing services that respond to the social and structural determinants of lung health, and building accountability for culturally safe, trauma- and violence-informed, and accessible care [18]. Guided by principles of cPOR the LHEAC operates as a shared governance structure that centres equity, power redistribution, and collective decision-making across all stages of the research process. The LHEAC currently comprises an interdisciplinary team of five patient partners with diverse lived/living experiences (BA, HF, JCC, TJ, VR) and seventeen health-system partners, including healthcare providers, policymakers, program implementers, and researchers (AL, AE, CF, DR, GB, JP, JH, JRPHR, JM, JNY, LM, PS, SD, VR, VW, VN). Two additional patient partners have passed since the inception of LHEAC (see acknowledgements). Consistent with cPOR and the LHEAC governance model, lived and living experience expertise is recognized as equal to professional and academic expertise, with partners contributing to priority setting, decision-making, interpretation, and knowledge mobilisation activities. Decision-making within the LHEAC is guided by consensus-building processes that occur within and across partner groups. Meeting agendas are developed collaboratively, partners are invited to identify discussion priorities, and project decisions are informed through iterative dialogue across patient and health-system partners to support shared influence and accountability. The LHEAC was intentionally designed to create ongoing opportunities for relationship-building, trust development, co-learning, and shared influence among patient, health-system, and research partners and to date has obtained five peer reviewed grants to co-create, co-implement and co-evaluate interventions aimed at improving lung health equity. All members contribute individually based on their expertise and experiences, within specific subgroups, and through collective discussions during LHEAC meetings, depending on project needs. Through recurring engagement over multiple years, this governance structure has supported the development of partnership synergy by creating conditions for collaborative problem-solving, mutual accountability, and the co-production of research, implementation, and knowledge mobilisation. A detailed description of our study governance including structure, function, and power distribution is detailed elsewhere [18].

Patient partners receive honoraria for their contributions (30 CAD per hour) in alignment with the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research Patient Engagement Framework [7]. Additional details regarding the LHEAC’s structure and operations have been described previously [19].

Protocol development

[2]This study stems from a patient partner meeting in June 2025, in which one member of the committee wondered about the ripple effects of the partnership’s work over time. This observation prompted a broader discussion about the impact of engagement activities and how these impacts shaped subsequent work within the research lab.

Following this discussion, four members of our research team (ZL, IC, SM, AS) explored the feasibility of conducting a study focused on engagement activities and ripple effects of the LHEAC. To further develop the concept, a member of the research team (ZL) then met virtually with each patient partner individually over 30-minutes meetings to discuss this project idea and to gather feedback and identify priorities for investigation. Insights from these discussions informed the initial study aims, research questions, and conceptual framing.

Following these meetings, a protocol was developed using patient partners’ perspectives and feedback. Through iterative consultations, partners provided feedback on study design and data collection tools (such as survey questions) and analysis approaches, proposed theoretical and conceptual frameworks. Patient and health-system partners will review, revise, and validate survey questions to reflect their experiences of engagement, trust-building, power-sharing, partnership synergy, and overall growth over time.

A version of this protocol was presented to patient partners and health-system partners at two virtual LHEAC meetings in October 2025. The protocol was further refined by research team members (ZL, IC) based on partners’ feedback, and a final draft was shared with all LHEAC patient partners, health-system partners, and research members to gather their feedback prior to submission. This iterative development process reflects the LHEAC’s shared governance approach, whereby patient and health-system partners, together with researchers collectively contribute to shaping research priorities, methods, interpretation and knowledge dissemination activities. In developing this protocol, we also used the GRIPP2 short form checklist (Supplemental File A) [16].

Theoretical and conceptual frameworks

This study is guided by two complementary analytical lenses which will guide how we explore experiences of engagement over time and unpack the relational conditions that sustain meaningful, equitable engagement to foster ongoing improvement (Fig. 2).

Ripple effects [13]

Ripple effects are the cumulative, evolving consequences that arise as partnership processes unfold over time, where the outcomes of one phase of work become part of the context shaping the next. Rather than occurring as isolated events, ripple effects emerge through interconnected pathways in which engagement processes, decisions, relationships, and outcomes influence subsequent actions and opportunities. In long-standing collaborations, trust-building, conflict resolution, shared decision-making, and partnership synergy operate as both mechanisms and outcomes, generating a series of reinforcing effects. These effects may be intended or unintended, positive or negative, and may occur at multiple levels including at individual level (e.g., increased confidence, skills, leadership, or capacity), partnership level (e.g., strengthened trust, communication, and collaboration), project level (e.g., new grants, publications, or initiatives), organisational level (e.g., changes to policies, processes, or practices), and health-system level (e.g., broader dissemination, implementation, or systems change). Examples may include strengthened relationships, increased readiness for engagement, enhanced capacity and confidence among partners, unanticipated new initiatives, sustained partnerships, spin-off projects, or broader programmatic and system-level influence. Over time, ripple effects may contribute to partnership sustainability, development of spin-off initiatives, and broader organisational or health-system level transformation. This perspective will guide the study’s attention to how engagement processes develop over time, how trust-building and partnership synergy generate ripple effects, and how iterative feedback may influence the contexts, mechanisms, and impacts that emerge across the study period.

Ripple Effects Mapping (REM) is a participatory evaluation approach used to identify, explore, and visualise ripple-effect pathways [20]. In this study, REM will serve as a synthesis and interpretation approach that supports collective reflection on how engagement activities, relationships, and outcomes generated ripple effects over time.

Engaging with purpose patient engagement framework [8]

EPPEF offers a structured lens for examining engagement processes through five interconnected pillars: Co-Build, Support, Mutual Respect, Inclusiveness, and Impact. These principles emphasize the relational and purpose-driven nature of engagement and will inform the development of data collection tools, deductive coding categories, and interpretive analysis. EPPEF will support exploration of how partners experience core engagement processes, how these experiences influence partnerships and communication, and how they shape the outcomes generated through collective work [8].

Sampling, recruitment, & consenting processes

Patient (n = 5) and health-system (n = 17) partners from the LHEAC, and members of the research team (n = 6), will be invited to participate in the project via email. The recruitment email will briefly introduce the project and include the next steps to participate, including reviewing an information sheet and signing an informed consent form. The information sheet will include a brief overview of project activities, expectations, risks, and benefits. The informed consent form will include a statement of consent to participate in the project which participants must sign prior to engaging in project activities (i.e., survey). All recruitment and consenting processes will be facilitated by the researchers on the team.

Data collection & storage

Data will be collected from project documents and surveys (Supplemental File B), to capture historical, current, and experiential perspectives on engagement processes, outputs, and outcomes. Survey questions are to be approved and edited by the LHEAC each time the instrument is used to collect data. All data will be stored on Women’s College Hospital’s secure, password-protected servers in accordance with institutional data-governance policies.

Project documents related to the LHEAC partnership since 2020 and stored on Women’s College Hospital’s secure, password-protected SharePoint (or publicly accessible online) will be reviewed for data relevant to the study aims. Surveys

Patient and health-system partners will be invited to complete a survey. To promote equity and accessibility, partners will be offered the choice of completing the survey verbally over a video/phone call to a research team member (IC) or independently online, to meet their communication preferences and promote inclusivity. Surveys will be administered using Microsoft Forms and are expected to take approximately 30 min to complete independently. Verbal survey responses will be audio-recorded and conducted over the Zoom conferencing platform. Patient partners will be offered the option to receive a $50 gift card of their choosing. Ripple effects mapping

Following triangulation, a Ripple Effects Mapping (REM) process will be conducted to support the identification and visualization of ripple-effect pathways. REM is a participatory evaluation approach designed to capture the wider, non-linear, and often unintended impacts of complex initiatives through partner reflection and collaborative sensemaking. It is underpinned by four key principles: appreciative inquiry, participatory engagement, interactive group reflection, and visual mapping of impacts [20].

The REM process will be completed with patient and health-system partners during regularly scheduled LHEAC meetings. Preliminary findings from the document and survey analysis will be presented to participants to support reflection on how engagement processes, decisions, relationships, and outcomes have evolved over time. Partners will identify key activities, outputs, outcomes, and ripple effects, and collaboratively map how one outcome became part of the context for subsequent partnership activities.

The resulting visual map will be refined by the research team and used alongside content analysis findings to support interpretation, member checking, and the co-development of final study conclusions. The REM process will function both as a method of data synthesis and as a reflective activity that may inform future partnership priorities and better engagement practices.

Discussion

This study will address important gaps in how knowledge-user engagement is examined and strengthened within research projects that include patient and health-system partners as core members of the research team, aligned with principles of team science. Existing approaches explore engagement practices that are time or project-bound, and so not adequately capture the relational work, power dynamics, and contextual conditions that underpin long-standing partnerships [5]. By exploring ripple effects of engagement, this study will generate insight into how engagement is experienced over time and how real-time learning can support more equitable and responsive partnership practices.

Methodologically, the study contributes to a novel approach by integrating EPPEF’s process-oriented pillars with a ripple-effects perspective, applying these lenses across multiple data sources, including project documents and surveys. This design enables exploration of engagement both historically and as it is currently experienced, offering a more nuanced understanding of how trust-building, communication, decision-making, and role clarity emerge and shift within long-standing equity-oriented partnerships. REM further strengthens this approach by providing a participatory mechanism for partners to collectively identify, visualise, and reflect on how one phase of partnership work shapes subsequent phases, and identify the intended and unintended consequences of engagement over time. The protocol may serve as a model for other research teams seeking to embed relational, iterative approaches to evaluating and improving engagement as a practice of team science.

We anticipate several challenges, including creating a safe environment for partners to provide their perspectives through surveys, maintaining anonymity within a close-knit team, and minimizing bias in data interpretation. To address these issues, the study incorporates multiple safeguards, including advance review of question guides by partners and transparent communication about data collection expectations. We will involve an additional researcher who is satellite to the core LHEAC research team (IC) to lead data collection, thereby reducing participation pressure; and de-identification of data sources before analysis to maintain participant anonymity. These strategies reflect our commitment to relational ethics, trauma- and violence-informed practice, and the creation of conditions under which all partners feel safe to contribute openly.

Findings from this work will be integrated into ongoing LHEAC operations to better engagement practices and advance equitable team science at the Advancing Cancer Care Equity (ACCE) Lab in Women’s College Hospital, Toronto, Canada. More broadly, the study aims to contribute transferable insights and tools, that may support other research teams in examining and improving their engagement practices within their own contexts, as the approach taken within this work is not bound by a specific health context (e.g., lung health) but rather by an interdisciplinary approach to team science. By centering lived and living experience in both the design and interpretation of this study, we hope to advance a more relational, equity-focused understanding of meaningful engagement practices that underpin critical patient-oriented research (cPOR) [2].

Conclusion

This protocol outlines a participatory, equity-oriented approach to explore how ripple effects within a researcher-knowledge user partnership are experienced in a long-standing partnership. The study will generate relational and contextual insights that are not captured through conventional methods. Through analysis of documents and surveys, and the collaborative development of a ripple effects map, this work aims to strengthen engagement practices, enhance shared decision-making, and support more equitable team science.

The findings will contribute to local improvement within the lung health equity program from which this project emerged, while offering a transferable approach for other teams seeking to embed iterative, partner-guided learning into their engagement processes. Ultimately, this study seeks to advance understanding of how meaningful engagement is built, sustained, and adapted over time, and to inform the development of relational, equity-focused approaches to patient-oriented research.
