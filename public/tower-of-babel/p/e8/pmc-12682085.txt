Long-term test and retest reliability of clinical vergence testing
Authors: Mitchell Scheiman, Aparna Raghuram, Christina Master, Matthew L Robich, Andrew Toole, Erica R Shelton, Gayathri Srinivasan, Sophia Marusic, Siva Meiyeppen, Lisa A Jordan, Tawna L Roberts, EPICC Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo establish long-term reliability measures for vergence testing in a control population of adolescents.MethodsHealthy participants between 12 and 17.5 years with normal binocular vision were recruited from 10 clinical sites. Cover test, near point of convergence (NPC), positive and negative fusional vergences, vergence facility (12∆ base-out/3∆ base-in) and vergence jumps (using the Oculomotor Assessment Tool) were performed at the initial visit and repeated at 90 days. The mean and standard deviation were calculated for the overall group for NPC, vergence facility and vergence jumps and by prism dioptre step value for PFV and NFV (1Δ or 2Δ if below 20∆ or 5Δ above 20∆). Agreement was assessed using Bland–Altman plots and 95% limits of agreement (LOA).ResultsNinety-three participants (mean age 14.3 ± 1.7 years, 52% female) were enrolled and 91 (98%) completed the initial and 90-day outcome evaluation. The mean differences were significantly greater than zero for vergence facility (p < 0.05) and the first and second 30 s of vergence jumps (p < 0.01). The 95% LOA were narrow for NPC (±2.5) and negative fusional vergence (±5.9), suggesting good repeatability. LOA were larger for positive fusional vergence (±17.8), vergence facility (±9.8) and vergence jumps (±16.2). Analysis of the positive fusional vergence data indicates that the different step sizes (1∆ or 2∆ vs. 5∆) in the horizontal prism bar contribute to considerably larger variability in these measures.ConclusionsIn participants with normal binocular vision and no concussion history, good reliability yielded comparable results 90 days apart for all vergence measures. The results provide values that can be used to interpret the effect of intervention for vergence disorders in clinical practice and research studies. An important outcome of this study is the understanding that 5∆ steps on the typical horizontal prism bar contribute to high variability in positive fusional vergence measures when findings are ≥20∆.Keywords: near point of convergence, step vergence, testing reliability, vergence, vergence amplitudes, vergence facility

INTRODUCTION

Clinical vergence testing is an essential component of both the diagnosis and management of oculomotor disorders.1–4 Typically, clinicians compare test results to currently accepted normative data, look for patterns among the results and patient history and make diagnostic and therapeutic decisions.5–7 When treating oculomotor disorders, such as convergence insufficiency, with observation, lenses, prism or vision rehabilitation, clinicians must decide whether changes in the vergence findings at follow-up visits are clinically meaningful. Researchers face a similar challenge when analysing and interpreting the results from outcome measures in clinical trials. To meet this challenge, clinicians and researchers rely on precision data.8 McAlinden et al.8 listed five factors that contribute to variability in repeated measures affecting reliability (i.e., precision), including the observer, device used, device calibration, environment and time interval. The effects on reliability due to time interval factors are particularly important when treating medical disorders. Questions related to the reliability of measurements frequently arise, such as: (1) What would have happened if observation had been prescribed rather than active treatment? or (2) Is the change in the measure following treatment clinically meaningful? While short-term (1–10 days) test–retest reliability data are available for some vergence measures such as the near point of convergence,9,10 fusional vergence amplitudes9–12 and vergence facility testing,13 longer term test–retest data are missing for all vergence measures.

The need for longer term reliability data has become more acute given the results of recent research showing a high frequency of oculomotor problems in adolescents who experience prolonged recovery after a concussion.14–17 Although many adolescents recover from their concussion within the first month post-injury, studies indicate that 10–30% have a prolonged recovery that lasts greater than 1 month,18–22 with a high percentage of adolescents having concussion-related oculomotor disorders.15,16,23 These disorders may contribute to their delayed recovery. Thus, clinicians who care for adolescents with persistent concussive symptoms need to make decisions about the management of concussion-related oculomotor disorders. The physicians managing the concussion need to determine whether a referral for vision care is necessary, and eye care professionals need to determine if active treatment of the oculomotor disorder is warranted. However, the lack of natural history data and expected changes over time makes it difficult for clinicians in neurology, sports medicine, physical rehabilitation, paediatrics and eye care to manage visual complaints in this highly symptomatic population.

This study's primary purpose was to establish precision measures for commonly used clinical vergence tests by determining the 3-month test–retest reliability of clinical vergence testing in adolescents with normal binocular vision. A secondary aim was to gain additional information about expected findings for vergence testing in this population and compare these results with previous research.

The data presented in this paper are from the control population of a more extensive study named Eye Problems in Concussed Children (EPICC, NIH/NEI R34EY030582). The long-term goal of the study group was to investigate the natural history of vision disorders following concussion in adolescents as well as to determine the associations between vision disorders and cognitive performance during recovery from concussion. The EPICC study aimed to develop test–retest reliability information for binocular vision assessments from individuals without concussion, as a comparison group to determine if true changes in clinical measures occur in individuals with concussion-related oculomotor problems from the initial (<28 days from injury) to a 90-day follow-up visit. Herein, only the results of clinical vergence testing of the control group with normal binocular vision are reported, as it establishes the reliability of vergence measures.

METHODS

The study was supported through a grant from the United States National Eye Institute (NEI) of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Department of Health and Human Services. It was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki at 10 clinical sites in the United States and Canada. Each US site executed a reliance agreement with The Ohio State University, which provided institutional review board (IRB) oversight for the study. The protocol and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant informed consent and assent documents were approved by the Ohio State University IRB. A parent or guardian provided written parental permission and adolescent participants provided written assent.

Participant selection

Participants, age ≥ 12 years and <17.5 years, were recruited from the local community, paediatricians, postings in athletic facilities and from children and families of clinic faculty and staff. Participants could also be recruited from the eye care clinic when seeking routine vision examinations; however, these recruitments were limited to half of the required controls per site to limit bias of the control population who may be seeking vision care. Eligible individuals could not have a history of a concussion, had to have normal binocular vision (Table 1) and best-corrected visual acuity of 6/7.5 or better in each eye.

Eligibility criteria.

Study visits

After informed consent was obtained, eligibility testing was performed by a certified study examiner. Table 1 summarises the eligibility criteria. All testing was performed without cycloplegia and with the participant wearing their current refractive correction (if applicable). Cycloplegia (1% cyclopentolate) was instilled at the end of the examination for all participants if they had not had a cycloplegic examination by an EPICC study-certified examiner within the last 3 months. If a participant had undergone a cycloplegic refraction within the previous 3 months, this refraction was used to determine if the control participant met the refractive eligibility requirements. If, at the end of the eligibility visit, the participant met all inclusion criteria except for the refractive error standard, and the participant was prescribed glasses as part of clinical care, they were permitted to return for a subsequent enrolment visit where all the testing was repeated after they had worn their new correction for a minimum of 2 weeks. Vergence testing was performed as part of a larger testing battery that included ImPACT testing,24,25 assessment of accommodation, visual tracking, visio-vestibular testing,26,27 contrast sensitivity, ocular health assessment and visual field assessment.

Examination procedures

Study-certified optometrists and neuro-ophthalmologists administered a standardised protocol of clinical tests at the initial examination, which was repeated at the 90-day follow-up visit. The tests were done in the same order for each participant, and the battery was performed in the same order at each time point. For this report, only details of the vergence testing performed (described below) are included. Cover test, vergence facility and vergence jumps were performed once, whereas near point of convergence (NPC), negative fusional vergence (NFV) and positive fusional vergence (PFV) were repeated three times and averaged to increase the reliability of the measures and to minimise random error. Tests that were timed (vergence facility and vergence jumps) were only performed once as each test was timed for 1 min and naturally involved multiple repetitions. All tests were conducted in the same order. Although this could have potentially impacted measurements later in the examination, this method was employed to maintain consistency with the pattern of testing used in clinical care.

Cover test

The cover–uncover (unilateral cover) test and the prism and alternate cover test (PACT) were performed at near (40 cm) using a 6/9 optotype (Gulden Fixi-Tic #15112, Gulden Ophthalmics, guldenophthalmics.com/) and at distance (3 m) using a 6/12 optotype to determine objectively the direction and magnitude of the heterophoria at the initial and 90-day follow-up visit. Participants wore their refractive correction, if required, and were asked to keep an isolated letter clear during testing. For PACT testing, a prism was added until the direction of movement was reversed (e.g., exo became eso). The largest prism that resulted in neutrality (before the first reversal movements, i.e., high neutral) was recorded as the magnitude of the deviation.

Near point of convergence (NPC)

The edge of the Oculomotor Assessment Tool (OMAT, Gulden Ophthalmics #18012, guldenophthalmics.com/) was placed on the centre of the participant's forehead just above the brow level (Figure 1a). The target of the vertical column of letters (6/9 equivalent) was placed at the 25 cm mark on the OMAT rule. The participant was instructed to report when the letters became double, but to try to keep the letters single as long as possible. The target was slowly (1–2 cm/second) moved towards the participant. When diplopia was reported, they were asked if the target remained two or came back into one. If the target came back into one, the target was again moved towards the participant until they were unable to regain fusion (NPC break). If the examiner observed a loss of fusion without a report of doubling, the point at which the examiner observed a loss of fusion was recorded as the NPC break. The target was then moved away from the participant's eyes until they reported single vision or the examiner observed recovery of fusion (NPC recovery). The NPC was measured three times, and 10 s was allowed between each measurement. The three measurements were averaged.

Near point of convergence (a) and vergence jumps (b) measured using the Oculomotor Assessment Tool.

Fusional vergence amplitude testing

The test was performed at near (40 cm) using a 6/9 letter target (Gulden Ophthalmics Fixi-Tic with Timer #15112, guldenophthalmics.com/) and a horizontal prism bar with the following prism increments (1∆, 2∆ to 20∆ in 2 ∆ steps and 20∆ to 45∆ in 5∆ steps). The examiner assessed NFV amplitudes three times followed by PFV amplitudes three times with a 10-s interval between each measure. The participant was instructed to report when the letters became blurred or double but was encouraged to try to keep the letters single and clear as long as possible. For NFV, a horizontal prism bar was held before the right eye, and base-in prism was gradually increased at approximately 2∆/second, pausing at each prism interval to confirm that the target was single and clear and the participant's eyes were aligned. If the participant reported blur, the prism amount was recorded as the base-in blur. The examiner continued to increase the amount of base-in prism until the participant reported double vision or the examiner noted manifest strabismus. If the participant reported double vision, they were asked if they could regain single vision or if the target remained double. If the participant regained fusion, base-in prism was increased until persistent diplopia was reported (base-in break). After increasing the base-in prism to dissociate the eyes fully, the base-in prism was reduced until the participant reported single vision (base-in recovery). NFV blur/break/recovery was repeated three times, waiting 10 s between each measurement. PFV was measured using the same protocol but using a base-out prism. The average of the three measurements was calculated for both NFV and PFV.

Vergence facility

The participant was instructed to fixate on a 6/9 letter column at 40 cm on the Gulden Fixi-Tic with Timer and report ‘clear’ as soon as the letters became single and clear through the 12∆ base-out (BO, Gulden Ophthalmics 12∆ BO/3∆ base-in (BI) stick prism #11208, guldenophthalmics.com/) held before the right eye. The stick prism was then switched to base-in, and the participant was again instructed to make the letters single and clear. This pattern was repeated for 1 min. The number of prism flips in 1 min was recorded and later converted to cycles per minute for analysis. The examiner also observed the participant during testing to confirm they converged/diverged appropriately with each prism.

Vergence facility jumps

The OMAT was assembled using the two plastic target holders with vertical lines. The longer vertical line was placed at the 25 cm mark (‘A’ on the ruler) and the shorter line at the 9 cm mark on the ruler (‘F’ on the ruler), creating two convergence demands (Figure 1b). The device was placed on the centre of the participant's forehead just above the level of the brow, and the participant was instructed to look at the back vertical line (lesser convergence demand) and say ‘now’ when it was single, then look as quickly as possible at the front line (greater convergence demand) and say ‘now’ when it was single. This pattern was repeated for 1 min. The examiner also observed the participant throughout testing to ensure a proper vergence movement was executed. The OMAT application (app) [both Android (android.com) and Apple (apple.com)] was used by the examiner to start the time and to count the number of jumps from one distance to another by tapping the counter on the screen. The app automatically stopped counting after 1 min and created a vibration on the device to inform the examiner that testing was complete. The number of jumps for the first 30 s and the second 30 s was recorded by the app and displayed on the screen at the end of the 1-min testing period.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were completed using SAS 9.4 (sas.com). The mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values were calculated for initial visit characteristics. Testing whether the change from baseline to 90 days was significantly different from zero was accomplished using a paired t-test for normally distributed variables or a Wilcoxon signed rank. The mean and standard deviation were calculated for the difference in vergence measures between the initial and 90-day visits (initial – 90 day). The normality of the differences was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests and the distribution of the data using the kurtosis and skew, comparing the means with the medians and the tails of the distribution (2.5th and 97.5th percentile). Bland–Altman plots were generated using the mean and mean difference in the measures between the initial and 90-day visits. The 95% limits of agreement (LOA) were calculated as the means of the differences ±1.96 times the standard deviation. The number of observations outside of the 95% LOA was calculated as a percentage and the half-width of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the LOA is presented.

To account for 5∆ steps in measurements at 20∆ and beyond for measures of PFV and NFV, participants were stratified into two groups. Participants with values <20∆ at both the baseline and 90-day visit were included in the under 20∆ group, and participants with a value ≥20∆ at either visit were included in the ≥20∆ group. The mean, standard deviation and 95% LOA were calculated for the overall group for NPC, vergence facility and vergence jumps and by the prism dioptre group for PFV and NFV. Bland–Altman plots for PFV and NFV were also generated for the overall group, the <20∆ group and the ≥20∆ group.

Reliability data

The mean differences between visits were small for all the tests and the 95% LOA were narrow for NPC and NFV break/recovery suggesting good reliability (Table 3). Normality was acceptable for all variables. Statistically significant changes using paired t-test were found for vergence facility and vergence jumps. Variability was larger for the PFV break/recovery, vergence facility and vergence jumps. In addition to the LOA, Table 3 presents the percentage of observations that fell within the 95% LOA, as well as the half-width of the 95% CI for the LOA. Bland–Altman plots illustrating the near point of convergence, PFV and NFV break and recovery data are provided in Figure 2a–i. The Bland–Altman plots for PFV and NFV revealed an interesting and unexpected pattern. The NFV plots showed narrower 95% LOA than the PFV plots. In addition, the larger 95% LOA for PFV appeared to vary depending on the magnitude of the values, with greater variability in participants with PFV values ≥20∆. This prompted an additional analysis of both the PFV and NFV data in which the sample was separated into two groups (both initial and 90-day visit values from 0 to <20∆ and ≥20∆ at either visit) (Table 4) as the prism bar makes 2∆ steps up from 2∆ to 20∆ and 5∆ steps thereafter. The mean difference for PFV recovery at ≥20∆ was borderline significant (0.046) by paired t-test. Normality of the differences for the subgroups was assessed and acceptable, with the exception of the PFV break among those who were <20∆ (n = 12). These differences were highly skewed requiring caution when evaluating the results of this analysis. Intraclass correlation coefficients were not calculated for the subgroups, as some do not allow for stable results. These Bland–Altman plots are illustrated in Figure 3 and confirm that, for the PFV break, the variability was greater in the sample that had ≥20∆ at either visit compared with the participants who had PFV break values <20∆ at both visits.

DISCUSSION

The primary purpose of this study was to establish measures of long-term test–retest reliability for vergence testing in a control population of adolescents with normal binocular vision. These data can help researchers and clinicians interpret the clinical significance of changes in vergence test results that occur over time when managing patients or study participants with vergence disorders. It was found that long-term (90 days) reliability of the NPC and NFV results was high in participants with normal binocular vision. The mean differences between visits were small for all vergence tests and the 95% LOA were narrow for NPC and NFV, suggesting good repeatability. However, variability was larger for the PFV vergence break/recovery, vergence facility and vergence jumps.

Based on the study results, recommendations are made regarding the changes in vergence measurements that can be considered clinically meaningful (Table 5). For NPC break, it was found that a change of 2.5 cm or greater fell outside the 95% LOA and should be regarded as clinically meaningful (Table 5). For NFV, the mean breaks at the initial visit and 90 days were 15.2∆ and 15.5∆, respectively, and the respective recovery measures were 12.2∆ and 12.3∆. For NFV break and recovery, changes as small as 3∆ and 5∆, respectively, are clinically meaningful. It is important to note that almost all NFV measures at the initial and 90-day visits were < 20∆. When testing fusional vergence ranges with a prism bar (step vergence), the prism increments are 2∆ for values from 2∆ to 20∆. The interpretation of the PFV results, however, is more complicated.

The initial analysis of the data indicated that a change of close to 20∆ would be considered clinically meaningful for the PFV break using the step vergence procedure. This finding is inconsistent with previous studies that used a change of 10∆ as the clinically meaningful change,10,32–34 including another clinical study that evaluated positive fusional vergence with the von Graefe smooth vergence test.10

Upon visualising the Bland–Altman plots (Figure 2e,f), it is apparent that there is a difference in outcome for deviations <20∆ compared to ≥20∆. We hypothesised that there is an inherent bias in the step vergence procedure using a horizontal prism bar that can explain this inconsistency. The prism values on the traditional horizontal prism bar include 1∆–2∆ in 1∆ increments, 2∆–20∆ in 2∆ increments and 20∆–45∆ in 5∆ increments. The non-linear steps in the prismatic demand appear to be the potential source of greater variability with prismatic values ≥20∆ and result in lower precision of the measures for patients with higher PFV break values, simply because of large (5∆) steps at any value above 20∆. This phenomenon has been noted before in optometric testing such as visual acuity.35,36 Raasch et al. noted that large size ratios in visual acuity charts result in greater variability with repeated measures and recommended smaller size increments to achieve improved repeatability.35 Similarly, Bailey et al. stated that the ‘…95% confidence limits for change are strongly influenced by the coarseness of the measuring scale’.36

One of the primary aims of this study was to establish values for each test that would help researchers and clinicians decide if meaningful changes occur after intervention. If these values are derived from normal participants with high PFV values, then the results are less precise and the findings suggest that larger changes must occur to be considered clinically meaningful. Understanding these values in the context of normal versus abnormal binocular vision is important. In clinical studies of convergence insufficiency, the average initial (before treatment) PFV values were 10–14∆.33,37,38 In addition, by definition,37–40 patients with convergence insufficiency will have both break and recovery values <20∆, except those with high exophoria who could have larger PFV, but fail Sheard's criterion.41 Thus, we suggest using values derived from participants with normal binocularity, with both scores <20∆ for interpreting changes as being meaningful in PFV break and recovery in individuals with abnormal binocular vision. These data suggest that changes as low as 4.9∆ for PFV break and 6.4∆ for PFV recovery should be considered a clinically meaningful change. Antona et al.11 also examined repeatability as well as the agreement of prism bar vergences and reported ±13.2∆ for the PFV break to be a meaningful change. They plotted Bland–Altman curves and reported a similar pattern as found here. Additionally, Antona et al. suggested that greater variability for PFV could be explained ‘by the fact that NFV and PFV functions have somewhat different underlying neural mechanisms.’ We believe the non-linear prism bar steps are a more likely explanation.

Regarding vergence facility and vergence jumps, the mean differences from the initial to the 90-day visit were near zero, but the 95% LOA were wide, suggesting possible poor reliability. For vergence jumps, this is the first report of reliability values, and these findings were obtained from individuals with normal binocular vision where a greater number of jumps were anticipated. It may be that less variability is expected for individuals with vergence disorders, who may show fewer jumps per minute. For vergence facility, a change in the test result <10 cpm would not be considered clinically meaningful based on the current results. The values shown in Table 5 are recommended to interpret changes in these measures between visits following treatment for a vergence disorder.

Clinicians can manage patients with binocular vision disorders in multiple ways, including observation, lenses, prism and vision rehabilitation. The challenge has been how to interpret changes in vergence testing results due to any of these interventions. Specifically, is the improvement in test results clinically significant, suggesting effectiveness of treatment, or is the change within the normal variability of the outcome measure? The results from this study provide the guidance necessary to make this decision more confidently. These recommendations are listed in Table 5.

The strength of the study includes the use of a standardised protocol, as well as training and certification of all investigators. There are a number of study limitations. One limitation is the small number of control subjects with positive fusional vergence <20∆, which may have impacted the data analysis. In future research, investigators should continue to explore the significance of the current method of assessing fusional vergence (i.e., <20 vs. ≥20Δ) using the step-vergence method or with smooth addition (Risley prism)-based measurements that are devoid of the large step sizes. Additionally, test and retest values of vergence jumps should be quantified in a group with known vergence disorders to determine if lower total jumps result in lesser or greater variability. However, it is not suggested that these test and retest values be performed in concussed participants as their oculomotor systems are expected to vary significantly during the first few weeks of recovery. Furthermore, the current sample was limited to adolescents from 12 to 17.5 years of age and may not be generalisable to younger children or adult populations. Since all tests were performed in the same order, the testing protocol could have influenced the measurements depending on fatigue levels and prism adaptation.

Finally, it was decided to exclude participants with binocular vision problems. We believed that this would reduce variability in the results, thereby establishing a more valid estimate of the variability to expect for vergence testing. One could argue that, in a more general sample of adolescents without concussion, there would undoubtedly be some with binocular vision problems who would have yielded different results.

CONCLUSIONS

In participants with normal binocular vision, both the long-term (90 days) precision and accuracy of the NPC results were high. The mean differences were small for all of the vergence tests, while the 95% LOA were narrow for NPC and NFV, suggesting good repeatability. Variability was larger for PFV, vergence facility and vergence jumps. The intrinsic characteristics of the commonly used horizontal prism bar contributed to an apparently large variability of PFV break and recovery and should be explored in future research. This study's results will allow clinicians and researchers to make better management decisions and assess treatment effectiveness in children experiencing prolonged recovery from concussion.
