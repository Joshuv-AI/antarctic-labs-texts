Risk factors for cerebral infarction in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Jingjing Xuan, Xiang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the risk factors, including relevant autoantibodies, for cerebral infarction in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA).MethodsThirty-nine RA patients with cerebral infarction admitted to Weifang People’s Hospital since January 2010 were selected as the RA cerebral infarction group. Seventy-two RA patients without cerebral infarction hospitalized during the same period were randomly selected as the RA non-cerebral infarction group. Gender, age, RA disease duration, rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen A (anti-SSA) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen B (anti-SSB) antibody, Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate(ESR), C-Reactive Protein(CRP), swollen joint count(SJC), tender joint count(TJC), patient global assessment(PGA), physician’s global assessment (PhGA), as well as the presence of comorbid hypertension, diabetes, and smoking status were compared between the two groups. Additionally, Disease Activity Score-28 using ESR (DAS28-ESR), Disease Activity Score-28 using CRP(DAS28-CRP), Simplified Disease Activity Index (SDAI), and Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) were compared between the two groups.ResultsUnivariate analysis showed that there were statistically significant differences between the two groups in DAS28-ESR, DAS28-CRP, CRP levels, SJC, PGA, PhGA, SDAI, CDAI, anti-SSB positivity, and comorbid hypertension (P < 0.05), while no statistically significant differences were found in the remaining variables (P > 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that anti-SSB positivity, SJC ≥ 11, and CRP ≥ 26.85 mg/l were independent risk factors for the occurrence of cerebral infarction in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. The most common clinical manifestation in the RA with cerebral infarction group was hemiplegia.ConclusionPositivity for anti-SSB antibody, SJC ≥ 11, and CRP ≥ 26.85 mg/l were independent risk factors for the occurrence of cerebral infarction in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease primarily characterized by erosive arthritis, with synovitis and vasculitis serving as its main pathological foundations. It can affect multiple organ systems throughout the body, including the lungs, brain, and peripheral nerves. In the past, it was believed that this disease rarely involved the central nervous system. However, in recent years, there have been an increasing number of reports on the co-occurrence of RA with ischemic stroke [1–4]. This study employed a retrospective approach to investigate the risk factors for cerebral infarction in patients with RA.

Objects and Methods

Objects

A total of 39 patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) complicated by cerebral infarction, admitted to Weifang People’s Hospital from January 2010 to August 2023, were collected through the electronic medical record system and designated as the RA cerebral infarction group. Among them, there were 11 males and 28 females, aged between 45 and 79 years, with an average age of 65.51 ± 8.22 years. The duration of RA ranged from 2 months to 30 years, with Median disease duration 10 years. Cerebral infarction occurred before the onset of joint pain symptoms in 4 patients, while the remaining cases occurred within 0–30 years of the RA course. The diagnostic criteria for acute cerebral infarction referred to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke 2018 [5], and the diagnostic criteria for RA referred to the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis [6].

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age between 45 and 80 years; (2) Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) findings consistent with the imaging manifestations of cerebral infarction; (3) Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score [7] at admission ≥ 1.

Exclusion criteria

(1) mRS score at admission < 1; (2) Age > 80 years; (3) Concomitant rheumatic immune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome, and ANCA-associated vasculitis, as well as tumors and atrial fibrillation.

Selection process of the control group

A total of 72 rheumatoid arthritis patients without cerebral infarction who were hospitalized at Weifang People’s Hospital during the same period were randomly selected as the control group. All patients were of Han ethnicity. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in age, sex, disease duration, or smoking history (P > 0.05), see Table 1. The age of the control group ranged from 41 to 79 years, with a mean age of 64.14 ± 9.11 years. The duration of rheumatoid arthritis ranged from 5 to 40 years, with Median disease duration 10 years. The diagnosis of RA was based on the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis [6].

Methods

Clinical data during hospitalization were collected for each patient, including gender, age, disease duration, rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated polypeptide (anti-CCP) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen A (anti-SSA) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen B (anti-SSB) antibody, and the presence of comorbid hypertension, diabetes, and smoking status. The number of tender joints (tender joint count, TJC) and swollen joints (swollen joint count, SJC) among a total of 28 joints, including the shoulder, elbow, wrist, metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal, and knee joints, were recorded. Additionally, the patient’s subjective evaluation of joint pain using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) (ranging from 0 to 10) [8], erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were recorded. The RA disease activity scores Disease Activity Score-28 using CRP(DAS28-CRP) and Disease Activity Score-28 using ESR (DAS28-ESR) [9]were calculated using the following formulas: DAS28-CRP = 0.56 × sqrt(TJC28) + 0.28 × sqrt(SJC28) + 0.36 × ln(CRP + 1) + 0.014 × GH + 0.96; DAS28-ESR = 0.56 × sqrt(TJC28) + 0.28 × sqrt(SJC28) + 0.70 × ln(ESR + 1) + 0.014 × GH. The clinical disease activity index (CDAI) and simplified disease activity index (SDAI) were also calculated. SDAI = TJC28 + SJC28 + PhGA + PGA + CRP (mg/dl) (0.1–86), CDAI = TJC28 + SJC28 + PhGA + PGA (0–76) [8]. These indices in RA cerebral infarction group were recorded during the initial hospitalization at the time of RA diagnosis, prior to the occurrence of cerebral infarction. Higher scores indicated greater disease activity. RF was measured using the rate nephelometry method, anti-CCP antibodies were measured using the chemiluminescence method, and anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies were measured using the immunoblotting method.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R software. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were expressed as median (p25, p75), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages (%), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (when the theoretical frequency was < 5). Variables with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were further subjected to binary logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors for cerebral infarction in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Univariate analysis

Comparison of general clinical data between the two groups

There were no statistically significant differences in sex, age, RA disease duration, diabetes, and smoking between the RA cerebral infarction group and the RA non-cerebral infarction group (P > 0.05). However, the proportion of patients with comorbid hypertension in the RA cerebral infarction group was significantly higher than that in the control group (P < 0.01). See Table 1.

Comparison of laboratory indicators between the two groups

When comparing the positive rates of rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen A (anti-SSA) antibody, and anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen B (anti-SSB) antibody between the two groups, the positive rate of anti-SSB antibody in the RA cerebral infarction group was higher than that in the non-cerebral infarction group (P < 0.05), see Table 1. Among RF-positive patients, the median RF level was 245.50 IU/mL (n = 60) in the RA non-cerebral infarction group and 417 IU/mL (n = 31) in the RA cerebral infarction group, with no significant intergroup difference (P > 0.05).

Comparison of RA disease activity between the two groups

The levels of CRP, swollen joint count (SJC), DAS28-ESR, DAS28-CRP, simplified disease activity index (SDAI), clinical disease activity index (CDAI), patient global assessment(PGA), and physician’s global assessment (PhGA) in the RA cerebral infarction group were significantly higher than those in the RA non-cerebral infarction group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05),see Table 1.

Discussion

There are numerous risk factors for cerebral infarction. In addition to traditional risk factors such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, and age, the relationship between some autoimmune diseases and cerebral infarction has garnered increasing attention in recent years. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease that can affect multiple systems both within and outside the joints, with involvement of both the peripheral and central nervous systems [10–12]. Central nervous system involvement in RA primarily includes meningitis, rheumatoid nodules, and rheumatoid vasculitis of the brain, with cerebral infarction being one of the clinical manifestations of nervous system involvement.

Previously, it was believed that RA rarely caused cerebral infarction. This may be because it takes a certain amount of time for the progression of most RA cases to lead to cerebral infarction, and the relationship between RA vasculitis and cerebral infarction has not been fully recognized. A meta - analysis [12] indicated that patients with RA, especially Caucasians, have a significantly increased risk of cerebral infarction compared to those without RA. A study on RA patients in the UK also reached the same conclusion [13]. In our study, among the 39 RA patients with cerebral infarction, 4 had cerebral infarction before the diagnosis of RA, and 3 of them were young to middle - aged men who experienced cerebral infarction before the age of 50, which is not a high - risk age group for cerebral infarction. Therefore, it is considered that cerebral infarction is closely related to RA.

The mechanisms by which RA causes cerebral infarction may be attributed to the following aspects. On one hand, patients with RA in the active disease phase often exhibit a hypercoagulable and prothrombotic state, which predisposes them to cerebral infarction [14, 15]. On the other hand, immune complexes, monocytes, and lymphocyte infiltration, along with fibrin and inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-20, IL-6, and IL-1, may damage the vascular endothelium and cause vasculitis, thereby leading to intimal hyperplasia and thrombus formation, which ultimately result in cerebral infarction. Studies have shown that inflammatory cytokines produced by the synovial tissue of RA patients act on the vascular endothelium, thereby promoting the development of atherosclerosis [16, 17]. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that disease activity indicators swollen joint count(SJC) ≥ 11 and CRP ≥ 26.85 mg/l in the RA with cerebral infarction group were significantly higher than those in the non-cerebral infarction group, suggesting that RA disease activity is associated with cerebral infarction.

Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies are specific antibodies secreted by B lymphocytes for the diagnosis of RA. They appear early in the disease process and can even be present before the onset of clinical symptoms in RA patients. They are associated with RA disease activity and prognosis [18–22]. High - titer levels of these antibodies can promote the production of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), which is involved in the activation and dysfunction of vascular endothelial cells, leading to cerebral vasculitis and stroke. However, in our study, there was no statistically significant difference in the positivity rate of anti-CCP antibodies between the RA without cerebral infarction group and the RA with cerebral infarction group. The possible reasons for this result may include: this was a single-center study with a small sample size and a short observation period. As age advances and the disease course of RA lengthens, a subset of anti-CCP antibody-positive patients in the RA non-cerebral infarction group may eventually develop cerebral infarction.

The positive rate of rheumatoid factor (RF) in RA patients is approximately 75% − 80%. However, RF is not a specific antibody for RA. It participates in the pathological process of RA, and high - titer IgM - RF often indicates more severe joint lesions [23]. The presence of IgG and IgM - RF is associated with RA vasculitis. Nevertheless, RA patients with negative RF can still be in a state of high inflammatory reactivity [24], which can lead to the occurrence of cerebrovascular diseases. Our study also observed that some RF-negative RA patients had high disease activity, and there were no statistically significant differences in the positive rate of RF between the RA cerebral infarction group and the RA non - cerebral infarction group.We further compared the RF levels between RF-positive patients in the two groups and no statistically significant difference in RF levels was observed between the two groups.

Some RA patients can develop secondary Sjögren’s syndrome (SS). SS is a systemic autoimmune disease that affects multiple organs throughout the body, with the lacrimal and salivary glands being the most commonly affected exocrine glands, as well as the lungs, kidneys, blood system, and nervous system. Both the central and peripheral nervous systems can be involved. Epidemiological data from domestic and foreign sources show that central nervous system involvement in primary SS patients ranges from 0% to 70%. Current research has found that anti-SSA/SSB antibodies can not only be used for the diagnosis of primary SS but also for the assessment of its complications. Patients positive for anti-SSA/SSB antibodies may be more prone to venous thromboembolism, multiple myeloma, and cerebrovascular diseases [25, 26]. Positivity for both anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies often indicates internal organ involvement, with more pronounced extraglandular lesions and vasculitis. Our study showed that the positive rate of anti-SSB antibodies in the RA cerebral infarction group was higher than that in the RA non-cerebral infarction group, while there was no difference in the positive rate of anti-SSA antibodies between the two groups.Our study showed that among the RA without cerebral infarction group, some patients who were positive for SSA antibodies were diagnosed with secondary Sjögren’s syndrome, while the remaining SSA or SSB antibody-positive patients did not exhibit any symptoms of sicca syndrome. In the RA with cerebral infarction group, one patient who was positive for both SSA and SSB antibodies was confirmed to have secondary Sjögren’s syndrome, while the rest were only positive for SSA or SSB antibodies alone and did not show any sicca symptoms. Our study demonstrated that anti-SSB antibodies are a risk factor for cerebral infarction.However, given the very small absolute numbers, this finding should be interpreted with extreme caution and may represent a statistical artifact. This result should be considered exploratory and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Primary hypertension can cause damage and dysfunction of vascular endothelial cells through mechanisms such as increased vascular shear force and inflammatory peroxidative stress, promoting the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle and a decrease in vascular wall elasticity, which induces atherosclerosis and plaque formation. It is an important risk factor for ischemic stroke [27–29]. Our study showed that the proportion of patients with comorbid hypertension in the RA cerebral infarction group was higher than that in the RA non-cerebral infarction group, with a statistically significant difference, suggesting a correlation between hypertension and the occurrence of cerebral infarction.

Lee DH et al. investigate the risk of ischemic stroke nationwide in patients with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis and controls who were matched in age and sex, and found that seropositive RA patients have an increased risk of ischemic stroke. In female, hypertension, non-diabetes, and non-dyslipidemia RA subgroups, even without the traditional risk factors for stroke (except for hypertension), increased the risk, which could be potentially attributed to RA [30]. Qiang F et al. employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to evaluate the likelihood of a causal association between RA and stroke, and RA may be causally associated with an increased risk of stroke [31]. Chen YR et al. examined effects of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) on risk of stroke recurrence and investigated the interaction between RA and traditional cardiovascular risk factors on recurrence risk after ischemic stroke (IS) or transient ischemic attack (TIA) and provided that RA significantly increased recurrence ischemic stroke risk [32].The aforementioned studies, employing various methodologies, have collectively confirmed that RA is a risk factor for cerebral infarction. Our retrospective study further identified several risk factors that promote the occurrence of cerebral infarction in RA patients, including anti-SSB antibodies, SJC ≥ 11, and CRP ≥ 26.85 mg/l.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective nature of the study design may introduce selection bias and limit the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, the relatively small sample size may restrict the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Third, the single-center nature of the study may limit the external validity of our findings. Fourth, detailed dyslipidaemia and treatment exposure data were not available, which may have confounded the association between RA and cerebral infarction. Thereafter, well-designed multicenter studies with large sample sizes, extended follow-up periods and comprehensive treatment data are warranted to further confirm the reliability of our findings.

In conclusion, We found that positivity for anti-SSB antibody, SJC ≥ 11, and CRP ≥ 26.85 mg/l were independent risk factors for the occurrence of cerebral infarction in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. This also reminds clinicians that when dealing with patients with cerebral infarction, in addition to considering traditional risk factors, they should also pay attention to screening for relevant autoimmune diseases and associated autoantibodies.
