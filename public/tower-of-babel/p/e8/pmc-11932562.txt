Comparative evaluation of serum heat shock protein 60 in chronic periodontitis patients with and without coronary heart disease - A case–control study
Authors: Anjhana Asok, Rahul Bhandary, Mamatha Shekar Shetty, Amitha Ramesh, Geethu Venugopalan, S Sai Sreeja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a group of stress proteins that participate in physiological processes such as cellular folding, assembly, translocation of polypeptides across membranes, and protein repair after cell damage. HSP60 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of both chronic periodontitis (CP) and coronary heart disease (CHD).Aim:The aim was to assess the possible link between CP and CHD by estimating and comparing the serum HSP60 levels in patients with CP with and without CHD.Settings and Design:Ninety patients in the age group of 30–60 years were involved in this study. The patients were categorized as CP (control group) and CP with CHD (case group).Materials and Methods:Gingival index (GI) and periodontal parameters such as probing pocket depth and clinical attachment levels were documented. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was used to determine the HSP60 levels in serum samples obtained from the patients.Statistical Analysis:Results were statistically analyzed using the SPSS version 16.0 software to estimate the mean and standard deviation using independent t-test and Mann–Whitney U-test.Results:The findings of the research indicated that patients with CP with CHD exhibited elevated serum HSP60 levels compared to CP without CHD (P < 0.05). In addition, a statistically significant rise in GI was noted in the case group. However, the periodontal measurements did not reveal any significant differences.Conclusion:As a result, HSP60 levels were observed to be elevated in the case group compared to the control group. Consequently, there is a connection between periodontal disease and CHD that contributes to the overall inflammatory burden.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is a multifactorial disease influenced by microbial challenges, genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and acquired risk elements and is characterized by the infiltration of mononuclear cells into gingival tissues, culminating in connective tissue destruction and alveolar bone resorption.[1] Several Gram-negative bacteria play a role in altering host response due to systemic or environmental influences, which eventually lead to periodontal destruction. Heat shock proteins (HSPs) represent specific target antigens in the context of this autoimmune response.[2] HSPs are stress proteins produced by cells when exposed to heat or chemical stimulation, and they have strong cytoprotective effects behaving as molecular chaperones for other cellular proteins. HSPs have been identified as versatile cellular entities, engaging in a multitude of functions such as facilitation of polypeptide folding and translocation, cellular protection, and intracellular assembly. These activities are particularly pronounced in response to heightened temperatures, viral infections, oxidative stress, exposure to chemical stimulation, nutritional deficiencies, and cytokines.[3]

HSPs can be divided into five family groups based on their size, structure, function, and molecular weight, namely HSP110, HSP90, HSP70, HSP60, and HSP27, and other small molecule HSPs.[4] The bacterial homolog of HSP60 family, known as the growth factor of Escherichia coli, is present in prokaryotes and is located in the mitochondria within the cytoplasm, playing a role in the translocation of intracellular proteins into mitochondria. In addition, HSPs serve as a proinflammatory mediator. An important aspect of HSPs is their potential use as indicators of cellular injury, as well as for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.[5]

The expression of HSP60, which is triggered by the periodontopathic bacteria, activates macrophages and other cells to release proinflammatory cytokines within the periodontium. This can lead to a chronic periodontal condition and subsequent tissue damage.[6] Consequently, HSP plays a crucial role in cellular defense and the remodeling of periodontium by ensuring homeostasis.[7] HSP60 is highly expressed in cardiac tissues and cardiac cells, predominantly found within mitochondria, while a smaller fraction of HSP60 is seen in the cytoplasm.[8910] The release of HSP60 could stimulate the innate immune system, leading to a proinflammatory condition that serves as an early catalyst for myocyte loss and the advancement of heart failure. HSP60 might serve as a significant early prognostic indicator in heart failure and enhance our comprehension of the mechanisms underlying disease progression.[9]

HSP60 in cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) plays a role in the activation of signaling pathways associated with the progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). An elevated level of HSP60 in cardiomyocytes is linked to heart failure and serves as a biomarker for heart failure.[10]

Coronary artery disease (CAD), alternatively recognized as coronary heart disease (CHD), stems from the accumulation of atheromatous plaques in the arterial walls. The regulatory role of HSP60 in the inflammation, proliferation, and migration of VSMCs accelerates the process of atherosclerosis. Individuals with periodontitis have a 25% increased risk of developing CAD.[1112] Evidence indicates that cross-reactivity emerges due to a 95% sequence similarity between bacterial HSPs and a 50%–55% sequence similarity between bacterial and human HSP60. Extensive research signifies an augmented risk of CHD in individuals with chronic periodontitis (CP), establishing HSP60 as both an immunomodulator and a biomarker of tissue damage.[13141516] The cross-reactivity of antibodies between periodontopathic bacterium and human HSP60 may contribute to the immune processes involved in periodontitis.[14] Periodontal disease has been linked to increased carotid intimal-medial wall thickness, which is an indicator of atherosclerosis.[16] Studies have established that periodontal diseases may be associated with atherosclerosis, thus causing CHD.[1516]

HSP60 may serve as a potential candidate through which periodontal disease influences CHD. The existing literature has very few studies that prove a relationship between CP with and without CHD. This study attempts to evaluate the hypothesis that serum HSP60 levels are elevated in CP patients who also have CHD compared to CP patients who do not have CHD. Hence, this study aims to measure and compare the levels of serum HSP60 in patients with periodontitis, both with and without CHD, and to identify a positive relationship between chronic periodontal disease and CHD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this comparative study, 90 patients aged 30–60 years who attended the institution’s outpatient department were chosen. The research was carried out between March 2016 and March 2017. Each patient who participated in this comparative study provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review committee and was in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000. Ethical clearance from the institution was obtained for the same (Cert no: ABSM/EC84/2015). Participant’s demographic details such as age, occupation, medical history, frequency of brushing, and diet were recorded. The study design has been summarized in the flowchart [Figure 1]. A standardized pro forma was employed to systematically gather comprehensive demographic information from patients, encompassing details such as age, gender, residence, occupation, oral hygiene practices, medical history, and nutritional supplements. The clinical assessment was meticulously conducted utilizing a sterile mouth mirror, Williams periodontal probe, and dental explorer, with clinical parameters such as gingival index (GI), PPD, and CAL thoroughly recorded during the initial session. Six sites per tooth have been examined using Williams probe, and the total distance from the cementoenamel junction to gingival margin (recession) has been calculated to measure the attachment loss. In addition, the distance from the sulcus to the base of the pocket has been measured to calculate pocket depth. The measurement was taken to the nearest millimeters.

All the 90 subjects taking part in the study were sampled for collecting blood sample. Approximately 3–5 ml of blood was collected from the median cubital vein by venipuncture. After transferring and clot formation, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm (revolutions per minute) for 10 min. The samples were stored at −20°C or below.

The assessment was performed using AssayMax™ Human HSP60 Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit by sandwich enzyme immunoassay technique. The HSP60 samples were sandwiched by immobilized antibody and biotinylated polyclonal antibody specific for HSP60. This is recognized by a streptavidin-peroxidase conjugate. After washing away the unbound material, a peroxidase enzyme substrate is added which gives blue color. When the stop solution is added, the color development is stopped and is changed to yellow. The intensity of color was then measured and read on a microplate reader.

All the data were statistically analyzed using SPSS Inc Chicago, Illinois, United States of America (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) software version 16.0. The collected information was summarized using percentage, frequency, mean, and standard deviation (descriptive statistics). To compare the difference in serum levels between HSP60, the independent “t”- test was used. If the data were not following normal distribution, the Mann Whitney U-test was used (inferential statistics).

The mean values of serum HSP60 levels in CP and CP + CHD were compared to be 41.23 ± 7.44 and 61.43 ± 27.14, respectively, as demonstrated in Table 1 (P < 0.001) [Table 1]. The data showed a significant increase in the serum HSP60 in Group B compared to Group A.

Although PPD and CAL were found to be slightly higher in the case group, the results were statistically insignificant [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Periodontal infection alters systemic physiology in CAD patients. Analytical epidemiological investigations have proven efficacious in elucidating risk factors associated with the initiation and progression of diseases and role of periodontal infection as a modifier of systemic health.[18]

Hasturk and Kantarci delineated two models explicating the genesis of systemic inflammation consequent to periodontitis. In one model, periodontal pathogens disseminate to remote sites, potentially inciting localized inflammation. The alternative model, as endorsed by a recent European/American Consensus Workshop, posits that periodontal bacteremia induces an acute-phase response mediated by the liver. This response involves the release of C-reactive protein (CRP), the production of interleukin-6, and the activation of peripheral blood leukocytes, specifically neutrophils, triggering the release of oxygen radicals and subsequently culminating in a peripheral oxidative stress response.[19]

HSP60 is a member of a protein family that has survived evolution.[20] The present study has attempted to explore the association between CP and CHD by assessing the level of HSP60, which could be used as a potential biomarker. In the present study, the mean serum HSP60 levels were reported to be 41.23 ± 7.44 and 61.43 ± 27.14 in the control group and the case group, respectively [Table 1]. This is similar to a study conducted by Gokulanathan et al., where serum HSP60 levels increased significantly in CVD compared to periodontal disease.[21] Concurrent findings by Rizzo et al. provide validation for the correlation between atherogenic dyslipidemia and elevated HSP60 levels, demonstrating an association with periodontal pathology.[22]

Building upon this, Ford et al. postulated that in certain individuals, the immune response’s cross-reactivity to bacterial HSPs, including those from periodontal pathogens, with arterial endothelial cells expressing human HSP60, could potentially serve as a mechanism linking atherosclerosis and periodontal infection.[23] In alignment with these insights, Fajromar and AL-Shamaa further substantiated that patients with CHD exhibited higher HSP60 levels, emphasizing an increased risk for CHD associated with elevated HSP60 levels, thereby reinforcing the findings of the present study.[24] In a study by Kadhim and Mohaymen, it was observed that HSP60 levels were higher in both acute and chronic cases of atherosclerotic CAD compared to healthy controls which supports the present study.[25]

In this study, the GIs recorded between both the groups were statistically significant [Table 1]. According to the research by Chrysanthakopoulos and Oikonomou, it was observed that gingival inflammation could lead to an increased risk for atherosclerosis, and individuals with gingival inflammation were likely to suffer from atherosclerotic CVDs like CHD.[26]

Moreover, in the current study, PPD and CAL showed slight increase among the case group when compared to the control group, and the data were statistically insignificant [Table 1] which was congruent to studies conducted by Chrysanthakopoulos and Oikonomou.[262728] More research studies are needed to validate this result.

Therefore, a positive association was found between the case and control groups for serum HSP60 levels. Thus, in the view of the present study, it can be interpreted that there is an association between periodontal disease and CHD which adds to the total burden of inflammation.

HSP60 may be expressed as autoantigens in the development of atherosclerosis. HSP60 contributes to periodontal disease in a manner similar to proinflammatory cytokines, thus leading to systemic inflammation. The present study explores the link between periodontal disease and CHD by assessing the level of HSP60, which could be used as a potential biomarker.

A study conducted by Nethravathy et al. aimed to assess the levels of HSP60 in both serum and in saliva in relation to periodontal health and disease, as well as to examine its correlation with high-sensitivity CRP. The results from the study showed that the presence of circulating HSP60 may contribute to the systemic inflammatory response associated with periodontal disease. However, salivary HSP60 may not serve as a reliable indicator for measuring systemic inflammation.[29]

In the current study, ELISA is used to estimate serum HSP60 levels in comparison to immunoblotting as ELISA is versatile, accurate, reproducible, and economical. Studies by Gokulanathan et al.,[21] Yamazaki et al.,[6] and Nethravathy et al.[29] have shown similar evidence to the present study.

In a study by Leishman et al., the association between the load of periodontal pathogens and the presence of anti-human HSP60 (hHSP60) antibodies in patients with established CVD was examined, and an increase in anti-hHSP60 antibodies was observed as the variety of bacterial species grew.[30] The link between periodontitis and CVD suggests that periodontal disease could eventually be included in the factors considered when evaluating patient’s risk profiles for CHD and stroke, aligning with findings from a study by Karnoutsos et al.[31]

While interventional studies and epidemiological research indicate a significant link between periodontitis and CHD, there is a notable association between periodontal disease and CVD. However, scientific evidence supporting a direct causal relationship between these two conditions is provided in the study by Jangid et al.[32]

When investigating the putative relationship between periodontitis and CHD, taking microbiological considerations into account, findings from Spahr et al. reveal a link between periodontitis and presence of CHD.[33] According to a meta-analysis study by Bahekar et al., periodontal disease is associated with considerably higher incidence and prevalence of CHD. As a result, periodontal disease might increase the risk of CHD. However, more prospective studies are needed to validate this hypothesis.[34]

The findings from a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Humphrey et al. indicated that periodontal disease serves as a risk factor or indicator for CHD, separate from established risk factors such as socioeconomic status.[35]

A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Teeuw et al. show that treating periodontal disease enhances endothelial function and lowers atherosclerotic disease biomarkers, particularly in individuals who have CVD and diabetes.[16] The importance of HSPs in relation to CVD has been recognized, along with a deeper understanding of how HSP, and HSP antibody measurements can provide prognostic insights, as established in Pockley and Frostegård’s study.[36] The role of extracellular HSPs and its role in CVDs have been established in a study by Patnaik et al.[37]

Confounding variables such as diet, smoking, stress history, and other associated diseases were not included in the study as these factors might have some putative association affecting periodontitis and CAD. This makes the present study superior to the other studies. Since CHD and CP are chronic diseases, interpretation of temporal relationship between exposure and outcome is difficult. A larger sample size would enhance the outcome of the study. The cross-sectional nature of the study is another limitation. Longer follow-ups are required to establish the nature of outcome of the study. Despite its limitations, this study forms a base for future studies to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of this protein.

CONCLUSION

Therefore, the higher values of HSP60 levels found in the case group indicate a possible candidate through which periodontal condition influences systemic state of inflammation and thus CVD. Thus, in congruence with the current data in the study, HSP60 can be associated with periodontal disease and systemic inflammation. Since the dual relationship between periodontal disease and CVD has been established, periodontitis may epitomize as an additional risk factor for CVD, suggesting that HSP60 may act as a potential risk marker in conjunction with other inflammatory markers. As a result, HSP60 acts as a double-edged sword that connects periodontal disease and CVD. However, more longitudinal and interventional studies are needed in future to establish the diagnostic value of this protein.
