Reported Causes of Device Embolization Following Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Gift Chiechekam Echefu, Olanrewaju Adabale, Tunji Sakiru Isa, Addison Bond, Mannu Nayyar, Uzoma N Ibebuogu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the last 2 decades, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become widely adopted for the treatment of aortic valve stenosis, and prosthetic valve embolization (PVE) is one of the rarely reported, but often catastrophic, complications of this procedure. The authors performed an updated systematic review of contemporary studies to determine the prevalence of post-TAVR PVE and its associated risk factors. A systematic search was conducted in multiple databases for all relevant published studies from 2014 to August 2024. Studies reporting on embolization were included. Data extracted encompassed participants' characteristics, study characteristics, and clinical outcomes. Pooled event rates were calculated using the random effects method. In the final analysis, 34 studies reported 501 cases of device embolization, of which 50% were male with a mean age of 80.7 ± 3.16 years while the other 50% were female. The rate of PVE was 1.4%. About 80% of the embolization occurred in the intraprocedural period, with 61% of the embolized valves requiring surgical treatment. The prevalence of embolization across included studies was 1.4%, with most embolization occurring during the procedure. The chance of mortality is significantly heightened at about 18% to 20% at 30 days and ~30% at 1 year in some series, compared with much lower baseline rates for uncomplicated TAVR. Prosthetic valve embolization complicates TAVR procedures, increasing procedural complexity, with over 50% of instances resulting in conversion to surgical intervention. Given that this complication is rare (~1%), the overall risk is low in the general TAVR population, but for the individual, it is serious and warrants aggressive management.Keywords: TAVR Embolization, TAVR migration, Trans-Catheter Aortic Valve Replacement, prosthetic valve embolization

Introduction

Several published studies have elucidated the advantages and procedural complications linked to transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). 1 Aortic valve stenosis (AS) is the second-most prevalent valvular pathology in the United States, especially among individuals over 75 years old. 2 TAVR is relatively less invasive and is a durable option for patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis at prohibitive risk or deemed unsuitable for a surgical approach. 3–6 Patient preference likely contributes to the uptick in TAVR. In exchange for the advantages attributed to TAVR, a large proportion of patients with AS are willing to accept substantial increases in clinical risk, such as the need for a pacemaker, due to their preference for TAVR over surgical aortic valve replacement intervention. 7 These reported procedural complications can occur either periprocedurally or beyond the immediate operative period. 3–6

Advances in TAVR made it imperative to develop the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC-3) criteria to define clinically relevant endpoints, 8 among which are prosthetic valve embolization (PVE). 2,8 According to VARC-3, device embolization refers to the movement or dislodgement of the device either cephalad or distal following the final placement site, leading to the complete detachment of the valve from the aortic annulus. This study aims to systematically review reported cases of valve embolization in patients who underwent TAVR, focusing on identifying the baseline demographic, procedural characteristics, management strategies, and clinical outcomes.

Methods and Statistical Analysis

SEARCH STRATEGY

The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement for reporting systematic reviews recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration (Figure 1). 9 Approval from an institutional review board was not required because data were obtained from previously published studies. Two authors (TI, OA) performed a systematic literature search across various databases, including PubMed (MEDLINE), Cochrane, EMBASE, and CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), to identify pertinent studies published up to March 2024. Studies documenting device embolization after TAVR, including case reports, case series, cohort studies, and randomized clinical trials, were reviewed. A Boolean search was performed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and combining the following keywords: “transcatheter aortic valve implantation” OR “transcatheter aortic valve replacement” AND “embolization” OR “dislocation.” No language restrictions were applied. In addition to database searches, the authors conducted manual searches of bibliographies of included reports, known reviews, and relevant reports.

Study Selection and Endpoints

Studies were included based on the following criteria. Studies must be either 1) randomized, controlled trials or observational studies, or 2) report on device embolization following deployment as defined by the VARC-3 criteria. Case reports, conference abstracts, review articles, and studies lacking clear numerical outcomes were excluded. Studies reporting social demographics, procedural characteristics, management, and clinical outcomes, specifically device embolization, were included. Studies reporting on various forms of device misplacement other than embolization were excluded. Data extracted encompassed participants’ characteristics, study characteristics, and clinical outcomes. Pooled estimates of proportions along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The authors assessed statistical heterogeneity across studies using the Cochran’s Q test for various outcomes. The measure of the proportion of the total variance attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error was calculated and reported as the I2 statistic.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM Corporation). Baseline characteristics, including demographics and medical history, were summarized by the treatment group for each study. Continuous variables were analyzed as either the mean and standard deviation or the median and interquartile range, as specified in each study. Descriptive measures and incidence of patient outcomes, including embolization and other outcomes based on VARC-3 criteria for reporting transcatheter valve implantation outcomes, were presented where reported in the studies. Meta-Analysis

The authors conducted a metaregression analysis using the “metafor” package in R (package version 2.4-0) to investigate the association between embolization and various predictor variables. All statistical tests were 2-tailed; a P value of < .05 was deemed significant. The analysis included information on the number of patients, embolization events, and various clinical characteristics. The outcome is logit-transformed proportions with the following predictors: sex_male, mean_age, balloon_expandable, stroke_incidence_percentage, PPM_requirement, conversion_to_open_heart_surgery, STS_risk_score, and annular_area_mm2. By selecting moderator variables for the metaregression model, the authors adopted a pragmatic approach based on data availability across studies. Specifically, they included variables that were clinically relevant and had sufficiently complete reporting in the studies included. Variables with excessive missing data were excluded to avoid substantial reduction in the number of studies included in the metaregression, which would limit statistical power and the generalizability of the results. First, the authors calculated the logit-transformed proportion of embolization for each study. Then, they conducted a metaregression to investigate the association between embolization and predictor variables.

Results

A total of 1184 potentially relevant studies were identified through the initial search. Following the screening of abstracts and the application of exclusion criteria, 34 studies were incorporated in the study (Figure 1). All studies were observational, and 16 were retrospective. Overall, 34 pertinent studies were identified that included a total of 36,008 patients, reporting 501 cases of TAVR device embolization (1.4%). Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of patients included in the studies. Males accounted for half of the study participants at 17,944 (50%). Of the reported embolization events, 235 occurred in the TRAVEL (TranscatheteR HeArt Valve EmboLization and Migration) registry, comprising 29,636 cohorts in which about 1% of the registry participants had an embolization event.

The mean age of current review cohorts was 80.7 ± 3.16 years, and the logistic European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) score was 15.90 ± 7.9. Of all cases, balloon-expandable valve (BEV) systems were used in 58% (21,222), self-expanding valve systems were used in 36% (13,008), Lotus valves were used in 2.5% (916), Direct Flow Medical valves were used in 17 (0.4%), and the type of valve used was not reported in the remaining 845 cases (Table 2). METAREGRESSION ANALYSIS

The overall estimated embolization rate across all studies was 0.01 (1%; 95% CI, 0.00–0.02). The true embolization rate is likely between 0% and 2%. Heterogeneity among studies was I² = 92 (P < .01). The observed heterogeneity is statistically significant, implying that differences across studies are unlikely due to random chance. Conversion to open-heart surgery was required in 304 patients (61%) for valve retrieval or replacement. Poorer outcomes were significantly associated with the conversion to open-heart surgery (β = 0.93; 95% CI, 0.57–1.3; P < .001). The incidence of stroke was higher in embolization cases (β = −1.4; 95% CI, −2.0 to −0.69; P < .001). The baseline Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk score showed a positive correlation with embolization (β = 1.4; 95% CI, 0.22–2.6; P = .02), suggesting that an elevated preprocedural risk is linked to less favorable results. No significant associations were found for sex (male; P = .3), mean age (P = .10), BEV type (P = .10), PPM (permanent pacemaker) requirement (P = .4), or annular area (P = .5). Discussion

The rate of TAVR procedures has increased substantially over the past 2 decades. The annual volume of TAVR now exceeds that of surgical aortic valve replacement in the United States. 44–46 There is growing evidence, including the PARTNER 3 (Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves) and Evolut Low Risk trials, demonstrating the noninferiority of TAVR to surgical aortic valve replacement, thus supporting the utilization of TAVR among intermediate risk patients with severe aortic stenosis. 44,45 The indications and safety of TAVR intervention have evolved due to technological advancements in prosthetic valves and equipment, combined with enhancements in the skill of interventional cardiologists. 46 This uptick in the use of TAVR has enabled interventional cardiologists to improve their skills, leading to a general decline in the incidence of valve embolization among other complications. The 2019 Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy (STS/ACC TVT) registry data reported a preponderance of BEVs as 72.3% vs 26.7% self-expandable valves, and 1% mechanically expanded valves, showing a shift from the traditional use of self-expandable valves. Changes in procedure performance (ie, shifting from general anesthesia to conscious sedation and majority adopting transfemoral access, which has been associated with lower rates of bleeding) reduced length of stay and contributed to improved outcomes after TAVR. 25 The authors noted a novel implementation of fracturing the surgical valve ring in valve-in-valve TAVR procedures, to mitigate patient-prosthetic mismatch. Among other notable factors responsible for improved procedural outcomes are decreasing age of patients undergoing TAVR and better risk profiles (demonstrated by lower risk scores compared with patient profiles from a decade ago). 46,47

In this study, PVE occurred in 1.4% of patients. The reported incidence across the studies included ranged from 0% to 20% (Table 2). The studies with the highest number of cases had relatively low incidence of PVE compared with studies with lower numbers of participants. The TRAVEL registry comprised 17 years of TAVR outcome data from 26 international institutions. 27 The authors reported PVE of 273 out of 29,636 cases (0.92%). Similarly, low embolization rates have been reported in PARTNER 3, a randomized controlled trial in patients undergoing TAVR. 28 The low incidence rates reported in clinical trials compared with real-life, single-center experiences and registry data could be attributed to stringent protocol and enlistment of experienced operators. Potential embolization sites include the aorta, left ventricle, or any other location in the systemic vasculature. 29 In the case of device migration, the valve remains attached to the aortic annulus but is positioned incorrectly in relation to the annular plane, either being too high (aortic) or too low (ventricular) without embolization. 48 Almost half of the embolized valves in the authors’ study migrated to the ascending aorta. This is consistent with findings from other studies that have identified the ascending aorta as the most common site of embolization. 49 Other commonly reported sites of embolization include the left ventricle, aortic root, and descending aorta. 49 Most cases of PVE occurred in the intraprocedural and immediate postprocedure period, similar to the findings from a systematic review conducted about a decade ago 50 and findings from recent studies. 51

Factors associated with PVE are technical (operator expertise and learning curve, positioning error, valve-in-valve procedures, valve retrieval), anatomical (bulky or paucity of calcified valve leaflets, aortic regurgitation), and device-related. Other studies have identified factors like horizontal aorta, implantation of self-expandable valve (SEV), and left ventricular ejection fraction less than 40%. 49 Reduction in TAVR patient outcomes has been attributed to operator expertise and centers with increasing experience. 52 For example, the PARTNER 2B (Placement of AoRTic TraNscathetER Valves II, Cohort B) trial reported no embolization event in more than 500 transfemoral implants, which the authors attributed to enhanced operator and center experience. 53 However, these authors did not report on the number of cases required by the operator and the sites to improve learning curve and achieve expertise. Wassef et al demonstrated that TAVR operator and site experience correlated with patient outcomes. Low-volume centers (< 50 cases per year) had 8.8% 30-day mortality vs 3.9% for high-volume centers (51–100 cases per year). Similarly, highly experienced TAVR operators (those who have performed > 300 cases) showed a mortality of 3.3%, which is lower than the reported outcomes according to the STS/ACC TVT registry. The authors showed that, to improve patient outcomes, operators require a minimum of 225 procedures to reach expert level, and annual institutional volume needs to exceed 50 cases. 36 In the US PARTNER trial, there was a noticeable reduction in valve embolization with increasing center experience and volume; this was further enhanced by improved expertise in transapical approach, whereas 83% of embolization occurred during the first 20 cases of the centers. The surgical approach has been suggested to play a role in the chance of embolization. Embolization tends to occur more via the transfemoral approach, but cases complicating transapical approach tend to have poorer outcomes, embolizing to the ventricle, and have higher mortality rates. In the authors’ study, causes of embolization were due to device malposition in 8 (1.6%) cases, loss of capture in 5 (1%) cases, and regurgitant homograft in 1 case; and possible causes of device embolization were not reported in the remaining 487 cases.

The optimal prosthetic valve system for TAVR patients has been the subject of inquiry. This study found that BEV systems were utilized more than other prosthetic valve systems. A retrospective study by Kim et al reported higher incidences of paravalvular leak and device embolization in the immediate postprocedure period in patients who had SEV, but these outcomes became similar by the end of the first year. 54 A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials showed that the BEV was noninferior to the SEV system. 55 That study also demonstrated a reduction in the rates of moderate to severe paravalvular leaks and permanent pacemaker implantation with BEV compared with SEV. 55 This was corroborated by the findings from a propensity-matched analysis by Eckel et al. 56 The 30-day mortality rate was reported at 3.7%; one study reported 30-day mortality rates ranging from 1.8% to 2.4%. 57,58 Due in large part to a higher probability of requiring hemodynamic support and conversion to open-heart surgery, the 1-year mortality rate following valve embolization is very high. 50 The chance of mortality is significantly heightened at about 18% to 20% at 30 days and ~30% at 1 year in some series, compared with much lower baseline rates for uncomplicated TAVR. 23

Management of an embolized prosthetic aortic valve depends on the site of embolization, among other factors. Many endovascular maneuvers have been utilized with good outcomes. 59,60 Embolization into the left ventricle is usually treated surgically. 59 Embolization can be prevented through adequate knowledge of the predisposing conditions, preprocedure planning, and appropriate techniques. 59,60 It is hoped this would update existing knowledge and serve as a reference to peer-reviewed data for scholarly discussions and evidence-based medicine for improved patient care and outcomes.

Study Limitations

There are several limitations to the current study. First, the observational design of the included studies renders the results susceptible to selection and confounding bias. To account for this, pooled estimates were calculated using random effects modeling to account for statistical heterogeneity. Secondly, the study population exhibits clinical heterogeneity due to the inclusion of research from various geographic regions worldwide. Third, most data were provided by registry database, and some studies did not include information on the site of and reason for embolization. The current recommendations advocate for reporting outcomes in accordance with the VARC-3 guidelines. However, many of the studies included, particularly the earlier ones, failed to report VARC-3 outcomes, leading to inconsistencies in outcome definitions within this meta-analysis. Despite these limitations, this meta-analysis aims to elucidate a vast and complex body of literature through systematic organization and aggregation of current data. Further research is needed to determine the anatomic, device, and procedural factors associated with device embolization.

Conclusion

Embolization of TAVR devices during interventions for aortic valve disease is rare but can lead to catastrophic consequences that necessitate targeted research. Meticulous planning and an understanding of the risks linked to embolization can markedly decrease their occurrence. Future research should focus on developing predictive risk models, optimizing device designs for improved positioning and retrieval, and enhancing procedural imaging to mitigate incorrect positioning. Large, collaborative global registries and comparative outcomes studies on management approaches will help shape best practices. Furthermore, research into biological parameters unique to each patient and simulation-based training can enhance device performance and operator readiness. Improving these areas will increase safety, minimize complications, and enhance outcomes in TAVR procedures.
