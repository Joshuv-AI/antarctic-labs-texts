The Favorable Short‐Term Efficacy of Preexisting COPD for Extensive‐Stage Small‐Cell Lung Cancer Receiving Immunotherapy
Authors: Ya Chen, Hongyu Liu, Junqiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPreexisting chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is associated with favorable clinical efficacy in NSCLC patients undergoing immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). But the impact of COPD comorbidities on SCLC patients that are receiving immunotherapy remains unclear.MethodsPatients with extensive‐stage SCLC (ES‐SCLC) received carboplatin combined with etoposide (carboplatin‐etoposide, EC group) or durvalumab plus carboplatin‐etoposide (EC + PD‐L1 group) treatment as first line therapy were collected. In EC + PD‐L1 group, survival of patients with preexisting COPD and patients without preexisting COPD were further compared.ResultsA total of 120 ES‐SCLC patients who met the eligibility criteria were included in this study. The median overall survival (OS) and the progression‐free survival (PFS) were significantly longer in the EC + PD‐L1 group than in the EC group, with a HR of 0.37 (95% CI, 0.16–0.88; p = 0.001) and 0.42 (95% CI, 0.23–0.77; p < 0.001), respectively. We further stratified groups by COPD status in EC + PD‐L1 group and EC group. In EC + PD‐L1 group, PFS was significantly prolonged in COPD group compared with the non‐COPD group, with a HR of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.35–0.96; p = 0.018). The COPD group demonstrated a numerically higher objective response rate than the non‐COPD group, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.292). No OS benefit was found between the groups.ConclusionPreexisting COPD status may be associated with favorable clinical efficacy in ES‐SCLC patients undergoing ICIs.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the primary cause of mortality in China and around the globe, which often coexists with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [1, 2]. COPD is characterized by progressive airflow limitation and tissue destruction, which has also been a major cause of morbidity, mortality, and health‐care use worldwide [3].

Largely, it has been revealed that there is a linear relationship between increasing COPD severity and lung cancer prevalence risk based on the link between persistent chronic inflammation and cancer development [4]. Patients with COPD demonstrate a 3‐ to 6‐fold increased risk of lung cancer development compared to individuals with normal pulmonary function [5]. Therefore, the overlap of COPD and lung cancer impacts a considerable number of individuals and portends a poor outcome, posing significant challenges for treatment but receiving insufficient attention [3, 6, 7].

Several studies aimed to investigate COPD's influence on NSCLC patients treated with ICIs and found that NSCLC patients with COPD or emphysema presented better PFS, OS, and ORR than NSCLC patients without COPD or emphysema [8, 9, 10, 11]. The mechanism remains unclear, partly because of a higher neoantigen burden, different genetic aberrations and pathways, and a higher level of CD68+ macrophages and CD8+ T lymphocytes in lung cancer patients with COPD [12, 13, 14]. However, research on the impact of COPD on the treatment response to ICIs in SCLC patients remains unexplored.

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 13%–15% of lung cancer cases and shows an aggressive form for a rapid proliferation rate, widespread metastases, and poor prognosis [15]. Extensive‐stage SCLC (ES‐SCLC) accounts for approximately two‐thirds of SCLC cases. Although SCLC initially responds well to platinum‐based chemotherapy, which has remained the first‐line regimen for the past 30 years, resistance happens rapidly, resulting in a disappointing 5‐year survival rate of less than 7%. Recent studies showed that SCLC harbors a high mutation burden and extensive chromosomal rearrangements, driving the development of immune‐checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as standard‐of‐care in first lines of treatment [15, 16, 17].

Given the observed correlation between COPD and enhanced efficacy of ICIs in NSCLC, this study aims to investigate the impact of COPD on the treatment response to ICIs in SCLC patients treated with durvalumab plus standard chemotherapy.

Materials and Methods

Patients

This analysis enrolled patients from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2024; the data cut‐off was 31 March 2025. One hundred twenty patients form the First Affiliated Hospital of USTC met the following inclusion criteria in this retrospective study: (1) Eligible patients were histologically or cytologically confirmed extensive‐stage SCLC as defined according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group staging system. (2) People who received carboplatin combined with etoposide (carboplatin‐etoposide, EC) or durvalumab plus carboplatin‐etoposide treatment as first line therapy. (3) Untreated measurable extensive stage small‐cell lung cancer according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. (4) An Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance‐status score of 0 or 1. Key exclusion criteria were a history of chemotherapy with or without immunotherapy as first line treatment or patients with acute exacerbation of COPD. Therapeutic schedule was decided by physician under the principle that patients with active autoimmune diseases, severe organ dysfunction, or those receiving high‐dose immunosuppressive therapy should avoid receiving immunotherapy. The flowchart for patient inclusion and exclusion was shown in Figure 1. Our study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki; it has been approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC with the approval number 2025‐RE‐337. The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

The flowchart for patient inclusion and exclusion.

Clinical Evaluation

Data regarding patient demographics, including age, sex, smoking history, ECOG performance status, disease stage, treatment, and pulmonary function were collected from medical records. The disease stage was decided on by the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group. Measurable ES‐SCLC was determined according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. Enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) and abdominal ultrasound scans were examined every 4–6 weeks or when symptoms progressed significantly. Enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed every 4–6 months if there was no brain metastasis at baseline and no symptoms thereafter. According to GOLD guidelines, COPD was defined as a pre‐bronchodilator ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity < 0.70 (FEV1/FVC < 0.70) and CT‐defined emphysema.

End Points and Assessments and Statistical Analysis

The categorical variables were compared by χ2 test. The end points were progression‐free survival (PFS, the time from first‐line therapy to disease progression or death whichever occurred first) and overall survival (the time from first‐line therapy to death from any cause) and ORR (the ratio of complete and partial response). Kaplan–Meier methodology was used to estimate PFS and OS. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to calculate the hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The multivariable model was adjusted for potential confounders, which strengthened the reliability and interpretability of the observed statistical significance.

Results

Clinical Feature

A total of 120 extensive‐stage small cell lung cancer (ES‐SCLC) patients who met the eligibility criteria and received either durvalumab plus carboplatin‐etoposide (EC + PD‐L1 group) or carboplatin‐etoposide alone (EC group) were included in this study. Baseline demographic, clinical characteristics, and lung function parameters including FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and DLCO%pre were well balanced between the two treatment groups (Table 1). The median age was 66 years (interquartile range [IQR], 59–73) in the EC + PD‐L1 group and 67 years (IQR, 59–74) in the EC group. The majority of patients were male (88% vs. 89%, respectively) and current or former smokers (62% vs. 64%, respectively). At baseline, in the EC + PD‐L1 group, 31 patients (34%) presented with CNS metastases and 17 (18%) had liver metastases; in the EC group, 5 patients (18%) had CNS metastases and 9 (32%) had liver metastases. COPD was present in 53 patients (58%) in the EC + PD‐L1 group and 14 patients (50%) in the EC group. The median FEV1/FVC was 64 (IQR 48–79) in the EC + PD‐L1 group and 68 (48–82) in the EC group. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups regarding age, sex, presence of CNS/liver metastases, COPD comorbidity, and lung function parameters including FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and DLCO%pre (all p > 0.05).

Survival Analysis of the Overall Population

Univariate analysis identified that patients with COPD (HR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.97, p = 0.036), those with liver metastasis (HR: 2.07, 95% CI: 1.28–3.33, p = 0.003), and patients who received EC + PD‐L1 (HR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.24–0.63, p = 0.001) were significantly associated with PFS. Multivariate analysis further demonstrated that the absence of liver metastasis and treatment with EC + PD‐L1 were independent prognostic factors for improved PFS. With regard to OS, univariate analysis identified patients with liver metastasis (HR: 3.01, 95% CI: 1.63–5.57, p = 0.001), and patients who received EC + PD‐L1 (HR: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.17–0.68, p = 0.002) were significantly associated with OS. Multivariate analysis further confirmed that the absence of liver metastasis and treatment with EC + PD‐L1 were independent predictive factors for prolonged OS (Table 2).

Survival Analysis of EC + PD‐L1 and EC Group as First‐Line Treatment in Patients With ES‐SCLC

At the data cut‐off, the median follow‐up duration was 10.57 months ([IQR], 7.8–14.2). Disease progression occurred in 70 patients (76%) in the EC + PD‐L1 group and 25 patients (89%) in the EC group. The median overall survival (OS) was significantly longer in the EC + PD‐L1 combination group (16.83 months; 95% CI, 10.04–23.63) than in the EC group (10.30 months; 95% CI, 6.73–13.88), with a HR of 0.37 (95% CI, 0.16–0.88; p = 0.001; Figure 2). Progression‐free survival (PFS) was significantly prolonged in the EC + PD‐L1 group compared with the EC group, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.42 (95% CI, 0.23–0.77; p < 0.001; Figure 3A). The median PFS was 7.73 months (95% CI, 6.29–9.18) in the EC + PD‐L1 group versus 5.47 months (95% CI, 4.68–6.25) in the EC group. The PFS benefit with EC + PD‐L1 was mostly observed across prespecified patient subgroups defined by baseline clinical and demographic characteristics, except for female patients, with brain metastasis, patients with liver metastasis, and non‐COPD patients (Figure 3B). In the female subgroup and metastatic subgroup, only a trend toward benefit with combination therapy was observed, without reaching statistical significance, which may be attributed to the small sample size of these subgroups. However, although the sample sizes were balanced between the non‐COPD and COPD subgroups, a differential treatment benefit was observed. Therefore, we further explored the treatment efficacy based on COPD comorbidity status.

We further stratified groups by COPD status in EC + PD‐L1 group and EC group. Baseline characteristic analysis demonstrated well‐balanced distributions between the groups (Table 3). In EC + PD‐L1 group, PFS was significantly prolonged in COPD group compared with the non‐COPD group, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.35–0.96; p = 0.018; Figure 4A). No OS benefit was found between the groups (Figure 4B). No significant differences in PFS or OS were observed between COPD and non‐COPD subgroups in the EC cohort (Figure 4C,D).

Tumor Response

53 (57.6%) of 92 patients had an objective response in the EC + PD‐L1 group compared with 13 (46.4%) of 28 patients in the EC group (p = 0.298; Figure 5A). In EC + PD‐L1 group, the COPD group demonstrated a numerically higher objective response rate (ORR) than the non‐COPD group, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.292; Figure 5B).

Adverse Events

Given the retrospective nature of this study, a complete analysis of subjective adverse events such as fatigue and vomiting was not feasible. We have conducted an analysis of the patients' laboratory and imaging data, focusing on adverse events of grade 3 or 4. Grade 3 or 4 pneumonia occurred in 5 (5%) patients in the EC + PD‐L1 group and 1 (4%) patient in the EC group. In terms of hematologic toxicity, the most common grade 3 or 4 adverse events were neutropenia, followed by anemia and decreased platelet count. Among patients with preexisting COPD, grade 3 or 4 pneumonia was observed in 3 (6%) patients in the EC + PD‐L1 group and 1 (7%) patient in the EC group. Discussion

In this real‐world study of previously untreated extensive‐stage small cell lung cancer (ES‐SCLC), we demonstrated significant clinical benefits in both PFS and OS with immunotherapy plus chemotherapy compared to chemotherapy alone, consistent with the findings of the CASPIAN trial [16]. Further prognostic analysis stratified by COPD status revealed that within the EC + PD‐L1 cohort, patients with COPD demonstrated significantly prolonged PFS compared to the non‐COPD group. In contrast, no significant association between COPD status and survival outcomes was found in the EC group, suggesting that COPD status may serve as a potential prognostic factor for immunotherapy response in this population. Further validation in prospective studies is warranted.

Approximately 40%–70% of patients with lung cancer also have COPD [5, 7]. The LC‐COPD patients presented significantly greater clinical management challenges. Studies found that the presence of emphysema was associated with longer progression‐free survival in NSCLC patients treated with immunotherapy [18]. Recently, a systematic review and meta‐analysis found that NSCLC patients that had preexisting COPD and CT‐defined emphysema were associated with favorable clinical efficacy in the immunotherapy era [19]. This may be attributed to the higher PD‐L1 expression and greater CD8+ T cell infiltration observed in the tumor microenvironment of NSCLC patients combined with COPD than those without COPD, though the underlying mechanisms remain under further investigation [8, 18]. Recently, genomic sequencing has revealed SCLC's complex mutational landscape, characterized by a high tumor mutational burden and frequent chromosomal rearrangements, suggesting a potential susceptibility to immunotherapy [20, 21]. For ES SCLC patients, CASPIAN and IMpower133 studies showed improved survival with PD‐L1 combined with traditional platinum‐based chemotherapy. Both atezolizumab and durvalumab were approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for ES‐SCLC, with improvement in survival increased by a little more than 2 months [16, 17]. However, research on the impact of COPD on the treatment response to ICIs in SCLC patients remains unexplored.

According to the international expert consensus on the management of COPD‐SCLC overlap patients, for patients with ECOG PS 0–2 and well‐controlled COPD (GOLD group A), first‐line therapy with platinum‐etoposide combined with PD‐L1 is recommended [22]. In our study, which aligns with the CASPIAN trial of durvalumab plus platinum‐etoposide, showed progression of immunotherapy in clinical outcomes for patients with ES‐SCLC treated in the first‐line setting [16]. Further study of the influence of COPD on ES SCLC patients receiving ICIs treatment found that in EC + PD‐L1 group, PFS was significantly prolonged in COPD group compared with the non‐COPD group, with a HR of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.35–0.96; p = 0.018). The COPD group demonstrated a numerically higher ORR than the non‐COPD group, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.292). No OS benefit was found between the COPD and non‐COPD groups. In the EC group, no significant difference in PFS or OS was observed based on COPD status. Although only the PFS improvement of COPD in the EC + PD‐L1 group reached statistical significance, a positive trend was also observed in ORR. The lack of statistically significant OS benefit may be attributed to limited sample size or the acute exacerbation of COPD itself on survival. These findings are partially consistent with results observed in NSCLC [19], which found preexisting COPD and CT‐defined emphysema were associated with favorable clinical efficacy (including ORR, DCR, PFS, and OS) in the immunotherapy era by meta‐analysis. The study suggests that the positive prognostic influence of COPD may be more pronounced in patients receiving immunotherapy rather than chemotherapy alone. The mechanistic basis for the differential responses of preexisting COPD to chemotherapy versus immunotherapy in lung cancer patients, particularly SCLC patients, remains poorly defined. Further study is warranted.

The application of ICIs has revolutionized oncological treatment; however, their use is associated with the development of checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis (CIP). In the CASPIAN trial, the incidence of grade 3–4 pneumonitis was 5 (2%) in the durvalumab plus platinum–etoposide group and 9 (3%) in the platinum–etoposide alone group [16]. In our study, the incidence of grade 3–4 pneumonitis was 5 (5%) and 1 (4%), respectively. Among patients treated with ICI plus chemotherapy, grade 3–4 pneumonitis occurred in 3 (6%) of those with COPD and 2 (5%) of those without COPD. In the chemotherapy‐alone group, grade 3–4 pneumonitis was observed in 1 (7%) of patients with COPD and 0 (0%) of those without COPD. Patients with COPD exhibited a relatively higher incidence of pneumonitis irrespective of the treatment regimen administered. Pre‐existing COPD has been recognized as a major risk factor for drug‐induced interstitial lung disease in patients with malignancies [23]. With the widespread application of immunotherapy in recent years, CIP has become increasingly common and has garnered growing research attention. Nevertheless, the impact of COPD on the risk of CIP in patients with lung cancer receiving ICIs remains controversial. Several studies have reported that pre‐existing COPD was not associated with an increased risk of CIP in lung cancer patients treated with ICI‐based therapy [9, 24]. But others revealed that lung cancer patients with COPD or CT‐defined emphysema undergoing immunotherapy have a significantly increased risk of developing CIP [25, 26]. Further investigations are warranted to clarify this issue.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to show an association between coexisting COPD and an improved response to ICIs in ES‐SCLC patients. There are some limitations in our study. First, our study is a single‐center research and the median follow‐up time was relatively short. Second, selection bias existed inevitably because of the nature of a retrospective study. However, the baseline clinical characteristics of patients between groups were balanced well, indicating that no large selection bias existed. Thirdly, the CASPIAN and IMpower133 trials conducted subgroup analyses of PD‐L1 status, whereas the present study did not show PD‐L1 status. This omission is attributable to the low cost‐effectiveness of PD‐L1 testing, which has led to its limited adoption in routine clinical practice. Recently, the CASPIAN and IMpower133 studies demonstrated that the observed immunotherapy plus chemotherapy survival benefit was irrespective of PD‐L1 status on subgroup analysis. It can therefore be inferred that the conclusions drawn in our study are not influenced by PD‐L1 status. Due to the limitations inherent in a retrospective study, the adverse reactions were not thoroughly detailed. Finally, the subgroup analysis stratified by COPD status was associated with limited statistical power (post hoc power = 59%), which was below the conventional 80% threshold due to the relatively small sample size within each subgroup. Despite this limitation, the difference in progression‐free survival between COPD and non‐COPD subgroups remained highly statistically significant (p = 0.018), suggesting that the observed clinical effect was sufficiently robust to be detected even in this subgroup with restricted statistical power. Therefore, while these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive, they provide clinically meaningful evidence warranting further validation in larger, prospective cohorts. Subsequent prospective studies will be conducted with rigorous documentation of multi‐dimensional COPD assessments and adverse events.

In conclusion, the present study found that COPD status may serve as a potential prognostic factor for immunotherapy response in the ES‐SCLC population, with significantly improved PFS and higher objective response in the COPD group.
