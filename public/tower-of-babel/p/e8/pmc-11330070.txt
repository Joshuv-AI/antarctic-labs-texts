Association between composite dietary antioxidant index and epilepsy in American population: a cross-sectional study from NHANES
Authors: Yuhao Zhang, Junyi Shen, Hao Su, Chunyan Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpilepsy is a major global health challenge, affecting approximately 50 million people across the globe and resulting in significant economic impacts on individuals and society. Oxidative stress is implicated in the pathogenesis of epilepsy, highlighting the potential of antioxidant-rich dietary patterns in offering preventive and protective benefits by mitigating oxidative stress. The Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI) provides a measure for assessing dietary antioxidant intake, yet its link to epilepsy remains unexplored.MethodsOur analysis utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2013 to 2018, including 20,180 screened participants. Weighted logistic regression models were employed to examine the association between the CDAI and epilepsy prevalence. Non-linear associations were explored through restricted cubic splines (RCS), and the relationships between individual antioxidant components within the CDAI and epilepsy were also assessed.ResultsAfter adjusting for potential confounders, a negative association between the CDAI and epilepsy was suggested (OR = 0.991; p = 0.087, 95% CI [0.819,1.014]). Stratification of CDAI into quartiles revealed a significantly reduced risk of epilepsy in higher CDAI quartiles (Q3 and Q4) compared to the lowest quartile (Q1) (Q3: OR = 0.419; p = 0.030, 95% CI [0.192, 0.914]; Q4: OR = 0.421; p = 0.004, 95% CI [0.239, 0.742]), with a significant trend observed across quartiles (p for trend = 0.013). RCS analysis suggested a nonlinear association between CDAI levels and epilepsy (non-linear p = 0.049), which, however, was not statistically significant after full adjustment (non-linear p = 0.103). Additionally, significant negative correlations with epilepsy were observed for vitamin A and zinc (Vitamin A: OR = 0.999; p = 0.012, 95% CI [0.998, 1.000]; Zinc: OR = 0.931; p = 0.042, 95% CI [0.869, 0.997]).ConclusionsOur research indicates a correlation where higher CDAI levels correspond to a reduced risk of epilepsy. Therefore, embracing a diet rich in antioxidants could be beneficial in preventing epilepsy. This finding holds considerable potential for shaping future strategies in both epilepsy prevention and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19794-y.Keywords: Antioxidant, Composite dietary antioxidant index (CDAI), Epilepsy, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), Oxidative stress

Introduction

Epilepsy is a severe brain disorder characterized by recurrent seizures, affecting nearly 50 million people worldwide. This condition not only leads to over 125,000 deaths annually but also results in significant disabilities, including cognitive impairment and motor disabilities [1]. As per the 2015 World Health Organization Burden of Epilepsy Report, epilepsy accounts for 0.5% of the total global burden of disease [2]. Current treatments primarily involve medication, yet most antiepileptic drugs fall short in terms of epilepsy prevention and neuroprotection [3], underlining the urgent need for more effective preventive and therapeutic approaches.

Oxidative stress, arising from an imbalance between reactive oxidizing substances (ROS) and antioxidants [4], has been increasingly recognized as a key player in the pathophysiology of epilepsy, contributing to neuonal damage and seizure exacerbation [5]. Excess ROS can oxidize DNA and lipids, inducing protein dysfunction that leads to cellular damage [6]. However, there is growing evidence that strategies targeting ROS can significantly protect neurons and curb the onset and progression of epilepsy, providing new insights into the prevention and treatment of epilepsy [7–10].

Diet plays a crucial role as an intervention targeting disease, and research on dietary patterns as tools for epilepsy prevention and treatment is burgeoning. Notably, strong evidence supports that the ketogenic diet prevents seizures in refractory epilepsy and offers long-lasting antiepileptic activity, thereby improving long-term disease outcomes [11]. The CDAI is a comprehensive tool for assessing antioxidant levels in dietary patterns, scored for six key dietary antioxidants (Vitamin A, Vitamin C, Vitamin E, selenium, zinc, and carotenoids), and has the potential to regulate oxidative stress. High CDAI scores, indicative of a dietary pattern rich in antioxidants, have been suggested to protect against various diseases, including diabetic nephropathy [12] and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [13], among others.

Based on this, we hypothesize that a higher CDAI diet may help prevent the onset of epilepsy, thereby providing more precise guidance for developing effective prevention strategies. However, these hypotheses still need to be validated through further experimental research.

Materials and methods

Study population

NHANES is a series of cross-sectional studies evaluating the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. This comprehensive survey employs methods such as household and telephone interviews, along with physical examinations. In-depth details about NHANES are available at other articles [14]. The National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board has granted approval for this survey. Prior to participation, informed written consent was obtained from all participants. From those who completed the NHANES 2013–2018 Medical Condition Questionnaire, we excluded individuals who did not provide adequate dietary information for calculating the CDAI or the subsequent day’s dietary follow-up data. This selection process resulted in a study cohort of 20,180 participants, which included 20,035 without epilepsy and 145 persons with epilepsy (Fig. 1).

The flow diagram of participants selection

Assessment of CDAI

Dietary intake data were collected via 24-h dietary recalls at the Mobile Examination Center. These data were then translated into nutrient intakes using the USDA Food and Nutritional Database for Dietary Studies. For each participant, dietary intake was assessed by averaging the information from two separate 24-h dietary recalls. The CDAI was determined by summing the standardized intakes of six vitamins and minerals (vitamins A, C, E, selenium, zinc, and carotenoids) sourced exclusively from food. The CDAI calculation involved aggregating these standardized dietary antioxidant intakes, followed by normalization through gender-specific means and standard deviations.

Evaluation of epilepsy

Epilepsy data were sourced from the NHANES questionnaire section titled "Prescription Drugs". Participants reported if they had used any medication in the past 30 days for treating "epilepsy and recurrent seizures" as classified under the International Classification of Diseases: G40. For this study, participants using such medications were identified as having epilepsy [15].

Covariates

Data on age, gender, race, annual family income, and histories of diabetes and hypertension were gathered through standardized household interviews. Racial categories included white, black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other races. Annual family income was divided into two categories: above or below $20,000. Diabetes and hypertension were identified based on participants' responses about having these conditions or taking respective medications. Physical measurements taken included height, weight, and waist circumference, from which body mass index (BMI) was calculated in kg/m^2. A BMI of ≥ 30 was classified as obesity.

Statistical analysis

In line with NCHS guidelines, we performed weighted omnibus analyses using two-day dietary sample weights from the NHANES 2013–2018 data. Participants were divided into quartiles based on their CDAI scores, with Quartile 1 representing the lowest and Quartile 4 the highest scores. Missing BMI data were substituted using median values. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations and analyzed using post hoc Student's t-test and ANOVA; categorical variables were expressed as percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and analyzed using the chi-square test. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% CIs for the likelihood of developing epilepsy were calculated based on CDAI using weighted logistic regression models. Three models were used: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for gender and age; and Model 3 further incorporated race, annual household income, BMI, and histories of diabetes and hypertension. The median of the CDAI quartiles was treated as a continuous variable in trend analysis. We included a RCS term in the weighted logistic regression for the CDAI, adjusted for confounders as per Model 3, using the "rcssci" package in R. The optimal number of knots, ranging from 3 to 7, was determined based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

The study encompassed 20,810 eligible individuals selected from three two-year cycles of NHANES data, with their baseline characteristics stratified by CDAI quartiles presented in Table 1. Notably, significant disparities emerged across CDAI quartiles in terms of age, race, BMI, annual household income, and hypertension prevalence (p < 0.001); while diabetes mellitus showed a borderline significance (p = 0.050) and was subsequently included as a study covariate. An increase in CDAI values was associated with an upward shift in the median age, a higher proportion of non-Hispanic whites, and an increase in higher annual household incomes. Furthermore, as CDAI escalated, the representation of participants with lower BMI diminished, in contrast to those with higher BMI. The prevalence of hypertension also rose with increasing CDAI values. Prior to adjustments for covariates, a correlation was observed between CDAI quartiles and epilepsy prevalence (p = 0.030).

The association between CDAI and epilepsy

Table 2 elucidates the outcomes of the weighted logistic regression analysis, investigating the link between CDAI and epilepsy. Analysis of CDAI as a continuous measure across Models 1, 2, and 3 hinted at a negative association with epilepsy, albeit suggestive (Model 1: OR = 0.910, 95% CI [0.818,1.011], p = 0.078; Model 2: OR = 0.904, 95% CI [0.813,1.004], p = 0.060; Model 3: OR = 0.911, 95% CI [0.819,1.014], p = 0.087). Further dissection into quartiles portrayed a significant negative correlation between higher CDAI quartiles (Q3 and Q4) and epilepsy across Models 1 and 2, which persisted in Model 3 after adjustment for all covariates (Q3 group Model 3: OR = 0.419, p = 0.030, 95% CI [0.192,0.914]; Q4 group Model 3: OR = 0.421, p = 0.004, 95% CI [0.239,0.742]). Trend tests across all models underscored this association (Model 3: p for trend = 0.013), suggesting a notably lower risk of epilepsy in participants with higher CDAI. A 4-knot RCS analysis based on the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles refined according to the AIC revealed a nonlinear relationship between CDAI and epilepsy pre-adjustment (nonlinear p = 0.049), which diminished post-adjustment (nonlinear p = 0.103), as depicted in Fig. 2.

The association between antioxidants and epilepsy

Subsequent analysis through a separate weighted logistic regression examined the influence of the six antioxidants constituting the CDAI on epilepsy. This analysis, summarized in Table 3, unveiled significant negative associations with epilepsy for vitamin A and zinc when considered as continuous variables (vitamin A: OR = 0.999; p = 0.012, 95% CI [0.998,1.000]; zinc: OR = 0.931; p = 0.042, 95% CI [0.869,0.997]). When classified into quartiles, vitamins A and C, carotenoids, and vitamin E displayed varying degrees of significant negative associations with epilepsy, highlighting the protective potential of higher antioxidant levels. The RCS analysis further revealed nonlinear dose–response relationships for carotenoids and zinc with epilepsy, manifesting as V-shaped curves (Fig. S1 and Fig. S2), indicating intricate associations between antioxidant levels and epileptogenesis.

Discussion

The study based on the NHANES database exploring the relationship between CDAI and epilepsy indicates a reduced prevalence of epilepsy among individuals with higher CDAI values. This suggests that dietary pattern with high antioxidant levels may significantly lower the risk of developing epilepsy. Future preventive strategies focusing on antioxidant-rich dietary interventions could potentially play a key role in lowering epilepsy incidence and alleviating the associated public health burden.

Prior to this study, the relationship between the CDAI and epilepsy had not been explored. Current research indicates that epilepsy classification and pathogenesis are complex [16], with oxidative stress proven to play an important role. Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between the production of oxidants and the capacity of endogenous antioxidants [17]. Reactive substances can cause damage to CNS neurons [18]. Oxidative stress, which increases ROS levels, can contribute to epilepsy by adversely affecting neuronal cells through influencing relevant signaling pathways or by causing nonspecific oxidative damage to biomolecules [17, 19]. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that a dietary pattern of high antioxidant levels may reduce the risk of epilepsy by decreasing oxidative stress in brain through the action of multiple antioxidants acting individually or in combination. This has been verified in a number of relevant studies. One study suggested that, the utilization of vitamin C in animal models has been shown to reduce neuronal damage caused by free radicals [20], and ascorbic acid pretreatment is observed to enhance superoxide dismutase and catalase activity, delay the onset of the first seizure, and reduce the severity of brain injury [20–22]. Similarly, vitamin E pretreatment has been found to lower the incidence of seizures, extend the time before the first seizure occurs, and improve survival rates [23, 24].

Interestingly, we observed that the relationship between dietary patterns characterized by high antioxidant levels and epilepsy may not exactly be a simple linear dose–response relationship. In our weighted logistic regression analysis of the CDAI grouped by quartiles, we found that the risk ratio for epilepsy onset was significantly reduced by more than half in the third quartile (Q3) compared to the fourth quartile (Q4), in contrast to the first quartile (Q1); however, such results were not observed in the second quartile (Q2). Although the non-linear p-value from the RCS analysis was not significant after adjusting for all covariates, the resulting curve still suggests a trend toward a more pronounced non-linear relationship. This trend implies that the CDAI value might need to reach a certain threshold to significantly reduce the risk of epilepsy. Similarly, non-linear relationships have been reported in the context of CDAI and other diseases. For instance, Ma et al. highlighted that the relationship between CDAI and coronary heart disease exhibited a non-linear negative correlation (L-type) in RCS analysis, with stable results in sensitivity analysis [25]; Wang et al.'s research on CDAI and stroke also underscored the presence of a non-linear negative correlation [26]. Therefore, the potential threshold effect and non-linear relationship of CDAI in the prevention of epilepsy warrant further research.

Our study analyzed six indicators of the CDAI in relation to epilepsy and found several positive results. For instance, vitamins A and zinc were found to reduce the risk of epilepsy, and partial quartile groupings of vitamins C and E, along with carotenoids, showed favorable effects. These findings align with previous research. For instance, a study involving 29 patients with epilepsy and 50 control subjects revealed significantly lower plasma levels of vitamins C and A in the epilepsy group. Post-treatment observations indicated normalization of plasma vitamins A, E, and C in treated patients [27]. Additionally, a randomized controlled trial by Ogunmekan et al. assessed the impact of vitamin E on seizure control. In this study, 10 out of 12 epileptic patients receiving 400 mg of D-alpha tocopheryl acetate daily experienced a significant reduction in seizures, while the control group showed no change in seizure frequency [28]. Moreover, our findings revealed a non-linear relationship between the intake of carotenoids and zinc and the incidence of epilepsy. The RCS curves for carotenoids and epilepsy formed a V-shape, indicating that both insufficient and excessive amounts of dietary carotenoids may not be beneficial in preventing epilepsy. In a similar vein, a study demonstrated that the relationship between β-carotene and hepatic steatosis was non-linear, with the protective effect in the T3 group with higher β-carotene being weaker than that in the T2 group [29]. The interaction between zinc and epilepsy appears to be more complex. Zinc, a crucial component of antioxidant mitochondrial metalloenzymes and capable of binding to metallothioneins to exert antioxidant effects [30], also plays a pivotal role in maintaining the balance between neuronal excitation and inhibition through modulation of various targets. This balance is thought to be fundamental to the pathophysiology of epileptic seizures [31]. Therefore, zinc's potential protective role against epilepsy, attributed not only to its antioxidant properties but also to its influence on the central nervous system's excitatory-inhibitory balance, results in a non-linear dose–response relationship with epilepsy, as evidenced in our study.

It’s important to recognize that foods, according to the dietary pattern approach, comprise multiple nutrients and components that are consumed in combination and may interact in complex ways [32]. This indicates that the effects of a single antioxidant might differ when it's part of a broader diet rich in antioxidants. In fact, the bulk of current research is geared towards understanding the therapeutic potential of antioxidant monomers as pharmacological agents, such as the previously mentioned RCT by Ogunmekan et al. [28]. However, different studies on the same single antioxidant have yielded inconsistent results. For instance, a randomized controlled trial assessed the impact of supplementing conventional antiepileptic drug therapy with D-alpha-tocopherol in 43 adults diagnosed with refractory epilepsy. Unfortunately, the findings did not corroborate the therapeutic benefits of vitamin E [33]. Similar challenges, such as small sample sizes and inconsistent results from different studies on the same antioxidant, have plagued research on other antioxidant supplements like melatonin and N-acetylcysteine [34, 35] in the context of epilepsy treatment. Therefore, final conclusions on the potential benefits of using antioxidant monomers as add-on neuroprotective therapies cannot be provided at this time [19]. In contrast to numerous preceding studies, we interpreted the benefits of antioxidants in epilepsy within the context of a holistic dietary pattern, rather than isolating the impact of individual antioxidants. Additionally, the dietary pattern-based antioxidant intake is markedly disparate from the manner in which antioxidant monomers were administered and the doses utilized as medications for epilepsy in previous studies. This discrepancy might explain the inconsistent and inconclusive results from studies focusing on individual antioxidants in relation to epilepsy, as opposed to the observed reduced epilepsy risk associated with dietary patterns high in antioxidants, as measured by the CDAI. Given the safety and scalability of dietary patterns, CDAI-based dietary intervention strategies hold considerable potential by guiding public diet to increase the intake of antioxidants such as vitamins A, C, and E, selenium, zinc, and carotenoids to prevent epilepsy. This study also offers a novel perspective on the intersection of nutrition and epilepsy prevention and treatment, highlighting the significance of dietary methods in managing oxidative stress. Future research should delve deeper into the relationships between antioxidant-rich dietary patterns, oxidative stress levels, and epilepsy incidence to provide more rigorous evidence supporting the findings of this study.

The robustness of our study is anchored in its reliance on data from the NHANES. Utilizing NHANES' stratified multistage probability sampling approach ensures high levels of reliability and representativeness of our research findings. Furthermore, by incorporating a range of covariates such as age, gender, race, annual household income, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes into our regression analyses, we were able to mitigate the influence of potential confounding factors significantly. Nevertheless, our investigation faces certain constraints. Primarily, its design as a cross-sectional survey inherently limits its capacity to establish causality between the CDAI and epilepsy. Additionally, our reliance on participants' self-reported medication histories to identify epilepsy cases, as opposed to clinical diagnoses by healthcare professionals, could lead to misclassification errors. The exclusion of individuals with incomplete dietary information might also have altered the characteristics of the study population, potentially influencing the accuracy of our findings. Despite the comprehensive range of covariates adjusted for in our analysis, it's conceivable that other unaccounted-for confounding variables could still affect the outcome of our study.

Conclusion

Our research indicates that an elevated CDAI correlates with a reduced epilepsy risk, suggesting that dietary patterns rich in antioxidants could play a preventive role against epilepsy. Nutrients such as Vitamin A and zinc appear to offer protective benefits against epilepsy. However, the evidence linking individual antioxidants with epilepsy risk remains inconsistent, warranting a cautious interpretation of these findings. The potential for a nonlinear correlation between CDAI levels and the incidence of epilepsy also merits deeper investigation. It underscores the need for future rigorous experimental and prospective cohort studies to explore the relationship between diets high in antioxidants, oxidative stress levels, and epilepsy, thereby further validating our findings.
