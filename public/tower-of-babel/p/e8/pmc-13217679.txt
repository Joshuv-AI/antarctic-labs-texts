HDAC6 inhibition reprograms endothelial and macrophage responses to protect the lung in endotoxemia
Authors: Yanlei Li, Yan Wang, Wei Li, Jinyan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndotoxemia causes pulmonary barrier disruption, endothelial dysfunction, and myeloid-mediated inflammatory responses leading to sepsis-associated acute lung injury. Histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) regulates cytoskeletal dynamics and inflammatory signaling; however, its cell-type-specific effects in inflammatory lung injury remain undetermined.MethodsWe conducted single-cell RNA sequencing in a mouse endotoxemia model to investigate the effects of HDAC6 inhibition on endothelial and macrophage responses. Key findings were confirmed using histology and bulk RNA sequencing studies.ResultsIn endothelial cells, lipopolysaccharide induced inflammation and STAT pathway activation. HDAC6 inhibition with CAY10603 suppressed these changes, lowered inflammatory and apoptotic scores, and maintained endothelial cells toward earlier pseudotime states. Concurrently, HDAC6 inhibition via CAY10603 treatment restored angiogenesis-associated gene expression (Acadl, Adrb2, Cd24a, Ecm1, and Ptgs1). In macrophages, CAY10603 treatment suppressed lipopolysaccharide-induced interferon, inflammasome, and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signatures and downregulated key gene expression (Isg15, Ifit1/2, Lcn2, Txnip, and Irf1), maintaining them in less activated states. These findings correlated with reduced alveolar congestion and leukocyte infiltration and suppressed inflammation signatures.ConclusionsSelective HDAC6 inhibition suppresses endothelial activation and macrophage inflammatory programs while promoting angiogenic transcriptional programs, supporting further evaluation of HDAC6-targeted strategies for sepsis-associated lung injury.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Histone deacetylase 6, Transcriptomics, Endotoxemia, Endothelial cell, Inflammation, Macrophage

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Sepsis remains a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide [2, 3]. Therefore, therapies that prevent organ injury and improve survival in patients with sepsis are urgently needed [4, 5]. Sepsis and endotoxemia are closely connected pathological settings that often occur together in severe infections [6, 7]. Endotoxemia refers to the release of endotoxins (lipopolysaccharides, LPS) derived from the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria into the bloodstream [7]. LPS is a potent activator of the immune system that triggers excessive inflammatory responses [7, 8]. Endotoxemia is a key initiating and amplifying factor in sepsis pathogenesis caused by gram-negative bacterial infections [6, 7]. When LPS enter the circulation, they activate immune cells, including macrophages, to release proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), leading to systemic inflammatory responses, endothelial injury, and altered coagulation [4, 8]. Clinical trials of sepsis therapies targeting individual inflammatory or immune mediators in sepsis have largely failed, reflecting the biological complexity of host responses [4, 5].

Mechanistic studies that can resolve pathogenic programs at cellular resolution are needed to identify therapeutic targets [9, 10].The lung is a major organ affected during sepsis and endotoxemia; pulmonary endothelial activation and dysfunction contribute to sepsis-associated acute lung injury [11, 12]. The activated endothelium, through upregulated expression of adhesion molecules and cytokines, increases leukocyte recruitment and vascular permeability, leading to lung edema, hypoxemia, and tissue damage [10, 12]. Concurrently, lung macrophages amplify inflammation via cytokine production, inflammasome activation, and inflammatory cell death, further aggravating lung injury [13, 14]. Therefore, interventions that maintain endothelial cell function while restraining macrophage inflammatory programs are attractive therapeutic strategies for preventing sepsis-induced lung injury.

Histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) is a cytoplasmic deacetylase that modulates nonhistone substrates, including α-tubulin and chaperone proteins [15]. HDAC6 regulates cytoskeletal dynamics, inflammatory signaling pathways, and barrier integrity [15, 16]. Additionally, HDAC6 regulates innate immune signaling and inflammasome biology [13, 14, 17]. During endotoxemia, HDAC6 expression substantially increases along with its enzymatic activity, thereby affecting downstream signaling pathways. Studies have shown that after LPS stimulation, the expression of HDAC6 considerably increases [18, 19].In LPS-stimulated macrophages (such as RAW 264.7 cells), the mRNA and protein levels of HDAC6 increase in a dose-dependent manner [20]. Similarly, it has been reported that the expression of HDAC6 substantially increased in LPS-stimulated oral squamous cell carcinoma cells (OSCC) and fibroblasts [19]. Furthermore, in our previous study involving animal experiments, we observed that LPS induces an increase in HDAC6 expression [18]. Importantly, the upregulation of HDAC6 expression induced by LPS is accompanied by a decrease in the acetylation level of α-tubulin as its classical substrate, confirming the enhancement of its enzymatic activity [20]. A previous study has reported that LPS stimulation may induce an increase in HDAC6 expression by reducing miR-22 level, thereby confirming the enhancement of its enzymatic activity [20]. The study also reported that after LPS stimulation, HDAC6 expression may increase through the reduction of miR-22 level and that in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells, the level of miR-22 decreased while the level of HDAC6 mRNA increased in a dose-dependent manner. HDAC6 is a direct target gene of miR-22 in macrophages [20, 21]. Selective HDAC6 inhibition preserves endothelial junctional proteins and prevents caspase activation in lung endothelium, reduces inflammasome activation and proinflammatory cytokine production, and ameliorates inflammatory lung injury, suggesting that HDAC6 is a potential target for regulating endothelial and macrophage responses in endotoxemia [14, 16, 22].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a powerful platform for elucidating heterogeneous cellular states and dynamic transcriptional programs underlying sepsis and acute lung injury [9]. Recent single-cell studies in human sepsis and experimental models have disclosed cell-type-specific and temporally evolving pathogenic signatures [9, 10]. The candidate regulatory pathways obtained from cell-resolved transcriptional maps could help identify therapeutic targets. Despite these insights, the cell-type-specific transcriptional programs modified by HDAC6 inhibition in lung inflammatory injury during endotoxemia have not been characterized at single-cell resolution. Thus, we performed scRNA-seq in a mouse model of endotoxemia using the selective HDAC6 inhibitor CAY10603, focusing on endothelial and macrophage populations and validating results via histology and bulk RNA-seq analyses.

Methods

Reagents

β-Actin, ICAM-1, phospho-STAT3, phospho-STAT1, STAT1, and STAT3 were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). TNF-ɑ, IL-6, and IL-1β enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were obtained from ELK Biotechnology (Wu Han, China). Cleaved caspase-3 ELISA kit was obtained from Ray Biotech (Guang Zhou, China). CAY10603 (CAY) was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). LPS from Escherichia coli 0111:B4 was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Collagenase type I, dispase, and DPBS were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Red blood cell lysis buffer was purchased from Miltenyi Biotec (Cologne, Germany).

Mouse model of endotoxemia

Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 9 weeks were purchased from the College of Basic Medical Sciences at Jilin University (Changchun, China). All experiments and animal care procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Jilin University. Briefly, 7.5 mg/kg LPS in phosphate-buffered saline was injected intraperitoneally (IP) to induce endotoxemia. Mice were pretreated with CAY10603 (3 mg/kg body weight) for 2 h via IP injection before LPS challenge. Experiments were terminated 24 h after LPS challenge. Lung tissues were then collected for analyses.

Lung histology

For histological analysis, paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate pulmonary inflammation and injury. The Smith scoring method was applied in a single-blind manner to perform quantitative pathological assessment. Semiquantitative evaluations were conducted for pulmonary edema, alveolar and interstitial inflammation, alveolar and interstitial hemorrhage, atelectasis, and hyaline membrane formation. Each parameter was scored on a 0–4 scale: 0, no injury; 1, lesions involving < 25% of the field; 2, 25%–50%; 3, 50%–75%; and 4, lesions occupying the entire field of view.

ELISA

TNF-ɑ, IL-6, IL-1β, and cleaved caspase-3 levels were measured using the corresponding ELISA kits. Briefly, 18 h after LPS injection, lung tissues were collected and homogenized in tissue lysis buffer. Lung tissue TNF-ɑ, IL-6, IL-1β, and cleaved caspase-3 levels were determined using ELISA following the steps in the kit manual.

Preparation of mouse lung tissue single-cell suspension and scRNA-seq

Finely minced lung tissues were digested in a solution containing collagenase type I (1 mg/mL) and dispase (0.5 mg/mL), followed by incubation in a 37 °C water bath shaker at 100 rpm for 30 min. After digestion, the resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 30-µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect cells. The cell pellet was resuspended in red blood cell lysis buffer to remove erythrocytes, and then washed twice with DPBS to eliminate debris and impurities. The cells were subsequently resuspended in 1× DPBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA; 130-091-376; Miltenyi Biotec) and passed through a 30-µm strainer to ensure a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was kept on ice until scRNA-seq.

Cell viability and concentration were assessed, with samples showing > 80% viability selected for downstream processing. The final single-cell suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 700–1200 cells/µL and loaded onto the 10× Genomics Chromium platform, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting sequencing libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) configuration, achieving a sequencing depth of at least 20,000 reads per cell.

Bioinformatic analysis

After sequencing, Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) was used to filter raw data, perform read alignment and quantification, identify recovered cells, and generate gene expression matrices for each cell. The resulting single-cell gene expression matrix was further analyzed using Scanpy, including steps for cell filtering, data normalization, cell subpopulation classification, differential gene expression analysis, and marker gene identification across subpopulations.

Cell identification

For this analysis, mouse-derived single-cell data were processed, yielding a total of 74 663 cells. After quality control and filtering, 68 515 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Clustering analysis revealed 30 distinct cell clusters. Preliminary cell type annotation revealed a diverse range of cell populations, including B-cells, T-cells, NK cells, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, neutrophils, alveolar type II (AT2) cells, alveolar type I (AT1) cells, megakaryocytes, and ciliated cells.

Cell subpopulation identification

Cell Ranger was used for sequencing data alignment and quantification, followed by downstream analysis with Scanpy. The main analytical steps included cell filtering, data normalization, batch effect correction, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction, cell clustering, marker gene identification, and cell type annotation.

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and scoring

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEApy package (version 1.1.1) to identify enriched pathways among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The HALLMARK and other MSigDB gene sets were used as reference functional gene sets to calculate enrichment scores for each cell. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied to assess significant differences in enrichment scores across different groups and cell types. Mouse-specific gene sets were employed for this analysis, and statistical significance was defined as a corrected p-value of < 0.05.

Pseudotime analysis

Single-cell trajectory analysis was conducted using Monocle2 with the DDR-Tree algorithm and default parameters. Prior to trajectory inference, cell expression data were selected and filtered based on cell clusters identified through Scanpy analysis. Monocle2 applies a reverse graph embedding machine learning approach to reconstruct the global trajectory of gene expression dynamics from single-cell data, positioning cells along a continuum that represents differentiation progression and defining the root state of the trajectory. Following pseudotime analysis, the Branch Expression Analysis Model (BEAM) was employed to identify and characterize gene expression changes across branching points of the trajectory.

Preparation of mouse lung tissue for bulk RNA-seq

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and RNA concentration was determined using the NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies). Only high-quality RNA samples were selected for library construction.

Library preparation and sequencing

RNA purification, reverse transcription, library preparation, and sequencing were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were generated using the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation kit (Illumina) with 1 µg of total RNA. Briefly, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) beads via poly(A) selection and subsequently fragmented using fragmentation buffer. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript Double-Stranded cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen) and random hexamer primers (Illumina). The resulting cDNA was subjected to end repair, phosphorylation, and 3′ adenylation following the standard library construction protocol by Illumina. Libraries were size-selected (~ 300 bp) using 2% Low Range Ultra Agarose, followed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification with Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) for 15 cycles. After quantification with Qubit 4.0, the paired-end libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina) using a 2 × 150 bp read configuration.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance and post hoc multiple comparison tests were used for multi-group comparisons and the Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons unless otherwise specified. The data are presented as the mean ± SE. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of < 0.05.

To explore the gene expression landscape of endothelial cells from control, we examined LPS-treated, CAY-treated mice and a subset of 2741 endothelial cells from the scRNA-seq dataset. Analysis of the DEGs in each group indicated that HDAC6 inhibition by CAY treatment modulated the expression of immune and inflammatory response genes in endotoxemia (Fig. 1b).

HDAC6 inhibition alleviates endothelial inflammatory signaling and pro-inflammatory responses during endotoxemia

Endothelial cell activation and dysfunction are the major contributors to tissue damage during endotoxemia [23, 24]. The GSVA and HALLMARK pathway analysis of endothelial cells in each group using the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 2a, b) revealed that endothelial cell inflammatory responses and activation were upregulated by LPS treatment, whereas they were suppressed by CAY treatment. Additionally, we evaluated the GSVA enrichment scores of inflammatory and cell activation pathways in endothelial cells across the three groups and found that these scores were higher in the LPS group than in the other groups (Fig. 2c, d). The STAT signaling pathways, including the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 and IL-2/STAT5 pathways, were activated following LPS treatment, whereas they were downregulated following CAY treatment (Fig. 2e). Our results showed that HDAC6 inhibition suppresses endothelial cell inflammatory responses and signaling during endotoxemia.

HDAC6 inhibition suppresses endothelial cell activation in endotoxemia

We conducted pseudotime analysis on lung endothelial cells to assess dynamic gene expression changes along the trajectory. The distribution of endothelial cells along the pseudotime trajectory indicated their relative cellular states (Fig. 3). Endothelial cells in the control and CAY-treated groups were distributed at the early stages of the trajectory, whereas those in the LPS group were mainly distributed at the late stages of the trajectory branches (Fig. 3a–c). Additionally, we analyzed the relative expression levels of representative genes. The expression of Jak1, Stat3, and Icam-1 was downregulated by CAY treatment compared with that in the LPS group. Along the pseudotime trajectory, the expression of these genes initially decreased (primarily in the control and CAY-treated groups) and subsequently increased (primarily in the LPS group) (Fig. 3d). These results suggest that HDAC6 inhibition suppresses endothelial cell activation during endotoxemia.

HDAC6 inhibition promotes endothelial cell angiogenesis in endotoxemia

Increased angiogenesis facilitates the repair of inflammatory damage [25, 26]. We conducted GSVA and heatmap analyses, which revealed that endothelial cell angiogenic responses were downregulated by LPS treatment, whereas they were upregulated by CAY treatment (Fig. 4a, b). The UMAP analysis elucidated the gene distribution in endothelial cells of the CAY group (Fig. 4c). Additionally, we analyzed the relative expression levels of representative genes. The expression of Acadl, Adrb2, Cd24a, Ecm-1, and Ptgs1 was upregulated by CAY treatment compared with that in the LPS group. Along the pseudotime trajectory, the expression of these genes increased in the CAT-treated group, but decreased in the LPS group (Fig. 4d).

We evaluated the GSVA enrichment scores of apoptotic pathways in endothelial cells across the three groups and found that these scores were higher in the LPS group than in the other groups (Fig. 5a). The expression of Cd14, Emp1, Timp3, Plat, Sod2, Tap1, Ifitm3, Mcl1, and Casp4 positively correlated with apoptotic activation [27–33], whereas that of Cav1, Timp2, Smad7, Cd38, and Ccnd1 showed a negative correlation [33–36]. Along the pseudotime trajectory, the expression of Cav1, Timp2, Smad7, Cd38, and Ccnd1 initially increased (primarily in cells from the control and LPS + CAY groups) and subsequently decreased (primarily in cells from the LPS groups) (Fig. 5b). In addition, the expression of several genes, including Cd14, Emp1, Timp3, Plat, Sod2, Tap1, Ifitm3, Mcl1, and Casp4, was downregulated following CAY treatment, as shown in the heatmap (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

Using scRNA-seq, we provided a cell-resolved atlas describing the means by which selective HDAC6 inhibition with CAY10603 remodels lung cellular programs during endotoxemia. The integration of high-quality single-cell profiles and focused investigation on endothelial cells allowed us to resolve coordinated transcriptomic shifts across endothelial and macrophage responses, revealing that HDAC6 inhibition simultaneously suppresses inflammatory and apoptotic programs, attenuates macrophage inflammasome activation, preserves endothelial angiogenic signatures, and reduces tissue leukocyte infiltration and lung inflammation. These findings provide mechanistic, cell-specific evidence that HDAC6 targeting may mitigate key pathophysiologic responses in sepsis-associated lung injury.

The translational rationale for focusing on endothelial and macrophage responses is well established [9, 11, 39]. Endothelial activation and loss of barrier integrity are major contributors to sepsis-associated acute lung injury [11, 39]. Activated endothelium exhibits upregulated expression of adhesion molecules and chemokines, recruits leukocytes, and increases vascular permeability, which collectively drive pulmonary edema and impaired gas exchange [10, 40]. Our observation that LPS drives endothelial cells into late pseudotime states characterized by upregulation of inflammatory pathways (including JAK1/STAT3 and ICAM1) aligns with established LPS/TLR4 signaling in endothelium and with the role of STAT3/JAK signaling in endothelial responses to endotoxin [40, 41]. HDAC6 inhibition reversed these transcriptomic signatures and shifted endothelial cells toward earlier pseudotime positions resembling controls, consistent with the suppression of endothelial activation and stabilization of endothelial cell function. The single-cell data helped reveal the cellular and molecular mechanisms of HDAC6 inhibition to endothelial-specific transcriptional programs that underlie reduced vascular leak and improved tissue architecture.

One of the intriguing results of our study was the dual action of HDAC6 inhibition in limiting injury pathways and promoting repair programs in endothelial cells. Prior experimental work has shown that HDAC6 inhibition preserves endothelial junctional proteins and prevents caspase-3 activation in lung endothelium and reduces inflammasome and inflammatory signaling in LPS models, supporting the plausibility of our findings [22, 42]. In macrophages, LPS triggers inflammatory and interferon-stimulated programs and drives inflammasome activation, a process that promotes IL-1β maturation, gasdermin-dependent pyroptosis, and tissue damage; these inflammasome mechanisms and their central role in inflammation are well documented [13, 14, 43]. Recent mechanistic work has also implicated HDAC6 as an important facilitator of NLRP3/pyrin inflammasome assembly, providing a direct mechanistic link between HDAC6 activity and inflammasome-dependent amplification of inflammation [14].

HDAC6 inhibition decreased inflammatory and apoptosis-related enrichment scores while increasing angiogenesis-related signatures and expression of genes (e.g., Acadl, Adrb2, Ecm1, and Ptgs1) linked to repair and vascular homeostasis [44–46]. Under endotoxemia, vascular permeability increases and the endothelial barrier is disrupted. Endothelial angiogenesis mainly exerts protective effects by improving the endothelial cell barrier disruption induced by endotoxemia. Reportedly, vasculotide prevents pulmonary vascular leakage caused by endotoxemia, protects the endothelial barrier, and increases the survival rate of septic mice. Its mechanism includes activating the Tie-2 protein and its downstream mediators, and promoting endothelial angiogenesis [47]. Roundabout 4 (Robo4) is a protein specifically expressed in vascular endothelium. In animal models of endotoxemia, Robo4 has been proven to play a role in preventing vascular leakage, and the corresponding regulation of endothelial angiogenesis is related to Robo4 [48]. HDAC6 inhibition not only suppresses damaging signaling cascades but also upregulates endothelial transcriptional programs toward recovery and barrier restoration, which is an attractive property for therapeutic candidates to possess as it reduces injury while permitting repair.

In the present study, macrophages showed a complementary response, including a robust LPS-induced activation of inflammatory, interferon-stimulated, and inflammasome-associated genes (including Isg15, Ifit1/2, Lcn2, Txnip, and Irf1), whose expression was suppressed by HDAC6 inhibition. Pseudotime patterns of macrophages mirrored the endothelial responses, with macrophages maintained at earlier and quiescent states after HDAC6 inhibition, whereas LPS-stimulated macrophages localized to later and activated states, indicating that HDAC6 inhibition restrains macrophage activation and inflammasome-driven amplification in inflammation. Given the critical role of macrophage inflammasome activation in IL-1β maturation, pyroptosis, and tissue damage, the downregulation of these transcriptional pathways by HDAC6 inhibition could contribute to the reduced lung inflammation and injury.

The single-cell findings were validated using histological examinations and ELISA. The lung tissues showed less alveolar congestion, edema, and cellular infiltration following HDAC6 inhibition. Increased lung tissue levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and cleaved caspase-3 induced by LPS were decreased by CAY treatment. Bulk RNA-seq of the lung tissues also showed suppressed inflammation signatures in the lung after HDAC6 inhibition. The cell-specific transcriptional changes and organ-level readouts support biological relevance and phenotypic consequences of transcriptional reprogramming.

Our study has some limitations. Although endotoxemia models recapitulate many key features of gram-negative bacterial toxin exposure, they do not capture the full heterogeneity and hemodynamic/host-comorbidity complexity of clinical sepsis [49–51]. Finally, although selective HDAC6 inhibitors (including CAY10603) show promising activity in preclinical models, careful attention to potential off-target effects and dose/selectivity issues is warranted when translating these findings to clinical practice [52, 53].

Conclusions

In summary, selective HDAC6 inhibition induces coordinated and cell type-specific transcriptomic changes that suppress inflammatory and apoptotic programs, inhibit macrophage inflammasome activation, and promote endothelial angiogenic and barrier-preserving signatures, leading to reduced lung inflammation and injury in endotoxemia. Our data provide mechanistic and cell-specific support for advancing HDAC6-targeted strategies as potential interventions to mitigate sepsis-associated lung injury.
