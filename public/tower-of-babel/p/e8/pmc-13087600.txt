A sensory-computational framework to decipher and predict binary aroma interactions: A case study of furaneol with five Co-odorants
Authors: Jingtao Wang, Wu Fan, Chen Cheng, Shan Wang, Zhongrong Jiang, Qidong Zhang, Qingzhao Shi, Tiqing Guan, Dongliang Li, Guobi Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the critical role of aroma interactions in shaping food flavor, their molecular mechanisms remain elusive. Here, binary interactions between furaneol and five co-odorants (guaiacol, acetylpyrazine, eugenol, 2-phenylethanol, valeric acid) with OR5M3 were investigated using an integrated sensory-computational approach. Sensory analysis classified furaneol-eugenol as synergistic, furaneol-valeric acid as masking, and the remaining pairs as additive or indifferent. Docking revealed that the synergistic pair had the most favorable binding energy (−10.11 kcal/mol), while the masking pair had the weakest (−7.95 kcal/mol). MD simulations showed synergy correlated with low RMSD and sustained H-bonds, whereas masking involved large RMSD fluctuations, fewer H-bonds. MM/PBSA quantified these differences: the synergistic complex displayed the strongest binding affinity (−23.64 kcal/mol), the masking complex the weakest (−4.24 kcal/mol) due to a large solvation penalty. This work establishes a predictive strategy based on the OR5M3 receptor model for decoding and designing aroma interactions at the molecular level.Keywords: Aroma compounds, S-curve method, Molecular docking, Molecular dynamics simulations, MM/PBSA

Introduction

The perception of food flavor is a complex sensory experience that arises not only from the presence of individual aroma compounds but, more critically, from their intricate interactions(Khorisantono et al., 2025). These interactions; which can be synergistic; additive; masking; or indifferent; profoundly influence the overall aroma profile and intensity; ultimately defining consumer preference and product character(Wang, Wang, et al., 2024). While the identification of key odorants is fundamental; understanding how these compounds behave in mixture is essential for accurately predicting and engineering flavor in food products; including fermented; roasted; and thermally processed items such as tobacco; coffee; and baked goods(X. Chen et al., 2023).

Among the diverse array of aroma compounds, furaneol (4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone) is recognized as a key contributor to caramel-like, sweet, and fruity notes, prevalent in strawberries(Moazzem et al., 2024); pineapples(Sasaki et al., 2015); thermally processed foods(Haag et al., 2021); and tobacco(Khachatoorian et al., 2022). Similarly; other compounds such as guaiacol (smoky; phenolic); acetylpyrazine (roasty; nutty); eugenol (spicy; clove-like); 2-phenylethanol (floral; rose-like); and valeric acid (cheesy; sweaty) are widely distributed in various food systems and are crucial for defining specific aroma attributes(Dunkel et al., 2014). The perception of these aroma compounds is mediated by olfactory receptors (ORs); which are G protein-coupled receptors expressed in olfactory sensory neurons(Buck & Axel, 1991). Upon odorant binding, ORs initiate a signal transduction cascade that ultimately leads to odor perception. Understanding how individual odorants interact with their cognate receptors is fundamental to deciphering the molecular basis of aroma perception. However, real-world food matrices contain complex mixtures of odorants, and the mechanisms by which multiple compounds collectively modulate receptor activation remain poorly understood. Therefore, selecting an appropriate receptor to investigate interactions between aroma compounds is particularly important.

The selection of OR5M3 as the receptor model in this study was based on grounded in comprehensive experimental evidence from Krautwurst et al.(Haag et al., 2021). Through bidirectional screening of 616 human odorant receptor variants against 187 key food odorants, they demonstrated that OR5M3 is exclusively and concentration-dependently activated by furaneol, establishing it as a narrowly tuned, dedicated receptor for this key aroma compound. This exceptional specificity makes OR5M3 an ideal physiological model for investigating how the presence of co-odorants modulates furaneol-mediated receptor activation, a question that extends beyond single-ligand screening to address the more ecologically relevant scenario of complex odor mixtures.

Recent evidence demonstrates that antagonistic interactions between odorants at the receptor level can profoundly alter human odor perception. Fukutani et al. showed that β-ionone, which does not activate sulfur-responsive receptors, acts as a competitive antagonist of OR2T11 and OR2T1 by binding to the same orthosteric pocket as their agonists methanethiol and hydrogen sulfide. Crucially, human sensory evaluation confirmed that β-ionone significantly reduced the perceived intensity and unpleasantness of these malodors. This suppression was abolished when the compounds were presented to different nostrils, demonstrating that the masking effect originated at the peripheral receptor level(Fukutani et al., 2023). This paradigm establishes that odorants can functionally interact within a single receptor's binding pocket, providing a mechanistic basis for investigating binary mixtures beyond simple agonist screening. The potential of the five co-odorants selected in this study (guaiacol, acetylpyrazine, eugenol, 2-phenylethanol, valeric acid) to influence furaneol binding through competitive or cooperative interactions within the same binding pocket forms the central hypothesis of this work.

To systematically investigate such interactions, the dose response S-curve method, combined with the three-alternative forced choice (3-AFC) sensory test, provides a robust psychophysical approach for determining odor thresholds and evaluating mixture effects(Wang, Dong, Liu, et al., 2025). This method allows the differentiation between synergistic; additive; masking; and neutral interactions by comparing observed thresholds with theoretical predictions(Zhu et al., 2025). However; while sensory studies reveal the perceptual outcome; the molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions remain largely unexplored. Computational approaches such as molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation offer powerful tools to probe the atomic-level behavior of odorants with olfactory receptors; revealing binding stability; conformational dynamics; and key interaction patterns that may explain observed sensory phenomena(Wang, Wang, Lang, et al., 2025).

In this study, we first determined the individual detection thresholds and interaction relationships of the six aroma compounds using the dose response S-curve method. Subsequently, molecular docking was employed to examine the binding modes of furaneol and the five other odorants with the olfactory receptor OR5M3. Based on the docking results, MD simulations were further conducted to characterize their dynamic binding behavior and complex stability. By integrating sensory analysis with in silico simulations, this work provides a theoretical basis for interpreting and predicting aroma interaction phenomena at the molecular level.

Materials and methods

Materials

The following high-purity chemicals were used in this study: Guaiacol (99% purity), 2-Acetylpyrazine (98% purity), Eugenol (99% purity), 2-Phenylethanol (99% purity), and Valeric acid (99% purity) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Furaneol (98% purity) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd.

Sensory evaluation

This study involved sixteen trained panelists (8 males and 8 females, aged 22–32 years) who were all non-smokers and free from rhinitis. All participants had prior experience in sensory evaluation. As this study was considered minimal-risk sensory analysis under applicable national regulations, formal ethical committee approval was not required, and an exemption was obtained. In the absence of a mandatory ethics review process, the following protocols were implemented to safeguard participant rights and welfare: written informed consent was obtained from each individual after a full explanation of the study's purpose, procedures, and data usage policies; participation was entirely voluntary, with no coercion; participants were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty; and all collected data were anonymized to protect privacy. No vulnerable populations were involved in this research.

The olfactory thresholds were measured using the three-alternative forced-choice (3-AFC) procedure(Wang, Wen, Zhang, et al., 2025). Odorant samples were prepared by serial dilution in ethanol with a dilution factor of 2 over 10 concentration steps. In each 3-AFC trial, panelists were presented with three samples: two containing only ethanol and one containing the odorant dissolved in ethanol at a specified concentration. Panelists were asked to identify the sample that differed from the other two. This design inherently controls for any potential sensory contribution from ethanol, as all samples contain identical ethanol concentrations. Detection of the odorant is therefore based solely on the presence of the additional aroma compound, with ethanol serving as a neutral background common to all stimuli. The correct response rate was adjusted using the correction factor P = (3*p-1)/2 to detect the probability of correct detection. In this context, x represents the logarithm of the concentration, x0 represents the logarithm of the threshold value, b = 1/D is the slope of the S-curve, and the concentration corresponding to the corrected detection probability P = 0.5 is considered the measured threshold value.

The theoretical detection probability of a binary mixture, P(AB), was calculated using the formula P(AB) = P(A) + P(B) – P(A)P(B), where P(A) and P(B) represent the detection probabilities of individual components A and B, respectively. A theoretical concentration-response (S) curve was then generated by plotting substance concentrations against these calculated probabilities, and the concentration corresponding to a corrected detection probability of P = 0.5 was defined as the theoretical threshold. The interaction between components was categorized based on the ratio of the experimentally measured threshold to this theoretical threshold: a ratio below 0.5 indicates synergy; a ratio between 0.5 and 1 suggests an additive effect; a ratio equal to 1 implies no interaction; and a ratio greater than 1 signifies a masking effect(Zeng et al., 2024).

Molecular docking

The protein was processed the following processing using AutoDockTools 1.5.6 software. First, the odorant receptor protein and small molecule ligand PDB files were added. Then, hydrogen atoms were introduced and Gasteiger charges were computed, resulting in output saved as a .pdbqt file. To define the binding site for docking, we adopted a targeted approach based on the orthosteric pocket characterized in human olfactory receptors. Recent cryo-EM structures of multiple ORs, including OR51E2(Billesbølle et al., 2023) and OR6A2(Wang et al., 2026); have revealed that the ligand-binding pocket is highly conserved across this receptor family. This pocket is located within the transmembrane bundle; formed by transmembrane helices 3; 4; 5; 6; and 7 (TMs 3–7); together with extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) that folds into the core of the binding cavity. Structural comparisons of consOR1; consOR2; consOR4; and consOR6 demonstrate that ligands consistently bind to this conserved cavity despite sequence variations(de March et al., 2024). The center of the docking grid box was set to encompass this conserved binding cavity. The size of the grid box was set to 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å, which was sufficient to cover the entire predicted binding pocket and allow for adequate ligand exploration.

For single-ligand docking, furaneol alone was placed into the binding pocket. For binary mixtures, both ligands were docked simultaneously into the same binding pocket by merging their respective docking parameter files (DPF) into a single configuration file, allowing both molecules to be treated as flexible ligands within the same docking run. This simultaneous docking approach allows both molecules to explore the available space within the pocket and compete for favorable binding positions, more accurately reflecting the scenario of co-binding in a mixture.

During docking, the receptor was kept rigid to reduce computational cost, a standard practice for high-throughput docking, while all rotatable bonds of both ligands were treated as flexible to enable full conformational sampling. The Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) was employed for conformational search, with the following parameters: number of GA runs set to 100, population size of 150, maximum number of energy evaluations set to 2,500,000, and maximum number of generations set to 27,000. Other parameters were kept at default values(Xiao et al., 2024). Docking was performed using AutoDock4. Binding energies were exported, and the resulting protein-ligand complexes were saved as .pdb files using PyMOL.

Molecular dynamics simulation

All MD simulations were carried out employing the GROMACS package (version 2023.2) coupled with the CHARMM36m force field. Simulation boxes were constructed utilizing CHARMM-GUI. For all protein-ligand complexes, the PPM 2.0 functionality was employed to align the transmembrane helices of the protein structure and insert them into a bilayer composed of 75% palmitoyl-oleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (POPC) and 25% deprotonated cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHSD), with CHSD placed around the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) structure(Billesbølle et al., 2023). Solvation was performed using the TIP3P water model, supplemented with 0.15 M potassium chloride ions for neutralization, while maintaining a temperature of 310 K. Energy minimization and equilibration were conducted iteratively six times, each equilibration phase lasting 5 ns. Subsequent to equilibration, the final MD simulations were executed with a time step of 2 fs over a total simulation duration of 100 ns.

To ensure the reliability of the subsequent analyses, the convergence of the simulations was assessed by monitoring the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone and the ligand heavy atoms relative to the initial minimized structure, as well as the stabilization of the total potential energy of the system over time. For all simulated complexes, the RMSD values reached a stable plateau (typically within the first 50 ns) and maintained consistent fluctuations thereafter, indicating that the systems had achieved equilibrium.

Finally, the dynamic binding of the ligands to the receptor was characterized by analyzing the RMSD, the number of hydrogen bonds, and the free energy landscape.

Binding free energy calculations of the receptor with ligand

The molecular mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method was employed to calculate the binding free energy of protein-ligand complexes. This method combines the van der Waals and electrostatic interaction energies from molecular mechanics with the solvation energy computed via the Poisson-Boltzmann equation. MD simulation trajectories were processed, and 100 evenly spaced snapshots extracted from the equilibrated portion (50–100 ns) were selected for MM/PBSA calculations using the gmx_MMPBSA suite(Wang, Chang, et al., 2024). The binding free energy was calculated using the formula: ΔGbind = ΔGgas + Δsolv - TΔS, where ΔGelec and ΔGvdw represent the electrostatic and van der Waals interaction energies, respectively; ΔGsolv is the solvation free energy, which includes the polar (ΔGpolar) and nonpolar (ΔGnon-polar) components; ΔGgas represents the molecular mechanics energy of the solute in vacuum, comprising bonded (bond, angle, dihedral) and nonbonded (electrostatic and van der Waals) interactions. The entropy term (TΔS) was estimated using normal mode analysis via the linear response method. Prior to entropy calculation, each snapshot was minimized using the conjugate gradient method with a convergence criterion of 10−4 kcal/mol Å−1 and a distance-dependent dielectric constant (ε = 4r) to implicitly account for solvent effects. All calculations were performed within the GROMACS software package.

Results and discussion

Assessment of odor thresholds

The odor detection thresholds (OTs) of the six individual aroma compounds were determined using the dose response S-curve method combined with the 3-AFC sensory test. This approach, which integrates psychophysical measurement with sigmoidal fitting, provides a robust and quantitative means to assess the sensitivity of the olfactory system to each compound, forming a fundamental basis for evaluating their interactions in mixtures.

As summarized in Fig. 1, the OTs varied considerably among the compounds, reflecting their differing potencies. Acetylpyrazine exhibited the lowest threshold (0.0018 μg/mL), indicating its high aroma potency even at trace concentrations. Furaneol showed a threshold of 0.12 μg/mL, consistent with its recognized role as a key caramel-like odorant. Eugenol (0.026 μg/mL) and valeric acid (0.67 μg/mL) displayed intermediate thresholds, while 2-phenylethanol required a higher concentration (1.10 μg/mL) to be detected. Guaiacol had the highest OT (6.58 μg/mL) among the set, suggesting a relatively lower impact on the aroma profile at equal concentrations. All fitted S-curves yielded R2 values above 0.95, confirming the reliability of the threshold determinations and the suitability of the model for describing the concentration-detection relationship.

Binary interactions via the S-curve method

The interactions between furaneol and the other five aroma compounds in binary mixtures were quantitatively assessed by comparing the experimentally measured detection thresholds with the theoretically predicted values (Fig. 2). The results revealed distinct interaction patterns, categorized as synergistic, additive, masking, or indifferent(Zhu et al., 2023). Specifically, the mixture of furaneol and eugenol exhibited a strong synergistic effect (ratio < 0.5), indicating that their co-presence significantly enhances the overall aroma intensity beyond a simple additive effect. In contrast, combinations of furaneol with guaiacol or acetylpyrazine showed additive effects (0.5 ≤ ratio < 1), where the combined perception approximates the sum of individual contributions. No significant interaction was observed between furaneol and 2-phenylethanol (ratio ≈ 1), while the mixture with valeric acid demonstrated a clear masking effect (ratio > 1), with valeric acid suppressing the detectable intensity of furaneol.

Classification of binary mixture interactions based on experimental vs. theoretical threshold ratios.

The observed synergistic and masking effects may be attributed to several potential mechanisms at the molecular and perceptual levels. Synergy, as seen in the furaneol-eugenol pair, could arise from complementary molecular structures that facilitate co-binding within the binding pocket of an olfactory receptor, leading to enhanced receptor activation or stabilized binding conformation(Zhu et al., 2025). Alternatively; these compounds might activate different but functionally coupled receptors; resulting in supra-additive neural signaling in the olfactory pathway(Reyes et al., 2019). In contrast; masking effects; such as that exerted by valeric acid over furaneol; may result from competitive binding at the same receptor site; where the higher affinity or concentration of one odorant reduces the effective receptor occupancy of the other(Fukutani et al., 2023). Moreover; perceptual interactions at the central nervous level; such as cross-modal suppression (where a stimulus in one sensory modality inhibits the perception in another) or cognitive overshadowing of sweeter notes by dominant fatty/cheesy odors; could also contribute to the observed reduction in detection(Wang, Wen, Zhang, et al., 2025). Although the precise mechanism requires further investigation, these interactions likely reflect a combination of olfactory receptor dynamics and central perceptual integration.

These findings have notable implications for the understanding and design of flavor systems in complex food matrices. The synergistic effect between furaneol and eugenol, for instance, may explain the enhanced sweet-spicy aroma profile in certain thermally processed foods, baked goods, or tobacco, where both compounds are naturally present(Huang et al., 2019). Such synergy can be strategically leveraged in flavor formulation to achieve desired sensory intensity with lower ingredient usage; offering potential for cost-effective and clean-label product development. The additive interactions observed with guaiacol and acetylpyrazine suggest that these compounds contribute independently in blends; allowing for more predictable and controllable flavor balancing in products like roasted coffee; smoked meats; or savory snacks(Hu et al., 2022; Lacalle-Bergeron et al., 2020). Conversely, the masking effect of valeric acid toward furaneol highlights the importance of compositional balance, as the presence of certain fatty or cheesy notes could undesirably dampen desired caramel-like sweetness-a consideration crucial in dairy-based or fermented flavor systems(Sui et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019). Overall, mapping these binary interactions provides a foundational sensory blueprint for optimizing aroma compositions and predicting perceptual outcomes in real food products.

Molecular docking

To elucidate the structural basis underlying the sensory interactions identified by the S-curve method, molecular docking was performed to investigate the binding modes of furaneol and the five other aroma compounds with the olfactory receptor OR5M3. The docking results provided insights into the potential molecular mechanisms responsible for the observed synergistic, additive, and masking effects.

As a single ligand, furaneol docked into the orthosteric binding pocket of OR5M3, forming a key hydrogen bond with His107 (Fig. 3A). This interaction suggests a stable binding mode that may contribute to its distinct caramel-like odor perception. When a second odorant was introduced, the binding conformation of furaneol shifted significantly: in most binary complexes, it no longer interacted with His107 but instead formed hydrogen bonds with Tyr176 and/or Asp179. The only exception occurred in the furaneol-eugenol pair, where furaneol formed a hydrogen bond with His174 (Fig. 3B).

Notably, the docking results revealed distinct interaction patterns that correlate well with the sensory-derived interaction categories. In the synergistic furaneol-eugenol pair, both ligands formed multiple hydrogen bonds within the binding pocket, with eugenol interacting specifically with Asp179. This complementary binding pattern likely stabilizes the receptor-ligand complex, providing a structural explanation for the observed supra-additive sensory effect. Valeric acid, which exhibited sensory masking toward furaneol, formed a hydrogen bond with Tyr257. This suggests direct competition for the binding site, likely displacing or destabilizing the optimal binding conformation of furaneol(C. Chen et al., 2025). This competitive interaction aligns with the observed reduction in furaneol detection in the mixture. For the additive pairs (furaneol-guaiacol and furaneol-acetylpyrazine), guaiacol did not form direct hydrogen bonds with OR5M3, while acetylpyrazine interacted with Tyr257. The absence of strong competitive or stabilizing interactions in these complexes may explain their additive sensory behavior. Similarly, the indifferent furaneol-2-phenylethanol pair showed no specific hydrogen-bonding interaction for 2-phenylethanol, consistent with its neutral sensory effect.

The binding energies derived from molecular docking provide a quantitative measure of the stability of the complexes, and these values show a clear correspondence with the sensory interaction patterns identified earlier (Table 1). Furaneol alone exhibited a binding energy of −4.694 kcal/mol. When docked as binary combinations, the synergistic pair furaneol-eugenol achieved the lowest binding energy (−10.11 kcal/mol), indicating the highest thermodynamic stability, which correlates with its pronounced perceptual synergy. In contrast, the antagonistic pair furaneol-valeric acid yielded the highest binding energy (−7.95 kcal/mol) among the mixtures, reflecting weaker binding and supporting the masking effect observed in sensory evaluation. The additive pairs, furaneol-guaiacol (−9.117 kcal/mol) and furaneol-acetylpyrazine (−9.58 kcal/mol), displayed intermediate binding energies, consistent with their combined but non-synergistic sensory impact. The indifferent combination furaneol-2-phenylethanol (−9.404 kcal/mol) also fell within this intermediate range, further supporting its neutral sensory interaction. These results suggest that binding energy can serve as a predictive indicator for the type of binary aroma interaction: lower (more negative) binding energies tend to accompany synergistic enhancements, whereas higher (less negative) energies align with masking or antagonistic effects.

These docking results suggest that the nature of binary aroma interactions may be governed by the complementarity or competition of binding poses within the olfactory receptor binding pocket. Synergistic pairs like furaneol-eugenol appear to engage in cooperative binding that enhances receptor activation, whereas masking pairs like furaneol-valeric acid likely compete for overlapping binding regions, reducing overall detection. This structure-based perspective bridges sensory phenomenology with molecular interaction models, offering a mechanistic framework for predicting and designing aroma combinations in complex flavor systems.

It is important to acknowledge that the molecular docking performed in this study employed a rigid receptor model. While this is a standard and computationally efficient approximation for high-throughput screening and initial binding mode prediction, it does not capture potential induced-fit effects or conformational rearrangements within the receptor's binding pocket that may occur upon ligand interaction. To address this limitation and to rigorously assess the stability and dynamic behavior of the predicted binding conformations, we extended our investigation with 100 ns MD simulations.

Molecular dynamics simulations

While molecular docking provides static binding poses, MD simulations offer critical insights into the temporal stability and conformational dynamics of ligand-receptor complexes. This approach allows us to assess whether the interactions predicted by docking are maintained under simulated physiological conditions and to link dynamic stability to the perceptual outcomes observed in sensory studies(Bi et al., 2025).

To assess the dynamic stability of the aroma-receptor complexes, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the ligand binding core relative to the initial docked pose was analyzed. The RMSD trajectories revealed pronounced differences in conformational stability that correlate directly with the sensory interaction patterns.

The complex of furaneol with eugenol, which exhibited strong synergy in sensory tests, demonstrated the most stable binding dynamics. Its RMSD equilibrated rapidly within the first 10 ns of simulation and maintained a consistently low fluctuation range of 1.5–2.0 Å for the remainder of the 100-ns trajectory. This indicates a tightly bound and conformationally locked state, suggesting that the synergistic enhancement arises from a highly stable dual-ligand binding mode that minimizes structural drift within the receptor pocket. In stark contrast, the furaneol-valeric acid complex, associated with sensory masking, displayed marked instability. Its RMSD values exhibited large-amplitude fluctuations between 3.5 and 5.5 Å throughout the simulation, without reaching a stable plateau. This substantial and persistent deviation indicates weak binding, frequent conformational rearrangements, or partial dissociation, providing a dynamic rationale for the observed antagonistic effect where one component interferes with the stable binding of the other. The additive (furaneol-guaiacol, furaneol-acetylpyrazine) and indifferent (furaneol-2-phenylethanol) complexes showed intermediate stability, equilibrating after ∼50 ns with fluctuations around 2 Å, similar to furaneol alone (Fig. 4).

RMSD of 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations.

The analysis of hydrogen bond dynamics further elucidates the intermolecular interactions underlying the observed sensory phenomena. Hydrogen bonds are crucial for stabilizing ligand-receptor complexes, and their abundance and persistence directly reflect binding affinity and specificity(Rahman et al., 2021). In this study, the number of hydrogen bonds formed during the 100-ns simulation varied significantly across different binary complexes and correlated with their respective interaction categories.

The synergistic furaneol-eugenol complex exhibited a high and stable hydrogen bond count (total 1888), with sustained density throughout the simulation (Fig. 5). This indicates that both compounds cooperatively engage with complementary residues in the binding pocket, forming a networked and stable interaction interface that likely enhances receptor activation and explains the perceptual synergy. Conversely, the antagonistic furaneol-valeric acid system showed a markedly lower hydrogen bond count (total 325), which notably declined after 50 ns. This reduction aligns with the unstable binding observed in RMSD analysis and suggests that valeric acid competes for binding sites or induces conformational changes that disrupt the hydrogen-bonding network essential for furaneol recognition. Thus, a significant decrease in hydrogen bonds can serve as a dynamic indicator of masking or antagonistic interactions. The additive pairs displayed moderate hydrogen bond counts (904 for guaiacol, 679 for acetylpyrazine).

Hydrogen bond dynamics between odorants and OR5M3 during molecular dynamics simulations.

A notable exception was the furaneol-2-phenylethanol pair, which despite forming a very high number of hydrogen bonds (total 2099), showed no significant sensory interaction (Fig. 5). This can be attributed to the hydroxyl group on 2-phenylethanol, which acts as an effective hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor, enabling extensive but non-specific interactions with polar residues on the receptor surface or solvent. Importantly, these interactions do not interfere with the binding pose or stability of furaneol, as evidenced by its RMSD profile mirroring that of furaneol alone. This case highlights that while hydrogen bond quantity reflects binding capability, it does not necessarily translate into functional synergy or interference; the spatial arrangement, specificity, and functional outcome of these bonds are critical.

The free energy landscape (FEL) analysis provides a thermodynamic perspective on the stability and conformational flexibility of the various ligand-OR5M3 complexes, directly complementing the dynamic metrics of RMSD and radius of gyration. By projecting the simulation trajectories onto two essential collective variables, the FEL visualizes the probability distribution of system states, where deeper and narrower energy minima correspond to more stable and conformationally restricted complexes(Feng et al., 2024).

The FEL of the synergistic furaneol-eugenol complex exhibited a single, deep, and well-defined global minimum (Fig. 6). This indicates that the system predominantly samples a highly stable binding pose with low conformational entropy, aligning with its rapid RMSD equilibration and sustained hydrogen-bond network. The depth of this energy well reflects a significant thermodynamic stabilization, which likely underlies the enhanced receptor activation and the perceived sensory synergy. In contrast, the antagonistic furaneol-valeric acid complex displayed a shallow and broad energy basin with multiple local minima (Fig. 6). This landscape signifies higher conformational freedom, lower thermodynamic stability, and the absence of a dominant, well-bound pose-consistent with the large RMSD fluctuations and the declining hydrogen bond count observed. The system's tendency to populate higher-energy states explains its unstable binding and provides a thermodynamic basis for the masking effect.

FEL analysis of odorant-OR5M3 complexes.

For the additive (furaneol-guaiacol, furaneol-acetylpyrazine) and indifferent (furaneol-2-phenylethanol) pairs, the FELs showed energy minima of moderate depth and breadth, situated between the extremes of synergy and antagonism (Fig. 6).

MM/PBSA calculations of binding energetics

Building upon the dynamic binding profiles revealed by MD simulations, which highlighted differences in conformational stability among the aroma receptor complexes, a quantitative assessment of binding affinity is essential to fully understand the thermodynamic driving forces behind the observed interactions. To this end, MM/PBSA method was employed to calculate the binding free energies of furaneol alone and its binary complexes with the five co-odorants. This approach provides an energetic perspective that complements the dynamic insights from MD simulation, linking structural stability with thermodynamic favorability and offering a deeper mechanistic explanation for the sensory interaction patterns previously identified(Wang, Chang, et al., 2024). The furaneol-eugenol complex, which exhibited strong synergy in sensory tests, displayed the most favorable binding free energy (ΔGbind = −23.64 ± 0.13 kcal/mol). Energy decomposition reveals that this stability originates from exceptionally strong van der Waals (ΔGvdw = −40.63 ± 0.10 kcal/mol) and electrostatic interactions (ΔGelec = −33.81 ± 0.16 kcal/mol). The dominant van der Waals contribution reflects optimal shape complementarity and hydrophobic packing between eugenol and the nonpolar residues lining the binding pocket, while the strong electrostatics arise from the specific hydrogen bonds observed in docking. This thermodynamically reinforced binding mode aligns with the low RMSD, high hydrogen-bond count, and deep free-energy well observed in MD simulations.

In contrast, the furaneol-valeric acid pair, associated with sensory masking, shows the weakest binding affinity (ΔGbind = −4.24 ± 0.15 kcal/mol). Notably, its negligible van der Waals contribution (ΔGvdw = −5.52 ± 0.22 kcal/mol) indicates poor shape complementarity, while the exceptionally strong gas-phase electrostatics (ΔGelec = −122.74 ± 1.95 kcal/mol) are nearly canceled by an equally large solvation penalty (ΔGsolv = 124.02 ± 1.97 kcal/mol). This suggests that the charged nature of valeric acid is poorly accommodated in the predominantly hydrophobic binding site, resulting in a loosely bound complex consistent with the high RMSD fluctuations and declining hydrogen bonds observed in MD simulation.

The furaneol-2-phenylethanol complex presents an instructive exception. It exhibits a fairly strong calculated binding energy (ΔGbind = −22.00 ± 0.14 kcal/mol), driven by favorable van der Waals (ΔGvdw = −33.28 ± 0.11 kcal/mol) and electrostatic (ΔGelec = −23.10 ± 0.15 kcal/mol) interactions. This aligns with the high hydrogen-bond count observed in MD simulation. However, this energetic stability did not translate into perceptual synergy or interference. The RMSD profile mirrored that of furaneol alone, indicating that 2-phenylethanol likely binds to an adjacent or peripheral site without perturbing furaneol's binding pose or functional efficacy. This underscores that favorable binding energetics must be coupled with functional relevance to produce perceptual effects. Specifically, a compound must be able to modulate the primary ligand's binding mode or receptor activation to influence sensory perception. This counterexample highlights that while computational approaches effectively calculate binding affinity, they do not capture the actual extent of receptor activation under physiological conditions, further reflecting the complexity of in vivo olfactory perception. Future studies should integrate computational predictions with functional assays (e.g., cAMP accumulation or calcium imaging) to comprehensively assess receptor activation, thereby distinguishing true agonists from antagonists and ultimately refining the predictive power of computational frameworks for flavor research.

The complexes corresponding to additive sensory effects, furaneol-guaiacol (ΔGbind = −12.80 ± 0.12 kcal/mol) and furaneol-acetylpyrazine (ΔGbind = −9.51 ± 0.12 kcal/mol), display intermediate binding affinities with correspondingly moderate van der Waals contributions (−28.51 ± 0.07 and − 24.89 ± 0.11 kcal/mol, respectively). These van der Waals values, while substantial, are considerably lower than those observed for the synergistic pair (−40.63 ± 0.10 kcal/mol), suggesting that these compounds achieve moderate shape complementarity within the binding pocket but lack the optimized hydrophobic packing characteristic of synergistic interactions.

The MM/PBSA analysis provides a key thermodynamic interpretation of the observed sensory interactions. The synergistic furaneol-eugenol pair combines strong van der Waals packing with favorable electrostatics and balanced solvation, indicating optimal shape complementarity and stable binding. In contrast, the masking furaneol-valeric acid pair exhibits negligible van der Waals contribution and a large solvation penalty that cancels its strong electrostatic attraction, reflecting poor hydrophobic accommodation despite favorable charge interactions. The additive pairs display intermediate van der Waals contributions consistent with moderate binding stability. The case of 2-phenylethanol further illustrates that strong binding energetics do not necessarily translate into perceptual effects, underscoring the importance of binding site context in determining functional outcomes.

Implications and future perspectives

Building on the correlations established between computational metrics and sensory outcomes, this work carries both practical implications and methodological considerations. From a practical standpoint, understanding aroma interactions is essential for food flavor optimization. Traditional sensory evaluation, while indispensable, is time-consuming and costly. Computational approaches that predict perceptual outcomes from molecular interactions offer a promising alternative for efficient, mechanism-guided flavor design. This study demonstrates this potential by establishing correlations between computationally derived metrics and sensory interaction patterns. These metrics include binding energy, hydrogen bond dynamics, and conformational stability. At a broader level, this work provides a methodological framework that could serve as a predictive tool for preliminary screening of aroma interactions prior to sensory evaluation.

At a more direct level, this study yields specific insights applicable to flavor formulation. First, the identification of eugenol as a furaneol synergist provides a mechanistic basis for enhancing sweet-caramel notes in baked goods and tobacco products while potentially reducing dosage of key aroma compounds. Second, the masking effect of valeric acid on furaneol detection highlights the need for careful compositional control in dairy-based or fermented products, where cheesy notes may inadvertently suppress desired sweetness.

Despite these contributions, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this work focused on binary mixtures in simplified solvent systems, whereas real food products contain dozens of volatile compounds alongside non-volatile matrices such as lipids, proteins, and polysaccharides. These components can alter odorant release and availability, potentially modifying interaction outcomes(Wang, Zhang, Qian, et al., 2025). Second; the binary mixtures in this study were evaluated at fixed concentration ratios corresponding to equal individual detection probabilities. While this approach is standard for classifying interaction types; it does not capture potential concentration-dependent effects where the nature or magnitude of interaction may vary with relative odorant concentrations(Guo et al., 2025). Future studies should systematically vary concentration ratios to establish a more comprehensive understanding of how mixture composition influences perceptual outcomes. Third, flavor perception extends beyond receptor binding to include signal transduction, neural encoding, and central cognitive integration. This computational approach captures only the initial binding events and cannot account for these downstream processes. Future studies that combine computational predictions with experimental validation, such as cellular signaling assays, could help establish a more complete understanding of how molecular interactions translate into perceptual outcomes.

Conclusions

Interactions among food flavor molecules are a common and crucial phenomenon that shapes the overall sensory profile of foods, yet their underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study adopted an integrated approach combining sensory evaluation with computational simulations to systematically investigate the interaction patterns and molecular basis of binary aroma mixtures.

These findings demonstrate that furaneol interacts differentially with five co-occurring odorants, exhibiting synergistic, additive, masking, or neutral effects as quantified by the dose response S-curve method. Molecular docking and MD simulations further revealed that these perceptual outcomes correlate strongly with specific binding modes, dynamic stability, and thermodynamic properties of the odorant-olfactory receptor complexes. Synergistic pairs such as furaneol-eugenol showed highly complementary binding, low conformational fluctuation, and favorable binding free energy, whereas antagonistic pairs such as furaneol-valeric acid displayed competitive binding, high structural instability, and weak thermodynamic affinity.

This work provides an important mechanistic framework, anchored in the OR5M3 receptor model, for understanding and predicting aroma interactions. The strong correlation observed between sensory data, dynamic stability, and binding energetics suggests that computational approaches can serve as valuable tools for screening and designing flavor combinations. However, it is important to recognize that real-world olfactory perception involves the integration of signals from multiple receptors, and the present findings are based on interactions within a single receptor model. Future studies should aim to validate and expand these predictions in more complex, multi-receptor contexts and consider the role of neural integration. Within this context, the results offer a theoretical basis for future studies on flavor interaction mechanisms and contribute to the rational development of optimized aroma formulations.
