Multimodal AI-based 28-day mortality prediction of pneumonia patients at ED discharge: a multicenter study
Authors: Sunjin Hwang, Sejin Heo, Sungjun Hong, Kyu-Hwan Jung, Won Chul Cha, Junsang Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study develops and evaluates an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven model to predict the 28-day mortality in patients with pneumonia by integrating AI-interpreted chest radiographs (CXR) and clinical data available at the time of emergency department (ED) disposition. This multicenter retrospective study included patients who visited the ED with pneumonia at a tertiary academic hospital in South Korea, as well as recorded in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV, v3.1) database during study periods. To compare AI-driven models with a traditional clinical scoring system, three survival prediction models were developed using a baseline CURB-65 score. Five variable sets were constructed by combining the CURB-65 score, AI-interpreted CXR findings, and additional clinical information. A total of 2,874 ED visits were analyzed. The random survival forest (RSF) model using the all-feature set (CURB-65, CXR interpretation, and clinical information) achieved a concordance index (C-index) of 0.872 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.861–0.886) in the test set, significantly outperforming the RSF model excluding the CXR interpretation information, which had a C-index of 0.865 (95% CI: 0.854–0.879). This study highlights the potential utility of a multimodal AI-driven prediction model to support prognosis estimation and clinical decision-making for patients with pneumonia in ED.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42378-2.Keywords: Emergency department, Machine learning, Prognosis prediction, Multimodal, Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS), PneumoniaSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Pneumonia is a common illness usually treated in the Emergency Department (ED) and is the leading cause of death from infectious diseases1. In South Korea, pneumonia accounted for 7.9% of all deaths in 2019, making it the third leading cause of death2. In the United States (US), pneumonia accounted for 2.1% of all deaths from 1999 to 2022 and ranked among the top 10 causes of death as of 2018, with high mortality and hospitalization rates worldwide3,4. Early identification of high-risk patients with pneumonia is important because timely and intensive management can improve outcomes5,6. In clinical practice, severity-of-illness scoring systems such as the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) and CURB-65 are widely utilized to predict the risk of 28- or 30-day mortality, thereby guiding initial triage and treatment decisions7–10.

Although chest radiographs (CXR) is the primary imaging modality for diagnosing pneumonia in the ED[6], incorporating radiographic findings into risk models has historically been challenging owing to inter-reader variability and difficulty in objectively extracting imaging biomarkers11. Consequently, the subjective nature of CXR interpretation limits its use in prognosis prediction. As deep learning has enabled the automated and quantitative analysis of CXRs12–14, recent research has shifted toward integrating quantitative radiographic metrics with clinical data to predict the prognosis of pneumonia15,16. One study demonstrated improved predictive performance by combining artificial intelligence (AI)-generated CXR consolidation scores (0–100%) with traditional clinical scores, such as CURB-65 or PSI17. Approaches such as manual scoring of the extent of pulmonary involvement and opacity in patients with Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) followed by deep learning prediction, have been proposed18.

Building on this research, this study aimed to develop and evaluate an AI-driven model that integrates CXR AI interpretation features with clinical data from an ED, actively utilizing AI-based CXR interpretation software in routine workflows. Specifically, we aimed to determine the optimal combination of imaging features and clinical variables to predict the 28-day mortality in patients with pneumonia, thereby enabling early identification and improved management of high-risk patients.

After the 2-step feature selection, 27 features were selected in the deep learning-based Cox proportional hazards model (DeepSurv) all-feature model, which used CURB-65, CXR, and clinical information. In the CXR information, the probability of pleural effusion and nodule in patients’ CXR were among the top 10 important variables. Regarding the feature ‘acuity,’ lower values correspond to higher severity in ED patients. The increase in SHAP value due to the decrease in feature value of ‘acuity’ is consistent with existing clinical knowledge in that the higher the patient’s severity, the higher the probability of poor prognosis. Risk stratification performance

The risk stratification performance of the RSF all-feature model and the CURB-65 score are described in Supplementary Table S5. Of the 62 patients who died within 28 days, 28 were classified as high risk based on a CURB-65 score ≥ 3, while the RSF identified 60 as high risk. Notably, all 28 patients classified as high risk by CURB-65 were correctly identified by the RSF model. In addition, among the 34 patients who died despite a CURB-65 score < 3, 32 were successfully classified into the high-risk group using the RSF model. At the given equivalent specificity level (RSF, 55.6%; CURB-65, 57%), the RSF model achieved approximately twice the sensitivity of the CURB-65 (RSF, 96.8%; CURB-65, 45.2%) and a higher positive predictive value (RSF, 20.8%; CURB-65, 11.3%). These results suggest that the RSF model enables the accurate identification of high-risk patients without increasing the number of false positives.

Post-hoc analysis

In the post-hoc analysis, the model trained on the pooled multi-institution dataset presented C-index value ranging from 0.839 to 0.875 and IBS values ranging from 0.034 to 0.049 across the test cohorts (Supplementary Table S6). However, these ranges were narrower than those observed for models trained on single-institution data, including the SMC-trained model (C-index: 0.752–0.831; IBS: 0.033–0.062) and the MIMIC-IV-trained model (C-index: 0.821–0.892; IBS:0.032–0.052). Moreover, the model trained on MIMIC-IV showed higher performance than the multicenter-trained model in the SMC cohort.

In the post-hoc sensitivity analysis using a leakage-free pipeline, the RSF model trained with the final selected features achieved a C-index of 0.871 (95% CI: 0.860–0.885), comparable to the primary results.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced an AI-driven model to predict the 28-day mortality at the time of ED disposition in patients with pneumonia using data routinely available in daily clinical practice. The model was trained and evaluated using a multinational dataset combining cohorts from South Korea and the United States. The RSF model, which incorporated both clinical data and AI-interpreted CXR features, achieved the highest performance on the test set, with a C-index of 0.872, outperforming the traditional CURB-65 score and the model using only clinical variables.

This model aims to enhance clinical trust. Accuracy alone is insufficient to ensure the successful adoption of AI-based clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) in real-world practice. Clinicians must also understand how predictions are generated and whether the system aligns with their clinical reasoning. This study did not seek to develop or evaluate a novel image interpretation algorithm; rather, it aimed to integrate outputs from a clinically deployed CXR interpretation AI system into a transparent prognostic prediction framework for use in the ED. In support of this hypothesis, our model incorporated CXR information as a key feature, reflecting its central role in assessing pneumonia severity in routine clinical practice. By integrating CXR interpretations, the model more closely mirrors real-world decision-making processes. An iterative review of the feature selection process was conducted to assess whether SHAP-derived feature importance and dependence patterns were consistent with established clinical knowledge, as evaluated by clinicians. This procedure ensured that the final algorithm was accurate, transparent, and clinically plausible, qualities that are essential for CDSS acceptance in real-world settings.

Furthermore, the AI model is designed for seamless integration into the clinical workflows. It uses input variables routinely collected in the ED, including patient demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, and CXR images. By excluding variables that require clinician judgement or manual documentation, such as confusion, the model relies solely on automatically extractable data, thereby imposing no additional burden on the care team. Consequently, the model operates without additional clinician input, minimizing workflow disruption. This design makes the model well-suited for real-time decision support, as it avoids additional workload and reduces ambiguity.

Previous studies have proposed machine learning models to address the limitations of traditional clinical scoring tools such as CURB-65 and PSI7,8. Early attempts to enhance CURB-65 using machine learning demonstrated improved performance. However, these models often lacked CXR information, which many clinicians consider essential for assessing pneumonia severity19. More recently, there has been growing interest in combining multimodal data with AI. Cascade approaches, in which the output of one AI system serves as the input for another, have gained substantial interest in radiology and pathology research15–18. Our study aligns with this emerging trend but distinguished by its rigorous and iterative feature selection process, which integrates model-derived feature importance with clinical expert judgement. By incorporating both clinical variables and CXR interpretation features at the point of ED disposition, the model provides a practical and timely approach for prognosis prediction in the ED setting. In situations with limited medical resources, our model supports the classification of pneumonia severity and facilitates more efficient resource distribution.

The proposed AI model enables the early identification of high-risk pneumonia patients and supports informed treatment decisions. According to the American Thoracic Society guidelines, patients with a CURB-65 score of 2 or higher are recommended for hospital admission and prompt antibiotic therapy6. However, in many tertiary hospitals in South Korea, where inpatient bed occupancy frequently exceeds 90%20, consistent implementation of these recommendations is challenging due to resource constraints. The proposed model enables more individualized risk assessment than CURB-65. While the conventional clinical score failed to detect over half of the patients who ultimately died, the RSF model classified a substantially larger proportion of these patients as high-risk while maintaining comparable specificity. By providing more accurate prognosis predictions at the time of ED disposition, particularly in resource-constrained settings, the model may support better clinical decision-making, facilitating timely admission and treatment for high-risk patients while reducing unnecessary hospitalization among those at lower risk.

This study has several limitations. First, a single commercially available AI-based interpretation algorithm, the Lunit INSIGHT CXR, was used, as it is clinically deployed at the study institution. Although this algorithm contributes to prognosis prediction in patients with pneumonia, the resulting model may have limited generalizability and may depend on the specific software employed. Further studies incorporating other interpretation algorithms or additional imaging perspectives are necessary to validate and strengthen the robustness of this model. Second, imputation and encoding were performed before data splitting, which may have introduced preprocessing-related information leakage and led to optimistic performance estimates. Although these procedures were label-agnostic, some degree of performance inflation cannot be excluded. To address this concern, a post-hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted using a leakage-free pipeline, yielding a comparable or slightly lower performance. Nevertheless, further studies using fully leakage-free pipelines and independent external cohorts are required. Third, the integration of the SMC and MIMIC-IV datasets may have influenced model performance due to differences in clinical settings and prognostic accuracy. The need to combine datasets, driven by the relatively low event rate in the single-center cohort, may have introduced spectrum bias and limited our ability to claim true external validation. Fourth, definite conclusions regarding model generalizability cannot be drawn. Although post-hoc analysis indicated that the multicenter-trained model demonstrated a narrower range of key performance indices than the single-institution model, the absence of an independent external dataset remains a limitation.

In conclusion, we developed and validated an AI model to predict 28-day mortality at the time of ED disposition in patients with pneumonia, using only data that can be automatically extracted and seamlessly integrated into the routine clinical workflow. The RSF model, which incorporated CXR interpretation data, demonstrated greater discriminative power than the conventional CURB-65 score without increasing the risk of missing high-risk patients. Our findings suggest that the proposed AI model may support earlier and improved risk stratification in ED disposition, particularly in resource-constrained tertiary academic hospitals, where the optimal resource allocation is crucial. Because the model relies solely on automatically extractable data, it can be seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows.

Methods

Study design and source of data

This retrospective study utilized two distinct databases. The first dataset was extracted from the Clinical Data Warehouse (CDW) DARWIN-C of the Samsung Medical Center (SMC), Seoul, South Korea, comprising ED visits between May 9, 2023, and December 27, 2023. The second dataset was extracted from the publicly available MIMIC-IV (v3.1)21,22, incorporating both the MIMIC-IV-ED (Emergency Department, v2.2)23 and MIMIC-CXR (chest radiograph, v2.0.0)24,25 databases from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), Boston, US. ED visits between 2011 and 2016 were included to enable linkage with the MIMIC-CXR database.

Participants

Patients diagnosed with pneumonia (defined as International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision [ICD-9] of 480–486, ICD-10 of J12-J18, clinical diagnosis of pneumonia, or recorded in the pneumonia registry of the SMC) after visiting the ED during the specified period were included.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged ≤ 18 years, those who visited the ED but did not receive medical care, those who died prior to ED disposition, those without a chest radiograph record, and those with missing data for essential variables required to calculate the CURB-65 score.

Predictors

We used predictors from three categories: conventional CURB-65 scores, AI-interpreted CXR information, and clinical information. We constructed five variable sets by combining the three categories (CURB-65-only, CXR-only, clinical-only, CURB-65-clinical, all-feature). The CURB-65-only and CURB-65-clinical variable sets incorporated the five individual binary components of the CURB-65. The entire variables used are shown in Supplementary Table S7.

For clinical information, we extracted general information (age, gender, and race), comorbidities, latest vital signs before ED disposition, and latest blood test results before ED disposition. As a vital sign-derived variable, the oxygen supply level was categorized into five levels based on the type of oxygen delivery device used. Both the highest-level during ED stay and the last level before ED disposition were recorded. To evaluate the patients’ respiratory status, the ratio of SpO2 (saturation of peripheral oxygen) to FiO2 (fraction of inspired oxygen) was calculated using ED measurements. The SpO2/FiO2 (S/F) ratio was used as the vital sign26. Additionally, the maximum, minimum, and linear regression estimates of the vital sign variables were calculated for both the entire ED stay and the 4 h preceding disposition.

During the study period, CXR performed in the SMC ED were interpreted using the AI software, Lunit INSIGHT CXR (version 3.1.5.1-S01). This AI software interprets the CXR images and estimates the probability of the presence of nine abnormal findings on a scale of 0–100%12. The nine abnormalities included pneumothorax, pneumoperitoneum, mediastinal widening, nodule, consolidation, pleural effusion, atelectasis, fibrosis, and cardiomegaly. For each patient, the latest CXR interpretation results before ED disposition were used as the CXR information. The CXR images extracted from the MIMIC-CXR database were interpreted using the same AI software, and the results were used in this study.

The CURB-65 score was calculated using the latest values before ED disposition7. In this calculation, “confusion” was defined as documentation in the pneumonia registry or any expression of confusion in nursing records, diagnoses, or chief complaints (e.g., disorientation, altered mental status, or confusion).

Data preprocessing

For variables matched with the MIMIC-IV dataset, those with a missing rate of 20% or higher were excluded, except for laboratory findings deemed clinically important. The variables included are erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), absolute neutrophil count (ANC), prothrombin time (international normalized ratio, INR), C-reactive protein (CRP), lactic acid, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and albumin.

The SMC and MIMIC-IV datasets were then merged into a combined dataset. Institutional information was added, and the race of the patients in the SMC dataset was assumed to be Asian, as race information was not recorded because of South Korea’s healthcare environment. The acuity levels recorded in the MIMIC-IV dataset were combined with the Korean Triage and Acuity Scale (KTAS) documented in the SMC dataset.

The last observation carried forward method was applied to create the derived variables for the vital signs. To handle missing values, Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was applied and constrained to the 1st and 99th percentiles of each observed distribution27. Categorical variables (oxygen supply level and acuity level) and integer variables (SpO2, blood pressure, FiO2, and respiratory rate) were rounded off after imputation.

The combined dataset was preprocessed for model training using appropriate encoding methods. Continuous numerical variables were standardized using the standard scaler, whereas ordinal variables such as acuity level, race, oxygen supply level, and CURB-65 score were encoded using the ordinal scaler. Binary variables and regression estimation values of the vital signs were used without transformation. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets (7:1:2) using stratified sampling to ensure an equal distribution of institution and outcome events.

Outcome

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality occurring within 28 days of ED disposition, which was analyzed as a time-to-event outcome. The date of death was initially ascertained from the hospital records of both cohorts. To improve the completeness of outcome capture, hospital records were supplemented with administrative mortality data specific to each country. Nationally collected registry data from the Ministry of the Interior and Safety were used for the SMC dataset, and state-level mortality records incorporated into the MIMIC-IV were used for the MIMIC-IV dataset21. When the date of death was recorded in the hospital records but not identified in the corresponding administrative registry, this discrepancy was considered to reflect administrative delay rather than loss to follow-up. For survival analysis, the index time was defined as the date of ED disposition. Event time was calculated as the number of days from ED disposition to death within 28 days, regardless of the disposition outcome. Patients transferred to another hospital were also considered to have experienced an event if death occurred within 28 days of transfer. Because mortality outcomes were supplemented with national registry data, incomplete death linkage was considered unlikely.

Model development

Three survival prediction models were used: Cox proportional hazards model with an elastic net penalty (Coxnet)28, random survival forest (RSF)29, and deep learning-based Cox proportional hazards model (DeepSurv)30. The Cox proportional hazards model trained using the CURB-65 score was used as the baseline model. The entire model development and evaluation process is shown in Fig. 5.

Feature selection for selective variable sets with clinical information (clinical-only, CURB-65-clinical, all-feature) was conducted in two steps. The feature selection process is shown in Fig. 6.

Feature selection process. The two step feature selection process of machine and clinician were assessed in three variable sets, including clinical information. CXR = chest radiographs; CURB-65 = confusion, urea, respiratory rate, blood pressure, age ≥ 65.

The first step is in the model training. During the initial training process, feature importance was assessed using different methods for each model: SHAP values for DeepSurv and permutation importance for Coxnet and RSF. We set the mandatory features as CURB-65 components and CXR abnormality scores, and additional variables were included sequentially in order of feature importance. The optimal set of variables was determined based on the highest average C-index observed in a 5-fold cross-validation of the training set. Additionally, the “confusion” component of CURB-65, which requires clinician judgement, was excluded from the feature-selected variable sets because it demonstrated low feature importance and was inconsistent with automatically extractable variables. Accordingly, the CURB-65 score, including the confusion component, was also excluded from those selected variable sets.

The second step was conducted using the best model identified during the validation. To evaluate how the model learned from the features, we examined the relationship between the actual feature values and SHAP values through dependence plots as well as the effect size, defined as the mean absolute SHAP value. Features were selected based on the clinical plausibility of their dependence plot as assessed by two clinicians, with an effect size threshold of 0.01. Clinicians independently reviewed the outputs and excluded variables that appeared clinically implausible (e.g., an increase in white blood cell count was associated with improved patient survival). The selected features were then applied to the other models. The validation and feature selection processes were repeated iteratively until no additional features were excluded.

After feature selection step 1, parameter optimization for all models was conducted using a grid search with 5-fold cross-validation in the training set. The parameter options used in the grid search are listed in Supplementary Table S8. Finally, the performances of the models were evaluated using a test set.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analysis was conducted to compare the characteristics of patients from the two institutions. After the normality test using the Shapiro–Wilk test, the median value and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables that did not satisfy normality and the percentages for categorical variables were calculated. Subsequently, the two datasets were compared using the Chi-square test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical significance was set at p value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed after excluding missing values. Additionally, characteristics across the training, validation, and test sets were compared using the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality, followed by the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (applied when the expected frequency was < 5) for categorical variables. The results are presented in Supplementary Table S9.

The C-index and IBS were used to evaluate the model performance on right-censored data. The 95% CI were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap iterations with a 90% resampling rate without replacement. Statistical comparisons across models and variable sets were performed using nonparametric tests. To analyze the results across different models and variable sets, the Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to assess normality, followed by the Kruskal–Wallis test for group comparisons. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction.

The cumulative/dynamic time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and time-dependent AUC were calculated to evaluate the prognostic performance of the models over time31. The mean and integrated AUC for 28 days from the time of ED disposition were calculated.

Data preprocessing and integration were conducted using R (version 4.3.1), whereas model development and evaluation were performed using Python (version 3.9.13, 3.12.4). Scikit-survival (version 0.22.2) and Pycox (version 0.3.0) survival prediction models were used, and scikit-learn (version 1.3.2), lifelines (version 0.28.0), and SciPy (version 1.13.1) packages were used for model evaluation. This study adhered to the TRIPOD reporting guidelines (Supplementary Table S10)32.

Post-hoc analysis

A post-hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the generalizability of the model. The best-performing model from the primary analysis—the RSF all-feature model using the final selected features trained on the original multi-institution data—was compared with the Coxnet, RSF, and DeepSurv models trained on single-institution data (SMC and MIMIC-IV) using the final selected features.

For single-institution analyses, the original training and validation sets were combined and stratified by the data source to construct institution-specific training sets that comprised 80% of the cohort. Institution-specific test sets were derived from the original test set by stratification based on data source. The model performance was evaluated using the original multi-institution and institution-specific test sets. The C-index and IBS, along with the 95% CI, were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap iterations with a 90% resampling rate without replacement. No additional feature selection was performed, and parameter optimization was conducted using the same strategy as in the primary analysis.

Separately, an additional post-hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted using a leakage-free preprocessing pipeline while preserving the same training, validation, and test splits as in the primary experiment. Imputation and encoding were fitted exclusively on the training set and subsequently applied to the validation and test sets. Models were trained using the final selected features with parameter optimization and evaluated using the same procedure as in the primary analysis.
