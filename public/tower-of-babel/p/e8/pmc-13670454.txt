Resistance Exercise and Peer Support with Bands/Balls and Digitally-based Supervision: (REPS w/BANDS): a feasibility study for women with PCOS
Authors: Pamela J. Wright, Cynthia F. Corbett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExercise is a first-line treatment for women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). However, research findings reveal that women with PCOS report low exercise motivation due to PCOS stigma-related stress and lack of social connection, inhibiting exercise in public and group settings. The purpose of this study was to assess the feasibility and acceptability of an in-home resistance exercise program supervised in real time by personal trainers using smart home video devices (SHVDs) with peer support through a buddy system.MethodsThis one-arm feasibility study incorporated strategies from self-determination theory to meet participants’ psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Participants (N = 10) received an SHVD and exercise equipment (bands, stability ball). Personal trainers supervised 2 individual RE sessions per week for 12 weeks per participant via the SHVD’s video feature. Participants were randomly paired with a buddy and encouraged to connect weekly for peer support. Feasibility, acceptability, depressive symptoms, anthropometrics, and muscular endurance were measured using surveys, fitness testing, and interviews. Quantitative data were analyzed using t-tests. Qualitative data were analyzed via thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants were 26.6 (8.8) years, mostly White (70%), educated (90% some college or more), and diagnosed with ≥ 1 comorbidity. Data yielded 100% recruitment, 74% attendance, 80% retention, and a 4.8 rating (excellent on 5-point scale) for acceptability. Further findings revealed pre-post signals of improvements in depressive symptoms, muscular endurance, and waist circumference. Only one buddy pair (20%) made consistent contact. Participants preferred a buddy with similar life experiences in terms of life phase (e.g., student, mother) along with the shared PCOS identity.ConclusionsREPS w/BANDS is feasible and has potential to improve mental and physical health outcomes. Participants reported increased exercise motivation due to rapport with the personal trainer and the personal trainer’s PCOS knowledge, use of SDT strategies, and interest in the participants’ fitness progress. REPS w/BANDS was revised based on findings and will be piloted as a larger randomized controlled trial.Trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06629883) on September 20, 2024.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) affects approximately 15–20% of US adult women across all races and ethnicities [1], and cost > 8 billion dollars for diagnosis during 2020 in the United States [2]. As a common chronic endocrinopathy among women, PCOS is a complex, heterogeneous collection of symptoms involving hormonal and metabolic dysregulation [3]. PCOS clinical features include obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia, all increasing risk of cardiometabolic diseases [4, 5] and reproductive cancers [6] by up to 50%. These features plus symptoms such as hirsutism (excessive male-pattern hair growth) and acanthosis nigricans (dark, velvety patches of skin) negatively impact health-related quality-of-life [7], especially in the psychological domain [8]. Women with PCOS are 3–8 times more likely than women without PCOS to have depressive symptoms [9] and 7 times more likely to attempt suicide [10]. Commonly prescribed medications are only moderately effective at controlling symptoms and preventing complications [1] and women with PCOS often report dissatisfaction with the typical pharmaceutical treatment model [11]. The 2023 Endocrine Society Practice Guidelines recommend exercise as first-line treatment [12] given its known metabolic benefits and impact on reducing depressive symptoms [13]. However, evidence suggests that providers do not routinely recommend exercise [14], and < 40% of women with PCOS engage in regular physical activity, and about 60% are sedentary [15, 16].

The 2023 Endocrine Society Practice Guidelines and the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans emphasize 150–300 min of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise [12]. However, moderate-to-vigorous intensity is known to significantly increase cortisol levels, which is disadvantageous among women with PCOS given their already elevated cortisol levels [17]. Emerging evidence indicates that intensity or effort may be more impactful on health outcomes compared to exercise volume (as defined by length of exercise bout and number of days per week) [18]. Resistance exercise (RE) involves muscular action against resistance, and contrary to aerobic exercise, RE can be performed at a lower volume (2–3 sets of ~ 8–10 repetitions over 20–30 s/set for 3–4 exercises on 2–3 days/week) with low-to-moderate effort or intensity (~ 40–60%). RE has also been shown to improve PCOS hormonal markers such as free testosterone and increase metabolic capacity via improved lean muscle and less insulin resistance more in women with PCOS than in women without PCOS [16, 19, 20].

Women with PCOS have reported low exercise motivation for any type of exercise due to facility issues (e.g., location and cost), PCOS stigma-related stress due to features such as hirsutism and obesity, and lack of social connection with others who understand PCOS, all of which inhibit exercise in a group and public places [11]. To address these exercise barriers, a personalized RE intervention with a buddy system underpinned by self-determination theory (SDT) called Resistance Exercise and Peer Support with Bands/Ball and Digitally Based Supervision (REPS w/BANDS) was designed and piloted to promote mental and physical health outcomes. Theory

Self-determination theory (SDT) was chosen to guide REPS w/BANDS given its underlying assumption that humans are naturally inspired and self-governing but require strategies to develop and maintain their potential. Self-determination theory purports that people can exert control over their behaviors based on the type and levels of their self-motivation. Intrinsic motivation, that of being fully self-determined, occurs when three innate psychological needs are fully met: autonomy (freedom to choose), competence (confidence to perform a behavior), and relatedness (sense of belonging and understanding from others). Motivation from external sources (extrinsic) occurs when the same psychological needs are partially unmet due to suppression by sociocultural contexts, creating a need for motivational strategies. Thus, when motivational strategies are used to meet psychological needs, a shift should occur toward greater intrinsic motivation. Motivation level impacts the degree of affective, cognitive, and behavioral engagement [21]. As such, REPS w/BANDS was designed to include exercise choice and use of neutral language (e.g., may, could) to foster autonomy, practice and positive feedback to increase competence, and peer support to promote relatedness [22].

Methods

Sample size

Consistent with the NIH (National Institutes of Health) goals for feasibility studies, we aimed to effectively inform the design and implementation of the main study, and we did not test hypotheses about the effects of the intervention but assessed the feasibility and acceptability of the approach [23]. Based on the principal investigator’s expertise in RE, a sample of 10 was deemed sufficient by its practicality, ability to provide preliminary estimates of variability, effectiveness in detecting major issues, alignment with methodological guidelines, and ethical considerations.

Design, setting, and participants

A one-arm feasibility study was piloted among adult women with PCOS in their homes using a smart home video device (SHVD; i.e., Amazon Show). Inclusion criteria were an age of 18 or older, a confirmed PCOS diagnosis by a healthcare provider, physical activity readiness as determined by the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q) [24], and residence within driving distance of the main university campus. Exclusion criteria were cardiorespiratory disease (e.g., congestive heart failure), hypertension, orthopedic injury (e.g., herniated disc), and pregnancy. Starting in July 2022, participants (n = 10) were recruited via flyers posted on a college campus and the snowball method.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki and 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2) and 45 CFR 46.111(a)(7). After an expedited review, the authors’ affiliated university Institutional Review Board approved the study (Pro00120540; 5/13/2022).

Potential participants scheduled an in-person appointment to discuss the study and its requirements and expectations. The PAR-Q was administered. For participants cleared by the PAR-Q, the principal investigator explained the study purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits and, afterward, obtained voluntary written informed consent. An appointment to obtain baseline measures was scheduled after a PCOS diagnosis was confirmed by a healthcare provider.

Procedures

After receiving confirmation of a PCOS diagnosis, participants were scheduled for an in-person fitness assessment and asked to complete an online survey. Once participants completed all baseline measures, they were matched with a personal trainer based on the participants’ preferred days and times for exercise training. After enrolling all ten participants, they were randomly paired to form a buddy system for social support. Meetings were scheduled with each participant to review expectations, the program booklet, and provide the SHVD, user guide for the SHVD, and exercise equipment (i.e., resistance bands and stability ball). Virtual meetings were available for any participant who required assistance setting up the SHVD.

Program components

The intervention was delivered by certified personal trainers who connected with participants in their homes using SHVDs for 12 weeks. Supervised training was individually delivered to participants twice weekly in real time and incorporated low-cost, portable resistance equipment (i.e., resistance bands, stability ball). Participants were encouraged to exercise a third time per week on their own. SDT-guided strategies (e.g., peer support, practice, positive feedback, affirmations) were incorporated to help increase exercise motivation. Peer support was addressed by randomly assigning participants with a buddy. Personal trainers demonstrated proper exercise form and encouraged participants to practice each exercise. Participants were instructed on how to use a free affirmation app on the SHVD and encouraged to use it at least weekly. Primary feasibility outcomes

Feasibility

Feasibility measures included rates of recruitment, attendance, and retention, which were calculated at the end of the study period in May 2023 and compared to the following standards: recruitment ≥ 75%, attendance ≥ 75%, and retention ≥ 80% [25]. The recruitment rate was calculated by dividing the total number of participants who entered the study by the number of participants who answered the call for participants and multiplying by 100. The attendance rate was determined by dividing the total number of sessions attended by the total number of planned sessions for all participants and multiplying by 100. The retention rate was calculated by dividing the number of participants completing all phases of the study by the number of participants entering the study and multiplying by 100.

Acceptability

Acceptability measures included participant interviews and Likert scales as well as personal trainer interviews. Following the post-study fitness assessment, each participant was asked seven open-ended questions and administered seven Likert scales to obtain their feedback about the REPS w/BANDS intervention. At the end of the study, the personal trainers were asked five open-ended questions for feedback about program logistics and recommendations for the next phase.

Fidelity

Fidelity to the intervention was measured using an a priori checklist, with results calculated by dividing the number of missed steps by the number of defined actions and multiplying by 100.

Other outcome measures were collected at baseline and at the end the 12-week intervention of 12 weeks. Given staggered enrollments, follow-up data was collected until February 2024.

Secondary patient-centered outcomes

Demographics

The demographic questionnaire included age, race, educational attainment, marital status, number of children, employment status, insurance, geographic location, and comorbid conditions.

Physical activity level

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) is an instrument used to assess self-reported frequency and intensity of physical activity levels across the activity domains of leisure, domestic work, gardening (yard), work-related, and transportation. The total amount of physical activity places participants in one of three categories: 1 = low/inactive, 2 = moderate, and 3 = high. Psychometric testing has revealed acceptable (rho > 0.30) and significant (p < 0.05) reliability [26].

Depressive symptoms

The Personal Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8) was used to assess prevalence and severity of depressive symptoms occurring within the past 2 weeks. The scale consists of 8 items with a 4-point rating ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). A score of ≥ 10 indicates major depression and ≥ 20 indicates severe major depression. Internal reliability was reported as 0.81 [27].

Anthropometric measures

Body weight and height were measured using a calibrated stadiometer during the fitness assessments. The fitness assessor measured waist and hip circumferences according to protocol established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention for the National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES) study [28].

Squat test

The purpose of the squat test was to estimate lower body muscular endurance. This test was chosen as a safer alternative than the one-rep-max test for sedentary or physically inactive participants and due to the availability of normative data. Using the protocol established by the American Council on Exercise, the fitness assessor virtually explained, demonstrated, and conducted the test with the participants. The squat test involves repetitively lowering the body to ± a sitting position and immediately lifting the body to a point of volitional fatigue. The score is the number of squats performed. Test-retest reliability of the squat test has ranged from 0.77 to 0.90 [29].

Modified push-up test

The purpose of the modified push-up test was to estimate upper body muscular endurance. This test was chosen as a safer alternative than one-rep-max tests for sedentary or physically inactive participants and due to the availability of normative data. Using the protocol of the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), the fitness assessor virtually explained, demonstrated, and conducted the test with the participants. The test involves performing push-ups in a modified position (knees bent with lower legs in contact with the mat). The score is the total number of modified push-ups performed with proper form in one minute. The norm-referenced test-retest reliability estimate using intraclass correlations from one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was R = 0.99 [30].

Curl-up test

The purpose of the curl-up test was to estimate abdominal muscular endurance. This test was chosen as a safer alternative than full sit-ups for sedentary or physically inactive participants and due to the availability of normative data. Using ACSM protocol, the fitness assessor virtually explained, demonstrated, and conducted the test with the participants. For this test, participants were in a supine position and repetitively lifted and lowered the upper body to a metronome set at 40 beats/minute. The score is the number of curl-ups performed with proper form to cadence. Available psychometric data include test-retest reliability (r = 0.98), inter-apparatus reliability (r = 0.71), and inter-tester reliability (r = 0.76) [30].

Data collection and analysis

All participants were assigned an identification code. Survey data was collected via email. Surveys were de-identified and labeled with the appropriate identification code. The survey data was then transferred into IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armon, NT, USA) by the PI. Data from fitness assessments were recorded on a program form by the fitness assessor and then transferred into SPSS.

Demographic data was analyzed by calculating descriptive statistics (i.e., frequencies and/or means ± standard deviations). All other data were checked for the normality assumption. All variables were found to be normally distributed as evident by a skewness value between − 1 and + 1, which is considered excellent, and kurtosis values less than 1.0. Data were then analyzed using paired t-tests.

Qualitative data from interviews with the personal trainers and participants were analyzed using the steps of reflexive thematic analysis [31]. The two researchers (PJW, CFC) independently coded the responses and then collaboratively discussed codes and emerging themes. Subsequently, an iterative categorization process was followed to elicit connections between the codes.

Discussion

The purpose of this one-arm pilot was to assess the feasibility and acceptability of REPS w/BANDS and evaluate for signals of improvement of depressive symptoms, anthropometrics, and muscular endurance among women with PCOS. After a one-arm pilot, REPS w/BANDS was found to be feasible and acceptable to women with PCOS, and it yielded signals of improvement in depressive symptoms, waist circumference, and muscular fitness.

Feasibility and acceptability

The feasibility of delivering a RE program via a SHVD was strong as evidenced by rates that exceeded or met standards of recruitment (≥ 75%), attendance (≥ 75%), and retention (≥ 80%) [25]. The first ten potential participants to inquire about the study met screening criteria and provided confirmation of PCOS diagnosis by a healthcare provider. Based on two personal training sessions per week for 12 weeks, ten participants were scheduled to complete 240 of the personal training sessions. One participant discontinued the program in week eight without notice and another during the post-assessment period due to illness. Occasionally, participants canceled or rescheduled personal training sessions for reasons of illness, travel, or family situations. A systematic review and meta-analysis about digitally based interventions found an average attrition rate of 40% [32]. Our attrition rate was significantly lower, which may be due to the theoretical underpinnings used to decrease barriers to exercise participation and increase intrinsic exercise motivation. These underpinnings are in line with the key factors identified as relevant toward increasing exercise adherence such as supervision, integration into daily living, social support, communication, feedback, monitoring [33]. However, this pilot did not evaluate pre-post differences in exercise motivation based on meeting the SDT psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Phase II of REPS w/BANDS will include these measures.

Overall, the acceptability of REPS w/BANDS was rated high, indicating great to excellent. Most all participants indicated that they were likely to continue RE training on their own and recommend the program to other women with PCOS. Of all the program components, the SHVD received the highest rating. The SHVD was easy to set up and use to receive exercise training in the home and provided other helpful features for free such as affirmations. SHVDs are widely accepted and used internationally with predicted continued growth [34]. The use of a SHVD can allow for a broader reach of participants by extending the program to all US states thus impacting health outcomes among many women with PCOS.

The buddy system received the lowest acceptability rating, indicating average. While each participant acknowledged the importance of a shared PCOS identity with their assigned buddy, they expressed preference for a buddy in a similar life phase (e.g., student, mother, employee). As such, a compatibility survey will be developed for use in Phase II, allowing for a more intentional matching process as opposed to random assignment. While the idea of individual-level support was valued among the participants, they also recommended a group support system. Group-level support would allow diverse perspectives on motivational strategies for exercise and a wider scope of ideas for managing PCOS symptomology without the commitment and rapport of individual-level support. Thus, the next phase of REPS w/BANDS will also include a private online group chat.

Outcomes

While non-randomized feasibility studies are not designed to test effectiveness, data analysis was still performed to assess for signals of improvement among secondary outcome measures. The baseline mean score for depressive symptoms indicated a mild level of depressive symptoms among this sample of women with PCOS. Post-intervention, the mean score for depressive symptoms improved, indicating no significant depressive symptoms. As the high prevalence of and indicators for depressive symptoms among women with PCOS are well documented [35], a non-pharmaceutical adjunct to help manage depressive symptoms is encouraging.

Total body weight, and therefore body mass index, did not show signals of improvement. Overall, body weight increased slightly. Given that the waist circumference was lower for most participants, the increase in body weight could reflect a gain in lean mass such as muscle and/or water. Body weight, especially when used to calculate BMI, is useful as a screening for obesity; however, it is not a direct measure of the amount or location of adiposity [36]. Waist circumference is often considered a more critical measure of health than total body weight because it provides insight into the distribution of body fat, particularly visceral fat [37]. Women with PCOS have a higher prevalence of visceral fat than age-matched and BMI-matched controls without PCOS [38, 39]. This increased visceral fat is associated with the metabolic imbalances characteristic of PCOS [38]. Therefore, monitoring waist circumference can offer a more accurate assessment of health risks and help guide more effective interventions for reducing cardiometabolic risks.

The waist-to-hip ratio data was not informative as a measure of progress, especially since most participants lost size proportionally in both the waist and hip areas. When both measurements decrease at the same rate, the ratio remains unchanged. Consequently, the waist-to-hip ratio may also not accurately reflect changes in body composition or fat distribution.

Each participant who completed the follow-up fitness assessment increased their scores on all muscular endurance tests. Muscular fitness is essential for overall health and well-being. Improved muscular fitness contributes to overall metabolic capacity, such that the resting metabolic rate will increase, and greater calories can be expended with physical activity. As such, weight management is enhanced. Muscular fitness also improves insulin sensitivity. Muscle tissue is a major site for glucose uptake in the body. With exercise, muscle contraction increases the translocation of glucose transporters (e.g., GLUT-4) to the cell membrane, allowing more glucose to enter the muscle cells, and improves the efficiency of insulin signaling pathways [40]. Muscular fitness improves physical performance, making activities of daily living easier and less tiring, while also supporting joint stability and improving posture. Lastly, muscular fitness has been found to be both directly and indirectly associated with improved self-esteem and body image, especially among individuals reporting with baseline low levels [41].

Limitations

As a phase I feasibility study, the sample size was small and there was no control group. A small sample size reduces the statistical power of the study, making it difficult to detect meaningful differences or generalize findings to a larger population. The absence of a control group can lead to biased results and make it challenging to attribute outcomes directly to the intervention or variable being studied. This phase I study of REPS w/BANDS did not include measures to assess the pre-post levels of the SDT psychological needs and autonomous motivation.

Conclusion

A phase I RE program with peer support delivered via a SHVD (i.e., REPS w/BANDS) was found feasible and acceptable to women with PCOS. Signals of improvements were also found with depressive symptoms, waist circumference, and muscular fitness. Program refinements will be made, such as developing a compatibility survey for the buddy system, adding a private online group chat for group-level support, and including additional equipment (e.g., loop bands, SHVD stands). The next step will be to pilot a randomized controlled trial of REPS w/BANDS and to include a larger sample size. Positive impact is expected as knowledge will be advanced about exercise motivation among women with PCOS and findings will help promote symptom management, risk reduction of comorbid conditions, and promotion of overall well-being among women with PCOS.
