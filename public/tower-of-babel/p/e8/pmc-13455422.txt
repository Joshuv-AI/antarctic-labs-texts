Breast cancer survival prediction using machine learning and multimodal data for personalized care plan
Authors: Nasibeh Rady Raz, Nahid Nafissi, Ebrahim Babaee, Pedram Fadavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a time-stratified breast cancer survival analysis that incorporates tumor characteristics, disease stage, and patient features, using machine learning (ML) algorithms to support personalized care planning. Considering nonlinear relationships between input data and the risk of death, as well as the dynamics of patient characteristics over time, we utilize 6 ML algorithms, including XGBoost, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Decision Tree, AdaBoost, and Multilayer Perceptron, for less than 6 months, 6 months, 1-, 2-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival rates prediction. We utilize multimodal data from 3,476 breast cancer patients, comprising 43 features. XGBoost outperforms the rest, achieving an accuracy of over 90%. The top five predictors, using Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), include DCIS, Ki-67, age, feeding, and lymph node grade. SHAP analysis indicated that lower values of DCIS and Ki-67, lower lymph node and tumor grade, less sentinel lymph node involvement, higher age, longer duration of breastfeeding, lower tumor size, medullary tumor type, PR+, ER+, less positive node, more pregnancy, DCIS pathology class, BCS surgery type for early-stage, lower tumor stage, no necrosis, HER2-, no family history, and no calcification are result in longer breast cancer survival. Furthermore, we validate the model’s performance using 5-fold cross-validation (CV) and nested CV. We also evaluate model performance using accuracy, precision, F1 score, and recall. All results show the proposed method outperforms. This paper presents clinical, molecular, and demographic insights into survival analysis, utilizing explainable ML techniques to support personalized treatment decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16283-w.

Introduction

Breast cancer, as the most common malignancy in women worldwide, is curable in early stages and non-metastatic cases [1]. In the case of distant metastases, it is considered incurable with available therapies. Additionally, breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease at the molecular level with various molecular features, such as HER2, estrogen, and progesterone receptors, in which treatment varies based on the molecular subtype.

Breast cancer treatment should be performed both locally and systemically [2], including surgery, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, anti-HER2 therapy, bone-stabilizing agents, targeted therapy, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors for BRCA mutation carriers, immunotherapy, and personalized treatment with adaptive therapy intensity based on tumor biology and initial treatment response.

Toward personalized treatment, one approach is to investigate the survival rate of cancer patients, which is a challenge due to the high complexity of breast cancer. Survival analysis studies the time until an event, such as death, occurs after diagnosis or treatment. It is worth noting that survival studies follow each patient until the event is documented. However, in reality, patients’ follow-up is hard, and they may die from other causes.

Classical approaches to modeling cancer survival include statistical methods, such as the semiparametric Cox proportional hazards (CPH) model [3]. Statistical methods have two significant limitations: they cannot capture nonlinear relationships between input data and the risk of death, and they do not account for the dynamics of patient characteristics over time.

To overcome the linearity limitation of CPH, cancer survival models based on Machine learning (ML) are recommended. These algorithms model highly complex nonlinear relationships. Accordingly, several recent studies have employed ML techniques to enhance the capacity of ML-based CPH. Notable examples include methods that leverage clinical and gene expression data, such as DeepSurv and Cox-nnet [4]. Other methods focus on imaging data, such as a cancer survival prediction model using histopathology slides [5].

Multimodal data, including clinical, imaging, and molecular data, is another aspect of using ML in survival analysis, as seen in deep learning-based Cox survival methods [6]. Moreover, other data modalities, including clinical information, digital pathology images, and genomic data, are used to predict across 20 cancers [6]. To eliminate the limitations of linearity and hazard proportionality, Dynamic-DeepHit is used to parameterize the mass function of the survival probability in [7]. However, these methods do not yet exploit multimodal data types and simultaneously capture nonlinear relationships. Therefore, a method is needed that accounts for nonlinear relationships, eliminates the proportionality constraint, and uses multimodal data to determine the survival rate of breast cancer patients. Previously, we used ML models to predict blood transfusion requirements for cancer patients [8].

Here, we predict breast cancer survival using a classification model that integrates six machine learning algorithms, including Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), AdaBoost, Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). We classify the survival time into 7 intervals: less than 6 months, 6 months, 1, 2, 3, 5, and 10 years. We utilize a dataset of 3,476 breast cancer patients with 44 features. We also perform feature importance analysis to determine the effect of predictors on survival outcomes. Furthermore, we validate the model’s performance using cross-validation and nested cross-validation. We also evaluate model performance using metrics such as accuracy, precision, F1 score, and recall. The proposed multi-horizon survival classification strategy, rather than a traditional time-to-event model, improves survival prediction accuracy and interpretation. The paper has five sections. In Sect. 2, we review relevant research works. Section 3 describes the proposed method. Experimental results and analysis are presented in Sect. 4. Finally, conclusions are explained in Sect. 5.

Literature review

Survival analysis is used to set a care plan, including three-year and five-year survival rates. For example, in [2], a Deep Neural Network (DNN) and 11 machine learning (ML) methods are used to predict 5-year survival, using a total of 2644 samples and 34 features, with external validation. Also, in [5], an early prediction of local and distant recurrence is presented, using Recurrence-Free survival analysis and MLs, based on four merged datasets and 23 features. They predict recurrence risk and regional/distant recurrences and identify potential metastatic sites.

Moreover, in [9], it is mentioned that breast cancer treatment is based on tumor characteristics, disease stage, and patient preference. Therefore, considering two surgical options — mastectomy and Breast Conserving Surgery (BCS) — they analyze survival outcomes, clinical and molecular factors, and explainable Machine Learning (ML) techniques. They express that BCS is suitable for younger patients, while older ones have higher risks in Mastectomy.

Mortality risk prediction using ML has also been reported in prior work for other diseases. For example, in [10], it is performed in patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia. In [11], ten ML algorithms are employed to predict survival to dementia, examining the effects of aging on cognition.

Furthermore, survival prediction in patients with bone metastases using COX regression analysis and XGBoost model is presented in [12]. Additionally, the survival rates of patients receiving chemotherapy in combination with surgery are compared to those receiving chemotherapy alone. The results indicate that the survival rate of the first-ranked group is higher. Also, due to a lack of screening and limited treatment, there is a survival gap in low- and middle-income countries [13]. In addition, the relationship of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) with breast cancer survival is presented in [14] among patients with type 2 diabetes. Cox models and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) are used for this study. The results show that MHT leads to a reduction in breast cancer. The survival rate of metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) is worse than that of metastatic non-TNBC. Research on this issue is presented in [15].

Method

In this section, we first discuss the dataset, data preprocessing methods, feature selection, and model selection for predicting breast cancer survival.

Dataset

We use a dataset of 3476 samples of patients with breast cancer. We exclude patients with incomplete records, those who are simultaneously suffering from another malignancy other than breast cancer, and those who die without having metastasis from the study to avoid bias in the data. Also, we exclude age < 20, in which breast cancer is extremely rare. This dataset includes 43 features of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and pathological data from Hazrat Rasool Hospital. Inclusion criteria are those patients who die with metathesis. The research did not affect clinical care; therefore, the review boards of the Iran University of Medical Sciences (IUMS) waived the requirement for individual patient consent. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines. The IUMS Review Boards approved all experimental protocols. The data has been de-identified and is not subject to the HIPAA Privacy Rule. We present patient characteristics and statistics in Tables 1, 2, 3 and S1-S2. Data preprocessing

The dataset consists of demographic data (age, surgery to metastasis time, metastasis to death time, surgery to death time, and family history, pregnancy, first pregnancy, feeding, OCP taking, abortion, death, death duo to metastasis, iort, neoadjuvant, radiotherapy type, and iort_bi), laboratory data (ki-67, ER, PR, HER2), and clinical data (DCIS, Lymph node, Positive node, size, surgery type, pathology, tumor grade, nuclear grade, multicentric, perineural invasion, vascular invasion, calcification, frozen margin, margin, breast side, tumor type, diagnosis type, TNM staging, stage, onco, flap, metastasis location, and metastasis recurrence). We exclude values with more than 90% missingness.

We apply the imputation criteria to values with more than 50% missingness. We use the mean to impute missing values for numeric features and the mode for categorical features. The sensitivity analyses of the imputed values by using multiple imputation indicate that they are stable, and we have employed valid imputation techniques. We further validated the imputed values using t-tests for numerical features and chi-square tests for categorical features. The calculated p-values above 0.05 indicate that there are no significant differences between the original and imputed data distributions. This indicates that imputation leads to no biases or alteration of data distributions. We also encode categorical variables as numerical representations, as shown in Tables II and III, and Tables S1and S2 (in the supplementary). Furthermore, outliers are removed, and continuous data are normalized before training. Finally, to evaluate parameter correlations, we use Pearson correlation for continuous variables. Then, to determine whether the observed correlation between variables is statistically significant or due to chance, we calculate p-values for the correlation analysis. We then plot a correlation heatmap with P-value masking, incorporating both the correlation matrix and the P-values.

Managing class imbalance

The distribution of the target value is unequal in the dataset with more surviving cases. Therefore, we address this issue by using SMOTE, which generates artificial samples from the minority class to balance the dataset. Here, we use SMOTE to generate synthetic data rather than duplicating existing data, thereby mitigating the risk of overfitting and enriching the feature space. We apply SMOTE only to the training dataset after splitting the data to avoid overfitting.

Model development

In this study, which will be conducted in a longitudinal analysis, the required information will be examined by reviewing the medical records of all patients with breast cancer who have been referred to Hazrat Rasool Akram (PBUH) in the last 10 years. Inclusion criteria are patients with breast cancer, according to the physician, and exclusion criteria are (1) patients with incomplete records, (2) patients who are simultaneously suffering from another malignancy other than breast cancer, and (3) those who die without having metathesis. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and medical image information are gathered from patient records. In the category of demographic information, the following variables are recorded: age, surgery to metastasis time, metastasis to death time, surgery to death time, family history, pregnancy, first pregnancy, feeding, OCP taking, abortion, death, death due to metastasis, iort, neoadjuvant, radiotherapy type, and iort_bi. Also, clinical signs obtained from mammography data, including DCIS, lymph node, positive node, size, surgery type, pathology, tumor grade, nuclear grade, multicentric, perineural invasion, vascular invasion, calcification, frozen margin, margin, breast side, tumor type, diagnosis type, TNM staging, stage, onco, flap, metastasis location, and metastasis recurrence, are recorded. Laboratory criteria, including Ki-67, ER, PR, and HER2, are recorded. In general, the dataset comprises 43 features: 16 geographical, 23 clinical, and 4 laboratory.

In the next step, the data are pre-processed and cleaned using the defined data processing processes. The dataset is then randomly split into training (80%), testing (10%), and validation (10%) sets. Then, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm is used to evaluate the survival rate of patients with breast cancer, and the results are compared with the 4 ML algorithms, including Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), AdaBoost, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Each model is evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation (CV) and nested CV to assess the model’s generalizability. In this case, the dataset is divided into 5 subsets: 4 for training and 1 for validation.

This process is repeated 5 times, and the results are then averaged to obtain a more robust, unbiased estimate. Additionally, L2 regularization is employed to mitigate learning noise by penalizing large coefficients in the Gradient Boosting model.

Model evaluation

We test the trained models using unseen data. The models’ performance is evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, precision, F1-score, recall, and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC). Accuracy measures the overall model correctness; Precision calculates the proportion of true positive predictions among all the positive predictions; Recall evaluates the model’s capability in capturing all the true positives; F1 score calculates the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall; and finally, AUC is used for checking the model’s ability in class detection at various thresholds.

Parameter selection

To identify the most important features for breast cancer survival prediction, we select the best-performing model based on accuracy, F1-score, and AUC, and then use both SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) value and F-score to assess feature importance.

SHAP values quantify each feature’s contribution to the model’s output by accounting for feature interactions, and they are used to interpret the model’s decision-making process and identify the main factors that determine survival. On the other hand, the F-score is a univariate feature selection method that scores each feature individually, without considering the combined effect of features.

Results

This study includes 3476 breast cancer patients. We first perform the parameter coloration evaluation. The results of the correlation heatmap with P-value masking are presented in Fig. 1, where the right-side spectrum indicates the intensity of the correlations. The results show a statistically significant correlation between ER and PR, positive node and lymph node status, and surgery-to-metastasis and surgery-to-death time. It means ER and PR positive tumors are more likely to have positive lymph nodes, since in this case, the cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes. Also, it means that if a tumor is positive for one receptor, it’s highly likely to also be positive for the other. Also, having longer intervals between surgery and metastasis or death is associated with ER and PR positive tumors. This longer survival times implies that these tumors might be slower-growing or respond better to treatment. Moreover, positive lymph node status is an important prognostic factor, with having more positive nodes leading to a worse outcome. Therefore, the lymph node ratio (LNR), the ratio of positive nodes to the total number of nodes, can be a predictor of survival.

Results also indicate a statistically significant correlation between Ki-67 value and HER2. A high Ki-67 value indicates a high proliferation rate, which is frequently associated with HER2 positivity. It means tumors with higher HER2 levels tend to have higher Ki-67 value, and both lead to a more aggressive tumor phenotype. Moreover, HER2 is statistically significantly correlated with metastasis-free survival time, positive node status, lymph node involvement, DCIS, and Ki-67 value. It means that overexpression of HER2 is associated with shorter metastasis-to-death times, more frequent positive node status, positive lymph node status, the presence of ductal.

carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and higher Ki-67 value.

Furthermore, Ki-67 value is statistically significantly correlated with metastasis-to-death time and tumor size, and inversely related to DCIS. It means a higher Ki-67 value (more than 20%) is associated with more aggressive tumors, including shorter survival times after metastasis, larger tumor sizes, and a greater likelihood of lymph node involvement. Therefore, Ki-67 value is an important marker of tumor aggressiveness; however, its interpretation should be considered in the context of other clinical and pathological features.

Size is statistically significantly correlated with Ki-67 value, lymph node status, and positive node status. It means that larger tumors tend to have higher Ki-67 values, which serve as a marker for a greater proportion of dividing cells. Additionally, positive lymph node involvement is associated with a higher Ki-67 value, indicating a more aggressive tumor behavior. Furthermore, size is inversely related to age, family history, ER, and PR. This means that, as these factors increase, breast tumor size is smaller in older age, family history is associated with larger tumor size, and ER + and PR+ indicate a smaller tumor size.

Furthermore, the Metastasis-to-Death time is statistically significantly correlated with HER2, Ki-67 value, positive node status, lymph node involvement, and tumor size. These factors are important indicators of cancer prognosis, and their combination can be used to predict time to death after metastasis. in a vertical format from top to bottom. Also, their higher values indicate a tumor with aggressive behavior, such as higher metastasis and poorer prognosis. Moreover, metastasis-to-Death time is inversely related to ER, PR, and DCIS. It means that these features are associated with a longer time to distant metastasis and death.

Additionally, the effect of each variable on survival is presented in Fig. 3. The SHAP value indicates that patients with low-grade or low-risk Ductal Carcinoma In Situ (DCIS) (SHAP = − 0.85) are associated with better long-term survival. The Ki-67 value measures cancer growth by quantifying the percentage of actively dividing cells. The results in Fig. 3 indicate that lower Ki-67 values are associated with higher survival rates. Additionally, a Ki-67 value (SHAP = 0.28) is associated with a good overall survival rate in very early-stage cancers. Moreover, the results in Fig. 3 indicate that higher age is associated with higher survival rates, and an age under 41 (SHAP = -0.23) is associated with worse outcomes.

Additionally, the results indicate that fewer positive nodes (SHAP = -0.06) and sentinel lymph node involvement (SHAP = -0.18) are associated with higher survival rates. Lymph node involvement (SHAP = -0.03) in lower-grade tumors is associated with improved survival. Therefore, lymph node involvement is an important factor for AI-based screening.

Furthermore, the results in Fig. 3 indicate that a longer duration of breastfeeding (SHAP = -0.18), specifically between 13 and 18 months, and having 1 to 2 pregnancies (SHAP = 0.01) are associated with higher survival rates. It reduced the risk of Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (20%) and BRCA1 mutations (22–50%). Therefore, women with a family history of breast cancer should be supported for breastfeeding to avoid breast cancer.

There are seven tumor classes in our data set: Comedo, solid, classic, medullary, NOS, mixed, and other. According to Fig. 3, the fourth tumor type, medullary, is associated with a higher survival rate (SHAP = -0.04).

Finally, the results show that not having multicentric breast cancer indicates longer survival. Furthermore, the results indicate that being ER+ (SHAP = -0.07), PR+ (SHAP = 0.12), and HER2- (SHAP = -0.04) is associated with a higher survival rate. However, there is no significant difference between the ER-/PR+ phenotype and the other phenotypes. Additionally, as mentioned in [16], tumors with HER2/neu, ER-, and PR- receptors have lower disease-free survival rates than ER+/PR+/HER2- tumors. Moreover, tumor size below 2 cm (SHAP = 0.11) is another effective predictor of higher survival, as indicated by the results in Fig. 3.

Additionally, being at a lower level of pathology (SHAP = -0.08) in Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC) is associated with a higher survival rate, as indicated by the results in Fig. 3. Surgical type is a key parameter in achieving higher survival rates. In our data set, we have BCS, auxiliary, subduct, and MRM. According to the results, a lower surgery type class, such as BCS for early-.

stage breast cancer, leads to a higher survival rate (SHAP = − 0.02).

Having a lower tumor grade and being in lower tumor stages of I and II (SHAP = − 0.05) also leads to a higher survival rate, as indicated in Fig. 3. Also, results show that the absence of necrosis (SHAP = − 0.03) is associated with longer survival. Moreover, having a family history of cancer can indicate a higher risk for breast cancer, whereas having no family history leads to longer survival.

However, it is not a precise distinguishing factor for longer survival. Moreover, the absence of calcification (SHAP = -0.01) is associated with a higher survival rate, as shown in Fig. 3.

We also use the F-score in Fig. 4 to evaluate feature importance based on the linear relationship between features and the target. According to the results, age, Ki-67, size, and DCIS are the most effective parameters, excluding combinational effects. Additionally, the SHAP values and F-scores are consistent with the clinical outcomes. Therefore, these parameters are most effective for survival.

Clinical discussion

Breast cancer, a heterogeneous malignancy, needs early detection and therapy. Hence, identifying effective parameters for a longer survival rate can help create an effective therapy plan. Here, we focus on identifying effective parameters for achieving a longer survival rate using machine learning algorithms. Also, we assess the predictive capabilities of these models. Research findings show the importance of having lower values of DCIS, lower Ki-67 value, higher age, longer duration of breastfeeding, lower grade of lymph node, lower number of sentinel lymph node involvement, lower tumor size, medullary tumor type, PR+, ER+, HER2- are the top 10 most effective parameters in longer survival rate. These findings are supported by [17–20].

The findings show that low-grade DCIS is associated with better long-term survival. The result is in accordance with [21], in which 10-year overall breast cancer-specific survival in low-risk DCIS was 98.9%. Additionally, a Ki-67 value in very early-stage cancers is associated with a favorable overall survival rate. This result is also mentioned in [22].

Moreover, our findings revealed that higher age is associated with a higher survival rate, whereas age under 41 is associated with worse outcomes. This result is similar to that in [23], which reports that age under 40 is associated with worse outcomes, independently of HR + and lower-grade disease, at 30-month follow-up.

The finding also states that fewer positive nodes and fewer sentinel lymph node-positive cases are associated with a higher survival rate. These results are also approved in [24]. Also, this result aligns with the research in [25] that highlights the significant impact of lymph node status on breast cancer survival, emphasizing the importance of accurate detection and screening follow-up during surgery. Therefore, lymph node involvement is an important factor for AI-based screening.

Our findings indicate that a longer duration of breastfeeding and more pregnancies are associated with higher survival rates. This result in [26] also supports this result, in which it is mentioned that the risk of breast cancer is reduced by 4.3% for every 12 months of breastfeeding, and a 7.0% decrease for each birth. It reduced the risk of Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (20%) and BRCA1 mutations (22–50%). Therefore, women with a family history of breast cancer should be supported in breastfeeding to avoid breast cancer.

In the current study, the medullary type is related to a higher survival rate. This result is supported by [27], which indicates that medullary carcinoma has superior survival rates compared to ductal carcinoma, with 10-year survival rates of 90% and 80%, respectively. Also, having multicentric breast cancer indicates longer survival, which is supported by [28].

Furthermore, being ER+, PR+, and HER2- is related to a higher survival rate. The results are also consistent with [29] and [30], which indicate that ER+/PR+ tumors are associated with longer survival than ER+/PR− tumors. However, there is no significant difference between the ER-/PR+ phenotype [29]. Additionally, as mentioned in [16], tumors with HER2/neu, ER, and PR receptors have lower disease-free survival rates than ER+/PR+/HER2- tumors. Moreover, a smaller tumor size is associated with a higher survival rate. This result is also supported by [31].

Additionally, studies have shown that lower pathology in IDC is associated with a higher survival rate, as supported by [32]. Also, BCS for early-stage breast cancer leads to a higher survival rate. This result is validated by the recent research in [18], in which it is mentioned that breast-conserving therapy (BCT), which includes a surgical component, is associated with an increase in overall survival (survival from any cause) and breast cancer-specific survival (survival from breast cancer) in different breast cancer subtypes.

Additional findings of this research indicate that Lower tumor grade and lower tumor stage lead to a higher survival rate, as supported by [33], which states that Tumor size and lymph node involvement affect breast cancer survival. The absence of necrosis is associated with longer survival, as discussed in [34], which found that high-risk tumor necrosis is linked to poor 5-year survival. However, having a family history of cancer is not a precise distinguishing factor for longer survival [34]. Moreover, the absence of calcification is related to a higher survival rate, as also supported by [16], in which the presence of calcifications is 6.1% among advanced breast cancer patients and 3.6% among others. Furthermore, the current study demonstrates the effectiveness of the XGBoost algorithm in predicting survival rates. This result is supported by [35], in which XGBoost is validated for predicting 90-day mortality of acute renal failure patients.

Study limitations

In this study, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and clinical experiments. Firstly, a single-center dataset may introduce selection bias, leading to incomplete or inaccurate data, as it relies on existing medical records. Also, the lack of external validation on other datasets may limit its generalizability. Furthermore, focusing on specific clinical features in survival data may overlook other significant factors that influence cancer survival. These limitations highlight areas for future research to enhance the understanding of breast cancer survival.

Conclusions and future works

Breast cancer (BC) with heterogeneity at the molecular level is the most common malignancy in women. Considering tumor biology and patient features, personalized BC detection and treatment are needed. Here, a time-stratified breast cancer survival analysis is presented that incorporates tumor characteristics, disease stage, and patient features, using machine learning (ML) algorithms to support personalized care planning. We utilize multimodal data from 3,476 breast cancer patients, comprising 43 features, and employ 6 ML algorithms to predict survival rates at less than 6 months, 6 months, 1-, 2-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year time points. The six algorithms include eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), AdaBoost, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). We perform the preprocessing, including imputation, class balancing, and feature selection. Results show that XGBoost achieves an accuracy of over 90%. Additionally, the top five important features, as determined using Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), are DCIS, Ki-67, age, feeding, and lymph node grade. Results show that lower values of DCIS and Ki-67, lower lymph node and tumor grade, less sentinel lymph node involvement, higher age, longer duration of breastfeeding, lower tumor size, medullary tumor type, PR + and ER+, less positive node, more pregnancy, DCIS pathology class, BCS surgery type for early-stage, lower tumor stage, no necrosis, HER2-, no family history, and no calcification are result in longer breast cancer survival. Furthermore, we perform model validation using both 5-fold cross-validation (CV) and nested CV. We also evaluate model performance using accuracy, precision, F1 score, and recall. All results show the proposed method outperforms. This paper presents clinical, molecular, and demographic insights into survival analysis, utilizing explainable ML techniques to support personalized treatment decisions. In the future, we aim to use the results to design an AI-based sensor for continuous survival monitoring and personalized treatment.
