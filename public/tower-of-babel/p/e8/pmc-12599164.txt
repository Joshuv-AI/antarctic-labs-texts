Essential Role of the AfudrnA Gene in Conidiation and Early Asexual Development in Aspergillus fumigatus
Authors: Mohammed A Abdo-Elgabbar, Sang-Cheol Jun, Yu-Kyung Kim, Saeed Alasmari, Bashir Salim, Kap-Hoon Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHelix-loop-helix (HLH) transcription factors are essential regulators of various cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and development. In Aspergillus fumigatus, a major fungal pathogen, the HLH transcription factor encoded by the AfudrnA gene plays a critical role in the organism’s asexual reproductive cycle. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of AfudrnA and its function in conidiation, a key asexual reproductive process. Through targeted deletion of AfudrnA, we observed a dramatic reduction in conidia production, leading to an aconidial phenotype, which suggests that AfudrnA is indispensable for conidiation in A. fumigatus. The deletion mutants, however, showed no significant changes in growth rate or sensitivity to antifungal agents, indicating that AfudrnA specifically influences conidiation rather than general growth or stress responses. To confirm the role of AfudrnA, we reintroduced the gene into the deletion mutants, which restored normal conidiation, further underscoring its essential role in the process. Moreover, expression analysis revealed that AfudrnA is highly expressed during the early stages of conidiation, with transcript levels peaking 6 h after induction of asexual development. Interestingly, the deletion of AfudrnA also led to a significant downregulation of brlA, a key regulator of conidiation, highlighting a potential regulatory interaction between these two factors. These findings elucidate the critical role of AfudrnA in the conidiation of A. fumigatus and contribute to our understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing asexual reproduction in filamentous fungi. This study not only enhances our knowledge of fungal biology but also provides potential targets for therapeutic intervention against fungal pathogens.Keywords: AfudrnA gene, Aspergillus fumigatus, asexual development, conidiation, gene deletion, helix-loop-helix (HLH) transcription factor

Introduction

HLH transcriptional factors are crucial regulators of cell growth and across a broad spectrum of developmental processes. These proteins function by forming homo- and/or heterodimers with other HLH proteins, allowing them to bind to E-box motifs in DNA. Due to their role as both activators and repressors of gene transcription, the regulations and functions of HLH transcription factors are highly complex [1–7].

The HLH transcription factors can be categorized into three groups: first group contains a basic region adjacent to HLH domain, which serves as DNA-binding interface; the second group known as bHLHZ proteins, includes additional leucine zipper dimerization domain; and the third group lacks a functional DNA-binding domain and act as negative regulators of bHLH proteins [1,7–11]. The bHLH domain, consisting of approximately 60 amino acids, is divided into two distinct regions: the basic region located at the domain-N terminus, which functions as a DNA-binding motif, and the HLH region, located at the C terminus, which serves as the dimerization domain. The HLH region’s hydrophobic residues form two amphipathic α helices connected by a flexible loop, enabling the interaction between HLH proteins to form either homodimers or heterodimers. Notably, many bHLH proteins bind to the E box (5′-CANNTG-3′), with the G box (5′-CACGTG-3′) being more prevalent [12]. The dimerization capability and DNA-binding specificity of HLH protein are influenced by the identity of their dimeric partners, leading to varied regulatory outcomes [7].

Inhibitor of DNA-binding (Id) proteins are key regulators that prevent cell differentiation, promote growth, and sustain tumor development by binding to E proteins and other transcription factors through HLH domain, thereby inhibiting transcription. Id proteins play essential roles in the lineage commitment regulation, cellular fate decisions, and the timing of differentiation during neurogenesis, lymphopoiesis, and angiogenesis. They are also essential for embryogenesis and cell cycle progression, function as positive regulators of cell proliferation [13,14]. Among the Id proteins, Id1, Id2, and Id3 exhibit strong binding affinity to E proteins and interact with the class B factors. Id1 and Id2 interact strongly with MyoD and Myf-5 and weakly with myogenin and MRF4/Myf-6, whereas Id3 interacts weakly with all four MRFs, with these binding differences attributed to specific amino acids variations in the first helix and adjacent residues [15].

In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein Ino4 forms a dimer with Ino2p to regulate phospholipid biosynthetic genes. It also dimerizes with other bHLH proteins such as Pho4p, Rtg1p, Rtg3p, and Sgc1p. Moreover, it can interact with additional proteins like Bck2p, YLR422W, and YNR064C. Ino4p’s interaction with Ino2p, Hcs1p, Apl2p, YMR317W, and YNL279W suggest its central role in coordinating multiple biological processes [7,16]. Specifically, the bHLH protein Pho4p induces PHO5 gene expression in response to phosphate depletion that by binding to UAS elements in the PHO5 promoter, with UASp3 playing a role in inositol regulation mediated by Ino2p/Ino4p [17]. Myc subfamily Cph2 regulates hyphal development in Candida albicans, with this regulation being dependent on Tec1 [18].

In Aspergillus species, bHLH transcription factors, such as AnBH1, devR, stuA, sclR, and ecdR, have been implicated in penicillin biosynthesis, asexual and sexual development, cell cycle progression, and other cellular functions [4,7,12,19–21]. In Aspergillus oryzae, disruption of the bHLH protein-encoding gene ecdR results in the overproduction of sclerotia with a significant reduction in conidia, a phenotype mirrored in sclR-overexpressing strains, whereas sclR knockout leads to dense conidia production with scattered sclerotia [7,12,21].

In this study, we identified and characterized a bHLH protein encoding gene, AfudrnA, the ortholog of Aspergillus nidulans drnA gene (Dependently Regulated by NsdD), which is expressed during early stages of the conidiation in A. fumigatus. Knockout AfudrnA results in an aconidial phenotype characterized by significantly reduced or absent conidia, without affecting growth or antifungal sensitivity. These findings underscore the necessity of AfudrnA gene for proper asexual development in A. fumigatus.

Materials and Methods

Strains, medium, and culture conditions

A. fumigatus strains used in this study include AF293, AF349, and AF349.1. The AF293, AF349 strains were obtained from the Fungal Genetics Stock Center (FGSC), with AF349.1 being a modified version of AF349 that includes the pyrG gene (Table 1). AF293 and AF349.1 served as wild-type and isogenic control strains (respectively), while AF349 was utilized as an auxotrophic marker host for transformation, following standard A. nidulans techniques as described by [22]. Genetic procedures were conducted according to the methods established by [23]. Minimal medium (MM) with appropriate supplements was prepared following protocols by [23–25]. Complete medium (CM) was based on MM but included 1.5 g of yeast extract, 1.5 g of casein hydrolysate, and 10 ml of vitamin solution per liter. All strains were incubated at 37 °C.

Database analyses of the A. fumigatus genome

The drnA gene, encoding HLH domain protein, was initially analyzed in A. nidulans (not published). To identify its ortholog in A. fumigatus genome, a BLASTP search was performed using DrnA homologous protein sequence from A. nidulans (locus ANID_04394). This search, conducted through the Broad institute (http://www.broadinstitute.org), identified the A. fumigatus the AfudrnA gene with locus ID Afu4g06880. Additionally, the BLASTP search revealed that A. oryzae ecdR is also an orthologue of the AfudrnA gene.

Construction of the AfudrnA gene deletion cassette

To delete AfudrnA gene, we employed AfupyrG marker using the Double-joint PCR method as described by [26]. The construction of deletion cassette was carried in three stages. First, upstream (509 bp) and downstream (604 bp) flanking sequences of AfudrnA gene were amplified along with AfupyrG selectable marker. In the second stage, these flanking sequences were ligated to the AfupyrG marker, creating a flanks-marker construct. In the final stage, the flanks-marker cassette was amplified using nested primer pairs to enhance specificity. The deletion cassette was then introduced into A. fumigatus via genetic transformation mediated by polyethylene glycol, following the method outlined by [22] (Figure 1(A)).

Construction of plasmid for re-complementation of AfudrnA gene

To reintroduce the AfudrnA gene into the AfudrnA deletion mutant’s genome, a complementation vector was constructed. A fragment of approximately 2.5 kb, containing the AfudrnA ORF (1.2 kb), promoter (1 kb), and terminator (0.3 kb) regions, was amplified by PCR using primer pairs AfudrnA complementary For-PacI and AfudrnA complementary Rev-AscI (Table 2) and pfu-X polymerase (SolGent™ Pfu-X DNA Polymerase SPX16-R250, South Korea). The PCR product was initially cloned into the pBlunt TOPO vector (Dr. Blunt TOPO cloning kit, DOCTOR PROTEIN, DR02202, South Korea). The AfudrnA gDNA fragment was then excised from the pBlunt TOPO vector through PacI-AscI double digestion.

Southern blot hybridization analysis

To confirm the deletion of the AfudrnA, Southern blot analysis was performed on the genomic DNA of A. fumigatus strains. Genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy plant Mini Kit (Cat. No. 69104 QIAGEN, Germany) and double-digested by NcoI and SalI. The resulted DNA fragments were separated by electrophoresis and transferred to a positively charged QIABRANE Nylon Plus membrane (Cat. No. 61010, QIAGEN, Germany). Southern hybridization was conducted following the protocol outlined in the DIG application manual (© 2008 by Roche Diagnostics GmbH). A 0.7 kb digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled probe was generated using the DIG High Prime DNA Labeling Detection Starter kit II (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Cat. No. 11585614910, Germany). Hybridization and signal detection were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Roche Diagnostics).

Northern blotting analyses

Total RNA preparation and Northern blot analyses were carried out as previously described [26]. Total RNA was isolated from the mycelia collected at different time points. Briefly, approximately 108 conidia were inoculated into 100 ml liquid CM in 250 ml flasks and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 r.p.m for 16 h. Then, the mycelia were harvested, washed three times with sterile double-distilled water, and rinsed with MM media before being plated onto MM plates and incubated at 37 °C. Samples were collected at 6, 12, 24, and 36 h. All samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, lyophilized, and stored at −80 °C until RNA isolation was required. For Northern blotting, 10 ug total RNA of each sample was blotted onto a QIABRANE membrane (QIAGEN). Results

AfudrnA encodes helix-loop-helix transcription factor

In this study, we characterized a putative gene, AfudrnA, whose deletion results in a loss of conidiation, leading to an aconidial phenotype. The drnA open reading frame (ORF) consists of 1226 bp, with 1161 bp encoding sequence, comprising two exons and one intron near to 5′ end of the gene. The intron is 65 bp in length and is flanked by SalI and BglII restriction sites (Supplementary Figure S2). The predicted the AfuDrnA protein is 386 amino acids long. Protein BLAST searches revealed orthologs in various Aspergillus species, including Aspergillus thermomutatus, Aspergillus clavatus, Aspergillus terreus, Aspergillus kawachii, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus flavus, A. oryzae, and A. nidulans. Additionally, AfuDrnA shares similarity with proteins in other filamentous fungi, such as Penicillium digitatum, Penicillium chrysogenum, Talaromyces marneffei, Talaromyces stipitatus. It also shows homology with protein from bacteria, including Leptospira borgpetersenii serovar Hardjo-bovis, Clostridium lentocellum. Interestingly, AfuDrnA also shares similarity with proteins in the colonial flagellate eukaryote Monosiga brevicollis and the higher eukaryotes such as Heterocephalus glaber and Cavia porcellus. According to NCBI BLAST, AfuDrnA likely belongs to the bHLH transcription factor family.

Construction of AfudrnA gene-deleted and complemented strains

The AfudrnA gene was deleted through a substitution with AfupyrG auxotrophin marker, as detailed in the Materials and Methods section. AfudrnA-deleted mutant was confirmed by the PCR using primer pairs AfudrnA 5′ Forward and AfudrnA 3′ Reverse, which amplified a ≅ 3.3 kb fragment in the deletion mutants compared a ≅ 3.0 kb fragment in the control strain (Figure 1(B)). Further digestion of the PCR products with PstI resulted in fragments of ≅1.5, ≅1.4, and ≅0.4 kb in the mutants, compared to ≅1.3, ≅1.2, and ≅0.4 kb fragments in the control strain (Figure 1(C)). The deletion of AfudrnA was also confirmed by Southern blotting, where genomic DNA was double-digested with NcoI and SalI and hybridized with 0.7 kb DIG-labeled probe. The control strain showed a ≅ 1.2 kb fragment, whereas AfudrnA deletion mutants displayed a ≅ 2 kb fragment (Figure 1(D)), confirming the successful deletion of the AfudrnA gene via the AfupyrG substitution.

To complement AfudrnA deletion, a strain was constructed using pyrithiamine resistant gene (ptrA) as a selectable marker. The complementation cassette and selection media are detailed in the Materials and Methods section. To confirm the integration of the pPTRI-PA plasmid into the genome of the AfudrnA deletion strain, a diagnostic PCR was performed using primer pairs, AfudrnA complementary For-PacI and ptr1 (P1 & P2, respectively) [27], (Figure 2(A)). The resulting 5.4 kb cassette (Figure 2(B)) was digested with BglI, producing two fragments of ≅3.9 and ≅1.4 kb (Figure 2(C)). To further validate these findings, the AfudrnA-complemented strain was cultured on media containing both 0.1 µg/ml and 0.5 µg/ml pyrithiamine. Notably, only the AfudrnA-complemented strain (C’AfudrnA) was able to grow, while the control and AfudrnA-deleted strains (parental strains) were unable to do so (Figures 2(D) & 2(E)).

Phenotypes of AfudrnA-deletion and complemented mutants

The generated AfudrnA-deleted and complemented mutants were point inoculated onto MM media to observe their phenotypes (Figure 3). The deletion mutant displayed white colonies with very few dispersed conidia. To assess the ability of this mutant to conidiate like the control strain, it was subjected to condition that induces asexual development by inoculation onto MM and CM media contains different concentrations of potassium chloride (0.3, 0.6, 1, and 2 M). Under osmotic stress, AfudrnA deletion mutant produced some conidia, but the amount was significantly less than that of the control strain (Supplementary Figure S3(A,B)). Interestingly, deletion of the orthologous locus in A. flavus resulted in a similar aconidial phenotype, characterized by the formation of numerous sclerotia and very few conidia (data not shown). This phenotype is comparable to that observed in ecdR-disrupted and sclR-overexpressed strains [7].

Phenotypes of ΔAfudrnA and complemented strains. The control strain, ΔAfudrnA, C’AfudrnA, strains were point inoculated onto solid MM media and colony photographs were taken at 96 h incubation at 37 °C (see the middle and downer rows for close-up views).

To investigate the role of AfudrnA in the antifungal sensitivity or resistance, the deletion mutant was streaked onto CM media, and Multodisc YEASTS (Liofilmchem, Cat. No. 95280, Italy) was applied. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the antifungal sensitivity was screened, revealing no significant difference between the control strain and AfudrnA deletion mutant (Supplementary Figure S4). The growth of AfudrnA deletion mutant was also assessed by point-inoculating it and the control strain onto CM and MM media, with triplicates incubated at 37 °C and 42 °C. Colony diameters were measured every 24 h over a 72-hour period. The AfudrnA deletion mutant exhibited a slight growth delay compared to the control strain, particularly at 37 °C and 42 °C, although this delay observed on CM at 42 °C (Supplementary Figure S5).

To confirm that the aconidial phenotype observed was directly related to the deletion of the AfudrnA gene, the AfudrnA deletion mutant was re-complemented with the entire AfudrnA gene. Reversion to the parental phenotype in the complemented strain confirm that the aconidial phenotype was indeed due to the deletion of AfudrnA gene (Figure 3).

Identification and expression of AfudrnA

A previous study [7] identified and characterized A. oryzae ecdR, which is similar to A. nidulans drnA gene, and plays a critical role in the early stage of conidiophore development. In this study, we identified, characterized, and confirmed the role of the A. fumigatus AfudrnA gene in the early stages of conidiation. Northern blotting was used to assess the levels of AfudrnA transcripts throughout the life cycle. AfudrnA gene was highly expressed during the conidiation and its mRNA accumulated in high levels at 6 h post asexual developmental induction. Interestingly, the A. fumigatus brlA gene (AfubrlA) expression seems to be affected by the AfudrnA deletion. That has been clarified by the decreased expression of AfubrlA comparing to the control strain (Figure 4).

Discussion

Asexual sporulation is a predominant reproductive strategy among most filamentous fungi, characterized by the production of conidia through mitotic division. Although the initiation mechanisms of asexual spore formation remain largely unclear, several key factors responsible for effective conidiation in A. nidulans have been identified. The brlA, abaA, and wetA genes are central regulators, orchestrating the expression of conidiation-specific genes during conidiophore development and spore maturation [28–30].

Research has also highlighted the role of the drnA gene in A. nidulans, which is regulated by nsdD and is crucial for both conidiation and sexual development. Deletion of the A. nidulans drnA gene inhibits conidiation, while its overexpression suppresses sexual development [31]. In A. oryzae, disruption of the sclR gene, which regulates sclerotia production, results in dense conidia production but sparse sclerotia formation, accompanied by dramatic cell lysis and protein degradation. Overexpression of sclR leads to the formation of a large number of initial hyphal aggregations and mature sclerotia, while also causing delayed and reduced conidiation. In contrast, the ecdR gene plays a crucial role in the early stages of conidiation and also controls sclerotia production. Disruption of ecdR has effects opposite to those observed in sclR, with the phenotypes of ecdR disruption and overexpression strains showing partial similarity to those of sclR overexpression and disruption strains, respectively [7,12].

Consistent with the findings in A. nidulans and A. oryzae, deletion of the AfudrnA gene in A. fumigatus and A. flavus (AfldrnA) also resulted in the loss of conidiation (unpublished data). Additionally, the non-conidiating phenotype associated with AfldrnA deletion was accompanied by the production of numerous sclerotia (unpublished data). However, when drnA was reintroduced into these Aspergilli, conidiation was restored, and the associated sclerotia production diminished, although some growth deformities were observed in A. flavus (unpublished data).

FluG is a key upstream activator of conidiation in Aspergillus. Loss-of-function mutants for fluG exhibit an aconidial fluffy phenotype, characterized by undifferentiated, cotton-like masses of vegetative cells [32]. The initiation of FluG-dependent development occurs through the de-repression of the SfgA protein [33]. The de-repression of conidiation by FluG activity subsequently leads to the activation of the downstream developmental regulator brlA [34,35]. Interestingly, the developmental switch triggered by FluG can be suppressed when fluG mutants are grown adjacent to wild-type, ΔbrlA, or ΔflbA mutants, suggesting that FluG may direct asexual sporulation through the production of extracellular signals [32]. In this study, we observed that point-inoculating ΔAfudrnA adjacent to another ΔAfudrnA mutant did not result in significant conidia formation at the colony margins, implying that ΔAfudrnA is not involved in the production of these extracellular signals.

In A. fumigatus, the velvet regulators play distinct roles in negatively regulating conidiation and the expression of brlA during development. While VeA and VelB serve as principal negative regulators of conidiation during vegetative growth, VosA and VelB are crucial for negative feedback regulation of conidiation and brlA expression, as they are essential for proper trehalose biosynthesis, stress tolerance in spores, and spore maturation [36–38]. The double deletion of veA/fluG or velB/fluG in A. flavus results in a loss of conidiation, even under light conditions. This conidiation loss can be partially restored by osmotic stress, though restoration is significantly reduced in the dark [39]. Cultivating ΔAfudrnA mutants on media containing varying concentrations of potassium chloride (KCl) or sodium chloride (NaCl) led to a slight increase in conidiation at the center of the colonies; however, this was not comparable to the control strain. Moreover, ΔAfudrnA mutants did not exhibit any significant differences from the control strain in terms of antifungal sensitivity or resistance, suggesting that AfudrnA may not be involved in antifungal resistance. Additionally, the growth rate of ΔAfudrnA mutants was nearly identical to that of the control strain, indicating that AfudrnA may not be responsible for growth regulation in A. fumigatus. Complementation of the ΔAfudrnA mutant with the intact AfudrnA gene restored conidiation to levels observed in the control strain, confirming that the inability of ΔAfudrnA mutants to produce conidia can be attributed to the deletion of the AfudrnA gene. A similar study conducted in our lab on A. flavus AfldrnA, an orthologue of AfudrnA, revealed that deletion of AfldrnA resulted in a phenotype similar to that of AfudrnA deletion, except that AfldrnA deletion was associated with the production of numerous sclerotia.

Conidiation in A. fumigatus involves the formation of conidiophores, which requires BrlA activity. Comparative genomic studies have shown that A. fumigatus AfubrlA is homologous to A. nidulans brlA, and the role of brlA in asexual development is conserved across these ascomycetes. AfufluG and AfuflbA function in conidiation, at least in part, by influencing AfubrlA expression, although it is possible that other genes downstream of AfufluG may also directly affect AfubrlA [30,35]. In A. oryzae, brlA expression was found to be influenced by ecdR disruption, with brlA levels significantly reduced in ecdR disrupted strains [7]. Given that ecdR and sclR have opposing functions, brlA and abaA expression were severely reduced in sclR overexpressing strains, while these genes did not show significant changes in the ΔsclR strain [12]. To investigate the expression pattern of AfudrnA, we plated the mycelia on minimal media and subjected them to normal aeration conditions. AfudrnA transcripts accumulated significantly at 6 h. Interestingly, AfubrlA expression was found to be influenced by AfudrnA deletion, as evidenced by altered brlA expression in the ΔAfudrnA strain.

The GATA-type transcription factor nsdD and the C2H2-type transcription factor nsdC activate sexual development in A. nidulans [40,41]. In A. flavus, NsdC and NsdD negatively regulate asexual reproduction by repressing genes involved in asexuality [42]. Microarray analysis of an A. nidulans nsdD deletion mutant revealed abundant drnA-orthologue transcripts, and deletion of drnA orthologue resulted in a non-conidiating phenotype. Conversely, overexpression of drnA negatively regulated sexual development [31], suggesting that nsdD may control drnA expression in A. nidulans. Overall, the precise roles of AfudrnA require further investigation; however, it is clear that it plays a critical role in asexual development. This study provides important insights into the conidiation process in A. fumigatus.
