Nonhormonal management of endometriosis: Role of dopamine D2 receptor agonists in preserving fertility and ovarian function
Authors: Rahi Tinni Basu, Pradip Kumar Mitra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) agonists represent a promising targeted, nonhormonal therapeutic strategy for endometriosis. These agents selectively downregulate vascular endothelial growth factor-A signaling, thereby inhibiting the aberrant angiogenesis that supports the survival and proliferation of endometriotic lesions. This review synthesizes preclinical and early clinical evidence on DRD2 targeting, with a focus on the clinical translation of cabergoline and quinagolide. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial, a 6-month cabergoline regimen significantly reduced chronic pelvic pain scores and improved pain relief without serious adverse events. Additionally, post hoc analysis of lesion regression showed that 61.3% of lesions in the quinagolide group demonstrated regression, while ovulatory function was preserved in 93% of participants, consistent with maintenance of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. Collectively, these data suggest that DRD2 agonists may represent a fertility-compatible, mechanistically targeted therapeutic approach and underscore the need for adequately powered phase 3 clinical trials.

Endometriosis affects approximately 10% of reproductive-age women and up to 60% of adolescents with chronic pelvic pain worldwide (1). Although conventional treatments such as gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists and progestins suppress estrogen production, they are frequently linked to bone mineral density loss and reversible infertility (2). Therefore, nonhormonal strategies that target disease biology without disrupting endocrine homeostasis are urgently needed (3). Dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) agonists address this therapeutic gap by inhibiting vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A)–mediated angiogenesis within ectopic lesions, thereby limiting disease progression, while preserving ovarian function (4, 5). This mini review evaluates the efficacy, safety profile, and underlying mechanisms of DRD2 agonists across multiple preclinical and clinical studies.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (2000–May 2026) to identify preclinical and clinical studies of DRD2 agonists in endometriosis. Boolean search strings combined terms related to DRD2, VEGF-A pathways, angiogenesis, endometriosis, treatment, and fertility outcomes. Eligibility was restricted to peer-reviewed, English-language reports evaluating endometriotic lesion dynamics, angiogenic markers, or reproductive outcomes. Studies with overlapping datasets or those not specific to endometriosis pathophysiology were excluded.

Pathophysiology

Basu et al (6) first established that dopamine attenuates VEGF-A–induced angiogenesis and vascular permeability by activating DRD2 on endothelial cells. Endometriotic lesions demonstrate significantly elevated VEGF-A expression compared with eutopic endometrium, with increased levels detected at both mRNA and protein levels across stromal, epithelial, and endothelial compartments, fueling aberrant neovascularization essential for implant survival and growth (7, 8). The VEGF-A–driven vascular permeability and angiogenesis can be inhibited by dopamine through DRD2 activation on endothelial cells (6). This dopamine action prevents VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR-2) phosphorylation and blocks downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase and focal adhesion kinase signaling (6). Immature neo vessels in endometriotic lesions express high VEGFR-2 levels, whereas mature corpus luteum vessels do not (1, 9). DRD2 is expressed in both human eutopic and ectopic endometrium, and DRD2 agonists have not been shown to disrupt normal ovarian functions such as ovulation, steroidogenesis, or folliculogenesis. Therefore, these drugs represent a promising nonhormonal, anti-angiogenic strategy that may selectively induce regression of ectopic endometrial implants, while preserving normal ovarian function and reproductive potential (10, 11, 12, 13).

Preclinical studies

Preclinical studies using human endometrial transplantation models have demonstrated DRD2 expression in endometriotic lesions, confirmed via polymerase chain reaction (mRNA) and immunohistochemistry (protein) (10). In these models, ovariectomized nude mice were implanted subcutaneously in the neck with sterile capsules containing 18 mg of 17β-estradiol (60-day release). Four days later, fresh eutopic human endometrial fragments, obtained from women with normal menstrual cycles and no history of endometriosis, were implanted into the peritoneal cavity (14, 15).

After three weeks, mice received oral treatment with the DRD2 agonists cabergoline (50 μg/kg/day) or quinagolide (50–200 μg/kg/day) for 14 days. These treatments significantly suppressed angiogenesis and promoted regression of endometriotic lesions compared with vehicle-treated controls. The reductions were statistically validated using nonparametric Mann–Whitney and Dunn’s multiple comparisons tests (P <.05) (R8). Mechanistically, these effects were associated with inhibition of VEGFR-2 phosphorylation, with no evidence of toxicity at therapeutic doses (14, 15).

Clinical studies

Despite missing its primary endpoint of reduction in total lesion size, a post hoc analysis of lesion regression in the QLARITY phase 2 trial demonstrated that 61.3% of lesions (n = 19/31) in the quinagolide (1080 μg) vaginal ring group regressed, compared with 35.5% (n = 11/31) in the placebo group (chi-square test; P = 0.04) (5) (Table 1).

Quinagolide Trial Interpretation

A nuanced interpretation of the phase 2 QLARITY trial is essential to understand the emerging clinical profile of DRD2 agonists. The phase 2 QLARITY trial requires cautious interpretation. The primary endpoint—change in total lesion volume from baseline to cycle 4—did not differ significantly between quinagolide and placebo, indicating no clear short-term volumetric benefit at the patient level. However, a post hoc lesion-level analysis showed a higher proportion of regressing individual lesions with quinagolide than with placebo (61.3% vs 35.5%, P = .04, unadjusted for multiplicity), suggesting a biologically plausible effect that may be masked when heterogeneous lesions are pooled into a single global volume measure. Although these findings are exploratory and limited by sample size, they support the prospective incorporation of lesion-level endpoints in future trials (5).

Furthermore, mechanistic and biomarker data are consistent with this signal. Exploratory analyses indicated more pronounced therapeutic effects in superficial and VEGF-A–high lesions, consistent with an angiostatic mechanism mediated via VEGFR-2 inhibition, whereas deep infiltrating endometriosis, characterized by a fibrotic, VEGF-A–independent vasculature, was inherently less susceptible to rapid volumetric reduction and likely contributed to the neutral primary outcome. Preservation of ovulatory cycles in 93% of participants demonstrates that DRD2 agonists can exert localized angiostatin activity without disrupting hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis function, providing proof-of-concept for a nonhormonal strategy preserving reproductive potential (5).

In summary, DRD2 agonists from different pharmacologic classes demonstrate a consistent biological and clinical signal in endometriosis (5, 16). Lesion regression rates exceeding 60%, a significant decrease in pain severity coupled with high ovulation preservation, reinforce the usefulness of DRD2 agonists in targeting the VEGF-A/VEGFR-2 axis in endometriosis (5, 16). Although current studies remain modest in size, the reproducible pattern of disease modification without hormonal suppression supports the expansion of this investigation to larger, phase 3 validation trials.

Clinical Comparison with Standard Therapies

Unlike hypoestrogenic agents that suppress ovulation broadly, DRD2 agonists target angiogenesis locally (16). Consequently, they can preserve fertility and bone health, making them suitable for adolescents and women planning pregnancy (5, 17). They are also more cost-effective than GnRH agonists. Trials also reported that DRD2 agonist therapy led to low discontinuation rates (∼3%) and transient side effects, including headache or nausea (5). No serious adverse or hypoestrogenic effects were reported, making these drugs well tolerated (5). Additionally, no patient experienced bone loss, thromboembolism, hypertension, or fertility risks (5). Cohorts have also exhibited excellent tolerability and pain relief (16, 23). However, further comparative trials are required to fully quantify the long-term efficacy and pain outcomes of DRD2 agonists relative to established hormonal treatments.

Future directions

Phase 3 trials should assess stratified dosing and combinations. Biomarkers such as VEGF-A expression and microvessel density, a well-established angiogenesis marker detected in the endometriotic tissues via immunohistochemistry, may aid in refining patient selection. Furthermore, long-acting agents may be formulated to enhance adherence. Finally, cost-effectiveness models and extended safety monitoring could support real-world implementation.

Conclusion

DRD2 agonists represent a targeted, fertility-preserving therapeutic approach for endometriosis (5, 16). Consistent with robust evidence from preclinical models, and QLARITY phase 2 clinical studies, these drugs can substantially regress endometrial lesions and reduce pain in key trials through selective VEGF-A pathway inhibition (5, 16). Meanwhile, they can preserve ovulation in most patients, confirming the integrity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis function and preventing hypoestrogenic side effects common to standard therapies (5). DRD2 agonists have evolved from a preclinical concept established in 2001 into a clinically viable platform for nonhormonal endometriosis therapy (5, 6, 16, 23). These agents are particularly promising for adolescents and reproductive-age women seeking long-term disease control compatible with fertility (16). Thus, they are well-positioned to redefine treatment paradigms. Furthermore, these agents exhibit favorable safety profiles, discontinuation rates, preserved bone mineral density, and cost-effectiveness (5, 17). Although the dose of DRD2 agonists used in the clinical trial of endometriosis has been shown to be safe with a low incidence of tolerable side effects (5, 16, 23), clinicians should nonetheless monitor for other manageable adverse effects observed in some Parkinson’s disease patients, including nausea, dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, peripheral edema, fatigue, hallucinations, impulse control disorders (e.g., compulsive gambling, buying, and hypersexuality), sudden sleep attacks, and withdrawal syndrome (24). Taken together, the overall benefit–risk profile supports progression toward regulatory approval and broader clinical adoption. Overall, DRD2 agonists address a critical unmet need by delivering durable, nonhormonal disease control while maintaining reproductive potential for millions affected worldwide.
