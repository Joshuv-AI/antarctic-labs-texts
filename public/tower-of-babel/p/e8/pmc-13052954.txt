Perioperative Antidepressant Use Increases the Risk of Opioid Abuse After Total Joint Arthroplasty
Authors: Alireza Mirahmadi, Jad Lawand, Kaveh Momenzadeh, Geoffrey Stoker, Mengnai Li, Ara Nazarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Antidepressants are commonly prescribed in patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty. While depression influences recovery, the association between perioperative antidepressant use and opioid-related outcomes remains unclear. Given the opioid crisis, we examined whether antidepressant exposure around surgery is independently associated with postoperative opioid abuse after total knee (TKA) and hip arthroplasty (THA), along with systemic and prosthetic complications over short-term and long-term follow-up.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network (2005-2025). Adults undergoing elective primary TKA or THA were identified using International Classification of Diseases-10/current procedural terminology codes. Patients with antidepressant prescriptions within 3 months before or after surgery were compared with nonusers. Propensity score matching (1:1) balanced demographics, comorbidities, and concurrent medications. Outcomes included opioid abuse diagnoses, opioid prescribing, systemic complications, and prosthetic failures at 90 days and 5 years. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated.Results:After matching, 90-day cohorts included 106,516 TKA patients and 46,227 THA patients per group; 5-year cohorts included 44,251 TKA and 16,019 THA patients per group. Despite modest prescribing differences, antidepressant use was associated with higher opioid-abuse diagnoses. At 90 days: TKA 0.10% vs. 0.02% (OR 4.3) and THA 0.12% vs. 0.02% (OR 5.7). At 5 years: TKA 0.74% vs. 0.12% (OR 6.0) and THA 0.42% vs. 0.09% (OR 4.5). Antidepressant users also had increased systemic and prosthetic complications, including prosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, and revision.Conclusions:Perioperative antidepressant use is independently associated with increased postoperative opioid abuse and higher complication rates after TKA and THA. These findings support preoperative psychiatric screening, medication review, multimodal pain strategies, and closer monitoring to mitigate opioid-related harm in vulnerable arthroplasty patients.Level of evidence:Level III. Introduction

Total joint arthroplasty (TJA), including total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and total hip arthroplasty (THA), remains one of the most effective interventions for end-stage degenerative joint disease, offering substantial pain relief and functional restoration for millions of patients annually in the United States1. However, a subset of patients experience suboptimal outcomes, including persistent pain, complications, and prolonged opioid use. Increasing attention has focused on psychological factors such as depression and anxiety, which are prevalent in up to 25% of patients undergoing TJA and are known to affect postoperative recovery and satisfaction negatively2-4.

Although previous studies have demonstrated depression that is associated with increased pain, worse functional outcomes, and higher resource utilization after TJA2,5, the role of antidepressant use, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), in shaping postoperative trajectories remains less well understood. Some studies have found that antidepressant users experience greater pain and inferior functional recovery after TKA or THA5-7, although often without large-scale validation or long-term follow-up. Moreover, despite growing concerns surrounding chronic opioid dependence after surgery, prior investigations have largely overlooked opioid abuse as a primary outcome in this context.

Opioid abuse after TJA is a pressing public health concern. Preoperative depression and prior opioid use have been independently associated with increased postoperative opioid requirements and prolonged use7,8. Building on this literature, this study aims to evaluate the association between antidepressant use and the risk of opioid abuse after TJA. Leveraging an extensive, national administrative database, we assessed both short-term and long-term complications, with a primary focus on opioid abuse as a clinically meaningful outcome. Our findings may offer critical insights for preoperative risk stratification, perioperative counseling, and the development of targeted interventions for vulnerable TJA patients.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We performed a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network (Cambridge, MA, USA), a federated, deidentified electronic health record database comprising data from over 100 US healthcare organizations, including demographics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory results, and clinical outcomes. The database is Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996-compliant and ISO 27001:2013 certified, and the study was exempt from internal review board review.

Cohort Definition and Inclusion Criteria

Adult patients undergoing elective, nontraumatic primary total joint arthroplasties, including THA and TKAs, between January 1, 2005, and March 31, 2025, were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 and current procedural terminology codes. Patients with malignancy, infection, or fracture within 6 months before surgery were excluded to minimize confounding from acute or nonelective indications. Patients were stratified by exposure to antidepressant medications (ATC code N06AA), encompassing SSRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, monoamine oxidase inhibitors, and related agents. The exposure cohort included patients with active antidepressant prescriptions within 3 months before or after the index arthroplasty, while controls had no antidepressant use during this perioperative period. The index date was the date of THA or TKA. Patients required at least 90 days of follow-up for short-term outcomes and a minimum of 5 years for long-term analyses.

Propensity Score Matching

Using logistic regression, 1:1 propensity score matching was performed separately for the THA and TKA cohorts to control for potential confounding. Matching variables included age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and concurrent use of analgesics and opioids. For opioid abuse analysis, patients with a previous history of opioid abuse were excluded from both groups. Covariate balance was confirmed using standardized mean differences < 0.1 across all matched variables.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was postoperative opioid abuse and opioid prescription use, identified using ICD-10 codes. Secondary outcomes included systemic complications such as pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, myocardial infarction, stroke, sepsis, and transfusion, as well as prosthesis-related complications including prosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, mechanical loosening, osteolysis, and revision arthroplasty. Outcomes were evaluated at 90 days and 5 years postoperatively. Matched cohorts were compared using chi-square and independent t-tests, with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals reported. Revision-free survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank testing. Analyses were conducted in TriNetX, with significance set at p < 0.01.

Results

Study Cohorts

Following 1:1 propensity score matching, 106,516 patients were included in each cohort for the TKA 90-day analysis, and 44,251 patients were retained per group for the 5-year follow-up. The 90-day matched cohorts for THA included 46,227 patients each, with 16,019 patients analyzed per group at 5 years. Baseline demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, and BMI, were well balanced between antidepressant users and controls across all cohorts (p > 0.05) (Tables I and II).

Opioid Outcomes

In both the TKA and THA cohorts, perioperative antidepressant use was associated with a significantly increased risk of opioid abuse, despite only modest differences in opioid prescribing rates (Fig. 1).

Opioid prescription and opioid abuse in short-term and long-term follow-ups. OR = odds ratio, SSRI = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, THA = total hip arthroplasty, and TKA = total knee arthroplasty.

Among patients undergoing TKA, opioid prescription rates at 90 days were slightly higher in the antidepressant group compared with controls (85.0% vs. 80.5%; odds ratios [OR], 1.05; p < 0.001), yet the incidence of opioid abuse was over “four-fold higher” (0.10% vs. 0.02%; OR, 4.3; 95% CI, 2.7-6.5; p < 0.001). At 5 years, opioid prescribing remained common in both groups but was more frequent in antidepressant users (96.1% vs. 89.6%; OR, 1.07; p < 0.001), with a “six-fold increase” in opioid abuse diagnoses observed (0.74% vs. 0.12%; OR, 6.0; 95% CI, 4.5-8; p < 0.001).

Similarly, in the THA cohort, 90-day opioid prescription rates were 25.0% among antidepressant users compared with 14.8% in controls (OR, 1.7; p < 0.001), while the corresponding rates of opioid abuse were 0.12% and 0.02%, respectively (OR, 5.7; 95% CI, 3-11; p < 0.001). At the 5-year mark, opioid prescriptions remained more common in the antidepressant group (50.0% vs. 43.2%; OR, 1.14; p < 0.001), with opioid abuse rates nearly 5 times higher than in controls (0.42% vs. 0.09%; OR, 4.5; 95% CI, 3-8; p < 0.001).

These findings highlight a disproportionate risk of opioid misuse among antidepressant users, suggesting a vulnerability not accounted for by prescribing frequency alone.

Short-Term Complications

In the 90-day postoperative period, antidepressant users undergoing TKA experienced higher rates of systemic complications, including deep vein thrombosis (1.4% vs. 1.1%; OR, 1.3), pulmonary embolism (PE; 1.1% vs. 0.76%; OR, 1.5), sepsis (0.78% vs. 0.44%; OR, 1.8), stroke (0.75% vs. 0.43%; OR, 1.7), and acute renal failure (2.3% vs. 1.5%; OR, 1.5), with all comparisons achieving statistical significance (p < 0.001).

Similar trends were noted among THA patients, who exhibited increased rates of PE (0.68% vs. 0.51%; OR, 1.3), infection following procedure (0.48% vs. 0.20%; OR, 2.4), transfusion (0.37% vs. 0.20%; OR, 1.8), myocardial infarction (0.53% vs. 0.36%; OR, 1.4), and stroke (0.73% vs. 0.42%; OR, 1.7), all with p values < 0.01 (Fig. 2).

Long-Term Complications

At 5 years, antidepressant use was associated with significantly higher rates of prosthetic-related complications in both surgical cohorts (Fig. 3). Among TKA patients, periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) occurred in 4.5% of antidepressant users compared with 2.5% of controls (OR, 1.8; p < 0.001), while periprosthetic fracture was observed in 2.4% vs. 1.2% (OR, 1.9; p < 0.001). Mechanical loosening occurred in 1.5% of users vs. 0.8% of controls (OR, 1.9; p < 0.001), and revision TKA was performed in 4.7% of users compared with 2.9% of controls (OR, 1.6; p < 0.001).

Odds ratio-based Forest plots for long-term complications after THA and TKA. THA = total hip arthroplasty, and TKA = total knee arthroplasty.

Among THA patients, PJI (0.75% vs. 0.52%; OR, 1.4; p = 0.01), dislocation or instability (0.80% vs. 0.43%; OR, 1.8; p < 0.001), and periprosthetic fracture (0.31% vs. 0.17%; OR, 1.8; p = 0.01) were also significantly more common among those with antidepressant exposure.

Mortality

Short-term mortality demonstrated mixed findings. In the TKA cohort, 90-day mortality was significantly lower among antidepressant users (0.38%) compared with controls (0.55%), corresponding to an OR of 0.69 (p < 0.001). This trend persisted at 5 years, with mortality rates of 1.9% in the antidepressant group and 2.7% in controls (OR, 0.74; p < 0.001).

Conversely, in the THA cohort, 90-day mortality was modestly higher among antidepressant users (0.25% vs. 0.15%; OR, 1.6; p = 0.001), although no significant difference in long-term mortality was observed.

Discussion

In this large matched-cohort analysis of patients undergoing THA and TKA, perioperative antidepressant use was associated with a significantly higher risk of postoperative opioid abuse, despite only modest increases in opioid prescribing. Antidepressant exposure was also linked to increased perioperative systemic complications and higher long-term rates of prosthetic failure, including infection, fracture, and revision surgery.

These results extend prior evidence indicating that antidepressant use is associated with poorer arthroplasty outcomes. Institutional studies have shown that patients with depression or on antidepressants report greater pain, reduced functional gains, and higher complication rates after TKA and THA2,5. A large national TKA analysis similarly demonstrated increased odds of short-term complications, including thromboembolism, sepsis, and wound issues, among antidepressant users9. However, earlier studies did not examine opioid abuse or account for perioperative antidepressant exposure timing.

The pronounced divergence between opioid prescribing and opioid abuse observed in our study aligns with prior research suggesting altered pain processing and reward mechanisms in patients with psychiatric comorbidity. Depression and chronic antidepressant use have been shown to affect pain sensitivity and opioid responsiveness, potentially heightening dependence risk. A previous study identified a synergistic effect between depression and preoperative opioid use, demonstrating that depression alone may not increase opioid needs unless paired with prior exposure8. Our findings expand on this by suggesting that antidepressant use, even without baseline opioid dependence, is associated with a substantial risk of future misuse.

Although antidepressant use was the exposure of interest in this study, it likely functions as a surrogate marker for underlying psychiatric conditions such as depression, anxiety, or bipolar disorder. The associations observed, most notably the increased risk of opioid misuse, may therefore reflect intrinsic neuropsychiatric vulnerability rather than a direct pharmacologic effect of antidepressant medications on opioid-related behaviors. In this context, antidepressant therapy may be better interpreted as a clinical indicator identifying patients with heightened behavioral or emotional risk profiles. Notably, this relationship was consistent across both TKA and THA cohorts, despite differences in baseline opioid exposure and complication profiles. This consistency suggests that shared neurobiological or behavioral mechanisms, rather than procedure-specific factors or prescribing practices, underlie the observed association between antidepressant use and opioid misuse.

Beyond opioid-related outcomes, we observed significantly elevated rates of prosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, and mechanical loosening among antidepressant users. These findings are consistent with prior reports attributing such complications to the physiologic effects of antidepressants, including impaired platelet function, immune modulation, and poor soft tissue healing6,7. While some studies have proposed that depression itself is the primary contributor to worse outcomes, independent of medication, other evidence suggests that antidepressant use remains an independent risk factor even after adjusting for psychiatric diagnoses10.

In this large matched cohort study of TKA and hip arthroplasty patients, perioperative antidepressant use was associated with a significantly higher risk of postoperative opioid abuse, despite only modest increases in opioid prescribing. This suggests an intrinsic vulnerability to misuse beyond prescription volume. Antidepressant use was also linked to higher perioperative systemic complications and increased long-term prosthetic failure, including infection, fracture, and revision.

Strengths of this study include the large, multicenter sample; rigorous propensity score matching and comprehensive capture of both short-term and long-term outcomes across 2 major joint procedures. Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration.

Although we adjusted for an extensive range of comorbidities and perioperative factors, residual confounding related to psychiatric disease severity or unmeasured behavioral variables cannot be fully excluded. Antidepressant use was used as a surrogate for underlying mood disorders; however, formal psychiatric diagnoses such as depression, anxiety, or bipolar disorder were not included as covariates. This was intentional, given the well-documented variability and inconsistency of ICD-based mental health coding across institutions, where overlapping and comorbid diagnoses are common and often unreliable. Incorporating these diagnoses risked introducing misclassification and statistical noise, potentially diminishing analytic power. We recognize this as a key limitation and an important focus for future studies using more granular, validated psychiatric data.

Second, antidepressant medications encompass a range of pharmacologic classes (e.g., SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclic antidepressants), each with distinct mechanisms of action and side effect profiles. Our study was unable to differentiate among these due to the constraints of administrative coding, which classifies antidepressants as a single category. Thus, the findings represent class-aggregated effects and may not reflect specific risks or benefits associated with individual agents.

Third, our analysis was based on prescription records, which do not capture medication adherence, dosage, or duration of use. Similarly, while the diagnosis of opioid abuse was identified using standardized ICD codes, such behaviors are often underreported or undocumented, likely leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence. Finally, as with all retrospective database studies, unmeasured confounding and coding inaccuracies remain inherent limitations.

Future studies should aim to incorporate validated psychiatric diagnoses alongside prescription data to better distinguish between the effects of antidepressant exposure and underlying mental health conditions. In addition, examining differential risks by antidepressant class could yield insights into which medications may confer greater or lesser postoperative risk, particularly regarding opioid misuse and surgical complications. Finally, prospective studies incorporating clinical assessment tools, patient-reported outcomes, and adherence tracking could offer a more nuanced understanding of the biopsychosocial factors influencing arthroplasty outcomes in patients with mental health comorbidity.

Perioperative antidepressant use is strongly associated with an increased risk of postoperative opioid abuse following TKA and THA. These patients also demonstrate elevated rates of prosthetic and systemic complications. Given the widespread use of antidepressants in surgical populations, our findings underscore the importance of multidisciplinary perioperative care, including psychological screening, pain management optimization, and closer postoperative monitoring to reduce the risk of opioid-related harm and improve long-term outcomes.
