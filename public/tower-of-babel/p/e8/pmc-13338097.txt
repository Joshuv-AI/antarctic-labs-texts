Rare Late Cardiac Involvement Following Resected Primary Limb Dedifferentiated Liposarcoma: A Case Report
Authors: Azin Alizadehasl, Saeedeh Baghyari, Mostafa Salimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) is a rare and aggressive subtype of soft tissue sarcoma, most commonly arising in the extremities or retroperitoneum. Cardiac involvement, whether primary or metastatic, is exceedingly rare and presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We present the case of a 68‐year‐old woman with a history of lower limb DDLPS who experienced progressive dyspnea and was subsequently found to have a large mediastinal mass with pericardial involvement. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) revealed imaging features consistent with a malignant lesion, including heterogeneous enhancement and tissue characteristics indicative of sarcoma. Given the patient's oncological history and the imaging findings, a diagnosis of metastatic cardiac DDLPS was favored. Surgical resection was deemed infeasible due to extensive pericardial involvement, and the patient underwent multiple lines of chemotherapy. Although there was initial disease control, she ultimately succumbed to septic shock resulting from a urinary tract infection. This case emphasizes the importance of considering metastatic sarcoma in the differential diagnosis of cardiac masses, particularly in patients with a known history of soft tissue malignancy. It also highlights the diagnostic value of CMR and the necessity for multidisciplinary management in complex, nonresectable cases.Keywords: cardiac tumor, case report, dedifferentiated liposarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) represent a rare and diverse group of malignant tumors that arise from mesenchymal tissues, constituting less than 1% of all adult malignancies [1]. Among these, liposarcoma stands out as one of the most common subtypes, which is further classified histologically into well‐differentiated, myxoid, round cell, pleomorphic, and dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) [2]. DDLPS is recognized as an aggressive variant, characterized by the presence of both well‐differentiated liposarcoma components and regions of high‐grade sarcoma that lack lipogenic differentiation. This variant is associated with a significant risk of recurrence and metastasis [3]. Liposarcomas typically develop in the extremities and retroperitoneum, with infrequent occurrences in other areas such as the mediastinum, gastrointestinal tract, and heart [4].

Primary cardiac sarcomas are exceptionally rare, accounting for approximately 25% of all primary cardiac tumors, which themselves have an incidence of only 0.001–0.03% in autopsy studies. Among malignant cardiac tumors, angiosarcoma is the most common histological type, whereas liposarcoma remains exceedingly unusual. Reports of cardiac involvement by DDLPS, whether as a primary tumor or as a secondary metastasis, are remarkably scarce [5].

Cardiac involvement in STS presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Clinical symptoms are often nonspecific and may include dyspnea, chest pain, and palpitations [6]. Additionally, imaging findings can resemble benign conditions such as thrombus or pericardial cysts. Advanced imaging techniques, particularly cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), are essential for the characterization of lesions [7]. While the definitive diagnosis of a cardiac mass remains histological, multimodality imaging provides essential noninvasive information regarding tissue characterization, vascularity, and metabolic activity, which is critical when biopsy is risky or unfeasible. Given the intricate anatomy and vital functions of cardiac structures, surgical resection is frequently not feasible, and systemic chemotherapy remains the predominant treatment option, albeit with limited efficacy [8]. This report presents a rare and instructive case of DDLPS affecting both the lower limb and the heart. It highlights the diagnostic process, radiologic findings, therapeutic approach, and clinical outcomes.

Case Presentation

A 68‐year‐old woman presented with complaints of progressively worsening exertional dyspnea and palpitations over the past 3 months. She noted a decrease in her exercise tolerance and experienced occasional orthopnea. There was no history of chest pain, syncope, fever, or recent infections, and she denied any previous cardiovascular disease. Her symptoms had developed insidiously and gradually worsened, leading her to seek further evaluation.

Upon presentation, the patient was afebrile (36.5°C) but appeared pale and tachypneic. Vital signs were notable for hypotension at 100/60 mmHg, tachycardia with a heart rate of 110 bpm, tachypnea at 33 breaths per minute, and significant hypoxemia with an SpO₂ of 83%. Physical examination revealed prominent jugular venous pressure, and cardiac auscultation confirmed tachycardia without murmurs. Chest examination was significant for diminished breath sounds on the left side with soft wheezing. A surgical scar and slight unilateral edema were noted on a lower extremity. Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (WBC: 15,600/μL), mild anemia (Hb: 11 g/dL), and an elevated C‐reactive protein (12 mg/L), while renal function, liver enzymes, cardiac biomarkers, and coagulation profiles were normal. An electrocardiogram was unremarkable.

Two years ago, the patient presented with unilateral lower extremity edema. Imaging at that time revealed a soft tissue mass, leading to a surgical excision. Histopathological analysis confirmed a diagnosis of DDLPS. Following surgery, she received postoperative chemotherapy consisting of Adriamycin, gemcitabine, and docetaxel. A subsequent follow‐up using fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET) revealed no fluorodeoxyglucose activity, and no signs of active malignancy were detected at that time.

Imaging Findings

Computed tomography (CT): A well‐defined, homogeneous mediastinal mass measuring 11.5 × 5.0 cm, exhibiting pericardial involvement, particularly adjacent to the lateral aspect of the left ventricle, with no signs of myocardial invasion.

Transthoracicechocardiography: This imaging confirmed the presence of a large (10.6 × 5 cm) solid mass. According to the Diagnostic Echocardiographic Malignancy (DEM) score [9], the mass exhibited multiple “red flags” suggestive of malignancy, including large dimensions (> 5 cm), pericardial effusion, and solid tissue echogenicity (Figure 1).

Differential Diagnosis

Initially, the findings indicated that malignancy was the most probable diagnosis, based on the patient's history. A biopsy of the cardiac mass was not pursued due to anatomical inaccessibility and the associated procedural risks; therefore, the diagnosis was inferred from imaging studies, the clinical context, and the patient's known oncologic history. The differential diagnosis included metastatic DDLPS, intracardiac thrombus, primary cardiac sarcoma, cardiac lymphoma, and benign masses such as myxoma or lipoma.

Histopathology

Limb tumor (2 Years Prior): Histopathological examination revealed infiltrative growth of spindle‐shaped atypical cells, with sporadic mitotic figures and multinucleated cells. Hemangiopericytoma vessels were partially identified. Immunostaining results demonstrated positivity for cdk4, MDM2, and S‐100. According to the WHO classification, the diagnosis was established as a DDLPS.

Cardiac mass (Current): The diagnosis was made based on radiological findings, taking into account the patient's history and imaging studies.

Evaluation and Treatment Plan

Multidisciplinary discussions were held among the Cardiology, Cardiovascular Surgery, and Oncology teams. Surgical resection was deemed unfeasible due to extensive tumor involvement; therefore, the treatment plan included neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy to assess the possibility of surgical resection, as the original tumor's extent was not considered suitable for local excision. Consequently, the decision was made to initiate chemotherapy.

The initial chemotherapy regimen consisted of Adriamycin (30 mg/m2), gemcitabine (900 mg/m2), and docetaxel (75 mg/m2), administered every 21 days, along with growth factor support. Maintenance therapy involved oral pazopanib (400 mg/day). Anticoagulation was initiated due to suspected thrombus.

Follow‐up PET scans showed no FDG uptake, indicating inactive disease, while a chest CT demonstrated a modest reduction (approximately 15%–20%) in the maximal tumor dimension. However, 2 months later, the patient presented with dyspnea, and a chest CT revealed that the previous mass had enlarged compared to the last imaging. As a result, second‐line chemotherapy was commenced, consisting of MESNA (750 mg/m2), Ifosfamide, and Adriamycin for a duration of 6 months.

Follow Up

The patient developed septic shock due to a urinary tract infection, and chemotherapy was discontinued, and the patient passed away after 3 months. Table 1 indicates the patient timeline.

Discussion

This report examines a rare case of late‐onset cardiac metastasis resulting from a primary DDLPS located in the lower limb. The clinical management of this patient underscores the complexities associated with advanced malignancies.

Liposarcomas, which are among the most prevalent types of STS, infrequently involve the heart. Primary cardiac liposarcomas are exceedingly rare, with only one reported case among 117 malignant cardiac tumors [12]. Likewise, a review of 31 cases of cardiac liposarcoma identified only a single instance of DDLPS [13].

The patient's primary symptom, progressive exertional dyspnea, is a common but nonspecific sign of cardiac tumors. In cases of cardiac metastases from STS, this can lead to congestive heart failure, a frequent cause of death [14, 15]. Although our patient was not formally diagnosed with heart failure, her symptoms, including tachypnea and elevated jugular venous pressure, indicated compromised cardiac function. Notably, many patients with cardiac metastases may be asymptomatic, highlighting the need for heightened awareness in STS patients with new cardiorespiratory issues [15].

The diagnostic approach in this case relied heavily on multimodality imaging. While echocardiography is the first‐line imaging modality for cardiac masses, it has limited tissue characterization capabilities. Nonetheless, as demonstrated in our case, it is vital for identifying “red flags” of malignancy, such as large size and effusion, which can be quantified using the DEM score [9]. CMR remains the noninvasive gold standard for tissue characterization and malignancy discrimination, with nearly perfect diagnostic performance [10, 11]. In this patient, CMR confirmed the malignant nature of the mass through signal heterogeneity and contrast enhancement. Furthermore, cardiac CT and PET play complementary roles; CT is excellent for evaluating calcifications and anatomical relationships, while PET provides metabolic data that aids in staging and assessing tumor activity.

The management of nonresectable cardiac sarcomas presents a significant challenge. Surgical resection, the only potentially curative treatment for liposarcoma, was deemed unfeasible due to the extensive involvement of the tumor. We considered aggressive surgical options, such as cardiac autotransplantation, which has been described for complex left‐sided heart tumors. However, this approach is typically reserved for primary cardiac malignancies without distant metastasis [16]. Given the metastatic nature of this patient's disease and the extent of mediastinal invasion, such high‐risk surgery was not indicated.

Systemic chemotherapy, the primary approach for unresectable disease, often yields limited results [17, 18]. Our patient underwent multiple lines of chemotherapy, initially achieving a modest reduction in tumor size before experiencing inevitable progression, which reflects the typical palliative benefit of this treatment. It is important to note that radiation therapy has been shown to enhance survival in patients with cardiac metastases and can be particularly effective in alleviating symptoms such as pericardial effusions [19]. The decision to implement chemotherapy for our patient was made by a multidisciplinary team, which is the standard of care for such complex cases. Tragically, the patient's eventual demise from septic shock, unrelated to her cancer progression, underscores the fragility of individuals undergoing extensive oncologic therapies.

Conclusion

This case highlights the significance of considering late cardiac metastasis in the differential diagnosis for patients with a history of DDLPS who present with new‐onset dyspnea. It emphasizes the critical role of multimodality imaging, particularly CMR, in achieving a noninvasive diagnosis when a biopsy is not feasible. Moreover, it underscores that while management presents challenges and the prognosis remains poor, a multidisciplinary approach involving palliative chemotherapy and other modalities, such as radiotherapy, can provide meaningful disease control.
