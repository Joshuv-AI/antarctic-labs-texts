Genome evolution and transposable element expansion reveal host-associated genomic features in Cladosporium cucumerinum
Authors: Xuanjun Lu, Qianxi Li, Yulan Zeng, Zhenfeng Bai, Mengrong Wang, Wenxiu Zheng, Hongkai Wang, Jiandong Bao, Xihui Xu, Xuetao Shi, Zhenzhu Su, Fucheng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow pathogens adapt to specific hosts remains an open question. Here, we investigate Cladosporium cucumerinum, a pathogen largely associated with Cucurbitaceae. Comparative genomic analysis of five newly Cladosporium sequenced genomes together with 19 publicly available genomes indicates that the diversification of C. cucumerinum occurred after that of its cucumber host lineage. We observe gene family contraction alongside lineage-specific expansion of long terminal repeat retrotransposons, accompanied by variation in gene repertoires including carbohydrate-active enzymes and secondary metabolite gene clusters. A lineage-specific β-glucosidase, CcBGL258, is required for pathogenicity but is dispensable for vegetative growth under in vitro conditions. Transcriptomic analyses reveal dynamic changes in gene expression during infection in both the pathogen and the host. Together, these results provide a genomic framework for host-associated features in C. cucumerinum, although we highlight that several associations observed in the study remain correlative and require further functional validation.Subject terms: Evolutionary biology, Evolutionary genetics, Pathogens, Fungal evolution

Introduction

Variation in host association among plant pathogens provides a useful system for investigating genomic features linked to host interaction and evolutionary diversification. Cucumber is an important vegetable crop all over the world. Cucumber scab, caused by C. cucumerinum, is a devastating disease that seriously affects cucumber yield1,2. C. cucumerinum was first discovered in the USA in 18873, and since then, it has occurred in many countries and has gradually become a widespread pathogen around the globe. C. cucumerinum is predominantly associated with Cucurbitaceae hosts, including watermelon (Citrullus lanatus), cucumber, melon (Cucumis melo L.), and zucchini (Cucurbita pepo L.)2,4. However, reports of hosts outside Cucurbitaceae remain limited and lack molecular support. Kwon et al. identified C. cucumerinum from eggplant (Solanum melongena) and sword bean (Canavalia ensiformis) in Korea5,6, but these observations have not been validated using molecular genetic approaches. Consequently, whether the host range of C. cucumerinum extends beyond Cucurbitaceae remains unresolved. This uncertainty highlights the importance of understanding the genomic basis of host association in this species.

C. cucumerinum belongs to the Cladosporium genus, which has drawn researchers’ attention due to its saprobic-pathogenic continuum2. Currently, the classification of the genus Cladosporium is well summarized by Bensch et al.1,2,7,8. However, genomic evolution within Cladosporium remains understudied9. In recent years, the development of comparative genomics has advanced the inference of the origin and evolutionary trajectory of fungi10,11. It has also aided in identifying the molecular sources of fungal pathogenicity12,13, such as the prediction and comparison of secreted effectors and virulence clusters.

Transposable elements (TEs), particularly long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs), are major drivers of genome evolution and structural variation14,15. Retrotransposons are genetic elements that can cause mutations in bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes16,17. Alternatively, LTR-RTs can act directly as virulence factors to enhance fungal pathogenicity16 or insert into or around essential genes to cause trait alterations17. LTR-RTs are primarily classified into Copia and Gypsy types. Copia elements are preferentially localized to the chromosome ends, whereas Gypsy elements exhibit insertion preference near centromeres, potentially triggering chromosomal breakage or non-allelic recombination18. Thus, LTR-RTs have emerged as pivotal elements in fungal pathogenicity and evolutionary studies.

Virulence factors represent a key research priority in plant pathogenic fungi. These pathogens colonize host plants by secreting effectors that compromise plant immune defenses19,20. Comparative genomic studies reveal that effectors frequently undergo diversifying selection and often exhibit elevated evolutionary rates during host–pathogen interactions13,21. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) help plant pathogens colonize hosts by breaking through the plant cuticle barrier, remodeling the fungal cell wall to avoid immune recognition22, and deconstructing the host cell wall. Previous studies have revealed that CAZyme content is a key indicator of fungal lifestyle23. Additionally, research has shown that the expansion of CAZymes in plant pathogens is driven by their antagonistic interactions with plants24. Therefore, variation in CAZyme repertoires may be associated with differences in host interaction strategies among plant pathogenic fungi. Some secondary metabolites are key virulence factors. Genomic annotations across diverse plant pathogenic fungi reveal abundant non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) genes whose encoded secondary metabolites serve as significant virulence factors25. Cytochrome P450s, major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporters, and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters additionally facilitate secondary metabolite synthesis, modification, and transport26–28.

Here, we sequenced and assembled five high-quality genomes from four Cladosporium species, including C. cucumerinum, and conducted a large-scale comparative genomic analysis with 19 publicly available genomes to investigate genomic features associated with host interaction and evolutionary history in C. cucumerinum. Our analysis reveals a pattern of concurrent gene family contraction and lineage-specific LTR-RT expansion, accompanied by evidence of genome restructuring. We further identify a truncated, secreted β-glucosidase, CcBGL258, as a factor required for pathogenicity, and characterize the transcriptional dynamics of both pathogen and host during infection. Together, these findings provide a genomic framework for exploring host-associated genomic features in C. cucumerinum, while highlighting the need for further functional validation of the observed patterns.

Result

Phylogenetic analysis and morphological characteristics

The internal transcribed spacer (ITS), translation elongation factor 1-α (TEF1-α), and actin (ACT) loci were sequenced and compared against the NCBI database using BLAST. Based on preliminary sequence similarity, 40 representative strains were selected for phylogenetic analysis using a concatenated ITS, TEF1-α, and ACT dataset. Bayesian inference was performed to reconstruct the phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 1). Combined phylogenetic and morphological analyses assigned ACCC 39335 to C. cucumerinum, ACCC 37043 and ACCC 36062 to C. tenuissimum, ACCC 36060 to C. cladosporioides, and A179 to C. rectoides.

We then analyzed the morphological characteristics and pathogenicity to investigate the phenotypic differences among the five strains. On PDA medium, the growth rate of these five strains was similar. While on SNA medium, ACCC 39335 and A179 grew significantly slower than the other strains (Fig. 1a, b), suggesting differential responses to nutrient conditions.

Based on lesion areas on cucumber leaves at 7 days post-inoculation, the ACCC 39335 strain exhibited the highest virulence, whereas A179 was the least pathogenic (Fig. 1c, d). These results indicate that C. cucumerinum exhibits relatively strong pathogenic capacity compared to closely related saprobic species. Notably, except for ACCC 39335, the other four strains sporulated profusely (Fig. 1e), differing from previous phenotypic descriptions of C. cucumerinum2. In addition, the conidiophore of ACCC 36062 was more slender than that of other strains, even compared with ACCC 37043 of the same species.

Genome sequencing and assembly

The genomes were sequenced by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 short-read sequencing and Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing technologies to generate high-quality contigs. The five sequenced genomes yielded an average number of 56.4 million short-reads and 1 million long-reads with an average N50 of 14.15 Kb (Supplementary Table 1). The size of the genomes ranged from 31.49 to 33.66 Mb, which is similar to the estimated genome size based on k-mer analysis (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). The final assembled contigs ranged from 23 to 49 with an average N50 of 2.06 Mb (Supplementary Table 2).

We annotated all 5 assemblies and 21 Cladosporium genomes downloaded from NCBI. According to Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs’ (BUSCO) analysis, 24 genomes with more than 90% completeness value were chosen, including 5 newly sequenced ones (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3).

We obtained 32 contigs from the C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335 genome, of which 15 are more than 1.5 Mb (Fig. 2a). When we compared the genomes of ACCC 39335 with ccnX229, which was assembled at a higher level (a total of 17 contigs), we found that more than 99% of the genes were present in each other’s genomes (Supplementary Fig. 4). This indicated that the assembly and annotation results of C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335 are reliable. Circle plots of the gene annotation results for the other four newly sequenced fungi are shown in the Supplementary Figs. 5–8.

Divergence timing and gene family construction in C. cucumerinum

We constructed a whole-genome phylogenetic tree based on 4853 single-copy genes in 24 Cladosporium fungi and Cercospora beticola strain 09-40 (GenBank accession number GCA_002742065.1 for genome assembly), which is chosen as the outgroup.

Using MCMCTree under the WAG amino acid substitution model, we estimated that C. cucumerinum and C. cladosporioides diverged approximately 12.73 MYA (95% CI: 11.04–13.61 MYA). The most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of extant C. cucumerinum strains was dated to 2.89 MYA (95% CI: 1.73–4.26 MYA; Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 9).

To assess robustness, we performed two calibration sensitivity analyses by (i) removing one internal node constraint and (ii) broadening its prior range. These approaches yielded MRCA estimates of 7.22 MYA (95% CI: 3.22–10.78 MYA) and 6.40 MYA (95% CI: 3.25–9.03 MYA), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10). Although point estimates differed, credibility intervals overlapped substantially. C. cucumerinum primarily infects members of Benincaseae and Cucurbiteae, whose divergence has been dated to approximately 27–31 MYA30. According to TIMETREE v5, cucumber is estimated to have originated at approximately 19 MYA, while the genus Cucumis dates to approximately 22 MYA31. This result temporarily demonstrates the plausibility of host-associated evolution in C. cucumerinum.

The genome size of C. cucumerinum is similar to that of closely related species. However, it possesses fewer genes, averaging 10812.5 (Fig. 2b). We found that Cladosporium species underwent significant gene family contraction at most evolutionary nodes (Fig. 2b). In the MRCA of C. cucumerinum strains, 478 gene families showed size changes. Among them, 25 families significantly expanded, whereas 352 families significantly contracted (p < 0.05; Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, most contracted families were completely absent in C. cucumerinum, with only 94 genes retained across these families (Supplementary Data 1), indicating a contraction-biased evolutionary trend. To evaluate whether the apparent gene family loss resulted from annotation bias, proteins from contracted families in a closely related species were randomly sampled and searched against the C. cucumerinum genome using tblastn. Using thresholds of ≥40% identity, ≥60% query coverage, and alignment length ≥200 aa, only 7 of 30 sampled proteins produced potential matches (Supplementary Table 4). These results suggest that most contracted gene families are genuinely absent rather than missed during annotation.

Although C. cucumerinum possesses fewer genes than some closely related species, such as C. tenuissimum (12,074 genes) and C. cladosporioides (11,663 genes), genus-wide comparisons revealed that its total gene number falls within the overall range observed across Cladosporium. No significant differences were observed in gene number or gene density between C. cucumerinum and other Cladosporium species (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating broadly comparable gene content at the genus level.

Genome restructuring and absence of whole-genome duplication in C. cucumerinum

Intrachromosomal rearrangements were prevalent in C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335. To investigate genome restructuring, we performed genome-wide collinearity analysis with C. oxysporum A3.I.1 and C. cladosporioides SYC63. The collinearity map revealed more complex intercontig connections between C. cucumerinum and C. oxysporum, indicative of frequent interchromosomal rearrangements and duplicated collinear links. In contrast, collinearity with C. cladosporioides appeared more conserved (Fig. 3a).

These structural differences were further supported by syntenic depth analysis, which showed a higher proportion of many-to-many relationships in the comparison with C. oxysporum. Approximately 30% of C. cucumerinum genes were associated with ≥ 2 syntenic blocks in C. oxysporum, compared to ~21% in C. cladosporioides (Fig. 3b), suggesting more extensive lineage-specific rearrangements and partial duplication events.

To further assess the evolutionary basis of these patterns, we analyzed Ks distributions. Orthologous comparisons showed peaks at Ks ≈ 0.294 (C. cladosporioides) and Ks ≈ 0.333 (C. oxysporum), consistent with the phylogenetic relationships inferred in Fig. 2b (Fig. 3c). In contrast, no distinct Ks peak was observed for paralogous gene pairs in C. cucumerinum, and Ks values were broadly distributed, often exceeding saturation. Similarly, syntenic gene pairs were too few to define a clear distribution (Fig. 3d).

Further analysis of collinearity within the C. cucumerinum genome showed that only a small fraction of genes (~4.91%) participated in collinear relationships, and the identified collinear blocks were short and fragmented (Supplementary Fig. 12). These features are inconsistent with the large-scale, duplicated structures expected from whole-genome duplication (WGD).

Collectively, these results indicate that genome restructuring in C. cucumerinum is unlikely to be driven by recent WGD. Instead, the observed genomic features point to alternative mechanisms of genome evolution, prompting us to investigate the role of TE expansion in this lineage.

Bursts of transposable elements in C. cucumerinum

Given the extensive genome restructuring and prevalence of many-to-many syntenic relationships in C. cucumerinum, we investigated the contribution of TEs to its genome evolution. Comparative annotation across Cladosporium species revealed a lineage-specific TE expansion in C. cucumerinum, with total TE content reaching ~10% of the genome in all examined strains, compared to less than 3% in most other species (Fig. 4a). LTR-RTs accounted for over 50% of total TEs, reaching nearly 70% in strain CBS 171.52, with Gypsy elements (~2–4%) far more abundant than Copia elements (~0.2–0.4%) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 5). Despite this expansion, genome size is not substantially increased relative to other Cladosporium species, suggesting a balance between TE accumulation and gene content reduction.

Insertion time analysis of intact LTR-RTs indicated that most insertions occurred around ~50 MYA (Fig. 4b). Density distributions further revealed that Copia elements exhibited a distinct insertion peak at ~24 MYA, which is more recent than the estimated divergence time of the host MRCA (27–31 MYA). However, given the limited number of intact Copia elements (n = 7), this pattern should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, Gypsy elements showed a broader distribution, with a minor recent enrichment around ~3.4 MYA (Fig. 4c). These temporal patterns suggest that LTR-RT activity in C. cucumerinum may have occurred in multiple phases and be associated with its evolutionary history.

Phylogenetic analysis of reverse transcriptase (RT) sequences grouped LTR-RTs into nine C. cucumerinum-specific clades, including two Copia and seven Gypsy clades (Fig. 4d). Among these, only one Copia clade lacked intact elements, whereas all others consisted of intact LTR-RTs. Within these clades, Gypsy elements typically contained 4–7 internal domains, with nine structurally complete elements identified (out of 19), while Copia elements contained 3–5 domains, and no structurally complete elements were detected (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14; Supplementary Data 2). Given that intact LTR-RTs are more likely to retain retrotransposition potential, we next examined genes located in proximity to these lineage-specific elements.

We identified genes within 3.5 kb flanking intact LTR-RT insertions in C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335 and analyzed their positional distribution relative to intact and lineage-specific LTR-RTs (Supplementary Fig. 15A, B). Excluding genes embedded within LTR-RT, 34 flanking genes were functionally annotated, of which nine were identified as pathogenicity-related, including homologs associated with leaf spot and fruit rot (Supplementary Data 3). Genome-wide enrichment analysis showed that pathogenicity-related genes are significantly overrepresented near LTR-RTs, accounting for 22.2% of genes in these regions compared with 10.1% genome-wide (false discovery rate (FDR) = 8.81 × 10−3). A similar enrichment was observed for genes associated with lineage-specific LTR-RTs (26.5%; FDR = 8.81 × 10−3). No significant difference was detected between lineage-specific and all LTR-RT-associated genes (FDR = 0.265), despite the latter including elements lacking RT domains or with unclear structure. This suggests that enrichment of pathogenicity-related genes is a general feature of LTR-RT-associated regions in C. cucumerinum (Supplementary Fig. 15C, D).

To assess the expression of genes associated with C. cucumerinum-specific LTR-RTs, we analyzed fungal transcriptomes from infected leaves at 3 and 7 dpi. Among the 34 genes examined, nine were differentially expressed, including three pathogenicity-related genes associated with smut, leaf spot, and animal infection (Fig. 4e). To capture potential early infection dynamics, a subset of pathogenicity-related genes was further analyzed across five time points (0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h). Two genes associated with fruit rot (3348_g) and leaf spot (8374_g) reached peak expression at 6 h and 3 h, respectively, before declining, while another pathogenicity-related gene (5806_g), annotated in animal infection, showed a transient ~40-fold induction between 6 and 12 h (Fig. 4f, g).

Together, these results indicate that lineage-specific LTR-RTs are not only expanded and evolutionarily dynamic in C. cucumerinum, but are also preferentially associated with pathogenicity-related genes and may be linked to their genomic distribution and transcriptional activity during infection. However, their functional impact remains to be further validated.

Changes in genes related to the pathogenicity of C. cucumerinum

To successfully infect plants, pathogens must overcome the physical barrier of the plant cell wall, which is composed of polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and lignin32. CAZymes hydrolyze these polysaccharides to access carbon sources33.

Across 24 Cladosporium genomes, total CAZyme gene numbers ranged from 630 to 820 per genome (mean 737.5; Supplementary Table 6). Glycoside hydrolases (GHs) were the most abundant family (mean 388 genes), followed by glycosyl transferases (GTs, 179.3), auxiliary activities (AAs, 81.5), carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs, 75.5), carbohydrate esterases (CEs, 44.8), and polysaccharide lyases (PLs, 21). C. cucumerinum genomes contained 700–707 CAZyme genes, with similar relative proportions among families (Fig. 5a).

Total CAZyme density in C. cucumerinum was significantly lower than in other Cladosporium species (Fig. 5b). Among CAZyme families, CBM and GH densities were markedly reduced, with complete separation from other species (FDR = 0.0066), while CE and AA were moderately lower (CE: FDR = 0.0094; AA: FDR = 0.0142), and GT and PL showed no significant difference (Supplementary Fig. 16). The CAZymes involved in plant cell wall degradation were present at relatively low levels in C. cucumerinum compared with other Cladosporium species (Fig. 5c). Substrate-based analysis revealed significantly lower CAZyme densities for (hemi)cellulose, pectin, and lignin in C. cucumerinum (Fig. 5d). The classification of plant cell wall degradation-related CAZyme families and their copy numbers across Cladosporium species are provided in Supplementary Data 4.

Despite this overall reduction, several CAZyme families remained present at moderate copy numbers in C. cucumerinum, including AA5, CE2, GH16, GH28, AA9, GH2, CE12, GH105, and PL3. These families vary significantly across the genus (SD > 0.5) but are not specifically expanded in C. cucumerinum (Supplementary Fig. 17, Supplementary Data 4). Instead, their copy numbers are generally comparable to the genus median, indicating that core plant cell wall-degrading functions are largely preserved despite the overall contraction of CAZyme repertoires.

Fungi produce secondary metabolites to adapt to diverse environments34. We conducted genomic analysis of enzymes involved in secondary metabolite synthesis, including non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS), fungal post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs), Type III polyketide synthases (T3PKS), type I polyketide synthases (T1PKS), beta-lactones, and terpene synthases. The heat map revealed that C. cucumerinum exhibited lower gene counts across secondary metabolite gene clusters compared to its closely related species, such as C. cladosporioides, C. oxysporum, and C. tenuissimum (Fig. 5e). This pattern may stem from broader host ranges or saprophytic lifestyles. Notably, C. cucumerinum harbors fewer genes for MFS transporters, ABC transporters, and cytochrome P450s than its closely related species. However, these numbers are not the lowest within the genus (Supplementary Fig. 18A). At the gene density level, C. cucumerinum showed a significant reduction in MFS transporters (FDR = 0.018). In contrast, ABC transporters and cytochrome P450 genes exhibited non-significant decreasing trends (Supplementary Fig. 18B). Overall, C. cucumerinum exhibits reduced gene content in secondary metabolite biosynthetic clusters and a selective contraction of transporter families (notably MFS), indicating gene repertoire reduction and functional convergence relative to closely related species.

Effector repertoire analysis indicated that C. cucumerinum tends to exhibit a higher effector density compared with other Cladosporium species (Fig. 5a, f). The mean effector density in C. cucumerinum (23.27 ± 0.45 per 1000 genes; n = 4) was modestly higher than that observed in other species (21.73 ± 1.86; n = 20), corresponding to a 6.6% relative increase. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0958). Effect size estimates (Cliff’s δ = 0.55; Cohen’s d = −0.887) and the 95% confidence interval of the mean difference (−2.487 to −0.659) suggest a potential shift in effector density, although the lack of statistical significance and small sample size limit the strength of this inference (Supplementary Table 7). Overall, these results are consistent with, but do not conclusively demonstrate, a relative enrichment of effector genes in C. cucumerinum within the genus.

An essential gene involved in pathogenesis

Gene family clustering identified 119 gene families shared exclusively by four C. cucumerinum strains, including 111 single-copy families (Supplementary Fig. 19). KEGG annotation indicated that there were 20 genes in the carbohydrate metabolism pathway and 15 genes in the amino acid metabolism pathway (Fig. 6a). Among carbohydrate metabolism-related genes, CcBGL258 was identified as a candidate virulence factor. Analysis using the Diverse Fungal Virulence Factors (DFVF) database revealed structural similarity between CcBGL258 and β-2-tomatinase from Septoria lycopersici.

CcBgl258 contains a signal peptide (SP) but lacks a transmembrane helix (Supplementary Fig. 20). A yeast signal sequence trap (YSST) assay was performed to validate the SP of CcBgl258 functionally. Furthermore, in the colorimetric assay, the SP of CcBgl258 transformants successfully reduced 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) to insoluble red colored 1,3,5-triphenylformazan (TPF) (Fig. 6b). These results indicated that the SP of CcBgl258 is functional. We got ΔCcbgl258 mutants, single-copy complemented mutants (Ccbgl258-c), and multi-copy complemented mutants (Ccbgl258-OE) by using the knockout technique (Supplementary Fig. 21). CcBgl258-GFP localized to vesicles and vacuoles, consistent with vesicle-mediated secretion (Fig. 6c).

No significant differences in colony morphology were observed between ΔCcbgl258 and the wild type on PDA (Fig. 6d). However, the ΔCcbgl258 lost pathogenicity on cucumber leaves, a phenotype reproducible in an independent knockout line (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 22), demonstrating that CcBGL258 is required for full pathogenicity.

Although functional annotation predicted β-glucosidase activity (GO:0008422), ΔCcbgl258 mutants showed no detectable defects in growth or morphology on various sole carbon sources (Fig. 6f), indicating that CcBGL258 is dispensable for vegetative growth under in vitro conditions. Notably, CcBGL258 expression was transiently induced at 3 h post-inoculation (hpi) and subsequently declined (Fig. 6g), indicating a potential role during early stages of host infection. Consistently, cucumber leaves infected by the ΔCcbgl258 mutant exhibited markedly reduced ROS accumulation compared to wild-type and complemented strains (Fig. 6h, i). Given the loss of pathogenicity, this reduction in ROS is likely a downstream consequence of reduced infection efficiency.

CcBGL258 represents a species-specific gene in C. cucumerinum

Phylogenetic analysis showed that CcBgl258 clustered with XP_069231973 from C. halotolerans (99% bootstrap support) but displayed substantial divergence (genetic distance ≈ 0.403). Notably, CcBgl258 (296 aa) is marked shorter than its homologs (∼800 aa), yet retains 7 of 8 annotated functional domains, supporting structural integrity despite truncation (Fig. 7a).

Multiple lines of genomic evidence confirm that this truncated gene structure is authentic rather than an assembly artifact. Illumina read mapping revealed uniform coverage (>200×) across the CcBGL258 locus and flanking regions, with no assembly gaps (Supplementary Fig. 23). In addition, the identical 1230 bp genomic sequence was independently observed in four C. cucumerinum genomes, excluding sequencing or assembly errors (Supplementary Fig. 24).

UniProt search identified B8NMR5 (Aspergillus flavus) as one of the closest homologs (52.44% sequence identity), annotated as a β-glucosidase (GO:0008422). Domain analysis indicated that CcBgl258 lacks the GH3 C-terminal domain and contains a truncated FnIII-like domain, which was also absent in the downstream genomic region (Fig. 7b; Supplementary Data 5). 3D protein structural predictions corroborated these findings: B8NMR5 and CcBgl258 shared key transglycosylase domain motifs (Supplementary Fig. 25), while B8NMR5 and XP_069231973 showed high structural congruence (Fig. 7c).

To assess evolutionary conservation and potential horizontal gene transfer, synteny analysis of CcBgl258 and its 10 flanking genes was performed. Upstream regions showed strong collinearity across species, indicating evolutionary constraint. In contrast, downstream regions displayed partial synteny limited to closely related Cladosporium species, with minimal conservation in C. colombiae (Fig. 7d). Notably, all orthologous loci encoded longer proteins than CcBgl258 in C. cucumerinum and formed a monophyletic clade with conserved β-glucosidase annotations (Supplementary Fig. 26). Together, these results indicate that CcBgl258 represents a lineage-specific truncated β-glucosidase, defining a distinct isoform that warrants further functional characterization.

Dual-transcriptome dynamics during C. cucumerinum infection of cucumber

Based on infection time-gradient sampling, we analyzed DEG dynamics and associated metabolic responses during infection by C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335, along with metabolic pathway response characteristics at distinct stages. Transcriptome quality metrics are shown in Supplementary Table 8. PCA indicated that the D7 (7 dpi) group diverged most from D0. In comparison, D3 (3 dpi) represented an intermediate state (Fig. 8a). Extensive transcriptional reprogramming was observed (Fig. 8b, c), with 753 DEGs identified between D7 and D3 (Fig. 8d and Supplementary Table 9). Together with the DEG heatmap (Fig. 8e), this indicates dynamic stage-dependent transcriptional changes.

Importantly, early infection (D3 vs D0) was characterized by pronounced metabolic reprogramming. Upregulated genes were significantly enriched in carbohydrate metabolism (e.g., starch and sucrose metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions), secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and lipid metabolism-related pathways, including fatty acid metabolism and peroxisome functions (Supplementary Fig. 27). In contrast, downregulated genes were primarily enriched in amino acid metabolism and nitrogen-related processes. These results suggest a rapid shift toward carbon utilization and metabolic activation during early host colonization.

Time-course trend analysis of DEGs identified three significantly clustered profiles (Fig. 8f). Profile 7 genes exhibited a continuous upregulation from D0 to D7 and were mainly enriched in carbohydrate metabolic processes (FDR < 0.05) and glycosyl bond hydrolysis-related functions, including hydrolase activity acting on glycosyl bonds and O-glycosyl compounds. In addition, oxidoreductase activity and transmembrane transport were also enriched, indicating progressively enhanced metabolic and transport activities during infection. Profile 6 genes showed an early upregulation at D3, followed by a relatively stable expression at D7. These genes were significantly enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, hydrolase activity, and carbohydrate-binding functions, with additional enrichment in extracellular region components, suggesting sustained involvement of extracellular enzymatic processes after initial induction. In contrast, Profile 0 genes displayed a continuous downregulation throughout infection and were primarily enriched in transmembrane transport, transporter activity, membrane components, and serine-type peptidase-related functions. This pattern reflects a progressive reduction in transport- and proteolysis-associated activities over the course of infection (Fig. 8g).

Further analysis of DEGs during cucumber leaf infection revealed distinct transcriptional dynamics. PCA demonstrated high inter-group divergence with strong intra-group reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. 28A). Substantial DEGs were detected in both D3 and D7 groups. At the same time, the D7 vs. D3 comparison showed fewer yet still considerable DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 28B–E). This suggests only a small fraction of genes change expression specifically at different infection stages, supported by the DEG heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 28F). Hierarchical clustering (H-cluster) analysis revealed functional partitioning: Cluster_4 genes were predominantly associated with core defense-related pathways, including plant MAPK signaling, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Conversely, Cluster_3 genes were mainly involved in fundamental physiological processes such as photosynthesis—antenna proteins, central carbon metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, and linoleic/α-linolenic acid metabolism—indicating their active suppression. Collectively, the coordinated activation of defense-related metabolism in Cluster_4 and suppression of photosynthetic processes in Cluster_3 suggest a reallocation of resources toward immune defense. Collectively, cucumber leaves showed limited transcriptional reprogramming after infection, indicating that host responses were largely established upon initial pathogen recognition rather than progressively altered across infection stages (Fig. 8h, i).

Discussion

Our comparative genomic analysis of Cladosporium species, incorporating five newly sequenced genomes, provides insight into host-associated genomic features and evolutionary history in C. cucumerinum. Integrating phylogenetic dating with genome-wide comparisons, we show that the radiation of C. cucumerinum strains postdates the diversification of their primary cucumber host lineage. Despite uncertainties in molecular clock calibration, the divergence of C. cucumerinum falls within an established cucurbit lineage rather than coinciding with host speciation, indicating a temporally compatible but non-causal relationship.

Genomic analyses further reveal a pattern of gene family contraction together with lineage-specific expansion of LTR-RTs, suggesting genome restructuring of C. cucumerinum. Although certain gene families are reduced, the overall gene content remains within the range observed across the genus, suggesting variation in gene repertoire composition rather than extreme genome reduction. While alternative explanations such as lineage-specific gene turnover, sampling effects, or genetic drift cannot be excluded35,36, the consistency of these patterns across functional categories suggests it may be a non-random restructuring of the gene repertoire, potentially reflecting shifts in ecological or host-associated interactions37,38.

LTR-RTs can contribute to genomic variation and influence gene expression in some systems17,39,40. Although less prevalent in fungi than in plants, LTR-RTs effectively invade fungal genomes, generating genomic variation and genetic diversity41. The expansion of LTR-RTs, particularly Gypsy elements, represents a notable genomic feature in C. cucumerinum. Pathogenicity-related genes were found to be enriched near LTR-RT insertions, suggesting a non-random genomic distribution. The current data do not establish a causal relationship between TE insertions and gene function or regulation. While some LTR-RT-associated genes show transcriptional changes during infection, it remains unclear whether these patterns are directly influenced by nearby TEs or reflect broader genomic context. Therefore, further functional validation is required to determine their biological relevance.

TE expansion is typically associated with an increase in genome size. Compared with its closely related species, C. tenuissimum and C. cladosporioides, the average genome size of C. cucumerinum is larger by 0.58 Mb and 0.05 Mb, respectively. The total intron length is comparable among the three species. However, the total coding sequence (CDS) length in C. cucumerinum is reduced, whereas TE content is increased. Given the similar average CDS length across species, this reduction is consistent with a decrease in gene number, which may partially offset TE-driven genome expansion (Supplementary Fig. 29 and Supplementary Table 10). Potential differences in telomeric or centromeric regions cannot be excluded.

Functionally, C. cucumerinum exhibits a streamlined yet selectively structured CAZyme repertoire. Although total gene numbers fall within the genus-wide range, CAZyme gene density is significantly lower than in other Cladosporium species. Reductions are most pronounced in GH and CBM families, as well as in enzymes targeting (hemi)cellulose, pectin, and lignin. Despite this reduction, several CAZyme families involved in plant cell wall degradation remain present at moderate copy numbers. The retention of enzymes targeting major plant cell wall polysaccharides may be consistent with, but not sufficient to explain, the composition of cucurbit cell walls. In Cucurbitaceae species, plant cell walls contain cellulose together with abundant pectic polysaccharides (e.g., homogalacturonan and rhamnogalacturonan) and hemicelluloses, forming the major structural and matrix components of the cell wall42–45. Consistent with this architecture, GH28, PL3, and GH105 participate in pectin backbone cleavage, whereas AA9 and GH16 contribute to oxidative or hydrolytic degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose. Although these families are not specifically expanded in C. cucumerinum, their maintenance suggests that key plant cell wall-degrading functions are preserved despite overall CAZyme contraction.

A similar pattern is observed in secondary metabolism-related genes, where expansion of some gene families occurs alongside overall gene number reduction. Together, these results suggest that gene family evolution has occurred in a functionally non-uniform manner, suggesting selective retention of gene sets potentially relevant to host colonization, rather than indiscriminate genome reduction. However, gene family contraction can also arise from lineage-specific gene turnover36 or stochastic processes, including genetic drift35, and functional studies will be required to determine whether these patterns are associated with host interaction.

Previous studies have identified key enzymes involved in secondary metabolism and secreted effectors as key determinants of host specificity46,47. Here, we identify CcBGL258 as a C. cucumerinum-specific gene that is essential for pathogenicity. Notably, it lacks the GH3 C-terminal domain, which is associated with catalytic efficiency48, and contains a truncated FnIII-like domain, usually implicated in substrate binding or cell wall association49. Secretome analysis indicates that CcBgl258 is secreted via the conventional pathway. Expression analysis further shows that CcBGL258 is transiently induced at an early stage of infection (3 hpi), suggesting a stage-specific role during host interaction. Although its precise biochemical function and substrate specificity remain unclear, these features support the hypothesis that CcBgl258 represents a structurally distinct β-glucosidase isoform with a specialized role in early infection and pathogenicity.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed dynamic transcriptional changes in C. cucumerinum during infection. Genes showing continuous or early upregulation were enriched in carbohydrate metabolic processes and glycosyl bond hydrolysis, whereas downregulated genes were associated with transmembrane transport and peptidase activity. These patterns suggest stage-dependent shifts in metabolic and transport-related functions. In the host, cucumber leaves exhibited coordinated transcriptional responses, including activation of defense-related pathways (e.g., MAPK signaling and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis) and suppression of photosynthesis-related processes. Such photosynthetic suppression after infection is consistent with previous reports that immune activation can be accompanied by reduced photosynthetic activity and enhanced chloroplast-associated ROS production50. However, whether similar regulatory mechanisms operate in cucumber remains unclear. These observations should be regarded as preliminary supporting evidence, and further functional studies are required to determine their biological relevance.

Overall, this study provides a comparative genomic framework for understanding genome evolution and host-associated features in C. cucumerinum. The integration of genomic and transcriptomic data highlights patterns of gene content variation, TE expansion, and transcriptional dynamics, while emphasizing the need for further functional validation.

Conclusion

In summary, comparative genomic analyses indicate that C. cucumerinum exhibits gene family contraction alongside lineage-specific expansion of LTR-RTs, consistent with genome restructuring and shifts in gene repertoire composition. Despite reductions in CAZyme and secondary metabolite gene families, overall gene content remains within the genus range, suggesting selective rather than extensive genome reduction. Functional analyses identify the species-specific gene CcBGL258 as important for pathogenicity, while transcriptomic profiling reveals dynamic, stage-dependent gene expression changes during infection and coordinated host responses. Together, these findings provide a genomic framework for understanding host-associated features in C. cucumerinum, although the extent to which these patterns contribute to host range or adaptation remains to be experimentally validated.

Methods

Strain materials, growth media, conditions, and morphology observation

Four strains—C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335, C. tenuissimum ACCC 36062, C. tenuissimum ACCC 37043, and C. cladosporioides ACCC 36060—were obtained from the Agricultural Culture Collection of China (ACCC). Cladosporium rectoides A179 was isolated from soil associated with the roots of Citrus reticulata Blanco in Zhejiang, China, and subsequently purified. The isolation method of fungi referred to Liang’s51. Routine cultivation was carried out on 2% potato-dextrose agar (PDA) for 14 days in the dark. Cucumis sativus L. (cucumber) cultivar Zhongnong-6 was grown under 16 h light/8 h dark, 25 °C conditions. The yeast YTK12 strain was grown on YPDA medium at 30°C in the dark.

Morphological observations of strains were made from colonies cultivated for 14 days under continuous darkness at 25 °C on 7 cm PDA medium. Conidial terminology follows Schubert et al. 52. For culture characteristics, mycelial agar discs (5 mm diameter) cut from fungal pathogen cultures were placed at the center of the PDA base plate and cultivated for 14 days at 25 °C in the dark. Photographs of mycelia and conidia were captured with a Zeiss Axio Imager A2 microscope, using the Nikon software NIS-elements D v. 4.50 (Carl Zeiss Inc., Germany).

Pathogenicity assay

Pathogenicity assays were performed on detached leaves from 2–3-week-old cucumber plants. For true leaves, the leaf surface was gently scraped with a sterile toothpick before inoculation; for cotyledons, this wounding step was omitted. The petiole of each leaf was wrapped with water-absorbent cotton to maintain moisture. Inoculation was performed by placing mycelial-agar discs (7 mm diameter), excised from the margin of actively growing fungal colonies on PDA, onto the leaf surface. The inoculated tissues were first incubated at 25 °C in darkness for 2 days, then transferred to a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 25 °C for an additional 10 days for true leaves or 5 days for cotyledons. For independent validation experiments using a second transformation background (Supplementary Fig. 22), pathogenicity assays were conducted on detached leaves from 1-week-old cucumber seedlings, following the same inoculation procedure. Each pathogenicity assay was performed in two independent experiments, each including three biological replicates (individual leaves). True leaves and cotyledons were analyzed separately. Statistical analysis was performed as described in the “Plots and statistical analysis” section.

Whole genome sequencing and assembly

High-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted from fungal mycelia using the QIAGEN Genomic-tip protocol (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). DNA quality and quantity were assessed prior to library preparation. For Oxford Nanopore sequencing, high-quality genomic DNA was size-selected to enrich long fragments, followed by DNA damage repair, end repair, dA-tailing, adapter ligation, and library purification using the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK110) and the Native Barcoding Expansion 96 kit (EXP-NBD196) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer and sequenced on the PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). In parallel, paired-end short-read libraries (150 bp) were prepared using the Hieff NGS® OnePot Pro DNA Library Prep Kit V4 (Cat. No. 12972, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. All sequencing was performed by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Raw Nanopore signal data were base-called using Guppy v3.2.653. Adapter sequences, low-quality reads, and reads shorter than 2 kb were removed prior to downstream analyses. The genome size and heterozygosity were estimated using GenomeScope v1.054 based on Illumina data; k-mer was set as 21. The Nanopore long reads were assembled by Canu v1.555 and then polished by Racon56. The Pilon v1.2357 was used to correct and improve the genome assembly with Illumina short reads. Illumina reads were mapped back to the assembled genome, and alignments were visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)58 for inspection of sequencing coverage at selected loci.

Genome annotation

GeneMark-ES59 predicted protein-coding genes of five new sequencing genomes. Then, the BRAKER v2.1.560 pipeline, which contains GeneMark-EP+61 and AUGUSTUS v3.17462–64, was used to train the gene model of all 21 genomes across Cladosporium downloads from NCBI65 (2022.7.11) with this protein evidence. Finally, all evidence was integrated to predict protein-coding genes by Maker v3.01.0366. We used BUSCO v5.1.267, with the fungi_odb10 dataset (www.orthodb.org), to evaluate the completeness of genomes and visualize the results. Prediction of noncoding RNA used tRNAscan-SE v1.3.168 and RNAmmer-1.269 to predict tRNA and rRNA, respectively. First, tRNAscan-SE v1.3.168 calls tRNAscan and EufindRNA to identify tRNA regions in the genomic sequence and then calls Cove for validation. Pathogenicity-related genes were annotated based on the Database of Known Fungal Virulence Factors (DFVF) database70.

The extensive de novo TE Annotator (EDTA) pipeline annotated transposable elements. Briefly, we constructed species-specific TE libraries for each species using EDTA v2.1.071 with the parameter “-sensitive 1”. LTR_Finder v1.172, LTRharvest v1.6.073, and LTR_retriever v2.9.074 identified intact LTR-RTs. We also used LTR_retriever v2.9.0 to estimate each intact LTR-RT insertion time with the parameter setting “-u 1e-9”75,76.

Functional annotation focused on CAZymes, secondary metabolite gene clusters, and predicted effectors. We used dbCAN277 to annotate CAZymes and antiSMASH v5.2.078 to annotate secondary metabolite gene clusters. Pfam domain annotated through eggNOG-mapper v2.179. CAZyme families were assigned to lignin, pectin, and (hemi)cellulose categories based on substrate annotations from dbCAN-seq and corresponding enzyme functional descriptions80. The resulting classification and copy number variation across Cladosporium species are summarized in Supplementary Data 4.

Candidate secreted proteins were identified using SignalP v6.081 and TMHMM v2.082 to detect signal peptides and transmembrane domains. Proteins containing a signal peptide and lacking transmembrane domains were retained as candidate secreted proteins. Effector prediction was then performed using fimep83, a Python-based deep learning framework that integrates outputs from multiple effector prediction tools. For fungal genomes, predictions incorporated EffectorP-3.084, deepredeff85, and WideEffHunter_1.086, and integrated results were used to define the final effector set.

Functional annotation of KEGG and GO terms was performed using eggNOG-mapper v2.18179 with default parameters. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using TBtools-II v2.45887 with default parameters. Statistical significance was assessed using the BH method for multiple testing correction, and terms with FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Comparisons of gene density between groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Multiple testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method was applied where appropriate. For analyses involving a single predefined comparison, adjustment was not performed, as no multiple hypothesis testing was conducted.

Phylogenetic analysis

Fungal DNA extraction follows Chi et al88. DNA amplification of ITS, ACT, and TEF1-α genes was performed using primer pairs ITS1/ITS489, EF1-728F/EF1-986R, and ACT-512F/ACT-783R90. All reference strains used for phylogenetic analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 11. The ITS, ACT, and TEF1-α sequences used in this study were aligned with those obtained from GenBank by MUSCLE v3.8.3191. Strain CBS 125988 of C. hillianum was used as an outgroup. The aligned DNA sequences were combined using PhyloSuite v1.2.392 after being trimmed and filtered using trimAl 1.4.rev2293. The partition homogeneity test in PAUP v4.0b1094 indicated that the three-locus lineages were congruent (p = 0.35). The length of the combined alignments was 1306 characteristics (557 bp for ITS, 520 bp for TEF1-α, and 229 bp for ACT). The phylogenetic analyses were performed using Bayesian inference (BI) and Maximum-Likelihood Approximation (IQ-Tree). For BI, the best evolutionary model for each partition was determined using PartitionFinder v2.1.195 and incorporated into the analyses by MrBayes v. 3.2.196 with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis. Bayesian MCMC was run for 5,000,000 generations, and trees were sampled every 1000 generations. The first 25% of the trees were discarded in the burn-in phase. Optimal model estimation and maximum-likelihood (ML) analysis were executed in IQ-Tree v1.6.1297. 5000 bootstrap replicates evaluated branch support. The visualization of the phylogenetic tree was accomplished using ITOL98. Multiple sequence alignments were visualized using N·ESPript99 to highlight conserved residues and domain features.

To construct a genome-wide phylogenetic tree, we selected Cercospora beticola strain 09–40 as the outgroup and determined gene families or orthologous groups by OrthoFinder v2.5.2100 with parameters “-M msa -T fasttree”. The protein sequences from 4853 single-copy gene families of each species were aligned and then concatenated into a supergene sequence. After being filtered by trimAl v1.4.rev2293 with parameter ‘-gt 0.6 -cons 60’, the supergene sequences were used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree by RAxML v8.2.12101. The divergence time of each node was estimated by MCMCTree, which is part of the PAML v4.9 package102. According to TIMETREE v531, two calibration nodes were used for divergence time estimation: the split of Cercospora and Cladosporium (113.3–148.6 MYA)11,103, and the divergence of C. cladosporioides and C. sphaerospermum (37 MYA)11. The maximum-likelihood phylogenetic species tree for the individual gene was inferred using IQ-Tree v1.6.1297.

To assess the robustness of divergence estimates, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative calibration strategies: (i) a reduced calibration set including only the Cercospora–Cladosporium node, and (ii) a broadened calibration range for the C. cladosporioides–C. sphaerospermum node (30–50 MYA).

Gene family evolution

Gene family expansion and contraction were analyzed using CAFÉ v5.0.0104. CAFÉ fits a stochastic birth–death model to gene family count data across a phylogenetic tree and computes a p value for each family, representing the likelihood of observing the observed size change under the null model. As CAFÉ does not implement multiple testing correction, significance was evaluated based on raw p values (p < 0.05), consistent with common practice in gene family evolution analyses105,106.

To further validate contracted families, proteins from these families in closely related species (C. cladosporioides ACCC 36060) were randomly sampled and searched against the C. cucumerinum genome using tblastn with thresholds of ≥ 40% identity, ≥ 60% query coverage, and alignment length ≥ 200 aa. The resulting matches are summarized in Supplementary Table 12.

Ks distribution of homologous gene pairs

Pairwise protein sequence alignment was performed using DIAMOND v2.0.9.147107 with an E-value cutoff of 1E-10. Ks values for homologous gene pairs were estimated using the ksd module implemented in wgd v1.1.2108. Gene pairs with Ks values between 0 and 10 were retained for downstream analyses. Intraspecies Ks values for syntenic gene pairs were obtained based on genome-wide collinearity analysis using MCScanX109. For visualization, Ks distributions were displayed as histograms (binwidth = 0.2) with overlaid kernel density curves (bandwidth adjusted to 2) for non-syntenic comparisons. In contrast, count-based histograms were used for syntenic gene pairs due to limited sample size.

LTR retrotransposon analysis

TEsorter v1.4.6110 was used to identify and extract the RT domains of each intact LTR-RT to analyze the evolution of LTR-RTs. The RT sequences were aligned by MAFFT v7.475111 with default parameters—the phylogenetic tree construction of Copia and Gypsy elements by IQ-Tree v1.6.1297.

A 3.5 kb window was used to define proximity between LTR-RTs and genes, as previous studies have shown that LTR retrotransposons located within several kilobases of genes may influence their transcription through regulatory or insertional effects17,112. To evaluate whether pathogenicity-related genes are preferentially associated with LTR-RTs, genes were classified into three groups: all genes, genes within 3.5 kb of any LTR-RT, and genes within 3.5 kb of species-specific LTR-RTs. Enrichment was assessed using one-tailed hypergeometric tests across three comparisons (species-specific LTR vs. all LTR, species-specific LTR vs. all genes, and all LTR vs. all genes), with fold enrichment calculated as observed/expected. Exact binomial tests were applied as complementary analyses for small sample sizes. P values were adjusted using the BH method, and results with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant.

Synteny analysis

To determine the synteny between C. cucumerinum and other Cladosporium fungi, we used the “blastp” command of BLAST v2.9.0113 to align all amino acid sequences with an e-value cutoff of 1E-10. Then, the annotation information file and aligned file were imported into MCScanX109 for evolutionary analysis of synteny and collinearity. Data for local synteny analysis were derived from gene family clustering results. JCVI v1.1.12114 visualized the results.

Gene knockout and complementation

To construct the CcBGL258 knockout vector, we referred to a high-throughput gene knockout system115 based on homologous recombination. The 1.1–1.3 kb upstream fragment (UF) and the 1.1–1.3 kb downstream fragment (DF) of CcBGL258 were amplified by PCR from genomic DNA of C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335 with the primers CcBGL258-up(F)/ CcBGL258-up(R) and CcBGL258-down(F)/CcBGL258-down(R) (Supplementary Table 13). The pKO1B vector was digested with XbaI and HindIII. Geneticin-resistant gene(G418) was inserted into the HindIII/XbaI sites of pKO1B with UF and DF. The constructed knockout vector transferred G418 into the wild-type strain C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335 to replace the target gene by the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (ATMT) strategy116. Positive transformants were screened using 200 μg/mL G418 (Sangon Biotech, A600958-0005), and the mutants were further validated with long fragment (LF) and short fragment (SF) using the primer pairs CcBGL258-Long(F)/G418-Long(R) and CcBGL258-short(F)/CcBGL258-short(R), respectively.

For the complementary strain, the full-length CcBGL258 was cloned from the genomic DNA of C. cucumerinum ACCC 39335, then fused with the pKD5-GFP vector117, and introduced into mutants using the ATMT method. To verify the copy number of the inserted target gene in knockout and complementary mutants, quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ kit (Takara, Japan), with the TUBULIN gene as a control. The Ct values were measured using a Mastercycler ep Realplex instrument (Eppendorf, Germany), and relative gene copy number was estimated based on 2−ΔCt values. These complementary mutants were also used for subcellular localization analysis, which was conducted using an LSM880 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss Inc., Germany). All primers are listed in Supplementary Table 13. Two independent transformants were analyzed for pathogenicity assays, and consistent results were observed between transformants.

Quantitative real-time PCR

To examine the expression dynamics of target genes, mycelial‑agar discs were inoculated onto cucumber leaves (or cotyledons) as described in the pathogenicity assay section. Inoculated tissues were collected at 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpi. Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China) and reverse‑transcribed to cDNA with the ABScript Neo RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover (ABclonal, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was then performed using the BrightCycle Blue Universal SYBR Green qPCR Mix with UDG (ABclonal, China). Each assay was performed with four technical replicates, and the experiment was independently repeated three times with similar results. Gene expression levels were normalized against the reference gene (TUBULIN) and presented as 2−ΔCt values. Statistical analysis was performed as described in the “Plots and statistical analysis” section. All primers were designed by Integrated DNA Technologies (https://sg.idtdna.com/pages) and are listed in Supplementary Table 13.

Functional validation of signal peptides

Functional validation of the CcBGL258 SP was performed using the yeast secretion system as described previously118–120. The pSUC2 vector contains the invertase gene but lacks the methionine codon and SP. First, the SP of CcBGL258 was amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 13. After introducing the target SP into the pSUC2 vector, the construct was transformed into the YTK12 yeast strain. Transformants were first selected on CMD-W medium (Coolaber, Beijing, China), and then positive colonies were transferred to YPRAA plates (Coolaber, Beijing, China) containing raffinose but lacking glucose. Meanwhile, a colorimetric assay was used to further verify the function of the SP. A 1 mL solution of 10% sucrose and a 1 mL solution of 1% TTC (Coolaber, Beijing, China) were added to the yeast culture. After mixing, the culture was incubated in a 35 °C water bath for 10 min, and color changes were observed. Functional signal peptides enable the yeast strain to secrete the invertase enzyme and reduce TTC to the insoluble red-colored TPF.

Detection of ROS in cucumber leaves

Leaf staining and decolorization were performed using a Plant Hydrogen Peroxide Staining Liquid (DAB) kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ROS accumulation was quantified based on the proportion of stained area relative to the total leaf area. Each treatment was independently repeated twice with similar results. Statistical analysis was performed as described in the “Plots and statistical analysis” section.

Prediction of protein structure

Protein structural domain visualization was performed using TBtools-II v2.45887 and IBS v2.0121 based on annotation results from the InterProScan database. For three-dimensional structure prediction, AlphaFold3 was employed, with subsequent structural visualization and comparative analysis conducted in PyMOL v2.5.7 (Schrödinger, LLC, 2017).

Transcriptome analysis

The inoculated leaves were kept under 16 h light/8 h dark, 25 °C conditions. Samples, including day-0 fungal cultures and leaf tissues separately, plus day-3 and day-7 pathogen-plant co-cultures, were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for −80°C storage. Total RNA was extracted from fungal cultures and infected cucumber leaf tissues using the RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (DP441; Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Briefly, mRNA was enriched from total RNA using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented prior to cDNA synthesis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Fast RNA-seq Lib Prep Kit V2 (Cat. No. RK20306, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), including end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, size selection, PCR amplification, and purification. Library quality was assessed using a Qubit fluorometer, real-time quantitative PCR, and a bioanalyzer. Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform to generate 150-bp paired-end (PE150) reads. Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp v0.23.2122 to remove adaptor sequences, poly-N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. Quality metrics, including Q20, Q30, and GC content, were calculated for the resulting clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1123. Gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts v2.0.6124 and normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Principal component analysis was performed on the FPKM matrix to assess transcriptomic variation among samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 v1.42.0125 with a negative binomial model. Genes with FDR ≤ 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed. The union of DEGs was subjected to STEM analysis to identify significant temporal expression patterns. Clustering was performed using the STEM126 clustering method with a maximum of 50 model profiles and a maximum unit change of 1 between time points. Gene expression data were log-normalized before analysis. Model significance was determined using permutation testing (50 permutations per gene), followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (adjusted p < 0.05). Parallelly, log2(FPKM + 1) transformed expression data of unioned DEGs underwent mean-centering before hierarchical clustering (H-cluster).

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1) unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless indicated otherwise. For comparisons involving more than two groups, statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test. For pairwise comparisons, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. Multiple testing correction was applied where appropriate using the BH method to control the FDR, with FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For single predefined comparisons, unadjusted p values are reported. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d and Cliff’s delta) were calculated in R using the effsize package based on pairwise group comparisons. Cliff’s delta was estimated using the cliff.delta function with 95% confidence intervals, and effect size magnitudes were interpreted according to standard thresholds. Data visualization was performed using ggplot2 (v3.2.1) and GraphPad Prism (v9.5.0).

Pathogenicity assays were conducted in two independent experiments, each including three biological replicates (individual leaves) per treatment. qRT-PCR analyses were performed using pooled samples derived from three biological replicates, with four technical replicates per assay. ROS quantification assays included three independent biological replicates per treatment. Transcriptome analyses were based on three independently collected biological samples at each time point.
