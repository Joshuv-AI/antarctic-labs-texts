Inquiry and innovation: Considering curiosity and creativity in autism
Authors: Rhiannon Luyster, Kyle Greene-Pendelton, Sudha Arunachalam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Curiosity and creativity are faculties that support innovation, achievement and emotional well-being. They are early-appearing and lifelong, serving a critical role in human development. In this paper, we suggest that these essential capabilities have been broadly overlooked in our understanding and description of development in autistic individuals. We critically evaluate two assumptions: that curiosity and creativity are (1) diminished and/or (2) different in autism. In support of future research, we provide a number of suggestions for how we, and other important stakeholders like educators, parents, and providers, can deepen our appreciation for the ways in which curiosity and creativity might be expressed in autism. We also aim to highlight the ways in which curiosity and creativity can support personal growth and flourishing across the lifespan in autistic individuals, just as they do for nonautistic individuals.

“I have no special talent. I am only passionately curious.”

“Talent is a pursued interest.”

Introduction

The above quotes – one from a renowned physicist and the other from a celebrated artist and public television host – showcase how experts known for their discoveries and generativity perceive the importance of personal interest. In other words, they underscore the overlapping roles of creativity and curiosity. What are these concepts of curiosity and creativity? Varied definitions have been presented over decades of research on these topics, as will be reviewed below. For the moment, it is useful to use a set of working definitions from Koutstaal et al. (2022), who define curiosity as “a desire to know, typified by such traits and activities as inquisitiveness, the exploration of novelty, and questioning” and creativity as “an exploratory endeavor involving the generation of original, useful, and valuable ideas, processes, or products” (p. 1).

While curiosity and creativity are conventionally understood to be related, curiosity is generally thought to be the earlier developmental achievement of the two, and as such it has been a long-standing topic of developmental scholars (e.g., Kagan, 1972; Piaget, 1969). Indeed Twomey and Westermann (2018) characterize infants as “curious learners who drive their own cognitive development by imposing structure on their learning environment as they explore.” In this way, curiosity is a powerful engine for the development of other related domains, including creativity. We wholeheartedly agree with Twomey and Westermann’s characterization of infants, and in fact, we would broaden this characterization to include (at least potentially) all people at all ages. Unfortunately, despite the theoretical generality of curiosity and creativity, they have primarily been considered in the context of “typical development,” with relatively little discussion of their application to individuals with developmental delays, disorders or disabilities, including autism. This has been noted as a shortcoming in the field (Jirout et al., 2024; Johnson, 2020), and it is, therefore, the focus of this paper. We discuss issues related to both how curiosity and creativity manifest in autistic individuals – that is, how autistic characteristics may result in differences in how these abilities are expressed – as well as how these constructs are commonly measured – that is, how standard tasks may underestimate or mischaracterize abilities in autistic individuals.

Our interest in curiosity and creativity is multifaceted and influenced by our life experiences, training and scholarship. The research team brings together expertise in autism, education, human development, and language, and includes neurodivergent, non-neurodivergent, and familial perspectives. Our own program of scholarship intersects with these larger constructs (via our research in domains such as teaching, learning, and inquiry). Here, we focus our attention on curiosity and creativity more directly, with a particular interest in how these phenomena might inform our understanding of autism, and how, in turn, our understanding of autism might shape our understanding of curiosity and creativity. We aim also to highlight how these broad developmental strengths might manifest in autism and ultimately support the flourishing of autistic individuals.

Curiosity and creativity: A brief review

Curiosity

Although this paper relies on Koutstaal et al.’s (2022) definition (shared above), a precise and universally accepted definition of curiosity has eluded researchers (e.g., Hidi & Renninger, 2019). A key component of curiosity is seeking information in response to a gap in one’s knowledge; however, the information seeking should be for its own sake, arising from an internal desire for information, rather than to fulfill some need or obtain a specific reward (e.g., Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020; Jirout et al., 2024). Nevertheless, curiosity does involve reward circuitry in the brain, as obtaining knowledge about the object or topic of curiosity is intrinsically rewarding (Litman, 2005).

Curiosity can be expressed (and therefore measured by researchers) in a variety of ways, including asking questions (though not all questions are curious, see Jirout et al., 2024 for a discussion), visual and manual exploration, and causal intervention (i.e., a goal-directed action to effect an outcome) – all of which provide insight into how individuals engage with their environments to pursue information (e.g., Liquin & Lombrozo, 2020). Curiosity (as a trait) is most commonly measured by self-report or, in young children, parent report (e.g., Altmann et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023; Piotrowski et al., 2014). Curiosity scales have been validated for both adults and children (e.g., Kashdan et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2023; Piotrowski et al., 2014). For example, with infants, parents are asked to rate behaviors such as interest in or inspection of a variety of objects, or engagement with others as a means to obtain information or solve problems (Altmann et al., 2025); similar tendencies apply across the lifespan (e.g., Kashdan et al., 2018). Implicit measures such as neuroimaging and eye-tracking have also been employed to understand neural correlates of curiosity (e.g., Gross et al., 2020).

With young children, parent-child interactions are understood to play a role in children’s expression of curiosity (e.g., Endsley et al., 1979; Lee et al., 2023; Medina & Sobel, 2020). While a preverbal infant may not ask questions, early behaviors such as vocalizing and pointing can serve the same inquisitive function (see, e.g., Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020 for review), and the parent must understand the child’s bid for information and help to satisfy that curiosity. Infants who display these curiosity-associated behaviors are well-poised to learn from their caregiver, and may thus be at a developmental advantage (Lehwald, 1991).

Curiosity has been widely identified by both researchers and educators as critical for the academic success of children and adolescents, as well as the career success of adults (e.g., Jirout et al., 2024; Nesbitt et al., 2025). Research has demonstrated benefits not only in cognitive strengths like learning and memory but also social-emotional skills and general well-being (see, e.g., Chen et al., 2025 and Jirout et al., 2024 for discussions). For example, curiosity has been found to correlate positively with self-esteem (Vaisarova & Lucca, 2024). A recent review by Jirout et al. (2024) indicated that curiosity promotes better learning (regardless of type and dosage of exploration), and it can also lead to greater attention, motivation, enjoyment and retention. Educators report seeing curiosity as a way to build relationships and empathy, and they also highlight the role of curiosity in “wonder”1 – as in “I wonder” – in supporting students’ abilities to see things from another person’s point of view or to ponder abstract and/or complex questions (Menning, 2018; see also Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020).

Creativity

Creativity, too, is a multifaceted construct that has been difficult to define. Thirty years ago, Treffinger (1996) reported that over 100 definitions had already been proposed. A recent review of the literature (Said-Metwaly et al., 2017) described four approaches that have been taken (over decades of research) to the conceptualization and definition of creativity: cognitive processes, individual characteristics, innovative products, and the dynamic between a person and his/her contextual demands. The former approach – focusing on the cognitive processes associated with creativity – is the most common in the empirical literature (Said-Metwaly et al., 2017) and can be defined as “perceiving problems or gaps in knowledge, developing hypotheses or propositions, testing and validating hypotheses and finally sharing the results” (Said-Metwaly et al., 2017, p. 241). The process approach identifies a role for both divergent thinking (generating multiple possible solutions to a problem) and convergent thinking (using logic and analysis to produce one well-defined, optimal solution) (Guilford, 1950). Thus, according to common convention, creativity encompasses both processes and outcomes of those processes (e.g., Evans et al., 2021; Evans & Jirout, 2023).

Given the aforementioned roles of the individual and the environment, creativity may take different forms across developmental and cultural contexts (e.g., Beghetto & Kaufman, 2017). With young children, creativity may be most easily observed in object play, imaginative play, and tool innovation (Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020). Resnick (2007) echoes this view, advocating for learning environments modeled on the exploratory, iterative, and expressive ethos of early education where creativity is scaffolded through hands-on, open-ended engagement. These fundamental human drives for play may scaffold creative products; for example, Howard-Jones et al. (2002) found that children who played with salt dough for 25 min later produced more creative tissue-paper collages than children who spent 25 min copying text.

Creativity can be assessed in a variety of ways, but most research evaluates the creative process (Said-Metwaly et al., 2017) using various divergent and convergent thinking tasks. Measures of divergent thinking can include activities requiring participants to list objects that share a common feature or category (e.g., things with wheels) or generate possible uses for an object (“alternate uses tasks”). There are a variety of assessments that vary in their breadth of coverage of divergent thinking, both in the number of activities included and whether they include tasks mediated by verbal and/or nonverbal skills (for instance, the Wallach-Kogen Creativity Tests [Wallach & Kogan, 1965]; the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking [Torrance, 1966]; the Structure of the Intellect Divergent Production Tests [Guilford, 1967]; and the Creativity Assessment Packet [Williams, 1980]). Convergent thinking has received much less attention in the measurement literature (Rawlings et al., 2025), but a number of studies use the “Remote Associates Test” (Mednick, 1962); this is a task in which participants are offered three words and have to come up with a fourth, related word. Other approaches can be found, too, including activities that merge the measurement of divergent and convergent thinking. For example, Evans and Jirout (2023) asked children to generate multiple questions about a mysterious circumstance – eliciting divergent thinking – and then to use what they learned to solve the mystery – eliciting convergent thinking. In a similar study, Rawlings et al. (2025) asked adults to complete standard measures of both divergent and convergent thinking and found that these measures were strongly, positively associated. Some studies measuring creativity focus more on related constructs like exploration of objects, materials or environments as proxies for creativity (see Evans et al., 2021).

As with curiosity, there are important implications of creativity for the well-being of individuals; importantly, a recent meta-analysis concluded that there is a significant positive relationship between creativity and well-being (Acar et al., 2020). For instance, well-being in adults is supported by everyday creative activity (Conner et al., 2018), creative growth (Tang et al., 2021), as well as self-reported creative motivation (Morse et al., 2021). College students and older adults who were primed using a creative task reported higher levels of subjective well-being than individuals primed using a non-creative task (Tan et al., 2021). These relations are reciprocal – positive moods result in more creativity (e.g., Hirt et al., 2008). Moreover, creativity has consequences not only for the individual but also for broader communities – the workplace, for example (e.g., Lucas, 2016; Mumford & Todd, 2019). A review by Lua et al. (2024) reported a variety of benefits for employee creativity at the individual level (e.g., job satisfaction, work engagement, positive work relationships), as well as at the team and organizational level (e.g., performance).

The relationship between curiosity and creativity

Curiosity and creativity may be driven by some of the same underlying mechanisms. For example, the pursuit of novelty, which invokes neural functions such as memory, cognitive control, attention, and reward-seeking (Ivancovsky et al., 2024), prompts exploration (e.g., Evans et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023). In early childhood, everything is novel, and therefore, not surprisingly, this period is characterized by tremendous curiosity. Moreover, executive function skills, which help to shape and narrow attentional focus, are limited early in development (e.g., Best & Miller, 2010; Chevalier, 2015), and therefore a variety of stimuli attract young children’s attention and prompt exploration (Vaisarova & Lucca, 2024). At the same time, because of immature executive function, children may not evaluate their ideas deeply, resulting in “more expansive but less refined creativity” (Vaisarova & Lucca, 2024, e116). As humans develop, decision-making plays an ever-stronger role in evaluating ideas and pitting them against each other (Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020), which results in the ability to evaluate creative products more critically. Understanding their own learning and exploration may require that children have more sophisticated metacognitive skills or understanding of mental states (e.g., Sobel & Letourneau, 2018). That is: curiosity requires identifying a gap in one’s knowledge (and being motivated to fill that gap). Metacognition may support (1) the ability to recognize one’s lack of information and (2) the realization that exploration could be helpful in locating that information (e.g., Goupil & Proust, 2023). Curiosity may also drive creativity; for instance, the desire to solve a puzzle supports idea generation (Hagtvedt et al., 2019).

A recent meta-analysis by Schutte and Malouff (2020) confirms that within an individual, curiosity and creativity are associated, at least in older children and adults. However, much of the work in this area has been criticized for relying on self-report (Gross et al., 2020; Koutstaal et al., 2022). A smaller number of studies have investigated this association using direct observation, finding some evidence that curiosity and creativity are indeed correlated in adults (Koutstaal et al., 2022), but they may not be in children (Evans & Jirout, 2023; Vaisarova & Lucca, 2024). It is likely that there are complex relations between curiosity and creativity, but these links have not yet been well articulated (e.g., Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020).

There is some debate in the literature about whether curiosity and creativity are best characterized as states – that is, momentary reactions to internal or external stimuli – or traits – that is, aspects of personality that show stability over time (e.g., Gross et al., 2020). While they tend to be treated as traits in the literature, particularly in the context of individual differences (and potentially, group differences related to neurotype), it is also widely acknowledged that both curiosity and creativity can be viewed as fluctuating states influenced by internal and external factors (e.g., Ma & Wei, 2023). Much of the literature, particularly that in organizational psychology, has therefore focused on the conditions under which curiosity and creativity can be enhanced.

Curiosity and creativity in autism

We consider it especially important to understand these constructs in autism for many reasons. First, we assume that autistic individuals (like all humans) experience curiosity and creativity, and numerous first-person accounts from autistic individuals are consistent with this assumption (e.g., Higashida, 2013; Kotowicz, 2022; Price, 2022). Both qualitative research (e.g., Lilley et al., 2022; Russell et al., 2019) and quantitative research (e.g., Cancer et al., 2024; de Schipper et al., 2016) confirm the varied experiences of curiosity and creativity in autistic individuals. Higashida (2013) writes in an oft-quoted passage: “I’m always hungry to learn about lots of different things. It really gets me down that people just don’t understand how hungry for knowledge people with autism actually are” (p. 47). These accounts also highlight that curiosity and creativity may manifest in different ways in autistic individuals than in nonautistic individuals; for example, Harrison (2023) writes: “I think curiosity looks very different in an autistic than in other people…. [I’ve] long suspected that I tend to prefer a deeper dive into knowledge than most people do”. Autistic blogger Aoife (Aoife, n.d.) comments, “Throughout my entire life I have been driven by my desire to acquire knowledge. The words who, what, when, where, why and how are rarely ever far from my lips. For me, it’s more than just a desire, it’s a need….If I don’t get the answers I crave, it drives me insane, getting under my skin like an itch I can’t scratch….You could say that I’m addicted to learning!” Importantly, Johnson (2020) suggests that the established methods (e.g., questionnaires, behavioral tasks) used to characterize and test curiosity and creativity, which were developed by and for nonautistic minds, may not serve us in understanding the experience of curiosity and/or creativity in autistic minds.

Second, it is essential to consider how best to support well-being and flourishing in autistic people. Curiosity and creativity are often included as part of a profile of flourishing (Hilton et al., 2019), and they have a number of benefits for learning and well-being, as reviewed earlier. There is good reason to believe that autistic individuals would experience the same advantages associated with curiosity and creativity. For instance, O’Neill (2023), an autistic teacher, identifies both curiosity and creativity as two “character strengths” essential to strengths-based support and education for autistic individuals. Given the common co-occurrence of cognitive delays or intellectual disability in autistic individuals (Maenner et al., 2021), it is worth noting that associations between creativity and well-being hold for individuals with intellectual disabilities, suggesting that it may be feasible to support curiosity and creativity in ways that are accessible to individuals with a range of cognitive profiles (e.g., Şahin, 2022; Vanutelli et al., 2022).

In our reading of the literature examining (either directly or indirectly) the intersection of curiosity, creativity and autism, two distinct assumptions commonly emerge: that these drives are diminished in autistic individuals (relative to nonautistic individuals), and that these drives manifest differently in autistic individuals (relative to nonautistic individuals). Each of these positions will be addressed below, where we will share our summary and appraisal of each assumption.

Are curiosity and creativity diminished in autism?

Assumptions about reduced curiosity and creativity in autism can be found in some of the earliest characterizations of autism, which mention diminished curiosity and exploratory behavior, as well as reduced creative play, beginning from infancy (Kolvin, 1972). In the broadest sense, autism is commonly associated with a variety of developmental challenges and differences in areas thought to be related to curiosity and creativity. For instance, individuals with autism often have co-occurring developmental delays (DD) or intellectual disability (ID); these differences may or may not hinder curiosity and creativity (the literature seems to be mixed on this point; see Jirout et al., 2024; Şahin, 2022; Vanutelli et al., 2022). Nevertheless, autistic individuals who do not have DD or ID may still have difficulties in higher order skills like executive function and metacognition, perhaps related to language difficulties (e.g., Friedman & Sterling, 2019), both thought to shape curiosity and creativity (Jirout et al., 2024). For example, Turner (1999) suggests that difficulty inhibiting inappropriate responses can decrease performance on tasks measuring the ability to generate creative responses. Standard creativity assessments often require the participant to think quickly and flexibly in the context of a social interaction with an examiner; this scenario might disadvantage autistic individuals who find social interactions particularly stressful or overwhelming. Additionally, creativity measures may quantify fluency, flexibility, and novelty across generalized (vs. specific) domains (Runco & Acar, 2012), and this approach might be misaligned with the ways creativity is best expressed by autistic thinkers (Hetzroni et al., 2019). In their study, Hetzroni et al. (2019) found that while autistic children showed variable performance in figural creativity tasks, they often outperformed their neurotypical peers in mathematical creativity tasks. This pattern of discrepant performance highlights the importance of considering domain-specific strengths and the risk of underrepresenting autistic creativity when relying solely on conventional assessment methods.

Moreover, patterns of behavior that may be particularly “autistic” according to the diagnostic criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) – including rigid or fixed thinking, inflexibility, or difficulty with new situations or experiences – have been suggested as impediments to creativity (Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020). Importantly, autistic people are more likely to display curiosity when they are physically well (e.g., being well-rested, having sufficient physical activity) (e.g., Kong et al., 2023), and unfortunately, autistic people often struggle to receive adequate sleep, nutrition and exercise (e.g., Yano & Hosokawa, 2023). In sum, then, there are many skills/areas/domains (whether ones that are behaviorally central to autism or ones that are co-occurring developmental challenges) that may introduce vulnerability for autistic individuals in the development and/or expression of curiosity and creativity.

A proposal that curiosity and creativity might be diminished in autism must also be carefully appraised using a consideration of how we (as observers) are understanding nonverbal behavior. To start, there is considerable overlap between earliest nonverbal signs of curiosity and earliest nonverbal features of autism. Jirout et al. (2024) suggests that some of the first expressions of curiosity are shown through infants’ use of gaze and gestures to elicit information from adults, and infants’ strategic use of gaze to attend (or not attend) to information they find interesting or novel; the authors further highlight the role of pointing as an expression of curiosity. Interestingly, these domains – gaze (including the phenomenon of “sticky attention”, Hughes & Russell, 1993), gesture and pointing – are all common areas of early delay or difficulty in autism (e.g., Tager-Flusberg & Caronna, 2007). We would argue that reduced use of these early nonverbal behaviors does not necessarily mean that autistic infants and toddlers are not curious, but it might easily (and unfortunately) be misconstrued as such. Similarly, play is a common context where we often look for evidence of curiosity and creativity (Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020) in young children. For instance, creativity is commonly thought to be observed in object play, imaginative play and tool innovation (Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020). Because symbolic play is often reduced in autism (e.g., Jarrold et al., 1993, 1996; Thiemann-Bourque et al., 2019; Wetherby & Prutting, 1984), and symbolic play is often conflated with creativity, children with autism may score poorly on play-based metrics of creativity (e.g., Hobson et al., 2013). Moreover, some older research explicitly posits that play differences (which are generally construed as deficits) in autism are related to a lack of curiosity (Kolvin, 1972; Libby et al., 1998).

The behaviors described above are primarily nonverbal, and it is worth also considering how language use in autistic individuals might shape our perception of expressed curiosity and creativity. As suggested by Vaisarova and Lucca (2024), both curiosity and creativity can be expressed in diverse ways, but in practice their expression is often mediated by language; as an example, curiosity is often conceptualized as question asking (at least in verbal individuals; research in infants/toddlers may consider nonverbal modes of expression, e.g., Altmann et al., 2025) and creativity is often captured in the articulation of ideas. The findings on links between curiosity, creativity, and language in nonautistic individuals are mixed. Vaisarova and Lucca (2024) found that neither curiosity nor creativity was significantly associated with receptive language, although expressive language does appear to be associated with creativity (e.g., Lin & Shih, 2016; Vaisarova & Carlson, 2021). Autism is commonly associated with language delays in childhood and/or with lifelong language impairment, so it is essential to consider the role of any co-occurring language delays and impairments in autism. We can find a particularly salient example in question-asking, which is a foremost avenue for expressing curiosity (with the caveat that not all questions signal curiosity). Interestingly, early research (e.g., Kanner, 1943; Rutter, 1971) suggested that question-asking in autism was excessive and “fixated” on personal interests, rather than an expression of curiosity per se. More recent research suggests that children with autism do ask varied questions, including those expressing curiosity (e.g., “why” questions), but findings differ as to whether they ask fewer questions than their language-matched nonautistic peers (Fleury & Ford, 2021; Jones & Schwartz, 2009; Luyster et al., 2023).

Are curiosity and creativity expressed differently in autism?

While some observers might interpret the behaviors of autistic individuals as signaling reduced curiosity and creativity, others might perceive the evidence in a different light. In doing so, it is essential to consider whether curiosity and creativity might be expressed in ways that have not been adequately recognized (at least by some stakeholders, including many researchers, educators and clinicians) in autism, and relatedly whether behaviors that have been construed as not curious or creative might have been misunderstood. To the extent that differences in brain connectivity between autistic and nonautistic individuals reflect different ways of experiencing the world, some researchers highlight this as a sign of originality–a component of, or perhaps prerequisite for, creativity (Pennisi et al., 2021). In other words, to the extent that brain structure and function is heterogeneous across individuals on and off the autism spectrum, the expression of creativity might also be heterogenous.

We suggest that a particularly fruitful approach (theoretically and practically) is to explore the second domain of diagnostic features of autism: “restricted, repetitive behaviors” (APA, 2013). Behaviors falling into this domain include: (1) routinized or unusual movements, speech or object use (e.g., using an object in ways “other than” an intended use, like lining them up or flipping them); (2) adherence to specific routines and/or rituals; (3) extremely strong or unconventional interests; and (4) under- or over-reactivity to sensory experiences, whether in terms of pleasure or aversion (APA, 2013). Using the literature on nonautistic curiosity and creativity, there is reason to believe that at least some of the behaviors referenced in the repetitive behavior domain of the diagnostic criteria might otherwise be perceived as expressing curiosity and creativity. For instance, a new parent questionnaire focusing on curiosity in nonautistic infants and toddlers identified one dimension capturing “sensory curiosity” (measured through sensory behaviors) and a second capturing “investigative curiosity” (measured through object use) (Altmann et al., 2025). Similarly, Bazhydai and Westermann (2020) claimed that object exploration, or using objects in “new” or “different” ways, can be a marker of creativity. Moreover, on the basis of the developmental literature, there is good reason to consider a possible link between interests and curiosity. Renninger (1992) found that when preschoolers play with a toy they have previously shown interest in, they use a greater variety of gestures to learn about the object and they play with it for longer (than with a toy in which they have not previously shown interest). Other work suggests that when elementary schoolers read about something they have an interest in, they learn more and remember more for longer (Engel, 2011). Findings like these lead scholars to observe that “interest often goes hand in hand with curiosity” (Engel, 2011, p. 627).

Importantly, because these behaviors are formalized as distinctive features of autism, they are (perhaps by definition) commonly pathologized when they occur in an autistic person. And indeed, tendencies that might fall under this category of behavior can often introduce practical, physical, social and emotional challenges for autistic individuals and their families (e.g., Shiloh et al., 2023; Turner-Brown & Frisch, 2020). However, we suggest that this cluster of behaviors is not a monolith, and that it is valuable for us as researchers, providers, educators and family members to consider the possibility that behaviors classically “dismissed” as restricted, repetitive behaviors might in fact express curiosity and/or creativity, and might therefore be (at least sometimes) an avenue to positive outcomes in skill development, community engagement and life satisfaction. To be clear, we are not implying that restricted, repetitive behaviors are synonymous with curiosity and creativity, nor that all behaviors included in this domain are necessarily expressions of curiosity and/or creativity. Rather, we suggest that some behaviors might be simplistically viewed as “just repetitive symptoms of autism” when with greater consideration, we might reframe them as (potentially) productive forms of curiosity and/or creativity.

In this endeavor, it is fruitful to explore how autistic individuals themselves describe their expression of curiosity and creativity. In her book, autistic author O’Neill (2023) suggests that curiosity might be expressed by autistic individuals through behaviors including: exploring environments and sensations; asking questions and wondering; being absorbed (a “flow state”) in a book, movie or puzzle; or being mesmerized by certain visual or auditory experiences. She similarly suggests that creativity might be expressed in autism through: being absorbed (a “flow state”) in a creative process or pursuit; finding novel uses for everyday objects; having original ideas or perspectives; selecting specific clothing or arranging a physical space; playing with toys (like Lego) or on a computer (e.g., Minecraft or writing code) (O’Neill, 2023). Similar suggestions have been found elsewhere (Grove et al., 2016) as well as from parents of autistic children, who have echoed the possibility that sensory pursuits could express curiosity (Johnson, 2020), even while they are also included as a diagnostic feature of autism (APA, 2013). Of course, these manifestations of curiosity and creativity are shared by many nonautistic individuals; these examples are intended to showcase that autistic individuals do identify specific behaviors as particularly indicative of their experiences of these constructs.

Moreover, there are a variety of other suggestions that exploration – which could be expressed through sensory exploration, object exploration, or exploration of a topic or idea – is another particularly salient expression of both curiosity and creativity in autism. In fact, in her consideration of curiosity in autism, Johnson (2020) argues that exploration is perhaps the most essential component. There are also veins of research attesting to the potential for exploration to be an important – and possibly overlooked – manifestation of curiosity and creativity in autism. For instance, Libby et al. (1998) found that, although autistic children showed lower levels of symbolic play (in comparison to nonautistic peers and those with Down syndrome), they showed higher levels of exploration, sensorimotor and functional play. Similarly, Yechiam et al. (2010) found higher levels of exploration in autistic adolescents relative to nonautistic peers and suggested that autistic individuals may be highly motivated by exploration even if they are less motivated by novelty (see Poli et al., 2024 for a similar pattern of results). Interestingly, Yechiam et al. (2010) conclude that this may result in a relative strength for autistic people in convergent thinking (examining multiple aspects of a problem and converging on the optimal solution) over divergent thinking (generating many different possible solutions). They note that while both skills are key components of creativity, most tasks in the literature measure divergent thinking (Rawlings et al., 2025; Said-Metwaly et al., 2017). This highlights an important measurement issue – the preference for sameness in autistic individuals may result in less exploration across domains (including areas which might be novel), but within a current focus of interest, autistic individuals may show intense curiosity. In sum, behaviors that may be simplistically viewed as “just autistic” – for instance, sensory exploration, unusual object use, persistent focus on an idea or topic – are easy to dismiss when using a deficit-based model of autism. However, we suggest that they might alternatively be perceived as indicative of curiosity and/or creativity, and that it would behoove us all to consider whether and how they could be harnessed for learning, growth, connection and satisfaction.

Another area of behaviors typically seen as “restricted” that could potentially signal curiosity and/or creativity in autism, but that has not conventionally been perceived as such, is passionate interests. Although historically often seen as problematic and restricting (e.g., Anthony et al., 2013; Gabriels et al., 2005), these interests can also prompt information-seeking and deep engagement. To be sure, curiosity and interest are not identical constructs (e.g., Grossnickle, 2016; Jirout et al., 2024; Renninger & Hidi, 2016), but they are certainly related, often occurring together and supporting each other (e.g., Murayama et al., 2019; Pekrun, 2019). There is evidence to suggest that the association between curiosity, creativity and interests is important in autistic individuals, too; in fact, Lyons and Fitzgerald (2004) point out that creativity in autism is often related to interests, and they suggest that creativity is perhaps more often sparked by the “reality” of an interest rather than imaginative pursuits. Johnson (2020) describes a series of cases in which she perceives meaningful ways that autistic children’s interests spark curiosity and creativity, even if that same child may not experience curiosity and creativity in relation to “everyday things.” This observation seems to be potentially in alignment with the description of “monotropism” in autism – the proposal that sensory and cognitive processing is devoted so strongly to the autistic person’s current focus of interest that other stimuli may be less salient to the individual (Murray, 1992). Similar examples of how interests can inspire curiosity and creativity are recounted by Prizant and Fields-Meyer (2015), who describe an observations of how interests can be inroads into conversations, community engagement, and problem solving; for instance, they recount a student who used his interests in cars and license plates to create a series of rich and detailed profiles/interviews of his school teachers and staff. The authors of the Special Interest Motivation Scale (Grove et al., 2016) found that autistic individuals, as compared to nonautistic individuals, had greater intrinsic (as compared to extrinsic) motivation to engage with their focused interests, were more motivated by increasing their knowledge about their interest (i.e., curiosity) and were more likely to associate pursuing their interest with a state of “flow”. An essential point of connection between passionate interests, curiosity and creativity is that – like curiosity and creativity – engagement with passionate interests is associated with heightened attention, enjoyment and well-being in individuals with autism (Patten Koenig & Hough Williams, 2017; Vismara & Lyons, 2007; Winter-Messiers, 2007).

A final set of features that falls under the DSM-5’s umbrella of restricted, repetitive behaviors (APA, 2013) is unconventional uses of language (see Luyster et al., 2022 for a review). While not all unconventional language can be construed as curious and/or creative, we propose here that at least some usages could be. For instance, Prizant and Fields-Meyer (2015) revisit the observation that some autistic individuals use “repetitive question asking”, and they comment that this desire to get information is coming from a place of reducing emotional uncertainty (i.e., not cognitive uncertainty, which is a more traditional motivator for curiosity in the literature); that is, these questions are a form of curiosity stemming from emotional needs rather than cognitive needs. Prizant and Fields-Meyer (2015) also recount a story about a boy who starting picking wool balls off Prizant’s sweater and examining them; on subsequent days, Prizant and the boy began exploring similar materials, including cotton balls and bits of sponge, with the student excitedly exclaiming “that’s a piece of sponge! Sponge sponge!” The next day, the boy returned to the same spot in the classroom and stated, “that’s a piece of sponge! Sponge sponge!” In Prizant’s eyes, the boy was retelling what they did the previous day. While we might conventionally refer to this as a form of repetitive language, Prizant suggests that this is a creative way of recollecting the previous day’s discoveries. Similarly, verbal expressions that are “unconventional” might otherwise be interpreted as linguistic creativity. In fact, Cancer et al. (2024) found higher levels of linguistic originality in autistic individuals than in nonautistic individuals; recent work has explored this profile under the heading of an autistic “linguatype” (Zane & Luyster, 2025). Similarly, Lyons and Fitzgerald (2004) point to creativity and originality in autistic speakers’ verbal expressions of humor (ranging from word-play to wit), and Pennisi et al. (2021) reported higher levels of originality and nonconventional thinking in autistic than nonautistic groups.

Where to go from here?

Our review of the literature reveals two central themes in need of further research, specifically in the context of autism: how curiosity and creativity are expressed by autistic individuals, and how we can support these faculties.

The expression of curiosity and creativity in autism

First, we challenge ourselves and our colleagues to consider fruitful ways to learn how autistic individuals might experience and express curiosity and creativity. In these pursuits, we must remember that the considerable variability across the autism spectrum, spanning information processing, language, sensations, attention or prediction (and many other aspects as well), may affect the experience of curiosity and creativity. For example, judging what one already knows and evaluating the gap in knowledge – thought to be initiators of curiosity – may look different depending on how one uses prediction in information processing (see Cannon et al., 2021 for a recent systematic review of prediction in autism). We must also consider how autistic individuals might express or otherwise signal curiosity and creativity. For instance, shifting one’s attention (whether sensory attention like visual/auditory, or cognitive focus) might play an important role in the expression of both curiosity and creativity, and variations in attention have often been observed in autism (e.g., Dwyer et al., 2024; Keehn et al., 2019; Sacrey et al., 2014). As another example, we must acknowledge that verbally mediated expressions of curiosity and creativity require certain language skills that some autistic individuals may not yet have developed; in other words, how might these capabilities be expressed in individuals who are non-speaking or minimally speaking (see Johnson, 2020, for a discussion)? One thought-provoking example comes from Lombardi (D. Lombardi, personal communication, April 23, 2025), who describes her son, RJ, navigating his augmentative and alternative communication device in search of a way to tell his parents that he wanted to eat breadsticks. RJ did not have an icon for “breadsticks”, and he very creatively solved his predicament by (while referencing the “bread” icon) spelling out the word “b-r-e-a-d” and then pressing the icon for the number “six”. In our eyes, this is a wonderful example of innovation in everyday life from a minimally speaking autistic individual; we suggest that there are sure to be many other examples for us to collectively consider.

It is therefore imperative to consider alternative ways in which curiosity and creativity might be expressed, given that autistic individuals may show highly variable behavior on existing tasks for a variety of reasons we have discussed (e.g., Turner, 1999). For example, an individual might not use shared gaze or pointing to signal curiosity, but this does not necessarily indicate an absence of curiosity. Jaswal and Akhtar (2018) use similar reasoning to highlight that a perceived lack of social interest in autistic individuals can be attributed to differences in key behaviors that signal social interest in nonautistic individuals, such as eye contact and pointing, rather than a true absence of interest. This approach to understanding autistic behaviors can be applied to other domains, such as curiosity and creativity. We suggest that there could also be a role for qualitative research asking autistic individuals how they express curiosity and creativity, and that it would be especially enlightening to include individuals across developmental, linguistic and chronological spectra using methodologies known to support communication across a range of ability levels (for example, see Aidonopoulou-Read, 2025; Courchesne et al., 2022). It is important to note that many scholars discuss cultural variation in the ways in which curiosity and creativity are expressed (e.g., Lee et al., 2023, but there are many, many others), and so we should be open to the possibility that – across the human experience – there could be considerable heterogeneity in how these tendencies manifest.

The difficulty of measuring curiosity and creativity is not limited to autism; a recurring theme in the literature concerns the lack of robust tasks (e.g., Said-Metwaly et al., 2017), and self-report measures are common (e.g., Gross et al., 2020). Self-report can perhaps be particularly useful with neurodivergent populations who may not perform as expected on highly constrained tasks but could nevertheless provide valuable first-hand accounts of their own experiences with curiosity and creativity. However, self-report naturally has limitations, including – especially in the case of autism – that it risks excluding those with lower language and communication skills. Innovative methods, such as eye-tracking, can be brought to bear on these issues; eye-tracking has been highlighted as a potentially valuable measure for many autistic individuals given its low task demands (e.g., Venker & Kover, 2015), and it has been invoked already in the study of curiosity, where pupil dilation can be taken as a measure of interest or surprise (e.g., Bazhydai & Westermann, 2020). Finally, we also recognize that there are some inquiries about curiosity and creativity that seem to have been entirely overlooked in autism. As an example, “tool innovation” (which is commonly explored in nonautistic children, see, e.g., Rawlings, 2022) appears to be generally neglected in the context of autism research, despite the obvious potential for autistic individuals to be remarkably inventive (e.g., Baron-Cohen, 2020).

Beyond the question of specific measures, the broader challenges of defining curiosity and creativity raise questions for understanding these constructs in autism and how they might be expressed behaviorally (or otherwise; see, for instance, Diener et al., 2014). We hope that taking into account the multifaceted and complex nature of these constructs and their interrelationship may help us to thoughtfully interpret autistic behaviors. Understanding curiosity and creativity as states, rather than only as traits, is also crucial. Although it is widely accepted that both approaches are valuable, more research has focused on these constructs as traits (e.g., Gross et al., 2020). When conceptualized as states, we open the door to more research evaluating how to evoke curiosity and creativity in daily life. This leads us to our second theme for future research.

Fostering curiosity and creativity in autism

Our second theme – how to support curiosity and creativity in autistic individuals – would also benefit from a better understanding of how curiosity and creativity are expressed in autistic individuals. It is widely accepted that supportive environments are key to supporting curiosity and creativity across the lifespan (e.g., Hennessey & Amabile, 2010; Twomey & Westermann, 2019), and there is a longstanding focus on the role of parents and families in supporting curiosity and creativity for nonautistic children (e.g., Maw & Maw, 1966; Saxe & Stollak, 1971; Shah et al., 2021, 2023). Young children’s attachment to a caregiver may play a role in this; attachment theory posits that exploration is possible within a secure attachment (Bowlby, 1969). Lee et al. (2023) found that an infant’s own curiosity and their caregiver’s curiosity-promoting behaviors (e.g., taking children to new places, encouraging autonomy during play) were distinct and that both of these aspects related to infants’ behavior in a violation of expectation task in which infants were exposed to impossible events (e.g., a solid object passing through another solid object). They suggest that infants’ attention to such physically impossible events might be an early expression of curiosity. In child development, there are clear effects of caregiver behavior on promoting child expressions of curiosity and creativity (e.g., Jirout et al., 2024). For example, Willard et al. (2019) found that the more parents explored an exhibit at a museum, the more their 4- to 6-year-old children did so.

Despite all the above research in nonautistic children, there is a dearth of research addressing the role of caregivers in promoting curiosity and creativity for autistic individuals in infancy, early childhood and beyond. We suggest that while this avenue of research is essential, it is likely to fall short without first thinking expansively about our first theme (in other words, considering how curiosity and creativity might be expressed in autism). If caregivers can discern how their child is expressing curiosity, for example, it will be easier for them to respond in a way that further fosters that curiosity. When parents and providers are equipped to recognize varied forms of curiosity and creativity in autistic individuals, they will be best prepared to support these capabilities in everyday interactions.

Other supportive contexts are worthy of consideration, too. Intervention studies in previous decades have sought to increase young autistic children’s expression of curiosity (i.e., asking questions about their environment), but these efforts have had mixed results (Hung, 1977; Taylor & Harris, 1995) and moreover, it remains unclear whether increases generalized to children’s curiosity more broadly or were limited to question-asking more specifically (Taylor & Harris, 1995). Overall well-being and flourishing are unlikely to be supported through this kind of intervention without an understanding of what children are curious about and what modes of discovery they use to explore these topics. More recently, autistic teacher O’Neill (2023) offers her own personal recommendations for supporting creativity and curiosity in autism. In fostering curiosity, she points to the importance of incorporating both novelty and challenge, as well as the value in identifying a “mentor” who might share a child’s topic of curiosity. For creativity, she advocates for ready access to art and crafts materials (as well as the means for other forms of self-expression like baking, music, writing or physical performance like dance), unscheduled time, and positive family involvement in creative pursuits and products.

Research supports a number of O’Neill’s proposals. For instance, movement and physical activity is linked to curiosity in autistic individuals (Kong et al., 2023), and other research points to the efficacy of embodied learning experiences or movement-related programs in supporting curiosity and creativity (for instance, drama, circus, and role-play, e.g., Fein, 2015; Şahin, 2022; Seymour & Wise, 2017; or nature-based activities, e.g., Byström et al., 2019). Children’s creativity and curiosity are also positively associated with time spent in social activities (e.g., with friends or in arts or sports activities) as well as with time spent reading books, but not with time spent on the internet (Zhang et al., 2024). Finally, there is a preponderance of studies exploring the role of technology to support curiosity in autism, including robots or “agents” (Abdelghani et al., 2022; Ali et al., 2025; Kozima et al., 2007), augmented reality (Nazaruddin & Efendi, 2018), toys (Ellis et al., 2021; Giocondo et al., 2022), and videogames (Van Ommen & Chaparro, 2021). Although causality cannot be established from these results, they merit further research, particularly with autistic individuals who may have a different relationship with technology (whether online, screen-based or otherwise) than nonautistic individuals.

We agree with previous accounts (Johnson, 2020) that supporting an autistic individual’s focused interests (as well aligning with other individual preferences, including various sensations) could be an excellent way to nurture both their curiosity and creativity. In our own work, we found no evidence that incorporating an autistic child’s interests detracted from their language learning (Arunachalam et al., 2024), and we believe that judicious use of interests may increase motivation and support autistic children’s learning in a variety of contexts. We are encouraged by efforts to develop ways of using these interests in daily life as well as in interventions (e.g., Harrop et al., 2019). Indeed, Elisondo and de la Barrera (2025), in their systematic review of autism interventions focusing on creativity, point out that interventions themselves are creative products, and that autistic individuals will benefit from intervention approaches that offer new ways to engage with and support their creative drives. A valuable strategy for informing such interventions is to engage in meaningful conversations with autistic individuals to confirm how they prefer to engage with their interests in daily life.

More broadly, asking autistic individuals about their strengths in the areas of curiosity and creativity, as well as how these strengths manifest, is likely to yield crucial insights (e.g., O’Neill, 2023); we might query, too, what the individual themself would like to learn and how important social partners can best provide support and encouragement. Resnick’s (2007) advocacy for an open-ended, exploratory ethos in early childhood education and caregiving aligns closely with strengths-based supports for autistic individuals, emphasizing the importance of environments that invite self-expression rather than impose conformity. Finally, we want to advocate not just for supporting children with autism but also adults with autism; in that vein, promoting curiosity and creativity in the workplace can be especially consequential. Considerable research in organizational psychology has been devoted to this issue (e.g., Mumford & Todd, 2019). It has been suggested that neurodiverse work environments (that is, workplaces that employ autistic individuals) can lead to more creativity, because of the representation of varied perspectives and life experiences (e.g., Austin & Pisano, 2017; Carrero et al., 2019; Kirby & Smith, 2021).

Conclusions

In closing, our goal in this paper was to draw attention to the importance of these positive faculties – curiosity and creativity – in growth and well-being for autistic individuals. We aimed to explore the presumptions that curiosity and creativity are (1) diminished and/or (2) different in autistic individuals relative to nonautistic individuals, including both a review of the roots of these presumptions as well as our appraisal of their validity. Finally, we leave the reader with two core suggestions for future work that might enhance our understanding of the experience, expression and nurturing of curiosity and creativity in autistic individuals. Throughout this work, our aim is to support the recognition of strengths in autistic individuals and to encourage the adoption of perspectives and actions that enhance well-being in autism across the lifespan.
