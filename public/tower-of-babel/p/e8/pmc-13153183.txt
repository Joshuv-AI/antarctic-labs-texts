The role of land weathering in carbon consumption and its impact on global carbon cycling since the Last Interglacial period
Authors: Shu Xu, Haibin Wu, Yue Yuan, Lixin Chen, Xingyue Huang, Yanyan Yu, Chunxia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtmospheric CO2 variations across glacial-interglacial cycles are tightly linked to oceanic and terrestrial carbon reservoirs, but the contribution of weathering remains poorly constrained. Using a new PCM-weathering model (Past terrestrial Carbon storage Model coupling weathering sub-model), we reconstructed global silicate and carbonate weathering fluxes over the past 120,000 years. Silicate weathering was higher during interglacials (~ 125–163 Tg C/yr) and lower at glacials (~ 119–122 Tg C/yr), tracking atmospheric CO2. In contrast, carbonate weathering increased during glacials (~ 303–320 Tg C/yr) due to expanded land exposure resulting from glacial sea-level fall and declined at interglacials (~ 168–265 Tg C/yr). On glacial-interglacial timescales, total carbon consumption by silicate and carbonate weathering far exceeded changes in oceanic and terrestrial organic carbon pools. These results highlight a dynamic, compensatory balance between silicate and carbonate weathering in modulating Quaternary carbon fluxes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44594-2.Keywords: Terrestrial ecosystem weathering, CO2 consumption, Quaternary glacial-interglacial cycle, PCM-weathering modelSubject terms: Biogeochemistry, Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Air bubbles trapped in polar ice cores reveal significant fluctuations in atmospheric CO2 concentration throughout the Quaternary glacial-interglacial cycles, ranging from ~ 180 ppmv at the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM, ~ 21,000 years ago) to ~ 280 ppmv during the pre-industrial period (PI)1. These fluctuations reflect the large-scale reorganizations within the coupled carbon-climate system and the redistributions among major carbon reservoirs during glacial-interglacial transitions2.

To date, the most widely accepted explanations for these atmospheric CO2 variations have focused on changes in marine and terrestrial carbon pools3,4. During glacial periods, the oceans are considered primarily sink for reducing atmospheric CO2 levels5–8, while terrestrial ecosystems—limited by colder and drier climates—were thought to store less carbon due to reduced vegetation cover and productivity9–14. However, during deglacial and interglacial periods, these oceanic and terrestrial carbon cycle processes exhibited the opposite behavior, with reduced oceanic uptake and enhanced terrestrial carbon storage driven by warmer, wetter conditions7.

However, the potential role of terrestrial chemical weathering—an important long-term CO2 sink—has often been overlooked. Modern riverine data and weathering models indicate that chemical weathering of carbonate and silicate rocks currently consumes ~ 0.089–0.148 Pg C/yr (1Pg = 1015 g) and ~ 0.133–0.169 Pg C/yr, respectively13–18. Over the geological time scales, silicate weathering removes carbon from atmosphere and oceans by converting it into additional carbonate19,20. While carbonate weathering, through its influence on seawater chemistry, can also modulate atmospheric CO2 levels during the glacial-interglacial transitions19.

Although several proxies have qualitatively reconstructed land weathering from marine20–28 and lacustrine29–33 sediment isotopic records, there remains no consensus on its quantitative impact on atmospheric CO2 variation over the glacial-interglacial circles. Chemical weathering is influenced by multiple factors, including exposed surfaces15, climate conditions34, and terrestrial vegetation cover35.

To address this gap, we introduce a new PCM-weathering model (Past terrestrial Carbon storage Model coupling weathering sub-model), constrained by lithology classification36, which integrates the terrestrial ecosystem carbon cycle model37 with silicate and carbonate weathering processes18,38,39. Compared to previous approaches38,39, the PCM-weathering model offers: (1) an improved representation of terrestrial ecosystem conditions and their influence on silicate and carbonate weathering processes; and (2) simulates of how key environmental drivers—including precipitation, temperature, atmospheric CO2 concentration, and glacially exposed land—modulate weathering fluxes. This framework enables a quantitative reconstruction of terrestrial weathering carbon uptake over the past 120,000 years and provides new insight into its role in regulating the Quaternary carbon cycle.

Results

Terrestrial weathering flux since Last Interglacial period

We applied the PCM-weathering model to reconstruct global silicate and carbonate weathering fluxes from the Last Interglacial to the present. Silicate weathering fluxes show a strong sensitivity to orbital-scale climate variations (Fig. 1). During the Last Interglacial, silicate weathering gradually decreased from ~ 165 to ~ 120 Tg C/yr (1Tg = 1012 g) in response to progressive cooling and reduced precipitation. Peak fluxes were concentrated in humid tropical and subtropical regions, including Gulf Coast, Amazon rainforest, Colombia, central Africa, India, Indonesia, and southwestern China (Fig. S4).

Shaded intervals indicate interglacial or interstadial stages42.

During the Last Glacial period, silicate weathering fluxes ranged from ~ 137 to ~ 105 Tg C/yr under colder and drier conditions, reaching a minimum at the LGM. Global fluxes were suppressed during MIS2 (Maine Isotopic Stage) and MIS3 (Fig. 1). Silicate weathering intensified during the Last Deglacial period, reaching ~ 170 Tg C/yr and stabilized at ~ 165 Tg C/yr throughout the Holocene, again with maxima in tropical zones (Fig. S4).

By contrast, carbonate weathering appeared an opposing temporal trend during the glacial-interglacial periods. Fluxes increased during the Last Interglacial from ~ 208 Tg C/yr to ~ 300 Tg C/yr (Fig. 1), despite cooling climate conditions. The highest fluxes remained centered in equatorial regions (Fig. S4). During the Last Glacial period, carbonate weathering the flux ranged further intensified (~ 275–370 Tg /yr), peaking at the LGM. This counterintuitive pattern was attributed to glacial sea-level fall, which exposed extensive carbonate platforms in the equator regions—particularly Southeast Asia—substantially expanding weatherable land area (Fig. S4). Carbonate weathering flux subsequently declined after the Last Deglaciation, reaching ~ 190 Tg C during the Holocene (Fig. 1).

Simulated biome distributions show pronounced glacial-interglacial shifts, with forest contraction during cold stages and expansion during warm stages, especially in tropical and temperate regions (Fig. S5A). Across all marine isotope stages, forest biomes contribute the largest fractions of both silicate and carbonate weathering fluxes, with tropical forests providing the dominant share, followed by temperate forests (Fig. S5B–C). Post-glacial forest expansion coincides with increased weathering intensity in low- and mid-latitude regions, whereas glacial-stage transitions toward tundra, shrubland, and arid ecosystems are associated with reduced weathering activity. These patterns indicate that vegetation redistribution contributes to the spatial and temporal variability of continental chemical weathering.

Continental-scale analyses reveal substantial regional heterogeneity in weathering dynamics since 120 ka (Figs. S4, S6). South America exhibits the highest average silicate weathering fluxes, followed by Southeast Asia and Africa, with all three regions showing pronounced glacial-interglacial variability characterized by enhanced interglacial and reduced glacial fluxes. Carbonate weathering displays a distinct spatial pattern, with the largest contributions originating from newly exposed shelf regions and persistently high fluxes in Southeast Asia, South America, and Africa.

Cumulative carbon uptake by silicate weathering during the warmer intervals—MIS5a, 5c, 5e and the Holocene—was estimated at ~ 1911, ~ 2270, ~ 944, and ~ 2242 Pg C (Fig. 2), respectively. Over the same periods, carbonate weathering carbon removed substantially larger amounts of carbon, totaling ~ 3526, ~ 3922, ~ 1265, and ~ 2855 Pg C. During colder intervals—MIS2, 3, and 4—silicate weathering contributed ~ 2315, ~ 2666, and ~ 1934 Pg C, while carbonate fluxes were markedly higher, reaching ~ 6422, ~ 6755, and ~ 4960 Pg C, respectively.

The cumulative carbon removal via silicate weathering during the Last Interglacial period (MIS 5e-5a) and the Last Glacial period (MIS 4-2) was estimated at ~ 6.7 × 103 Pg C and ~ 6.9 × 103 Pg C, respectively. In contrast, cumulative carbonate weathering fluxes reached ~ 11.8 × 103 Pg C over the Last Interglacial, and substantially higher values of ~ 18.1 × 103 Pg C during the Last Glacial period. These results underscore the disproportionately large contribution of carbonate weathering to atmospheric CO2 drawdown, particularly under glacial periods when falling sea levels exposed extensive carbonate platforms.

Contribution of climate factors

Global silicate weathering flux exhibited a strong positive sensitivity to atmospheric CO2 concentration and precipitation but negative sensitivity to temperature and the expansion of land area; while ice-sheet variability played a minor role (Fig. 3). During the Last Interglacial period, rising atmospheric CO2 accounted for ~ 94% of the observed variability in silicate weathering flux. In comparison, precipitation contributed ~ 47%, whereas temperature and additional land exerted suppressive effects of ~ − 19%, and ~ − 31% (Fig. 3A), respectively. The dominant role of atmospheric CO2 persisted into the Last Glacial period, contributing ~ 106% to flux variations, with precipitation, temperature and additional land contributing ~ 37%, ~ − 25%, and ~ − 26%, respectively—values broadly consistent with the prior interglacial phase.

Colored lines show the isolated contributions of atmospheric CO2 concentration (green), precipitation (blue), temperature (red), ice cap (purple), and additional exposed land area due to sea-level changes (yellow). Shaded intervals indicate interglacial or interstadial stage42.

In the Last Deglacial period, atmospheric CO2 remained the primary driver, contributing ~ 113% (Fig. 3A), followed by precipitation (~ 48%). The influences of temperature and additional land area were again negative, at ~ − 30% and ~ − 38%, respectively. However, a marked shift in controlling factors emerged during Holocene period: climatic variables—precipitation (~ 194%), and temperature (~ 191%)—became the dominant drivers of silicate weathering, while the influence of atmospheric CO2 turned negative (~ − 156%). Land area exerted a positive but more moderate effect (~ 49%).

Collectively, these patterns indicate that atmospheric CO2 was the primary control on silicate weathering throughout most of glacial-interglacial circle, while the relative importance of precipitation and temperature increased notably during the Holocene.

In contrast, global carbonate weathering flux showed positive sensitivity to changes in temperature and land area but negative sensitivity to atmospheric CO2 concentration and precipitation, with ice-sheet variability exerting only a weak influence (Fig. 3B). During both the Last Interglacial and Last Glacial periods, newly exposed land area was the dominant factor, contributing ~ 108% and ~ 115%, respectively. In these periods, atmospheric CO2 exerted a suppressive influence (~ − 58% and ~ − 30%), while the effects of precipitation, and temperature remained minimal (precipitation: ~ − 4% and ~ 1%; temperature: ~ 7% and ~ 5%).

This pattern persisted through the Last Deglacial period, with land area contributing ~ 112%, and CO2, precipitation, and temperature contributing ~ − 46%, ~ − 4%, and ~ 6% (Fig. 3B), respectively. In the Holocene, no single driver dominated. Instead, relative contributions were more evenly distributed: in land area (~ 60%), precipitation (~ 55%), temperature (~ 48%), and atmospheric CO2 (~ − 12%).

These results highlight a fundamental distinction in the climatic sensitivity of the two weathering regimes. Silicate weathering was primarily regulated by atmospheric CO2 across glacial intervals, but shifted in the Holocene to strong control by precipitation and temperature. In contrast, carbonate weathering was consistently governed by land exposure, with CO2 exerting a suppressive role. Yet, unlike earlier interglacials, the Holocene marked a departure in which precipitation and temperature assumed comparable importance, yielding a more balanced set of controls.

Although global silicate and carbonate weathering exhibit opposite trends at glacial-interglacial timescales, short-term synchronous variations occur during several intervals (e.g., ~ 30–20 ka and ~ 70–60 ka; Fig. 1). These apparent in-phase behaviors do not indicate a common driving mechanism, but instead reflect the superposition of different dominant climatic controls acting simultaneously on the two weathering pathways. Silicate weathering variability is mainly governed by hydroclimatic and CO2-related effects, whereas carbonate weathering is primarily controlled by sea-level-driven changes in exposed land area (Fig. 3). Consequently, transient alignment of these independent drivers can produce short-term coherence, while the long-term contrasting responses remain dominant over glacial-interglacial cycles.

Future terrestrial weathering flux under different scenarios

Our simulations also project terrestrial weathering responses under three future warming scenarios (Fig. S7). Between 1950 to 2005, global weathering fluxes ranged from about 280–330 Tg C/yr for silicates and 230–280 Tg C/yr for carbonates. Under the low-emission RCP2.6 scenario, these values are projected to increase to ~ 416 Tg C/yr and ~ 339 Tg C/yr for silicate and carbonate weathering, respectively. In contrast, under the high-emission RCP8.5 scenario, silicate weathering fluxes could reach ~ 960 Tg C/yr, while carbonate fluxes might rise to ~ 820 Tg C/yr.

Compared with previous interglacial warming, during multiple climatic and boundary-condition factors jointly controlled weathering, future weathering responses are projected to be systematically amplified, primarily driven by CO2-induced increases in net primary productivity and soil CO2 concentrations (Fig. S8). These results indicate that under future warming conditions, enhanced chemical weathering will serve as a significant natural sink for atmospheric CO2. The intensified weathering rates—particularly under high-emission trajectories—could contribute meaningfully to long-term climate regulation by partially offsetting anthropogenic CO2 emissions.

Discussion and conclusion

Despite decades of research, both qualitative and quantitative assessments of global terrestrial weathering during the glacial-interglacial cycles remain highly debated (Table S1). Proxy records based on marine Os isotope suggests that decreased seawater 187Os/188Os ratios reflect reduced continental chemical weathering during times of extreme cold and aridity25, aligning with higher weathering rates in interglacial periods. Similarly, Fe isotope data from subtropical and tropical monsoon regions indicate that silicate weathering intensified during humid interglacial periods, varying synchronously with precession cycle and dominated by rainfall variability24. These patterns are further supported by clay mineral assemblages from the Irrawaddy River basin30, the Congo31, the northern Okinawa Trough32, and Bering Sea24.

Quantitative constraints from marine and lacustrine Sr22 and Be23 isotopes systems imply limited change in global weathering fluxes, with variations within ± 4.9 ppmv (Table S1). In contrast, Pb isotope reconstructions point to an average increase in chemical weathering rate of ~ 20–50% during interglacial periods27,28. But more recent studies33 using Sr–Nd isotopes and clay mineralogy from the South China Sea sediments have found more intense silicate weathering during glacial periods, likely driven by exposure of tropical shelf sediments. This process may have accounted for ~ 9% of the atmospheric CO2 drawdown during glacial periods. Despite these insights, regionally focused reconstructions cannot fully capture the spectrum of global climatic and environmental variability, leaving considerable uncertainty regarding the magnitude and timing of global terrestrial weathering fluxes20.

To address these discrepancies, we develop a novel framework for simulating past weathering using the PCM-weathering model, which integrates key climatic and terrestrial variables across time. A major innovation of our approach lies in coupling a process-based global carbon model—capable of reproducing paleo-vegetation distributions—with minimal input data: monthly temperature, precipitation, and atmospheric CO2 concentration43. This allowed for dynamic simulation of global vegetation patterns and land surface conditions, producing more realistic reconstructions of silicate and carbonate weathering rates (see Materials and methods).

Our results show that silicate weathering fluxes varied from ~ 119 to ~ 158 Tg C/yr across glacial-interglacial cycles, while carbonate fluxes ranged from ~ 168 to ~ 320 Tg C/yr. The ~ 33% increase in silicate weathering from glacial to interglacial periods is consistent with isotope-based reconstructions22,27 and Earth system model results11, which report ~ 20–50% higher rates during interglacials (Fig. S9). Conversely, carbonate weathering decreased by nearly half from glacial to interglacial periods, largely driven by the availability of newly exposed land, which consistent with Foster and Vance’s inference of enhanced glacial carbonate weathering due to sea-level regression27.

Since the LGM, silicate weathering flux increased markedly, from ~ 105 to ~ 160 Tg C/yr, whereas carbonate weathering flux declined substantially, from ~ 290 to ~ 121 Tg C/yr. The net change between the LGM and PI amounted to an additional ~ 55 Tg C/yr of silicate weathering and a reduction of ~ 169 Tg C/yr in carbonate weathering. These opposing trends are broadly consistent with the simulations of Munhoven et al.21, although our absolute flux values are higher. Taken together, these results highlight the critical role of weathering feedbacks in modulating atmospheric CO2 levels and sustaining long-term carbon cycle stability across glacial-interglacial transitions.

Discrepancies with earlier studies may arise from differences in lithological classification, climate forcings, and land-surface representation. For example, Munhoven’s reconstructions relied on lithological maps with a six-type rock categorization derived from GKWM and GEM-CO2 land masks21,44,45, whereas our study employed a more recent 22-type lithological dataset36, (Table S5), providing finer spatial resolution and a more realistic depiction of land exposure during glacial periods. Furthermore, while Munhoven’s model linked CO2 consumption, and HCO3- production primarily to runoff via empirical flux-runoff relationships, our PCM-weathering framework incorporates additional controlling factors, including temperature, slope, land vegetation conditions, atmospheric CO2, thereby achieving higher spatial fidelity and improved accuracy. Zhao et al.24 likewise employed NCAR-CCSM3 simulations to drive the GEOCLIM model with GLiM-based lithological constraints, but their approach did not explicitly represent vegetation-weathering interactions. By integrating these interactions alongside lithological and climatic controls, our framework captures essential land-climate-lithology feedbacks and establishes a more robust basis for quantifying the role of chemical weathering in regulating atmospheric CO2 across glacial-interglacial cycles.

Previous studies20–33,46 have largely relied on correlative approaches between weathering proxies—such as K/Al ratios, clay mineral assemblages, and marine or lacustrine isotope systems—and paleoclimate indicators derived from δ18O, Mg/Ca ratios, and pollen records to infer driving factors of weathering. Although these approaches have yielded valuable insights, they remain limited in their ability to discriminate the relative contributions of co-varying climatic factors to silicate and carbonate weathering fluxes, making it difficult to accurately identify the dominant controls.

In this study, we quantitatively dissect these controls over the past 120 kyr. Our results show that changes in global silicate weathering were primarily driven by atmospheric CO2 levels (accounting for ∼65% of the total variation), whereas carbonate weathering fluxes were dominated by the exposure of continental shelves during glacial intervals (∼115%). By transcending qualitative correlations to provide a quantitative attribution of causality, our analysis establishes a mechanistic framework for weathering dynamics on orbital timescales.

Our findings underscore the critical role of chemical weathering in modulating atmospheric CO2 levels throughout glacial-interglacial carbon cycle. During the Holocene, total carbon uptake via silicate and carbonate weathering was 2242 and ~ 2856 Pg C (Fig. 2), respectively. Across the full durations of the Last Interglacial (MIS 5e-5a) and the Last Glacial period (MIS 4-2), cumulative carbon removal via silicate weathering was estimated at ~ 6.7 × 103 Pg C and ~ 6.9 × 103 Pg C, respectively. By contrast, carbonate weathering sequestered ~ 11.8 × 103 Pg C during the Last Interglacial and rose substantially to ~ 18.1 × 103 Pg C during the Last Glacial.

Over long-time scales, silicate weathering serves as a durable carbon sink by fixing atmospheric CO2 into marine carbonate23. In contrast, although carbonate weathering consumes CO2 through dissolution, much of it is returned to the atmosphere through via oceanic carbonate precipitation47. Nonetheless, carbonate weathering can also exert meaningful effects on atmospheric CO2 concentration over short time scales (< 3 ka)21,48.

Previous model results indicate that terrestrial carbon storage averaged ~ 1770 Pg C during the LIG, declined to ~ 1629 Pg C during MIS3, and reached a minimum of ~ 1520 Pg C at the LGM, followed by a rebound to a Holocene maximum of ~ 1917 Pg C (Table S2). Terrestrial carbon stocks varied by ~ 62–258 Pg C across individual stages from MIS5 to MIS1 (Fig. 2). On glacial-interglacial timescales, proxy-based reconstructions and model simulations indicate a reduction of ~ 547–1708 Pg C in terrestrial carbon storage during the LGM relative to the Holocene, accompanied by a concurrent increase of ~ 510–980 Pg C in oceanic carbon storage. Our model estimates that the cumulative carbon uptake from terrestrial weathering processes between the LGM and PI interval was ~ 3027 Pg C for silicate and ~ 4803 Pg C for carbonate (Fig. 2). These magnitudes underscore the substantial role of chemical weathering in restructuring global carbon reservoirs and modulating atmospheric CO2 levels during the glacial-interglacial cycle.

Looking ahead, model simulations predict that global silicate weathering flux will continue to rise under warming scenarios, increasing by ~ 10% under RCP 4.5 and ~ 17% under RCP 8.518,49. However, our projections further reveal a clear acceleration in both silicate and carbonate weathering across all RCP scenarios (Fig. S7). This amplification is largely driven by CO2-induced enhancement in Net Primary Productivity (NPP)—a mechanism underrepresented in previous models49. Such intensification highlights the potential of strengthened chemical weathering to act as negative feedback on rising CO2 levels. When implemented at scale, enhanced rock weathering could offer a promising, nature-based strategy to help meet global “dual carbon” targets by sequestering atmospheric CO2 in a stable, long-term form.

Materials and methods

PCM-weathering model

A schematic overview of the PCM-weathering modeling framework is shown in Fig. S1. This framework integrates the PCM model37 with established weathering models38,39 to simulate terrestrial chemical weathering over geological timescales. The PCM framework couples the BIOME4 vegetation model with the DEMETER carbon model, forming a robust, process-based global carbon model simulation system capable of accurately reconstructing paleo-vegetation conditions. It relies on a limited set of easily accessible inputs, including monthly temperature, precipitation, sunshine, absolute minimum temperature, soil texture, and atmospheric CO2 concentration. PCM outputs include spatially resolved estimates of vegetation types and carbon pools in vegetation, litter, and soils. However, it does not include explicit representations of chemical weathering processes.

The weathering component is based on GEOCARB38 and SCION39. which estimate silicate and carbonate weathering fluxes using key climate variables such as monthly temperature, precipitation, slope, and atmospheric CO2 concentration. While effective for long-term assessments, these models simplify terrestrial ecosystem interactions and lack detailed lithological representation, limiting their accuracy for simulating weathering since the Last Interglacial period.

To overcome these limitations, we coupled the weathering module with runoff and net primary production (NPP) outputs from PCM simulations. These two variables intensify the influence of vegetation on chemical weathering dynamics and offer a spatially and temporally responsive depiction of terrestrial ecosystem function. This coupling allows for a more process-based and realistic representation of both silicate and carbonate weathering fluxes, as well as their climatic drivers.

PCM model

The PCM model consists of two submodules: vegetation and carbon. The vegetation submodule is based on BIOME4, which simulates the steady-state global distribution of vegetation and associated biogeochemical processes. It dynamically represents the physiological responses of plants to atmospheric CO2, including effects on photosynthesis, stomata conductance, and the leaf area index (LAI), through a coupled carbon–water flux scheme. BIOME4 includes 12 plant functional types (PFT), each defined by a set of bioclimatic limits and physiological parameters50.

A coupled carbon–water flux model is applied to calculate the seasonal maximum LAI that maximizes NPP, using daily soil water balance and monthly simulations of canopy conductance, photosynthesis, respiration, and phonology. Biome classification is achieved by ranking combinations of woody and grass PFTs according to criteria such as LAI, NPP, and mean annual soil moisture. These rankings are then used to assign one of 25 biome types, which serve as inputs for DEMETER.

In carbon submodule follows the structures of BIOME450 and DEMETER51, simulating carbon cycling within vegetation, litter, and soil. Photosynthesis and plant respiration are calculated as in BIOME4. The allocation of annual NPP into leaf, stem, and root components follows DEMETER’s scheme. Soil organic carbon is categorized into fast and slow carbon pools, modeled to a depth of 1 m, using a simplified version of the CENTURY model52, excluding nitrogen dynamics.

Weathering sub-model

Continental weathering is influenced is governed by a complex interplay factor, including the mineralogy of the exposed surfaces15, atmospheric CO2 concentration53, temperature54, runoff55, vegetation cover56, and erosion rate57. This multifactorial dependence makes it difficult to quantify global weathering to climate change with high precision.

In contrast, carbonate weathering (Eq. 2) returns most of the consumed CO2 to the atmosphere via marine carbonate precipitation, though it still affects atmospheric CO2 levels over glacial-interglacial timescales21.

We calculate weathering fluxes using the equations from SCION model39, which estimates basin-scale silicate weathering rates depending on runoff, temperature and erosion rate60. Unlike long-term weathering models such as GEOCARB38 and COPSE61, SCION provides a more mechanistic and dynamic coupling between climate variables (e.g., precipitation, temperature and slope) and chemical weathering rates.

For carbonate weathering, SCION used an empirical runoff-based formula39, assuming negligible influence from temperature and erosion. This method is applied to pure carbonate lithologies (i.e. carbonate sedimentary rocks), where weathering is primarily driven by runoff45. For mixed lithologies, we adopt formulations from Börker et al.62, which partition total weathering into fixed carbonate and silicate components, offering a more a more realistic representation of natural surfaces49 (more details in appendix).

Lithology data

Lithological composition is a major control on weathering potential, and varies considerably at regional or local scales66. Accurately estimating terrestrial weathering fluxes requires a high-resolution global lithological map and an appropriate partitioning of silicate and carbonate contents for each rock unit36.

In this study, silicate weathering includes combinations from both granitic and basaltic substrates. The global land surface was categorized into 19 rock types36, and each assigned specific chemical proportions and a unique weathering parameter ‘b’62, (see Table S5). To account for land area variations driven by glacio-eustatic sea level fluctuations since the Last Interglacial, we applied corrections to lithological coverage by incorporate newly exposed landmasses62,67 (Table S5).

Ice cap data

Weathering flux can initially increase during the early stages of ice-sheet growth; however, once the ice sheet expands beyond a critical extent, the weatherable surface area becomes progressively restricted and the increasingly cold conditions suppress chemical weathering68. In this study, we assume that chemical weathering ceases entirely in areas covered by ice. The ice-cap reconstructions over the past 120,000 years are based on Zeller et al.69.

Model evaluation

To validate the PCM-weathering model, we first compared its modern global output with estimates from measured riverine data and other established weathering models16,18.

Using the new developed PCM-weathering model, we estimated modern global carbon consumption by silicates and carbonates weathering to be ~ 204 Tg C/yr and ~ 128 Tg C/yr (Fig. S2), respectively. These simulated values generally fall within the range of observed global weathering fluxes— ~ 94–104 Tg C/yr for silicates13,43 and ~ 110–300 Tg C/yr for carbonates13,14,17,70—but are somewhat higher for silicates and slightly lower for carbonates than previous simulations (~ 80–167 for silicates15,16,47,49,71 and ~ 141–320 Tg C/yr or carbonates18,47,49.

Simulated basin-scale weathering fluxes show strong agreement with observation-based river chemistry estimates (Fig. S3), with regression slopes close to the 1:1 line and coefficients of determination of 0.81 for silicate and 0.70 for carbonate weathering, indicating that the model reproduces the first-order spatial heterogeneity of modern chemical weathering. Vegetation-driven amplification further enhances spatial contrasts, producing elevated weathering rates in warm and humid regions and reduced fluxes in cold and arid environments (Figs. S10, S11). In particular, pronounced weathering signals are simulated in tropical and monsoonal regions, including the Amazon Basin, Southeast Asia, South Asia, and central Africa, consistent with major global weathering hotspots.

Overall, the PCM-weathering model—by coupling terrestrial ecosystem carbon cycle with lithology-based weathering processes—provides reasonable and robust estimates of global silicate and carbonate weathering carbon fluxes.

Sensitivity analysis: contribution of climate drivers

To quantify the individual contribution of temperature, precipitation and atmospheric CO2 concentration to weathering-driven carbon consumption, a series of sensitivity experiments was designed:

Experiment 1 varied temperature from 120 ka BP to its present-day values, while keeping precipitation and atmospheric CO2 level fixed at their initial levels. Similar experiments were conducted for precipitation (Experiment 2) and atmospheric CO2 concentration (Experiment 3).

The contribution of each factor was calculated as the difference of weathering flux between the control (all variables evolving together) and the respective single-variable experiment. Additionally, we independently quantified the contribution of land area expansion due to sea level fall, which exposed new lithologies and altered the spatial extent of weatherable surfaces during the glacial-interglacial cycles. We further accounted for the influence of ice caps, which reduce terrestrial weathering by covering the land surface.
