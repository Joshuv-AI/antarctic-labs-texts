Bronchial thermoplasty for severe asthma: Systematic review and meta-analysis of benefits and harms
Authors: Daniel G Rayner, Alexandro WL Chu, Gerard Cox, Leonardo Ologundudu, Melanie M Wong, Nazmul Islam, Peter Bonta, Pallav L Shah, Dirk-Jan Slebos, Jouke Annema, Xiajing Chu, Bradley Chipps, John Oppenheimer, Elliot Israel, Katherine Rivera-Spoljaric, Tamara T Perry, Sharmilee M Nyenhuis, Flavia Hoyte, Kaharu Sumino, Paul M O’Byrne, Lindsay E Shade, Gordon H Guyatt, Valerie G Press, Susana Rangel, Ellen McCabe, Angel Melendez, Dia Sue-Wah-Sing, Hilarry Orr, Donna D Gardner, Tonya Winders, Kathyrn Przywara, Matthew A Rank, Giselle Mosnaim, Leonard B Bacharier, Derek K Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy and safety of bronchial thermoplasty (BT) for severe asthma remains unclear.ObjectiveWe systematically synthesized the efficacy and safety of BT in patients with severe asthma.MethodsAs part of the upcoming American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology/American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology Joint Task Force on Practice Parameters severe asthma guidelines, we searched the Medline, Embase, and Central databases to July 29, 2025, for randomized trials comparing BT to control for asthma. Paired reviewers independently screened records and extracted data. Random-effects meta-analyses addressed asthma control (Asthma Control Questionnaire 6 [scale, 0-6; lower better]), asthma-related quality of life (Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire [range, 1-7; higher better]), severe asthma exacerbations, and harms, stratified by procedural, postprocedural, and overall. The GRADE approach informed certainty-of-evidence ratings (PROSPERO: CRD42023408565).ResultsSix trials randomized 573 adults. Procedurally, BT likely increases severe exacerbations (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 2.95; 95% CI, 1.20-7.25; risk difference [RD] 0.08 more events per patient-year; moderate certainty) and serious respiratory adverse events (IRR 5.98; 95% CI, 1.07-33.29; RD 0.18 more events per patient-year; moderate certainty). Postprocedurally, BT may reduce severe exacerbations (IRR 0.67; 95% CI, 0.50-0.89; RD 0.67 fewer events per patient-year; low certainty). Overall, BT may improve asthma control (mean difference −0.37; 95% CI, −0.67 to −0.07; RD for 0.5-point decrease: 15.9% more; low certainty), asthma-related quality of life (mean difference 0.55; 95% CI, 0.28-0.82; RD for 0.5-point increase: 19.1% more; low certainty), and severe exacerbations (IRR 0.81; 95% CI, 0.68-0.97; RD 0.11 fewer events per patient-year; low certainty).ConclusionAmong adults with severe asthma, BT may improve asthma control and asthma-related quality of life and may reduce overall severe exacerbations, but it likely increases procedural severe exacerbations and serious respiratory harms.Key words: Asthma, asthma control, bronchial thermoplasty, efficacy, exacerbations, GRADE, harms, meta-analysis, quality of life, radiofrequency ablation, safety, systematic review, thermoplasty

Asthma is a chronic airway disorder marked by variable and recurring airflow obstruction, heightened bronchial responsiveness, and inflammation. Globally, it affects approximately 262 million individuals. In the United States alone, the estimated 20-year economic burden of asthma approaches $1 trillion,1 encompassing both direct medical costs and productivity losses due to missed work and school days.2 Individuals with severe asthma, defined as disease requiring high-dose inhaled corticosteroids plus a second controller medication and/or systemic corticosteroids, comprise 5% to 10% of the overall asthma population.3 This subgroup bears a disproportionately high burden, incurring nearly triple the direct health care costs, over 9 times more missed work and school days, and 10 times higher indirect costs per person per year compared to individuals with mild asthma.2

Bronchial thermoplasty (BT) is a minimally invasive bronchoscopic procedure performed under moderate or deep sedation.4 It applies thermal energy to the airways to reduce bronchial hyperreactivity and inflammation, reduce the mass of airway smooth muscle and its release of mast cell–activating and proinflammatory mediators, and inhibit mediators involved in fibroblast proliferation in airway epithelial cells.5 These combined mechanisms aim to remodel the airway structure, thereby decreasing the frequency and severity of asthma symptoms and exacerbations.

The benefits and risk of BT in treating severe asthma, however, remain uncertain. Earlier reviews focused exclusively on studies used for initial marketing approval and did not account for recent randomized trials evaluating their efficacy and safety.6 This omission limits the confidence in effect estimates and evidence generalizability. Moreover, none of the reviews used optimal statistical methods, such as adjusting for baseline asthma severity to synthesize outcomes like asthma control and asthma-related quality of life.

As part of updating the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology and American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology Joint Task Force on Practice Parameters severe and difficult-to-treat asthma guidelines, we performed a systematic review of the efficacy and safety of BT for severe asthma.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed Cochrane Collaboration,7 GRADE (Grades of Recommendation Assessment, Development, and Evaluation),8 and PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis)9 guidance (see Appendix E1 in this article’s Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). This review follows similar methods to previous task force reviews10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews; CRD42023408565).

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

We searched the Medline, Embase, and Central databases from January 1, 2014, to July 29, 2025, for published and unpublished randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing the use of BT to sham therapy or standard of care (see Appendix E2 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). We included trials published before 2014 from a previous systematic review.6 To identify additional potentially relevant studies, we cross-referenced other systematic reviews,6,18,19 conducted forward and backward citation analyses of all included studies using the Web of Science (all databases), and consulted with clinical experts from the linked guideline panel.

Data collection

Paired and calibrated reviewers (D.G.R., A.W.L.C., L.O., M.M.W., X.C.) independently screened titles and abstracts and reviewed full text with Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia). These reviewers independently extracted bibliographic information, study design, participant characteristics, outcomes according to the intention-to-treat principle, and sources of funding. They resolved disagreements by consensus, and, if necessary, through discussion with a third reviewer (D.K.C.).

For multiple records pertaining to the same trial (eg, conference abstracts, post hoc analyses of included trials), after collecting all relevant data, we conducted analysis as a single study.

When studies presented outcome data only in figures, we extracted data with WebPlotDigitizer v5.2 software (Automeris, Pacifica, Calif). For trials that did not report means or standard deviations (SDs), we imputed data according to Furukawa et al,20 Weir et al,21 and Cochrane guidance.7 If a trial reported data as median and interquartile range or range, we assessed skewness, and if not present, we converted values to means and SDs according to Shi et al22 and Cochrane guidance.7 If present, we estimated mean and SD values by using quantile estimation, Box-Cox transformation, or approximate Bayesian computation, preferring the estimate that was the most consistent with other values within and between trials. We contacted authors for clarification in case of missing, unpublished, or unclear data (see Appendix E3 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org).

Outcomes

In accordance with fulfilling trustworthy guideline development processes, we discussed with our multistakeholder guideline development group, including asthma experts (respirologists and allergists), frontline clinicians (pediatricians, internists, physician assistants, nurses and nurse practitioners, and asthma educators), and patient and caregiver partners, to prioritize patient-important outcomes.23,24 We assessed asthma control, asthma-related quality of life, severe asthma exacerbations, respiratory-specific serious adverse events (excluding severe asthma exacerbations), respiratory-specific adverse events (moderate [discomfort enough to cause interference with patients’ usual activities] and severe [incapacitating, with inability to do work or usual activities]), all-cause serious adverse events, and all-cause mortality. Appendix E4 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org describes relevant outcome measures. Where possible, we stratified analyses for outcomes occurring procedurally (during 6 weeks of treatment and up to 6 weeks after treatment), postprocedurally (after 6 weeks’ posttreatment), and overall. We analyzed outcomes at the longest duration of randomized follow-up.

Risk of bias assessment

Paired and calibrated reviewers (L.O., M.M.W.) assessed risk of bias independently for each trial outcome by a modified Cochrane Risk of Bias v2 tool for randomized trials.25 We subclassified “some concerns” judgments as “some concerns, probably high” and “some concerns, probably low.” If at least one domain was high or probably high risk, we considered the outcome at high risk of bias for that trial. We assessed publication bias by evaluating the relationship between the results and study funding.

Data analysis

We summarized continuous outcomes as mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using generic inverse variance random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood estimators of variance to meta-analyze ANCOVA change from baseline values. If the instruments used to measure an outcome varied between trials, we followed GRADE guidance to convert each measure to the one most familiar to clinicians according to the established minimally important difference (MID) of each measure.26 To facilitate interpretation, we performed responder analyses by calculating the risk difference (RD) and 95% CI to obtain an MID. The established MID for the Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ-6) and the Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (AQLQ) are both 0.5 points.

Using random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood estimators of variance, we summarized severe asthma exacerbations and safety outcomes as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% CIs and/or as RDs and 95% CIs (when rare event rates were observed). One-stage (multilevel random intercept random slope) and 2-stage (random effects inverse variance) Poisson models addressed count outcomes (recurrent events as in asthma exacerbations and safety outcomes) for comparative and single-arm meta-analyses.

We evaluated the certainty (quality) of the evidence with the GRADE approach,8,27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33 including assessment of risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias; we report our findings using standardized terminology.34 We interpret and present findings with a target of certainty rating of a nonzero effect35 (ie, any benefit or any harm, relative to sham therapy or standard care) and calculated the number needed to treat or number needed to harm for each outcome.36

We performed prespecified subgroup analyses on age (<65 years vs ≥65 years), risk of bias (low vs high), and baseline eosinophil level (<150 cells/μL vs ≥150 cells/μL and <300 cells/μL vs ≥300 cells/μL). We hypothesized that relative effects would be greater among older adults, among trials at high risk of bias, and among participants with higher eosinophil counts. We appraised the credibility of subgroup effects by the Instrument for Assessing the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN).37 We performed additional sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of results by multiple data transformation and imputation methods.

We conducted analyses by Stata v18 software (StataCorp, College Station, Tex).

Severe asthma exacerbations

Six trials (n = 536) informed effects of BT on overall severe asthma exacerbations (Fig 2, A). BT may reduce overall severe asthma exacerbations (IRR 0.81; 95% CI, 0.68 to 0.97; rate difference, 0.11 fewer events per patient-year; 95% CI, 0.19 to 0.02 fewer; low certainty).38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43 Most of this reduction occurred during the postprocedural period (6 weeks after BT; IRR 0.67; 95% CI, 0.50 to 0.89; rate difference, 0.67 fewer events per patient-year; 95% CI, 1.02 to 0.22 fewer; low certainty; Fig 2, B), with BT likely increasing severe exacerbations during the procedural period (up to 6 weeks after treatment; IRR 2.95; 95% CI, 1.20 to 7.25; rate difference, 0.08 more events per patient-year; 95% CI, 0.01 to 0.28 more; moderate certainty; Fig 2, C).

Asthma control

Five trials (n = 476) informed effects on asthma control, presented as measured by the ACQ-6 (0-6, with lower scores indicating better control; Fig 3, A).38, 39, 40, 41,43 BT may improve asthma control (MD −0.37; 95% CI, −0.67 to −0.07; RD to obtain a decrease of 0.5 points, 15.9% more; 95% CI, 3.0% to 28.4% more; low certainty).

Forest plot of BT vs control on (A) asthma control and (B) asthma-related quality of life.

Asthma-related quality of life

Six trials (n = 536) informed effects on asthma-related quality of life, presented as measured by the AQLQ (1-7, with higher scores indicating better quality; Fig 3, B).38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43 BT may improve asthma-related quality of life (MD 0.55; 95% CI, 0.28 to 0.82; RD to obtain an increase of 0.5 points, 19.1% more; 95% CI, 9.9% to 27.6%; low certainty).

Safety

Three trials (n = 492) informed effects on serious adverse events.38,39,43 BT likely increases the frequency of serious respiratory adverse events (excluding severe asthma exacerbations) over a median 52-week follow-up period (IRR 2.95; 95% CI, 1.20 to 7.25; rate difference, 0.01 more events per patient-year; 95% CI, 0 to 0.03 more; moderate certainty; see Fig E1 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). Most events occurred during the procedural period up to 6 weeks after treatment (IRR 5.98; 95% CI, 1.07 to 33.29; rate difference, 0.18 events per patient-year; 95% CI, 0.06 to 0.30; see Figs E2-E4 in the Online Repository) compared to the postprocedural period beyond 6 weeks (see Figs E5 and E6 in the Online Repository). Serious respiratory adverse events included atelectasis, hemoptysis, infection, and aspiration events requiring escalation of medical management (see Table E3 in the Online Repository). We found similar findings for all-cause serious adverse events (see Figs E7 and E8 in the Online Repository).

Similarly, compared to control, BT increased procedural severe (incapacitating, with inability to do work or usual activities; IRR 5.19; 95% CI, 2.20 to 12.23; see Fig E9 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org) and moderate (discomfort enough to cause interference with patients’ usual activities; IRR 2.40; 95% CI, 1.69 to 3.41; see Fig E10 in the Online Repository) respiratory adverse events. Most adverse events were reported as occurring within 1 day of the BT procedure and resolving on average within 7 days. Events requiring hospitalization or prolonging it resolved with standard therapy (eg, when indicated, hemoptysis managed with bronchial artery embolization).

Mortality

Three trials (n = 421) informed effects on overall mortality.38,39,43 One death was reported across the trials, attributed to a motor vehicle accident in a patient receiving BT. BT likely does not increase overall mortality (RD 0.4% more; 95% CI, 1.3% fewer to 2.1% more; moderate certainty; see Fig E11 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Overall, subgroup analyses showed no differential treatment effects according to risk of bias or baseline eosinophil level (see Tables E4 and E5 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). We were unable to perform subgroup analyses because of insufficient data reported between age groups, despite multiple attempts to contact study authors for age data (Appendix E3). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness across different data analysis and imputation strategies.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 RCTs involving 573 participants found that BT may reduce overall severe asthma exacerbations and may improve asthma control and asthma-related quality of life (low certainty), but it likely increases the risk of procedural severe asthma exacerbations and serious respiratory adverse events (moderate certainty).

Key strengths of this review include its being the most comprehensive analysis of BT in the management of severe asthma, incorporating all available RCTs investigating patient-important outcomes; its analysis of unpublished data from trial investigators to differentiate asthma exacerbations stratified by period (procedural, postprocedural, and overall); its incorporation of multidisciplinary input from asthma experts, frontline clinicians, and patient and caregiver partners;23,24 and its interpretation of the results by using structured GRADE8 and ICEMAN37 approaches and meeting GRADE requirements in full.44

Limitations of this review arise from constraints of the available evidence. First, BT has been shown to reduce both serum45 and bronchoalveolar lavage46 fluid eosinophil counts. Observational data have shown conflicting findings on the influence of eosinophilia toward clinical response to BT, with some studies suggesting a greater benefit in eosinophilic asthma and others suggesting greater benefit in noneosinophilic asthma.47,48 Only one trial, however, stratified analyses by baseline eosinophil levels.40 Communication with the company that did the AIR, AIR2, and RISA trials revealed that its trial statistician retired and its data were archived, noting that it would take several years to identify, clean, and transfer the data, so it was not feasible to share the information for our analyses (as outlined in Appendix E3). Consequently, our findings may not adequately represent the subgroup of patients with severe asthma who could derive the greatest benefit from BT. Given that BT is an invasive and resource-intensive intervention, future trials should report baseline eosinophil levels to better identify which patient subgroups benefit most from the therapy, and until future research provides clarity, candidate selection should be driven by response to known predictors49 as well as patient values and preferences.50 Second, 5 of the 6 trials lacked blinding of participants and outcome assessors, leading to a high risk of bias in measures of asthma control, asthma-related quality of life, and possibly severe exacerbations. The subgroup analyses, however, suggested low credibility for effect modification due to risk of bias. Recent methodologic studies have found no significant differences in treatment effects between blinded and unblinded studies, particularly for more objective outcomes such as severe asthma exacerbations, potentially questioning the necessity of using sham controls for procedural interventions in specific scenarios and outcomes.51, 52, 53 Despite this, given the potential influence of performance and detection bias in an unblinded setting, we perhaps overcautiously rated down the certainty of the evidence to account for the potential serious risk of bias. Third, no trials compared BT to other established therapies for severe asthma, such as biologics alone or in combination with BT. Given the complex nature of severe, treatment-resistant asthma, large, robust trials are needed to address key evidence gaps.3

The findings that BT may improve asthma control and asthma-related quality of life align with findings suggested in prior reviews.6,54 However, the applicability of previous findings was questioned because appraisals occurred before most patients initiated therapy with biologics. Expanding the applicability of BT, our updated review incorporates new trials that include patients receiving concurrent biologics and showed consistent effects with the previous trials. Moreover, we were able to systematically evaluate BT's effect on exacerbations by period (procedural, postprocedural, and overall) and found low-certainty evidence that BT may reduce the risk of long-term severe asthma exacerbations. These findings are consistent with observational studies,55 including the PAS2,56 BTGR,57 UKSAR,58 and BT10+59 cohorts, which reported sustained clinical benefits over up to 10 years after BT and similarly high rates of procedural serious respiratory adverse events (PAS2: 63 events in 279 patients; 0.23 events per patient; UKSAR: 14 events in 128 patients; 0.11 events per patient; BTGR: 44/157 [28.0%] patients experiencing one or more events). Additionally, the PAS256 and BTGR57 cohorts evaluated the long-term safety of BT, with low rates of serious respiratory adverse events at year 3 (17/250 [6.8%] patients experiencing one or more events; 0.10 events per patient) and year 5 (11/235 [4.7%] patients experiencing one or more events; 0.06 events per patient) after treatment.56 However, data from the BT10+ cohort found that 6 (6.7%) of 89 patients receiving BT developed new bronchiectasis during long-term follow-up, with no control participants developing bronchiectasis (0 of 18 patients).59 Thus, compared to anti-inflammatory therapy alone, the lung changes, if any, at 10 years or more after BT remain uncertain.

When considering BT for individual patients, the possible long-term improvements in a reduction in severe exacerbations, asthma symptom control, and asthma-related quality of life must be weighed against its potential procedural and long-term harms, resource implications, accessibility, and alignment with patient values and preferences. BT is a resource-intensive intervention requiring a skilled bronchoscopist, an anesthesiologist capable of administering moderate to deep sedation, coadministration of systemic corticosteroids, and typically 3 bronchoscopic sessions. This may confine BT to specialized centers. It is contraindicated in patients with implantable devices or those whose condition is too unstable to permit sedation and bronchoscopy, and it requires travel to designated facilities—factors that limit accessibility. Further, situations in medicine where there is uncertainty in the trade-off between treatment benefits and harms leads to preference-sensitive decisions and the need for personalization through shared decision-making.24,33 Therefore, patients with severe asthma that is refractory to standard care, patients who can access BT easily, and patients who prioritize low-certainty benefits in exacerbation reduction over the burdens and harms of added systemic corticosteroid bursts, moderate-certainty short-term harms of atelectasis, hemoptysis, infection, and aspiration events and uncertain long-term risks of bronchiectasis may favor BT. Conversely, those who are harm/risk averse, steroid avoidant, or face barriers accessing BT may prefer alternative severe asthma treatments with more favorable safety and availability profiles, including add-on long-acting muscarinic antagonists,15,60 macrolide antibiotics,61 and biologics.62, 63, 64 These findings highlight the need for updated guidelines that address the role of BT in severe asthma management and for clinicians to integrate patient values and preferences into the clinical decision-making process.50

At the systems level, policymakers, health systems, and insurers must weigh broader considerations. Notably, Boston Scientific, the manufacturers of the US Food and Drug Administration–approved Alair thermoplasty device, announced plans to discontinue servicing these systems by the end of 2025 because of evolving regulatory requirements and market conditions. However, a 1:1 randomized trial (BATA, NCT03765307) of 160 patients with severe asthma that is still symptomatic despite treatment with high-dose inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting β-agonists is comparing the similarity of Boston Scientific’s Alair system to SyMap’s Elation Bronchial Ablation system, with the primary end point being the rate of severe exacerbations per year over 12 months. This study’s planned completion date is in December 2028, and the findings of this trial and our review may inform further research and development of the Elation and other thermoplasty systems.
