Click-polymerized polyenamine membranes for efficient lithium extraction
Authors: Ziye Song, Wendi Zhang, Saisai Yu, Guangfeng Li, Ming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clean energy transition drives burgeoning global demand for lithium, highlighting the critical role of nanofiltration (NF) and reverse osmosis (RO) membranes for selective lithium extraction. However, conventional polyamide NF membranes, typically fabricated from hydrolysis-susceptible acyl chlorides, exhibit limited Li+/Mg2+ separation efficiency. Here, we report the fabrication of polyenamine membranes via amino-yne Michael addition reaction by substituting hydrolysis-susceptible acyl chlorides with activated alkyne monomers. The resulting NF membranes feature a positively charged surface, narrow pore size distribution, and exceptional chemical stability, achieving a high Li+/Mg2+ selectivity of 309. Similarly, by employing amine monomers that generate reduced intrinsic cavity, the same chemistry also affords RO membranes with NaCl rejection > 97%. Module-scale evaluations validated the potential of coupling click-polymerized NF and RO membranes for battery-grade Li2CO3 (> 99.9%) production from salt-lake brines and spent lithium-ion batteries. This versatile click-chemistry platform provides an alternative to conventional polyamides for sustainable critical resource recovery.

Introduction

With the constantly expanding development of energy storage materials, lithium (Li) is surging demand and forecast to increase production by 20 folds by 20501. At present, Li resources are mainly form salt-lake brines, accounting for ~60% of global Li resources2. Conventional industrial extraction processes, including absorption, precipitation, and calcination, are hindered by substantial energy consumption, environmental unfriendliness, and suboptimal extraction efficiencies3. Membrane-based separation technologies emerge as a highly promising alternative for sustainable lithium recovery, reducing energy consumption while enhancing separation efficiency and operational simplicity4,5. This potential is evidenced in applications ranging from monovalent/divalent ion separations (e.g., Cl⁻/SO42-) to reverse osmosis desalination. A typical lithium extraction process comprises sequential stages: evaporating to precipitate Na and K salts, nanofiltration to separate Li+ from Mg2+, reverse osmosis to concentrate Li+ in the NF permeate, and final precipitation to yield Li2CO3 as the end product (Fig. 1a)6. Since its emergence in the late 1970s, interfacial polymerization has had a transformative impact on desalination and nanofiltration, enabling the facile fabrication of ultrathin, highly cross-linking selective layers with exceptional scalability8. Conventional interfacial polymerization chemistry typically relies on the Schotten-Baumann-type polycondensation of acyl chloride monomers, such as trimesoyl chloride (TMC), with m-phenylenediamine (MPD) or piperazine (PIP)9,10, rendering polyamide membranes the prevailing choice for NF and RO8,11. Nevertheless, conventional polyamide membranes fabricated from amine-acyl chloride chemistry still suffer from several limitations, including the presence of side reactions of acyl chloride hydrolysis, susceptibility to chlorine oxidation and membrane fouling12,13. As the efficiency of NF membranes for Li+/Mg2+ separation critically depends on the synergistic interplay of size exclusion and Donnan (electrostatic) exclusion mechanisms6,14. Polyamide NF membranes are typically negatively charged with a wide pore size distribution arising from the hydrolysis of acyl chloride, which significantly decrease the separation selectivity of monovalent Li+ and divalent Mg2+, usually less than 2015. Strategies including molecular design, interface engineering, and post modification9,12,15–18 have been developed in the past few decades to enhance the separation performance of polyamide membranes, while exploring new chemistries for interfacial polymerization to form ultrathin membranes have been less explored19–21. Consequently, developing new interfacial polymerization chemistry beyond conventional amine-acyl chloride polycondensation chemistry is crucial for creating next-generation TFC membranes capable of high-selectivity mono-/divalent cation separations, an essential capability for sustainable resource recovery and water purification, yet unmet by current industrial-scale interfacial polymerization manufacturing.

To fabricate ultrathin membranes via interfacial polymerization, the chosen chemistry must exhibit rapid, high-yielding reaction kinetics at ambient temperature, with by-products that readily diffuse away from the interface. Click chemistry, renowned for its high efficiency, atom economy, and mild reaction requirements, represents a promising candidate and has been used for the synthesis of functional polymers22,23. In addition to the classical copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cyclo-addition (CuAAC)24,25, Diels-Alder reaction, and thiol-ene/yne addition, emerging amino-yne, hydroxyl-yne, and thiol-yne reactions have garnered growing interest due to their broader reactant scope, enhanced safety, and facile synthetic conditions26,27. Herein, we explore the amino-yne Michael addition reaction as an alternative to the conventional amine-acyl chloride nucleophilic substitution, to fabricate NF membranes via interfacial polymerization. Specifically, in the amino-yne nucleophilic addition reaction, activated alkyne monomers (ynones) were used instead of acyl chlorides to react with amines at room temperature, without the need for catalysts, yielding polyenamine networks. This amine-yne Michael reaction demonstrates rapid reaction kinetics under room temperature, facilitating the successfully fabrication of membranes via interfacial polymerization. More importantly, it circumvents the hydrolysis of acyl chlorides in conventional polyamide membranes and promotes the formation of a positive charged membrane surface with fewer defects in pore-size distribution.

The resultant polyenamine NF membranes exhibit high Li⁺/Mg2⁺ selectivity (309), albeit accompanied by relatively low water permeance, outperforming its polyamide counterparts and most state-of-the-art NF membranes. By employing the same chemistry, amine monomers with decreased intrinsic cavity sizes yielded RO membranes with high salt rejection (> 97% for NaCl), enabling further concentration of Li+ from NF permeates. Moreover, the chemical stability enables the polyenamine membranes to extract lithium from spent lithium-ion batteries. Module-scale evaluations demonstrate that the NF and RO TFC membranes, both fabricated via the amino-yne Michael reaction, exhibit enhanced lithium extraction performance, including high selectivity for Li+ over Mg2+ and efficient lithium concentration, thereby producing battery-grade Li2CO3.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of polyenamine membranes

Compared with other click reactions, amino-yne click polymerization proceeds rapidly under mild conditions and does not require toxic metal catalysts or external initiators such as UV irradiation20,25. Amines, which are key building blocks in polyamides and polyurethanes and are widely available as commercial monomers due to their low cost, were chosen to react with activated alkynes to prepare polyenamine membranes (Fig. 1b). This catalyst-free and ambient-condition process provides a simple, scalable, and economically attractive route for fabricating polyenamine membranes. The primary challenge to introducing new types of monomers into interfacial polymerization chemistry lies in identifying a suitable organic solvent that satisfies the solubility requirements of both the monomers and the resulting polymer, while maintaining low miscibility with water to preserve a well-defined interface. Unlike traditional acyl chlorides, which exhibit good solubility in hexane, the prevalent solvent in interfacial polymerization for ultrathin film preparation8,9, alkyne-based monomers show minimal solubility in this non-polar solvent. After numerous solvent optimizations, ethyl acetate was selected as the organic phase for membrane fabrication via amino-yne click nucleophilic addition reaction27 (Supplementary Fig. 5). As illustrated in Fig. 1c, membranes were prepared via interfacial polymerization, wherein keto-enamine bonds formed between the alkyne and amine monomers, while amide bonds formed between acyl chloride and amine in the control sample. Images of obtained polyenamine TFC membranes exhibit a uniform coating of the pale yellow-colored nanofilm (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 6), whereas the polyamide membrane exhibits a transparent coloration (Fig. 2b). The resultant polyenamine membrane demonstrated a rougher surface than the polyamide membrane, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 2c, d) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Fig. 2g, h). Furthermore, cross-sectional SEM images revealed a pronounced thickness disparity, with the polyenamine membrane measured at 873 nm, whereas the polyamide membrane was significantly thinner at 75 nm (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). This pronounced difference in membrane thickness and roughness could be attributable to the intrinsically slower reaction kinetics of the amino-yne Michael reaction relative to acyl chloride-amine condensation reaction28, as well as the different polarity of the organic phase. While the relatively slower kinetics of polyenamine formation result in increased membrane thickness, it avoids the uncontrolled hydrolysis of acyl chloride monomers that plagues conventional interfacial polymerization chemistry9,29, leading to the formation of a positively charged membrane with a narrow pore size distribution. In addition, employing the same chemistry with reduced cavity of amine monomers produced smaller pore size of polyenamine membranes, and the resulting membrane thicknesses follow the order PE-RCC1 > PE-4N > PE-3N, which is consistent with the trend of reaction rate monitored by in-situ UV-Vis of alkyne monomer consumption rates during interfacial polymerization (Supplementary Fig. 10). The amino-yne click polymerization between secondary amines and ynones proceeds via a catalyst-free, atom-economical Michael addition mechanism. Initially, the nucleophilic nitrogen of the secondary amine attacks the electron-deficient β-carbon of the carbonyl-activated alkyne, generating a zwitterionic allenyl enolate intermediate. Subsequent proton transfer from the positively charged nitrogen to the α-carbon yields a stable enamine Michael adduct31. Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (SSNMR) analysis revealed the disappearance of the alkyne characteristic resonance, while a new peak corresponding to the alkene carbon (94 ppm) in the keto-enamine linkage emerged (Fig. 2i), confirming the complete reaction specificity in the resulting membrane via interfacial polymerization. In contrast, the control sample ― TPC-RCC1 membrane exhibited characteristic amide carbonyl resonances at 173 ppm and carboxyl resonances at 166 ppm, indicating the hydrolysis of unreacted acyl chloride groups. Furthermore, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy corroborated these findings, demonstrating signature absorptions for amide bonds32 and keto-enamine bonds (Supplementary Fig. 12). Notably, the FTIR spectrum of TPC-RCC1 displayed a prominent peak at 1280 cm−1, assigned to the C-O stretching vibration of carboxylic acid groups (-COOH)33. This peak serve as a diagnostic of the hydrolysis of unreacted acyl chloride groups during interfacial polymerization, a pervasive side reaction in conventional polyamide chemistry34. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the PE-RCC1 membrane suggested the formation of keto-enamine bonds (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Figs. 13–16). Moreover, the XPS spectrum of TPC-RCC1 provided further evidence of the hydrolytic degradation of acyl chloride. The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum of TPC-RCC1 exhibited a distinct component at approximately 286.5 eV, corresponding to the carbon signal of the carboxyl group, while the O 1 s spectrum displayed a peak at 533.1 eV, characteristic of carboxylic acid oxygen (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Fig. 14b)32,33. In contrast, the absence of these carboxyl group signatures in the PE-RCC1 membrane across all aforementioned techniques provides compelling evidence that the Michael reaction, which utilizes non-hydrolyzable alkyne-amino coupling, effectively avoids the hydrolysis-induced defect generation and the unwanted formation of carboxyl groups commonly observed in acyl chloride-based interfacial polymerization. Benefiting from the absence of hydrolyzed carboxyl groups, the zeta potential of the polyenamine membrane can be effectively enhanced, thereby improving its Donnan exclusion toward divalent cations. Furthermore, thermal gravity analysis (TGA) results demonstrated that the PE-RCC1 membrane was stable up to 300 °C (Supplementary Fig. 17). Grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results revealed that polyenamine membranes are primarily amorphous with some crystalline domains (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19), in contrast to the typically homogeneous amorphous structure of polyamide membranes.

To verify whether amino-yne Michael addition could generate positively charged surface membranes, zeta potential measurements were conducted to assess the surface potential of the membranes. Compared to the TPC-RCC1 membrane, which exhibited a significant negative charge of − 12 mV at pH 5.9 (Fig. 2l), consistent with the presence of hydrolytically generated carboxylate groups (-COO⁻) identified by FTIR and XPS results33, the PE-RCC1/4 N/3 N membranes synthesized via amino-yne Michael reaction displayed a positively charged surface of approximately + 9.2 mV, + 7 mV, and 0 mV, respectively. This fundamental shift from negative to neutral/positive charge can be attributed to the elimination of acyl chloride hydrolysis, a common issue in traditional polyamide interfacial polymerization chemistry9. In addition, the positive charge originates from the protonation of the tertiary amine within the network, generating quaternary ammonium species (Supplementary Fig. 20)20. These results suggest that the absence of acyl chloride hydrolysis during polymerization enables the alkyne monomers to effectively enhance the membrane’s positive surface charge, facilitating reducing structural defects in the resulting membranes. Noticeably, the isoelectronic points of PE-3N ( ~ 5.8) and PE-4N ( ~ 7.2) are significantly higher than previous studies that utilized grafting strategies to block the formation of carboxyl groups, suggesting these membranes may exhibit exceptional anti-fouling performance against small cationic organic foulants like dodecyl trimethylammonium bromide when applied in reverse osmosis wastewater treatment processes12. Water contact angle (WCA) measurements revealed subtle yet meaningful differences in surface hydrophilicity (Supplementary Fig. 21). The TPC-RCC1 membrane displayed a slightly lower contact angle of 31.9°, indicative of higher hydrophilicity, primarily due to the presence of abundant polar amide bonds and hydrolytically generated carboxyl groups30. In addition, the PE-RCC1 membrane exhibited a moderately higher water contact angle of 36.9°, reflecting the different chemical composition of the enamine network. The WCA of polyenamine membranes follows the order of PE-4N < PE-RCC1 < PE-3N, which can be attributed to the fewer hydrophobic aromatic rings in the PE-4N membrane and the lower content of hydrophilic nitrogen atoms in PE-3N35.

To validate the hypothesis that the amino-yne Michael reaction could reduce structural defects and thereby yield membranes with a narrower pore size distribution, the molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) and pore size distribution of membranes were determined based on the rejection of PEG solutes with diverse molecular weights. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, the PE-RCC1 membrane exhibited a MWCO of 552 Da, which is comparable to that of the TPC-RCC1 membrane (581 Da). However, notable differences were observed in pore size distribution, the TPC-RCC1 membrane exhibited a significantly broader distribution, while the PE-RCC1 membrane displayed a much narrower and more defined peak, although both centered around 6.5 Å (Fig. 2m). This structural disparity is attributed to the presence of hydrolysis-induced defects within the polyamide network, where uncontrolled formation of carboxyl groups (-COOH) disrupts polymer chain packing and compromises structural uniformity9,34. In contrast, the Michael addition reaction, by avoiding acyl chloride hydrolysis, enables the formation of a more homogeneous network architecture with reduced defects. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analyses suggested that the porosity of the membranes followed the order of PE-RCC1 (134.5 m2 g−1)> PE-4N (38.6 m2 g−1) > PE-3N (28.7 m2 g−1) > TPC-RCC1 (5.6 m2 g−1), as determined by CO2 sorption analyses at 195 K because of its smaller kinetic diameter (3.30 Å) than N2 (3.64 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 23a). The pore size distribution of membranes obtained from BET analysis closely aligned with PEG rejection results, further corroborating the observed trends in membrane porosity (Supplementary Fig. 23b).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to simulate the structures and pore size distributions of the polyenamine and polyamide membranes. The calculated fractional free volume (FFV, indicated by the gray region) (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 1) decreased from 24.5% for TPC-RCC1 to 21.7% (PE-RCC1), 12.6% (PE-4N) and 10.6% (PE-3N), following the order of the cavity size of the amine monomers. Furthermore, the calculated pore size distribution of the PE-RCC1 membrane was slightly narrower compared to that of TPC-RCC1 (Fig. 2n), which is consistent with experimental PEG rejection analyses and BET results. Due to the flexible amine monomers, the FFV of membrane models obtained at 10 bar is slightly lower than that at 1 bar (Supplementary Fig. 25), indicating a denser structure and smaller pore size distribution. This convergence of experimental results and simulations conclusively suggested that the broader pore size distribution of conventional polyamide chemistry attributed to hydrolysis-generated carboxyl defects. The structural integrity of the click-polymerized membranes, combined with their desirable positive charge, positions them as promising candidates for energy-efficient and high-selectivity separations in lithium extraction. Notably, the mean effective pore size (µp) of the PE-RRC1 membrane lies between the sizes of dehydrated monovalent and hydrated divalent ions (with monovalent ions being more prone to dehydration due to their lower hydration energies as reported by the previous studies; Fig. 2o and Supplementary Table 2)15,36, suggesting a molecular sieving mechanism, particularly for selective Li+/Mg2+ separation. Moreover, by employing the same amino-yne Michael reaction with amine monomers possessing smaller intrinsic cavity sizes, the resulting PE-3N/4 N membranes exhibit a reduced pore size (~ 3.5 Å), which is suitable for Li+ concentration and desalination, given that the stokes diameter of Li+ is 4.8 Å (and Li+ ion is prone to partial dehydration under pressure-driven separation due to its relatively lower hydration energy)15,36. As interfacial polymerization is intrinsically a reaction-diffusion-driven process, the diffusion kinetics of the aqueous monomers fundamentally determine the final membrane architecture. To quantitatively probe this, diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) was employed to monitor the amine monomers in solution. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 26, the monomer diffusion coefficients exhibit a strict inverse dependence on their molecular weights. Specifically, the bulkier RCC1 monomer experiences significantly hindered diffusion (2.14 × 10−10 m2 s−1) compared to its smaller counterparts, 4 N (3.82 × 10−10 m2 s−1) and 3 N (4.42 × 10−10 m2 s−1). During the interfacial reaction, this sluggish diffusion restricts the mass transfer of RCC1 to the reactive zone. This diffusion-limited monomer supply inherently limits the local cross-linking density, ultimately driving the formation of a looser, thicker, higher free volume membrane structure of PE-RCC137,38.

Ion separation performance of polyenamine membranes

The ion separation performance of polyamide and polyenamine TFC membranes was investigated using a cross-flow device (Supplementary Fig. 27). The monomer concentration and reaction time used for preparing the polyenamine membranes were systematically optimized (Supplementary Figs. 28–38). As depicted in Fig. 3a, the PE-RCC1 membrane exhibited comparable divalent cation rejection performance to TPC-RCC1 (Mg2+: 97.1% rejection for PE-RCC1, 95.9% rejection for TPC-RCC1), while displaying higher rejection of monovalent cation (Li+: 69.9% rejection for PE-RCC1, 58.5% rejection for TPC-RCC1) due to its positively charged surface and narrower pore size distribution. As expected, the salt rejection of the TPC-RCC1 membrane followed the typical trend for negatively charged NF membranes: Na2SO4 > MgCl2 > NaCl > LiCl (Supplementary Fig. 39)17. The high rejection of MgCl2 by TPC-RCC1, despite its negative surface charge, is likely dominated by size sieving rather than Donnan exclusion. Moreover, the PE-3N and PE-4N membranes demonstrated high rejection rates for both mono- and divalent cations (> 95%), indicating their potential for RO desalination or Li+ concentration applications. To elucidate the roles of surface charge and pore size in NF performance, a mixed salt solution of 1000 ppm MgCl2 and 1000 ppm LiCl was used to evaluate the Li+/Mg2+ separation performance of TPC-RCC1 and PE-RCC1. The PE-RCC1 membrane exhibited significantly improved performance, achieving 99.83% rejection of Mg2+ and 47.45% rejection of Li⁺, resulting in a Li+/Mg2+ selectivity of 309, over 20 times higher than that of TPC-RCC1 (15.1) (Fig. 3b). The relatively low Li+ rejection observed in Li+/Mg2+ mixture compared to single-salt LiCl solution indicates the co-ion competition and Donnan equilibrium, where the lower charge density and higher diffusion of Li+ favor its transport over Mg2+39,40,. Compared with the positively charged PE-RCC1 membranes, the negatively charged polyamide TPC-RCC1 membrane exhibited lower LiCl rejection (25%) and Li+/Mg2+ selectivity, which can be attributed to the counter-ion competition effect39. The limited Li+/Mg2+ selectivity of TPC-RCC1 will compromise the purity of the recovered Li2CO3 and may necessitate additional purification steps to meet battery-grade specifications. It is worth noting that the PE-RCC1 membrane exhibited lower water permeance (16.9 LMH) than TPC-RCC1 (26.2 LMH), a reduction attributable to its lower surface hydrophilicity, thicker membrane and finer pore structure. This coupling of high selectivity and low permeance intrinsically stems from the ubiquitous permeability-selectivity trade-off governing separation membranes41–43. The effect of Mg2+/Li+ ratio (MLR) on separation performance was also evaluated to simulate lithium extraction from salt-lake brines of varying quality. As shown in Fig. 3d, the PE-RCC1 membrane maintained > 99% Mg2+ rejection across a wide MLR range, while sustaining high Li+/Mg2+ selectivity. However, as the MLR increased, LiCl rejection decreased slightly, likely due to intensified co-ion competition under Donnan equilidrium6,39. Figure 3e shows that the permeance of PE-RCC1 membrane decreased slightly with increased Mg2+ concentration, while no apparent decreased rejection of Mg2+ was observed, confirming its robustness under practical separation conditions. In addition, as operation pressure increased, the water flux of PE-RCC1 rose accordingly, while maintaining high Mg2+ rejection (> 95%) (Fig. 3f). Moreover, the effect of feed pH on the separation efficacy of PE-RCC1 was systematically evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 40). The membrane exhibits an inverse relationship between pH and selectivity, with the Li+/Mg2+ selectivity decreasing from 387.6 at pH = 1 to 259.8 at pH = 9. At highly acidic conditions, the increased positive surface charge of the membrane intensifies electrostatic repulsion with cations. Because this enhanced Donnan effect restricts the divalent Mg2+ much more severely than the monovalent Li⁺, the selectivity peaks at low pH. Importantly, the results confirm that PE-RCC1 demonstrates chemical stability, maintaining high Li+/Mg2+ selectivity across a wide pH range.

The above results demonstrate that the PE-RCC1 membrane efficiently separates Li+/Mg2+. Nevertheless, Li+ concentration is also a critical step in practical lithium extraction, where RO membranes are typically empolyed3. In contrast to previous studies that often rely on heating or rotary evaporation to concentrate Li+ ― a process that requires evaporating large volumes of water following nanofiltration―the PE-3N/4 N membranes prepared via the same Michael addition chemistry were chosen as RO membranes in this study to enable further Li+ concentration16,45. Compared to the PE-RCC1 membrane, the PE-3N and PE-4N membranes achieved high Li+ rejection (> 97%) due to their smaller pore size (~ 3.5 Å), which are significantly smaller than Li+ stokes diameter (4.8 Å) (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, the PE-3N membrane exhibited consistent rejection of LiCl under different salt concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 32) and operation pressures (Supplementary Fig. 33). Long-term stability tests revealed that the PE-3N, PE-4N, and PE-RCC1 membranes maintained stable for at least 20 days, with the rejection rates for Mg2+ remaining consistently high (greater than 98 %), thus suggesting the excellent long-term stability of these click-polymerized polyenamine membranes (Supplementary Fig. 41).

Benefiting from its positive charge surface and narrow pore size distribution, the PE-RCC1 membrane exhibited a rapid lithium transport rate and improved Li+/Mg2+ selectivity, outperforming most of the conventional polyamide membranes and advanced materials-based membranes such as metal organic frameworks (MOFs), COFs, and porous organic cages (POCs). As illustrated in Fig. 3h, the PE-RCC1 membrane achieves a remarkably high Li+/Mg2+ selectivity (309) together with a decent Li+ transport rate (0.2 mol m−2 h−1), which is comparable to―or even surpasses―other membrane-based separation technologies such as electrodialysis (ED)46 and forward osmosis (FO)47 (Supplementary Table 3). Compared with state-of-the-art lithium extraction nanofiltration membranes, such as the polyamide QSPIP-TMC45 and MOF-based membranes48 (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Table 4), the PE-RCC1 membrane exhibits high ion selectivity, albeit with slightly lower permeance―a trade-off commonly observed between permeance and selectivity41. In addition, the rapid kinetics of amino-yne Michael reaction provide a significant advantage in membrane fabrication, enabling markedly shorter preparation times compared to other non-polyamide state-of-the-art membranes, including COF-, MOF-, and POC-based membranes. (Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Table 5). Meanwhile, for RO, the PE-3N and PE-4N membranes demonstrate high NaCl rejection compared to previous reported COF-, MOF- and polymer-based membranes, and their performance is comparable to that of conventional polyamide membranes (Fig. 3j and Supplementary Table 6). These results show that the polyenamine membrane is a promising platform for lithium extraction, a process vital for securing strategic mineral resources, while its robust performance also highlights its potential as a competitive technology for desalination, where achieving high salt rejection remains a central challenge8.

Chemical stability of the polyenamine membranes

Given the widespread application of chlorine as disinfectants in water treatment processes, the chlorine resistance of polyamide NF and RO membranes represents a critical factor governing their long-term operational stability13,49. To evaluate this property, the PE-RCC1/4 N/3 N, TPC-RCC1, the previously reported positively charged polyamide PEI-TMC50 and two commercialized membranes (NF90, SW30) were immersed in a 1000 ppm sodium hypochlorite solution for 24 h, thoroughly washed, and subsequently tested. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the PE-RCC1/4 N/3 N membranes retained their MgCl2 rejection efficiency, exhibiting no measurable decline, due to the presence of chemically robust enamine linkages within the polymer network27. In contrast, the MgCl2 rejection of the TPC-RCC1 membrane was decreased from 95.9% to 31.7%, accompanied by a marked increase in water permeance increased (71.4 L m−2 h−1 MPa−1), indicating structure degradation of the membrane after NaClO treatment. It is well recognized that exposure of polyamide membranes to NaClO can induce either irreversible aromatic-ring chlorination or reversible N-chlorination reactions, both of which compromise amide bonds integrity and ultimately degrade the membrane structure51,52. As a result, the PEI-TMC and commercial (NF90 and SW30) membranes showed pronounced reductions in MgCl2 rejection after hypochlorite treatment. Complementary evidence from FTIR spectroscopy further corroborated these findings. In contrast, pronounced spectral changes were observed for the TPC-RCC1 and PEI-TMC membranes, indicative of amide linkage degradation (Supplementary Fig. 43). Given the growing demand for lithium, its recovery from spent lithium-ion batteries has emerged as a crucial supplementary approach to extraction from salt-lake brines, offering a more sustainable lithium supply strategy20. Therefore, acid and alkali stability tests were also performed to evaluate the chemical stability of the membranes, as conventional polyamide membranes typically degradation when pH < 2 due to the hydrolysis of amide bonds53. All polyenamine membranes exhibited stability under both strongly acidic and alkaline conditions, with no observable decline in separation performance (Fig. 4d). Notably, the FTIR spectra of click-polymerized membranes exhibited no change after immersed in acid and alkali (Supplementary Fig. 45), further confirming their chemical robustness. Furthermore, we evaluated the long-term chemical stability of the click-polymerized membranes. While initial tests confirmed robust stability after 7 days of immersion in acidic and alkaline solutions (Supplementary Fig. 46), practical lithium extraction from spent batteries typically involves highly aggressive acidic environments. To emulate these harsh conditions, the polyenamine membranes and a state-of-the-art commercial benchmark (MPS-34) were subjected to immerse 2 M H2SO4 for 7 days. Strikingly, the click-polymerized membranes fully retained their exceptional separation performance. In stark contrast, the commercial MPS-34 membrane suffered degradation, evidenced by a marked decline in MgCl2 rejection (from 94.6% to 86.7%) and a concomitant increase in water permeance (Fig. 4e). Corroborating this macroscopic stability, FTIR spectroscopy revealed absolutely no structural alterations in our membranes following the extreme acid exposure (Supplementary Fig. 47), unequivocally demonstrating the chemical robustness. Collectively, these results highlight the exceptional structural robustness and chemical durability of polyenamine membranes under harsh conditions, including oxidative (chlorine), strongly acid, and alkali environments.

To demonstrate the practical viability of these chemical-stable click-polymerized membranes for real-world lithium recycling, spent lithium-ion battery cathode materials (NCM-523, LiNi0.5Co0.2Mn0.3O2) were leached with 2 M H2SO4. The resulting leachate was processed via a two-stage nanofiltration cascade, followed by reverse osmosis to concentrate the NF permeate (Supplementary Fig. 48). As depicted in Fig. 4f, the PE-RCC1 membrane dramatically suppressed the permeation of divalent metal ions, reducing Ni2+ from 3308 to a mere 2.2 ppm, while allowing the unimpeded passage of most Li+. Notably, the initial Mn2+ concentration in the leachate was inherently low. This is because manganese predominantly exists in the tetravalent state (Mn4+) within the cathode lattice, which readily precipitates as insoluble MnO2 during H2SO4 leaching and is removed by pre-filtration54,55. Following the RO stage, the Li+ concentration in the purified permeate was successfully enriched from 689.7 to 2068 ppm. The purity of the obtained Li2CO3 product was high (99.83%), and the PXRD pattern matched well with the standard Li2CO3 phase (Supplementary Fig. 49). Collectively, these results highlight the immense industrial potential of click-polymerized membranes for sustainable, closed-loop battery recycling.

Molecular dynamic simulation of ions transport through the membranes

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to elucidate the relationship between the structural characteristics of the membranes and their separation performance by investigating the transport behavior of mixed ionic species. Next, we investigated the mean square displacements (MSD) (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 51), which revealed that Cl- ions traverse the PE-RCC1 and TPC-RCC1 membranes more rapidly than the counter cations (Fig. 4h, i). The calculated diffusion coefficients of Li+ and Mg2+ ions were 8.16 and 5.77 Å2 s−1, respectively, for the PE-RCC1 membrane, and 14.52 and 9.48 Å2 s−1 for the TPC-RCC1 membrane (Supplementary Fig. 52). The Cl- diffusion coefficients of PE-RCC1 (8.85 Å2 s−1) were higher than that of TPC-RCC1 (15.53 Å2 s−1) across the PE-RCC1 and TPC-RCC1 membranes, respectively. Notably, the diffusion of cation species into the PE-RCC1 membrane were slightly lower than those across the TPC-RCC1 membrane. This indicates that the Mg2+ ions experience greater resistance within the positively charged PE-RCC1 membrane compared with the negatively charged TPC-RCC1 membrane due to the Donnan effect, thereby yielding the markedly higher Li+/Mg2+ selectivity observed for the click-polymerized membrane. This enhancement is attributed to the stronger electrostatic exclusion and a narrower pore size distribution, in agreement with experimental observations.

To elucidate the thermodynamic barriers governing ion translocation, potential of mean force (PMF) profiles were extracted from molecular dynamic simulations (Figs. 4j, k and Supplementary Fig. 53). The simulations reveal that PE-RCC1 imposes a substantially higher energy barrier for Mg2+ permeation (324.8 kJ mol−1) compared to TPC-RCC1 (236.6 kJ mol−1). Similarly, Li+ experiences a greater translocation barrier through PE-RCC1 (205.9 kJ mol−1) than through TPC-RCC1 (139.2 kJ mol−1). This heightened resistance can be attributed to the intrinsic nature of PE-RCC1: its narrower pore dimensions intensify steric hindrance, while its positively charged surface exerts strong electrostatic repulsion toward cations. Crucially, the increase in the energy barrier from TPC-RCC1 to PE-RCC1 is significantly more pronounced for the divalent Mg2+ (an increase of 88.2 kJ mol−1) than for the monovalent Li+ (66.7 kJ mol−1). This differential thermodynamic penalty effectively suppresses Mg2+ transport relative to Li+, thereby fundamentally rationalizing the high Li+/Mg2+ selectivity observed in the experiments.

Preparation of polyenamine membrane modules for lithium extraction

To assess the practical applicability of the polyenamine membranes, a streamlined separation protocol was developed as a proof of concept for lithium extraction from a simulated high Mg2+/Li+ ratio brine (Mg2+/Li+ = 40; Fig. 5a). The simulated salt-lake brine solution56 (60 L, containing 5.64 g/L MgCl2 and 0.141 g/L LiCl) was employed as the feed model to demonstrate the practical application potential for lithium extraction and concentration (Fig. 5d). A two-stage nanofiltration and reverse osmosis systems were employed. To further validate the practical utility of the click-polymerized membranes under complex and demanding real-world conditions, we evaluated their lithium extraction performance using raw brine sourced from the Qaidam salt-lake (Supplementary Fig. 57a). Following a two-stage nanofiltration cascade, the concentrations of competing divalent cations decreased drastically (Mg2+: from 76,746 to 1.5 ppm; Ca2+: from 52 to 0.007 ppm), demonstrating near-perfect rejection (Fig. 5i). The highly purified Li+ permeate (1008 ppm) was subsequently concentrated via a RO process, yielding a Li+-rich retentate of 4013.3 ppm. Finally, thermal precipitation with excess Na2CO3 successfully converted the concentrated Li+ into solid Li2CO3 with a high purity of exceeding 99% (Supplementary Fig. 57c). These results confirm a highly efficient, scalable pathway from unrefined brine to high-purity lithium materials. Collectively, these findings underscore the strong potential of polyenamine membranes for high-efficiency lithium extraction from complex brine matrices.

Discussion

In summary, an amino-yne click Michael reaction was employed to fabricate NF and RO membranes. Unlike traditional polyamide membranes, which are prone to hydrolysis and consequently develop negatively charged surfaces and irregular pore structures, the resulting polyenamine membrane exhibited a positively surface charge and a more homogeneous, narrower pore size distribution. The polyenamine NF membrane achieved an exceptional Li+/Mg2+ selectivity of 309―over 20 times higher than that of its negatively charged polyamide counterpart. Moreover, owing to the presence of keto-enamine linkages instead of amide bonds, the polyenamine membranes demonstrated resistance to chlorine, acidic, and alkaline conditions. Using the same chemistry, RO membranes were prepared by employing monomers with smaller cavity sizes, achieving high NaCl rejection. When integrated into continuous spiral-wound membrane modules, the polyenamine NF and RO membranes enable direct lithium extraction from salt-lake brines and spent lithium-ion batteries, producing battery-grade Li2CO3. Collectively, this work highlights the amino-yne Michael addition reaction as a versatile interfacial polymerization platform for fabricating high-performance separation membranes with great potential for applications in resource recovery and water purification.

Methods

General

Chemicals and materials used in this work have been shown as chemicals and materials in the Supplementary Information.

Preparation of polyenamine TFC membranes

The PVDF ultrafiltration membrane was used as a substrate for TFC membrane fabrication. Before used, the PVDF membrane was immersed in PEG solution (400 Da, 20 wt%) overnight to improve its hydrophilicity. Then the PVDF membrane was washed and placed in a PTFE holder module, and an RCC1 solution (0.5 w/v%) was poured onto the PVDF membrane and kept for 5 min. Then the excess aqueous solution was removed and dried under ambient temperature. Next, a solution of PE in ethyl acetate (0.25 w/v%) was poured over the PVDF substrate and allowed reaction for 15 min. After reaction, the membrane was dried in an oven at 333 K for 10 minutes and rinsed with ethyl acetate and ethanol and immersed in water for further tests. PE-4N and PE-3N TFC membranes were fabricated with similar methods.

Preparation of polyamide TFC membrane

The PVDF ultrafiltration membrane was used as a substrate for TFC membrane fabrication. Before used, the PVDF membrane was immersed in PEG solution (400 Da, 20 wt%) overnight to improve its hydrophilicity. Then the PVDF membrane was washed and placed in a PTFE holder module, and an RCC1 solution (0.5 w/v%) was poured onto the PVDF membrane and kept for 5 min. Then the excess aqueous solution was removed and dried under ambient temperature. Next, a solution of TPC in hexane (0.5 w/v%) was poured over the PVDF substrate and allowed reaction for 15 min. After reaction, the membrane was dried in oven at 333 K for 10 min and rinsed with hexane and immersed in water for further tests.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations

Models of monomer molecules are constructed and described with the general AMBER force field58. Here, stoichiometric quantities of each monomer were placed in a non-periodic simulation box. The implemented crosslinking algorithm of GMXPolymer59 based on Gromacs-2023.6, periodically calculated the distance between predefined reactive sites on the monomers every 100 ps. A bond was formed between reaction sites if the distance fell below a predetermined threshold, which was established based on the maximum crosslinking observed in preliminary runs and the target experimental degree of reaction, with a maximum reaction distance of 6 Angstrom used. Post-bonding, a minimization algorithm was applied to equilibrate the newly formed, and initially elongated, bonds. Any unreacted sites were subsequently saturated, and residual monomers were removed from the system. After crosslinked, each containing a polymer centrally placed underwent equilibration over 50 ns using NVT (number volume temperature) MD simulations. The adequacy of equilibration was confirmed by stable measurements of total potential energies and densities, with the last 20 ns of simulation showing no significant structural changes. In all simulations, GMXPolymer59 is used in generating amorphous polymer models and polymerization. Gromacs-2023.6 software package60 is used in both the MD step of polymerization and equilibration.

For the ion diffusion simulations inside the protonated membrane, the box sizes of systems TPC-RCC1 and PE-RCC1 were 5.7 × 5.7 × 18.0 nm3 and 6.2 × 6.2 × 18.0 nm3, respectively. The thickness of the simulated membranes is ~ 5.5 nm. The number of water molecules were approximately 12000 and 15000 for each system, respectively. The feed side initially contained 40 Li⁺, 40 Mg2+, and 120 Cl− ions, corresponding to 40 LiCl and 40 MgCl2 formula units. Because conventional MD simulations in this study can hardly sample sufficient spontaneous ion-entry events within an accessible simulation time, additional ions were inserted into the membrane region close to the feed side to accelerate the sampling of ion-in-membrane configurations. Specifically, 100 random water molecules in this region were removed and replaced by 20 Li⁺, 20 Mg2+, and 60 Cl− ions. This strategy was used to investigate the local stability and diffusion behavior of ions after entering the membrane phase, rather than to describe the spontaneous ion-entry process or to calculate the ion permeation rate. To prevent ions from crossing the z-direction periodic boundary and entering the opposite side of the membrane, a graphene barrier layer was introduced near the z-boundary during model construction. The force-field topology of the graphene layer was generated using OBGMX61, and the graphene atoms were position-restrained during the simulations. The complete systems were then energy-minimized using the steepest descent algorithm, followed by 10 ns NVT equilibration and 10 ns NPT equilibration. The final configurations from the NPT equilibration were used as the initial structures for 30 ns NVT production simulations. The integration timestep was 2 fs, and the simulation trajectories were recorded every 1 ps. The temperature was maintained at 298 K using the v-rescale thermostat. During the NPT equilibration, the pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the Berendsen method62. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method63,64. A cutoff distance of 1.2 nm was used for van der Waals interactions, and the LINCS algorithm65 was employed to constrain covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms. The mean square displacement (MSD) analysis was performed using only the 30 ns NVT production trajectories. The apparent diffusion coefficients of ions within the membrane phase were obtained by fitting the MSD curves over the 10–30 ns interval of the production trajectories.

The potential of mean force (PMF) for the insertion of Li and Mg ions into the protonated membrane systems was computed using the umbrella sampling technique within the Gromacs-2023.6 software package. The reaction coordinate, ξ, was defined as the z-coordinate of the one Li or Mg ions relative to the membrane. A total of 400 independent sampling windows were established along this coordinate, spaced about 0.3 Å intervals, covering the desired pathway. In each window, the Li or Mg ions was harmonically restrained to a target position using a virtual spring with a force constant of 1000 kj mol−1 nm−2, implemented via the fix spring command. The initial target for the first window was defined as the Li or Mg ions at the start of the simulation. The simulation protocol comprised two phases per window. First, a 1000 ps equilibration run in the NVT ensemble was performed at 298 K, allowing the system to relax near the target position. This was followed by a 1000 ps production run for data acquisition.
