Transport Properties of NaCl in Aqueous Solution and Hydrogen Solubility in Brine
Authors: Junfang Zhang, Michael B. Clennell, David N. Dewhurst
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Ion transport properties and hydrogen solubility in brine play pivotal roles in various engineering and scientific scopes including chemical, physical, geochemical, and geothermal domains. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to obtain transport properties of NaCl in the binary H2O + NaCl system using different force fields. Brine density, ion diffusivity, molar conductivity, conductivity, and hydrogen solubilities were obtained as functions of temperature and salt concentration. We compared the performance of different force fields against the experimental correlation model and developed three mathematical models. The first was the modified brine density model based on the simulated brine density over a wide range of salinity levels, and the second and third analytical mathematical models were derived for the ion diffusivity and molar conductivity as a function of salinity and temperature. The results of this study illustrated that the modified brine density model not only produced the same results of the previous model for lower salinity levels but also applied well to predict the brine density for a higher salinity level. The derived mathematical models indicated that the ion diffusivity and molar conductivity decreased linearly with salinity, and the slope and y-intercept of the lines of diffusivity and molar conductivity versus temperature were third-order polynomials of temperature. The developed models provided the mechanism for the behavior of decreasing molar conductivity with increasing salinity and increasing conductivity with increasing salinity. The directions of the effect of salinity on the molar conductivity and conductivity were opposite. The molar conductivity increased with a decreasing salinity level. However, the conductivity increased with increasing salinity, as the contribution of the ion concentration or salinity level to conductivity dominated over that of the ion movement.

Introduction

The investment in new technologies such as in production, storage, and use of hydrogen (H2) is becoming increasingly important as the world moves toward net zero emissions. Hydrogen as a clean energy produced on an industrial scale and used across power production, transportation, and for applications such as steel production requires large-scale pipelines and storage facilities, including potentially underground storage. Therefore, understanding ion transport properties and hydrogen solubility in underground brine water is critical for predicting the long-term performance of a storage reservoir.

Classical nonpolarizable force fields can predict the reasonable density of aqueous NaCl solutions but fail to describe the viscosity or the diffusion coefficient at moderate high salinities. The difficulty of predicting the transport properties of ionic aqueous solutions comes from the fact that polarizability plays an important role. A useful and pragmatic approach consists of “correcting” the point charges (scaling the effective dipole moment of molecules). This is the choice adopted by different authors.1 However, this scaling should be performed carefully. Other choices for correction are plausible, for instance, using the dielectric constant (epsilon) of the solvent.2,3 Jorge et al.4 addressed the question of “what is the correct degree of polarization that a fixed-charge model should possess to provide the best balance of interactions?” Jorge et al.5 reported an improvement and extension of their recently developed self-consistent electrostatic embedding (SCEE) method and applied it to determine the dipole moment of pure liquid alcohols. However, for a chemist, it is difficult to accept scaled charges, since the unscaled values have provided excellent results for ionic crystals.

The description of molecular hydrogen with semiempirical force fields or other common thermodynamic models, like EOS, is challenging due to its small mass and quantum effects. These effects have a strong influence on macroscopic thermodynamic properties, especially at low temperatures.6–8 The quantum effects are diminished as temperature increases and density decreases. As the quantum effects vanish, the classical values and quantum computations become close and eventually coincide within statistical uncertainty. Molecular hydrogen generally comprises ortho- and para-hydrogen with various quantum spin states, contains two nuclei, and has a nonspherical charge distribution.9 In general, the effective potential method was used to study the quantum motion of hydrogen molecules.8 Some researchers have been working on the modeling of H2 using different semiempirical potential models including single-site and two-site Lennard-Jones (LJ) potentials, and more complex ones, which account for many-body polarization interactions.7,8,10–13

In low-temperature condensed phases, hydrogen molecules behave as free rotors in their ground rotational states, with para-hydrogen acting as a spherical atom-like entity while ortho-hydrogen could be viewed as having a far more elongated body.10 Cracknell8 reported that an elongated model (two-site model) for hydrogen makes little difference to the simulated adsorption of H2 in graphitic nanofibers when compared to the spherical model of hydrogen developed by Buch10 and suggested that quantum mechanical effects make a relatively small difference to the simulation results at ambient temperature. Nagashima et al.14 investigated the quantum effect of the hydrogen molecule on its thermodynamic and transport properties using the MD method based on the path integral method, concluding that the quantum effect is large and that taking quantum nature into account results in weaker intermolecular interaction energy and broader repulsive force than classical representation. There seems to be no consensus in the literature as to which water and H2 models are the most accurate for determining the hydrogen solubility properties.

Therefore, the objective of this work was to investigate the solubility behavior of H2 in brine from a molecular perspective, including the effects of finite size, force fields, and temperature. To accomplish this, two water models (the three-site SPC/E model15 and four-site TIP4P/2005 model16), three NaCl models of Smith and Dang17 (SD model), Chandrasekhar et al.18 (OPLS model), and Joung and Cheatham19 (JC model), and two H2 models (spherical H2 model of Darkrim et al.20 and elongated H2 model of Yang and Zhong11) were evaluated by comparing the simulation results with the published experimental data. As a result, suitable models for water, NaCl, and H2 over a wide range of temperatures were identified. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the computational methods and the model potentials employed to compute the H2 solubility properties in brine water; in Section 3, we reported the results, including brine density, ion diffusivity, conductivity, and H2 solubility; and finally, in Section 4, we summarize our findings.

Molecular Models

Water Models

In this study, we compared the SPC/E and TIP4P/2005 models. The three-site SPC/E model15 has three interaction sites corresponding to the three atoms of the water molecule. Each site has a point charge, with the negative charge located at the position of the oxygen atom and positive charges placed on the hydrogen atoms. The site corresponding to the oxygen atom also has LJ parameters. The four-site TIP4P/2005 model16 has four interaction sites. Three of them are placed at the oxygen and hydrogen atom positions, respectively. The other site, a dummy site called “M” site, is coplanar with the O and H sites and is located at the bisector of the H–O–H angle. The dummy site has only a negative charge. This model improves the electrostatic distribution around the water molecule and has a general parametrization for simulating the entire phase diagram of condensed water. The fixed O–H distance and H–O–H angle are 0.0957 nm and 104.52°, respectively. The parameters involved in the SPC/E and TIP4P/2005 models are recalled in Table 1.

H2 Models

Molecular hydrogen contains two nuclei, nonspherical charge distribution, and typically consists of ortho- and para-hydrogen with different quantum spin states.9 We employed the following two H2 models: a one-site LJ sphere with a quadrupole moment described with three charges, two positive charges on the hydrogen nuclei and a negative charge on the LJ center according to Darkrim et al.20 (HMS1); two-site LJ spheres centered on the hydrogen nuclei without atomic charges, according to the parametrization of Yang and Zhong11 (HMS2). The choice of the second model was motivated by our previous study on the effects of force fields on hydrogen solubility in water24 which reproduced the expected behaviors of the H2O + H2 system. NaCl Models

We evaluated the accuracy of the following three NaCl models to simulate the brine density and H2 solubility in an aqueous NaCl solution: Smith and Dang17 (SD model), Chandrasekhar et al.18 (OPLS model), and Joung and Cheatham19 (JC model). The choice of the OPLS model for NaCl was made because its combination with the TIP4P/2005 water model allows to correctly reproduce the densities and osmotic pressures of aqueous NaCl solutions over large temperature and salinity ranges.25 The SD model17 was developed through fitting to both experimental gas-phase binding enthalpies for small ion–water clusters and to the measured structures and solvation enthalpies of ionic solutions. This model was reported to give a good representation of the experimentally derived properties of aqueous sodium chloride solutions. The JC model19,26 considered Na+ and Cl– at 298 K and 1 atm in conjunction with the LJ plus point charge molecular interaction model and the Lorentz–Berthelot mixing rules. The ion ϵ and σ values were fitted using the training set properties of individual ions and total electrolyte hydration free energy at infinite dilution, crystal lattice energy, and crystal density. MD Simulation Implementation

The nonbonded interactions were described using the Coulomb and LJ potentials1where qi, qj, rij, εij, and σij are the charges on the atoms i and j, the separation, LJ well depth, and LJ size for the paired atoms of i and j, respectively. The Coulomb interactions in the system were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with an interpolation order of 4 and a grid spacing of 0.095 nm for periodic systems.27,28 The cutoff distance of 1.4 nm was introduced to exclude energy and force computations if the separation between atoms was over this cutoff value. We used a truncated and shifted potential at the cutoff distance. Bond lengths were constrained using the LINCS algorithm.29 The cross interactions between unlike atoms were obtained using the Lorentz–Berthelot mixing rules as given below23

We used the same methodology as our previous study on H2 solubility in water.24 Classical MD simulations in the constant NPT (constant pressure, temperature, and number of particles) ensemble were carried out for aqueous NaCl solution using Gromacs software (Gromacs/2020.3-gpuvolta).30–35 The temperature was set fixed for each simulation over a temperature range of 273–433 K using the V-rescale algorithm.36 The pressure was isotropically controlled by a Parrinello–Rahman barostat37 at a reference of 1 bar with a coupling constant of 5 ps. The equations of motion were integrated using the leapfrog algorithm with a time step of 1.5 fs. Each MD simulation started with a 100 ps energy minimization using the steepest descent method to prevent crashes of the simulation due to the extremely large force between atoms, followed by a production run of 75 ns to produce good statistics. The instantaneous configurations were sampled every 10 ps from the production trajectories. These configurations were used for Widom insertion calculation of the excess chemical potential. For each configuration, 5 × 105 random space insertions were carried out using the Widom insertion method. Parallel MD simulations were carried out using OpenMPI (message passing interface) at the Gadi supercomputer (GPU: NVIDIA Tesla V100-SXM2-32GB; CPU: Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8268 CPU at 2.90 GHz) in the National Computational Infrastructure (NCI), Australia.

Results and Discussion

Brine Density: Effect of Water Models

The aim of this section is to compare the performances of the two different water models, TIP4P/2005 and SPC/E, to simulate brine density in a large temperature range. We used the NaCl force field developed by Smith and Dang17 in conjunction with the two water models. More details on the choice of the NaCl model of Smith and Dang17 is elaborated in Section 3.2. Densities of aqueous NaCl solution were computed at 298 K and 1 bar over a salinity range of 0–5 m (i.e., number of moles of NaCl per kg of water). The results were compared between the two force fields and shown in Figure 2 and Table 4. The brine densities from both the TIP4P/2005 and SPC/E models increased with increasing salinity. SPC/E and TIP4P/2005 obtained very similar values for the brine density at salinities lower than 3m and compared rather well with the experimental correlation prediction by Laliberté and Cooper.48 With increasing salinity, the deviation between these two models increased. The densities from the TIP4P/2005 model had a relative deviation of 0.09–0.9% from the experimental correlation data, and the deviation of the SPC/E model was 0.18–1.7% over the salinity range of 1–5 m. As observed in Figure 2, the TIP4P/2005 model reproduced the brine density more accurately over the entire salinity range. Since the size (σ) parameters of the LJ potentials were the same for the two water models, the differences between brine densities computed using the TIP4P/2005 and SPC/E force fields were small at low salinity. The increased differences with increasing salinity between these two models were due to the higher energy parameter of TIP4P/2005 than that of SPC/E and the shorter O–H bond than that of the latter. Water molecules tend to aggregate and form more packed liquid structures. This increases the density of the liquid, leading to higher brine densities of TIP4P/2005 compared with that of SPC/E at high salinity.

Modified Brine Density Model

In Section 3.2, we identified that the combination of the TIP4P/2005 water model and the SD NaCl model reproduced the closest experimental brine density. Therefore, in this section, the combined TIP4P/2005 water and SD NaCl models were used to illustrate that at higher salinity, the simulated brine densities deviated from the correlation model developed by Laliberté and Cooper48 for calculating the density of aqueous solutions of electrolytes based on apparent molal volumes. In Figure 4a, the simulated brine densities as a function of temperature at molalities of 0–5 m were compared with the experimental correlation model’s prediction by Laliberté and Cooper.48 As shown in Figure 4a, at a salinity of 5 m, the simulated brine density deviated from the correlation model’s prediction by Laliberté and Cooper. This was not surprising as the model parameters of Laliberté and Cooper were established on the literature values of measured densities. For NaCl solution, the model included the experimental data with varying ionic strength levels up to 3.6 m over a range of temperatures up to 423 K (1 m,49,50 1.2 m,51 1.5 m,52 2.5 m,53 and 3 m(54)). For a higher ionic strength of 5 m, only the temperature of 298 K was considered.55–57 The correlation of Laliberté and Cooper48 worked well for NaCl wt % from 0 to 14.8, which is equivalent to 0–3 m.

System-Size Dependence of Self-Diffusion Coefficients

The system-size dependence of the self-diffusion coefficients was investigated in detail. The value of the self-diffusion coefficient increases monotonously with the increasing system size (L) prior to the onset of a plateau (Figure 6a1–a3). The L value, which corresponded to the onset of the plateau phase, increased with increasing salinity (or system density). Some authors61–63 found that the diffusion coefficient depended linearly on 1/L. They obtained size-corrected diffusion coefficient by extrapolating the diffusion coefficient versus the inverse of system size (1/L) to 1/L = 0. By performing this correction, Gereben and Pusztai63 found that the corrected self-diffusion constant of SPC/E water at 293 K was higher than the experimental value. In this study, we illustrated that the self-diffusion constants of Na+ and Cl– in TIP4P/2005 water at 298 K and 1 bar were nonlinearly dependent on 1/L, as shown in Figure 6b1–b3. Once 1/L decreased (or L increased) to a system-specific value, DNa and DCl reached a plateau. Therefore, the “corrected diffusion coefficient” obtained from a straight-line fit extrapolation of the DNa and DCl versus 1/L plot to the intercept with the D-axis at 1/L = 0 overestimated the real diffusion coefficient for an infinite system.

It is worth investigating if this finding of nonlinear dependency of DNa and DCl on 1/L applies to other types of solutions. To the best of our knowledge, this finding has not been reported. We believe that longer trajectories and smaller timescales play an important role in the diffusivity calculation. In this study, we ran simulations up to 75 ns.

Temperature and Salinity Effect on the Diffusivity of Na+ and Cl–

In Figure 7 and Table 6, the diffusion coefficients of Na+ and Cl– as a function of temperature at specified salinities and 1 bar are presented. As expected, DNa (Figure 7a) and DCl (Figure 7b) increased with increasing temperature. This is because when temperature increases, the kinetic energy of the ions is increased. The increased motion of the ions causes them to diffuse faster. The ionic radii of Na+ and Cl– are 0.102 and 0.181 nm, respectively. In general, small and light molecules diffuse more quickly than large and heavy ones. Interestingly, DCl is higher than DNa, although Cl– ions have greater mass and size. Further analyses on the structure of the aqueous solution, the radial distribution function (RDF) of the ions around water, indicated that the water molecules involve a higher degree of electrostatic association with Na+ ions than Cl– ions (Figure 8). More Na+ ions are packed around water in a solid-like structure within a short distance. The bond between water oxygen and Na+ is around 0.24 nm. However, the RDF of Cl– around water showed a liquid-like structure that allows mobility. The slower diffusion of the Na+ ions agrees with the conclusion of Gurtovenko and Vattulainen64 that the strong interaction of the Na+ ions with the other groups considerably slows down their permeation through water compared with Cl– ions.

In Figure 9, DNa and DCl as a function of salinity at specified temperatures and 1 bar are presented. As expected, both DNa and DCl decreased with increasing salinity. If the medium that a given particle diffuses through is denser, then the particle will have a harder time diffusing through it. On the other hand, if the medium is less dense, then the ions will be able to move more quickly and will diffuse faster. As Figure 4 indicates, at a given temperature and pressure, the higher salinity led to higher density. Therefore, diffusion is lower at higher salinity. The conductivity Λ is affected by the combined effects of concentration and mobility, as indicated by eq 23. The two effects influence Λ in opposite directions. Note that for all of the simulations, H2 solubility was systematically underestimated. This was also reported by Ferrando and Ungerer.23Figure 14 shows that the HMS1 model performed better to predict H2 solubility than the HMS2 model. This result was not surprising because the HMS1 model which includes the quadrupole moment of the hydrogen molecule has more physical significance. Furthermore, the simulated H2 using the HMS1 model produced the expected behaviors of the brine + H2 system, such as a minimum hydrogen solubility close to 322 K and a reduction in the Henry constant with increasing temperature from 273 to 322 K, followed by an increase from 322 to 433 K. This observed behavior was due to the fact that Henry’s constant, expressed as e–βμex/(ρmolRT) (see eq 6), is not only explicitly dependent on T but also implicitly dependent on T, through e–βμex and ρmol, and the expression e–βμex/(ρmolRT) as a function of T has a minimum, which was determined by calculating its first derivative with respect to T and finding the point of T where the first derivative was 0.

Figure 14 shows that the effect of the three SD, JC, and OPLS models of NaCl had a negligible effect on simulated H2 solubility in brine with the salinity level of 1 m. Figure 3 shows that the three NaCl models produced almost the same brine density at this salinity level. The negligible effect of the NaCl models on brine density might lead to the negligible effect of these NaCl models on the H2 solubility in brine. This supported the hypothesis posited by Paschek77 that modeling of the correct density played an important role in modeling the solubility of hydrophobic solutes. The brine density may be an important aspect in appropriately modeling the solubility.

Conclusions

MD simulations were carried out to investigate brine density, ion diffusivity, molar conductivity, conductivity, and H2 solubility in brine at a temperature range of 273–433 K and 1 bar. The effects of molecular models and temperature on brine density and H2 solubility and the finite-size effects on the diffusivity were studied.

The results confirmed that the four-site TIP4P/2005 model reproduced the experimental brine density more accurately over the entire salinity range of 0–5 m. Among the three NaCl models, the SD model produced the most accurate results. This study proposed a modified analytical model for describing the brine density over a broad range of temperatures 273–433 K and ionic strength levels of 0–5 m.

The results confirmed the ability of the modified analytical model to perform more accurate brine density calculations over a wider range of temperatures and salinities. The ion diffusivity was found nonlinearly dependent on the inverse of system size 1/L. Once 1/L decreased (or L increased) to a system-specific value, the diffusivity reached a plateau. Therefore, the corrected diffusion coefficient obtained from a straight-line fit extrapolation of the diffusivity versus 1/L plot to the intercept with the diffusivity axis at 1/L = 0 (suggested by published research) might overestimate the real diffusion coefficient for the infinite system.

The simulated ion diffusivity decreased linearly with increasing salinity. Based on this, we proposed an analytical diffusivity model and derived analytic models for the ions’ molar conductivity and conductivity. We found that the contribution from the ion concentration rather than the ion mobility dominated the behavior of the conductivity, and the conductivity increased with the concentration. Comparisons of the simulated H2 solubilities from the two H2 models and experimental data indicated that the HMS1 model, which had a one-site LJ sphere with a quadrupole moment described with three charges: two positive charges on the hydrogen nuclei and a negative charge on the LJ center, produced better results than the uncharged two-site LJ model, although both produced the expected behaviors of the brine + H2 system, such as an inverse bell-shaped temperature-dependent effect. We might infer that in the calculation of H2 solubility in brine, the H2 model considering quadrupole moment, which targets the properties of hydrogen mixtures with other gases or adsorbent, is better than the uncharged two-site H2 model, which was developed by targeting the thermodynamic and dynamic properties of bulk H2. We found that classical nonpolarizable force fields can predict a reasonable density of aqueous NaCl solutions at a broad range of salinity levels and transport properties of diffusivity and conductivity at low salinity. The difficulty of predicting the transport properties of ionic aqueous solutions comes from the fact that polarizability plays an important role. However, using polarizable force fields is not always an easy and evident choice. An interesting direction for future work would be to investigate the effect of different “corrections” on H2 solubility in water or brine.
