Low dose systemic AAV-exendin-4 gene therapy for Prader-Willi syndrome and dietary obesity
Authors: Wei Huang, Min Xiao, Xunchang Zou, Tawfiq Mohammed, Zhenyang Feng, Lei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) patients display developmental delays, endocrine dysfunction, excessive eating, central obesity, and various behavioral abnormalities. Effective and sustained treatments are limited, highlighting the need for new therapeutic strategies. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) have revolutionized obesity treatment while their efficacy in PWS population remains inconsistent and data in PWS animal models are lacking. Here, we assessed the efficacy of a newly developed AAV platform to deliver a GLP-1RA exendin-4 via an engineered hybrid capsid Rec2. Intraperitoneal administration of Rec2-exendin-4 at the dose of 2 × 1010 viral genome per mouse normalized metabolic dysfunction in the Magel2-null mouse model of PWS. Systemic Rec2-exendin-4 treatment reversed genotype-driven excessive adiposity, impaired glycemic control, hyperleptinemia, and adipose gene expression signatures. Moreover, intraperitoneal injection of Rec2-exendin-4 (4 × 1010 viral genome/mouse) exerted high levels of efficacy in diet-induced obesity model—decreasing food intake; preventing excessive weight gain and obesity; improving glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity; and reversing fatty liver. Metabolic improvements were maintained at least 5 months. These data demonstrate the therapeutic potential of a systemic AAV-mediated exendin-4 gene therapy for PWS-related metabolic abnormalities in Magel2-null model and dietary obesity.

Introduction

Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare genetic disease that occurs in approximately 1 in 15,000 individuals,1 due to the loss of function of paternally inherited copy of chromosome 15q11-q13.2,3 Individuals with PWS display developmental delays, cognitive impairment, behavioral, and neuropsychiatric abnormalities such as obsessive-compulsive behavior and temper tantrums, endocrine dysfunction, hypothalamic hypogonadism, and constant sense of hunger leading to obesity and associated metabolic syndromes.4 Up to date, effective, and sustained treatments are limited to address the metabolic dysregulation, highlighting the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies.

MAGEL2 (MAGE family member L2) is one of the five protein-coding genes in the PWS-critical domain on chromosome 15q11-q13. Loss of function of MAGEL2 is thought to contribute to some aspects of PWS pathophysiology.5 Magel2-null mice, one of the most commonly used animal models for PWS, recapitulate key aspects of PWS pathophysiology including disturbance in the hypothalamic leptin-proopiomelanocortin (POMC) pathway6 and feeding circuitry,7,8 elevated adiposity, impaired glucose metabolism, and alterations in neuroendocrine responses.9

Our previous research has demonstrated that a hypothalamic adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) gene therapy normalized metabolic disturbance and improved some behavioral abnormalities in the Magel2-null mice with high level of efficacy and safety.10,11 However, the translation of a hypothalamic BDNF gene therapy remains challenging. Thus, systemic gene therapy approaches are worthy of investigation for this genetic disease potentially requiring lifelong treatment.

Incretin mimetics are agonists at one or more satiety-related receptors that have revolutionized obesity treatment such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) semaglutide.12,13 However, the efficacy of incretin mimetics in individuals with PWS remains inconsistent14,15 and scarce evidence in PWS relevant animal models is available. Incretin mimetics, such as many peptide drugs, require frequent injection and long-term use, positing challenges of patient compliance, risk of side effects, and high cost.16 As such, AAV-based gene therapy may have advantages compared to current standard of care for some peptide drugs such as GLP-1RA. Exendin-4 (Ex4) is a peptide isolated from the venom of Gila monster which has 53% homology with the biological active form of GLP-1(7–37), but acts as a full agonist of GLP-1R and therefore has been developed as a drug for diabetes.16,17 To assess the therapeutic potential of a systemic incretin mimetics gene therapy as a novel, long lasting, and safe treatment for PWS obesity, we designed a transgene of Ex4 whose peptide product can be secreted from transduced cells. This transgene was packaged to an engineered hybrid AAV capsid Rec2, which can achieve high level transgene expression by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection.18,19,20,21,22,23 In this study, we investigated the therapeutic effects of this Rec2-exendin-4 (Rec2-Ex4) gene therapy via i.p. administration in the preclinical model of PWS—Magel2-null mice, and diet-induced obesity model in wildtype mice.

Pilot study of Rec2-Ex4 in Magel2-null mice

To estimate the dose of Rec2-Ex4, we conducted a pilot study by i.p. injection of Rec2-Ex4 in Magel2-null mice or Rec2-GFP as a control, at the dose of 4 × 1010 viral genome (vg) per mouse. Mice receiving Rec2-Ex4 exhibited a sharp drop in food intake, 35% in week-1 and 65% in week-2 post AAV injection as compared to mice receiving Rec2-GFP (Figure S2C). Two out of five Rec2-Ex4-treated mice lost >40% body weight within 2 weeks and the experiment was terminated due to extreme low body weight (12–15 g) (Figures S2A and S2B). These data suggest Magel2-null mice are highly sensitive to Rec2-Ex4, and the dose of 4 × 1010 vg per mouse was too high. Thus, we lowered the dose to 2 × 1010 vg per mouse in a study examining the genotype and Ex4 treatment effects.

I.p. injection of Rec2-Ex4 normalizes genotype-driven metabolic abnormalities in Magel2-null mice

Male Magel2-null mice and age-matched wild-type (WT) littermates were randomized to receive an i.p. injection of either Rec2-Ex4 or Rec2-GFP at the dose of 2 × 1010 vg per mouse and subject to in vivo metabolic assessments and behavioral testing as outlined in Figure 1B. The baseline body weight of Magel2-null mice was significantly higher than WT counterpart (two-way ANOVA, genotype p < 0.01) prior to vector administration. Magel2-null mice receiving Rec2-GFP (Magel2-null GFP) maintained higher body weight as compared to WT mice throughout the experiment (Figures 1C and 1D). In contrast, Magel2-null mice receiving Rec2-Ex4 (Magel2-null Ex4) displayed significant weight loss (Figures 1E and 1F), alleviating the genotype-driven increase in body weight (Figures 1C and 1D). Rec2-Ex4 treatment resulted in milder body weight effect in WT mice than that observed in the Magel2-null mice (Figures 1C–1F).

At 4-weeks post AAV injection, a glucose tolerance test (GTT) was performed to assess glycemic control. Magel2-null GFP mice exhibited impaired GTT over WT controls (Figures 1G and 1H). This genotype-driven deficit was rescued by Rec2-Ex4 treatment as Magel2-null Ex4 mice performed similarly to WT GFP mice (Figures 1G and 1H). Of note, WT Ex4 mice displayed further improvement in GTT as compared to WT GFP mice (Figures 1G and 1H) in the absence of significant change in body weight (Figures 1C and 1D). Magel2-null mice exhibited elevated fasting blood glucose, which was ameliorated by Rec2-Ex4 treatment (Figure 1I).

Energy expenditure was examined by indirect calorimetry between 7- and 8-weeks post AAV injections. Energy expenditure or heat, RER (respiratory exchange ratio, calculated as VCO2/VO2), and ambulation over 24 h showed no significant difference among the four groups (Figure S3A). No genotype or treatment effects were found in the average heat, average RER, or average ambulation (Figure S3B).

Body composition was determined by EchoMRI following the completion of indirect calorimetry. Magel2-null GFP mice displayed significant increase in absolute fat mass (Figure S3C) and relative fat mass (Figure 1J) as compared to WT mice. This genotype-driven increase in fat mass was reversed by Rec2-Ex4 treatment (Figure 1J; Figure S3C). Absolute lean mass was similar among the four groups (Figure S3D), whereas relative lean mass was increased by Rec2-Ex4 treatment regardless of genotypes (Figure 1K).

It is reported that Magel2-null mice do not exhibit hyperphagia fed on normal diet, despite manifestation of various other metabolic abnormalities.24,25 Mice were singly housed prior to indirect calorimetry to collect food intake data till the termination of the study. No Ex4 treatment effects on average food intake (Figure S4A) or relative food intake (calibrated to body weight, Figure S4B) were observed.

Behavioral assessments in Magel2-null mice

Our previous research has demonstrated that hypothalamic BDNF gene therapy has no adverse behavioral effects and instead normalizes genotype-driven behavior in female Magel2-null mice including open field test and novel object recognition test.11 These two behavioral tests were performed at 5-weeks post AAV injection to assess safety and determine whether Rec2-Ex4 alters behavior in male Magel2-null mice. In the open field test assessing exploratory activity and anxiety-like behavior,26 a significant genotype-driven decrease in total distance traveled was observed in Magel2-null mice (Figure S5A), which was lessened by Rec2-Ex4 treatment. No significant genotype or treatment effects on the percentage of distance traveled in the center of the open field arena were observed indicating no changes in anxiety-like behavior.

In addition, novel object recognition test was performed as previous report has shown that female Magel2-null mice are averse to novel objects and environments, manifesting a reduced discrimination index.27 However, no genotype or treatment effects were observed in the male Magel2-null mice (Figure S5D).

Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy reduces adipose tissue mass and improves circulating biomarkers in Magel2-null mice

Mice were euthanized at 10-weeks post AAV injection and tissues were collected. Both genotype- and treatment-effect was observed regarding the final body weight (Figure 2A). Rec2-Ex4 ameliorated excessive weight in the Magel2-null mice. Consistent with increase in adiposity measured by EchoMRI (Figure 1J; Figure S3C) due to loss of Magel2, Magel2-null GFP mice exhibited increased mass of the brown adipose tissue (BAT) (Figure 2B) and three white adipose tissue (WAT) depots—inguinal WAT (iWAT, Figure 2C), gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT, Figure 2D), and retroperitoneal adipose tissue (rWAT, Figure 2E)—compared to WT counterparts, ranging from 72% to 163% increase. Increases in Magel2-null adipose depots were reversed following Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy (Figures 2B–2E). Relative BAT and WAT weights calibrated to body weight were also normalized by Rec2-Ex4 treatment (Figures S6A–S6D). A significant Ex4 treatment effect on liver weight was observed (Figure 2F). The liver weight in Magel2-null Ex4 mice was significantly reduced as compared to Magel2-null GFP mice (Figure 2F). Relative liver weight was significantly affected by both genotype and Ex4 treatment (Figure S6E). Pancreas weight was increased in Rec2-Ex4-treated mice regardless of genotypes (Figure 2G; Figure S6F) consistent with previous report that GLP-1RAs including Ex4 increase pancreas mass through induction of protein synthesis.28 Muscle loss has become a concern of GLP-1RA as weight loss treatment. Serum levels of Ex4 were measured by ELISA validating the secretion of Ex4 transgene product in Rec2-Ex4-treated mice (Figure 3A, WT Ex4: 28.5 ± 5.4 ng/mL; Magel2-null Ex4: 10.6 ± 2.6 ng/mL). Serum biomarkers were profiled to assess changes in systemic metabolism affected by genotype and Rec2-Ex4 treatment. No genotype-induced changes were observed in circulating glucose levels (Figure 3B). Rec2-Ex4 treatment resulted in significant reduction in serum glucose levels after 4-h fast, an effect more prominent in WT mice (Figure 3B). No genotype or Ex4 treatment effects on fasting serum insulin were observed (Figure 3C). Similar results were found in the HOMA-IR (homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance) index (Figure 3D). Loss of Magel2 causes leptin resistance in the hypothalamic POMC neurons contributing to the metabolic dysfunctions in Magel2-null mice6 and hyperleptinemia. Indeed, Magel2-null GFP mice exhibited 3.4-folds increase in serum leptin over WT counterparts (Figure 3E). The genotype-driven hyperleptinemia was reversed by Rec2-Ex4 treatment (Figure 3E). Adiponectin is another key adipokine that plays important roles in insulin sensitivity, glucose homeostasis, and systemic metabolic function.29 The adiponectin/leptin ratio (with adiponectin level expressed in μg/mL and leptin level expressed in ng/mL) is negatively correlated with insulin resistance and low-grade chronic inflammation. This index is considered a predictive marker for adipose dysfunction and cardiometabolic risk associated with obesity better than leptin or adiponectin alone.30 Rec2-Ex4 treatment resulted in a significant increase in the adiponectin/leptin ratio, and the effect was more robust in the WT mice (Figure 3G). No treatment-induced changes in serum triglyceride were observed (Figure 3H). Liver function markers alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were not affected by genotype (Figures 3I and 3J). Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy normalizes adipose gene expression in Magel2-null mice

Given the robust effects on adipose tissue mass induced by both genotype and Ex4 treatment, gene expression was profiled in the largest visceral WAT depot, the eWAT (Figure 4A) by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR). Magel2-null mice showed a trend of Adipoq (encoding adiponectin) downregulation in the eWAT, which was reversed by Rec2-Ex4 treatment. Similarly, a genotype-driven downregulation of Cfd (encoding complement factor D, or adipsin) was rescued by Rec2-Ex4 treatment. Magel2-null mice exhibited a significant upregulation of Lep (encoding leptin) in eWAT as compared to WT, suggesting transcription changes contributing to the hyperleptinemia in addition to increases in total fat mass. Rec2-Ex4 treatment normalized the Lep expression level to the level in WT. Consistent with the alteration in Lep expression, a genotype-induced reduction in Adrb3 (encoding adrenoceptor β3), mediating sympathetic regulation, was observed, which was rescued by Rec2-Ex4 treatment. The expression of BDNF receptor—TrkB-FL (encoding tropomyosin receptor kinase B full length) was upregulated by Rec2-Ex4 treatment. As Rec2-Ex4 treatment led to a decrease in liver weight (Figure 2F), we examined the gene expression of enzymes and signaling molecules critical for glucose and lipid metabolism (Figure 5B). Rec2-Ex4 treatment led to significant downregulation of Fasn (encoding fatty acid synthase). No genotype- or gene therapy-induced changes were observed in the expression of Srebp1c (encoding sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1, isoform c) or Scd2 (encoding stearoyl-coenzyme A desaturase 2). Protein kinase Cε (PKCε) is implicated in lipid-induced insulin resistance in the liver and subsequent impaired insulin-induced suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis.34,35 A gene therapy-induced downregulation of Prkce (encoding PKCε) was observed in concomitant with a decrease in the expression of G6pc, encoding glucose-6-phosphatase, the major gluconeogenic enzyme (Figure 5B), suggesting regulation of hepatic glucose production, possibly in part, contributing to the improved glucose tolerance in Rec2-Ex4 treated mice. No genotype-driven changes in Gck (encoding glucokinase) were observed while a trend toward downregulation was associated with Rec2-Ex4 treatment.

We previously reported that Rec2 capsid vector primarily transduced liver and visceral fat via i.p. injection.18,23 Consistent with previous findings, woodchuck post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE) within the transgene transcript was detected in the liver and eWAT but not in the BAT or hypothalamus (data not shown).

I.p. administration of Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy ameliorates diet-induced obesity and associated metabolic syndromes

Next, the therapeutic potential of Rec2-Ex4 was evaluated in a nongenetic obesity model. C57BL/6 mice were randomized to receive Rec2-Ex4 (4 × 1010 vg per mouse) or AAV buffer as control via i.p. injection. Mice were maintained on high fat diet (HFD) throughout the experiment. Ex4 mice displayed significantly lower body weight as early as 1-week post AAV injection and sustained throughout the 21-week experiment (Figure 6A). The average total weight gain was reduced by 39% following Rec2-Ex4 treatment as compared to control mice (Figure 6B). The decrease in weight gain was associated with significant reduction in food intake (Figure 6C) but not changes in energy expenditure (Figure S7). At 5-weeks post AAV injection, relative fat mass in Ex4 mice was 76% lower than that in control mice (Figure 6E). Behavioral assessments were conducted between 13 and 14 weeks post AAV injection. Ex4 mice showed a significant increase in exploratory activity in the open field test while had no effect on anxiety-like behavior (Figure 6H). Rec2-Ex4 did not alter novel object discrimination index in the novel object recognition test (Figure 6I).

At 21-week post AAV injection, a pyruvate tolerance test (PTT) was performed to examine hepatic glucose production.36 Out of the five control mice, one died during the PTT and two more died overnight likely due to pyruvate-induced torpor in obese mice.37 In contrast, all Rec2-Ex4-treated mice remained healthy. The experiment was terminated because the remaining two control mice appeared in risk of death. Livers from control mice were visibly pale. H&E staining revealed vast liver steatosis in the control mice, which was completely prevented by Rec2-Ex4 treatment (Figure 6J).

HA-tagged Ex4 gene therapy prevents dietary obesity and related metabolic dysfunction

Because the long-term diet induced obesity (DIO) experiment was terminated unexpectedly, we repeated the Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy in DIO model to collect tissues properly. Moreover, we added a hemagglutinin (HA) tag to the C terminus of Ex4 transgene as a proxy for Ex4 transgene expression allowing better detection by immunoblotting. The amino acid sequence of the Ex4-HA transgene was shown in Figure S8A. HA signal was detected in the cell lysate and culture media confirming secretion of the Ex4-HA peptide (Figure S8B).

C57BL/6 mice were fed on HFD for 5 weeks and then randomized to three groups: Rec2-GFP, Rec2-Ex4, and Rec2-Ex4-HA, at the dose of 4 × 1010 vg per mouse via i.p. injection. Mice were maintained on HFD throughout the experiment. Rec2-GFP-treated mice continued to gain weight while both Rec2-Ex4- and Rec2-Ex4-HA-treated mice lost weight within the first week after injection. HFD-induced excessive weight gain was completely prevented by Rec2-Ex4 or Rec2-Ex4-HA treatment (Figures 7A–7C). Discussion

Growth hormone therapy and strict monitoring of daily food intake continue to be the standard of care for patients diagnosed with PWS.38,39 Growth hormone therapy has several drawbacks, such as the need for patient compliance, lacking efficacy evidence in older adults, and the exclusion of patients who have common comorbidities such as severe obesity, uncontrolled diabetes, or active psychosis.40 Caregivers for individuals with PWS experience high levels of caregiver burden due to strict supervision of their daily food intake and management of their emotional reactivity, which may surpass that of caregivers for patients with Alzheimer’s disease and traumatic brain injury.41,42,43,44 Melanotan II and setmelanotide have been among the recent pharmacological developments targeting the hypothalamic leptin-POMC pathway.6,45 Additional categories of therapeutics have been evaluated in animal models and clinical trials at various stages.9 Diazoxide choline extended-release tablet has recently been approved by FDA to treat severe hyperphagia in patients with PWS.46,47 However, this therapy is ineffective in patients without severe baseline hyperphagia and may be contraindicated for subsets of PWS patients due to serious side effects including hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, and peripheral edema. Bariatric surgeries in PWS patients have much higher levels of complications than those observed in non-PWS obese patients48 and require post-operative compliance from the patient or caregiver to ensure adequate weight loss.49 Behavioral and exercise therapies have shown benefits in managing some symptoms50,51,52 but long-term patient compliance is difficult to achieve. As such, new therapeutic strategies for PWS remain an urgent and unmet need.

Our previous work on an autoregulatory BDNF vector represents the first AAV-based gene therapy for PWS.11 In addition to the PWS model Magel2-null mice, the hypothalamic BDNF gene therapy has shown high efficacy and safety in various genetic and dietary models of obesity including MC4R deficiency,53 leptin receptor deficiency,54 DIO,54 and normal aging model.55 However, brain-targeted gene therapies may subject to more stringent consideration of the risk/benefit ratio and administration to the hypothalamus remain highly challenging.

In this study, an i.p. injection of Rec2-Ex4 at a low dose normalized the metabolic abnormalities induced by loss of Magel2 including excessive adiposity, impaired glycemic control, and hyperleptinemia. I.p. Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy achieved metabolic improvements comparable to the efficacy of hypothalamic AAV1-BDNF gene therapy in the same PWS model.11 It is worthy of noting that the dose of this systemic gene therapy is equivalent to the dose of hypothalamic injection, 2 × 1010 vg/mouse. When converted to a per kilogram basis, the effective dose of Rec2-Ex4 is approximately 5 × 1011 vg/Kg, one to two orders lower as compared to clinical systemic gene therapies that are often dosed 1013–1014 vg/Kg. Magel2-null mice appeared highly sensitive to Rec2-Ex4 treatment exhibiting drastic weight loss and suppression in food intake at the dose of 4 × 1010 vg/mouse. We recently report an i.p. injection of an adipose-targeting Rec2-leptin vector rescues congenital leptin deficiency at a dose as low as 1 × 109 vg/mouse.23 It is plausible that the effective dose of Rec2-Ex4 can be furtherly reduced in the Magel2-null model of PWS, and a dose-deescalating study is warranted.

GLP-1RA drugs require repeated dosing and weight regain occurs following drug discontinuation.56 A recent study of a large US cohort with overweight or obesity finds that most patients discontinued GLP-1RA within 1 year, 47% of patients with and 65% without type 2 diabetes.57 The dropping-out rate is reported to increase to 85% after 2 years. The high discontinuation rates are associated with high cost, side effects, loss of reward, and other factors, causing concerns about the sustainability of weight loss to achieve long-term health benefits. On this note, an AAV-based GLP-1RA gene therapy that can be administered once to achieve long-term efficacy could offer a therapeutic option to patients with genetic obesity (e.g., PWS), who might need continuous treatment. Moreover, gene therapy induces a stable circulating GLP-1RA level that might be associated with milder or different pattern of side effects as compared to repeated bogus dosing of a peptide drug, warranting further investigation.

One limitation of the current study lies with animal modeling of PWS. MAGEL2 is one of many genes within the PWS deletion region of chromosome 15q11-q13. The single-gene knockout models of PWS such as Magel2-null mice, recapitulate some, but not all, PWS phenotypes.9,58 Notable, Magel2-null mice do not mirror the hyperphagia of human PWS when being maintained on normal chow diet.24,25 It is interesting to see whether Rec2-Ex4 exerts more pronounced effects in models or conditions manifesting hyperphagia. Moreover, testing in other preclinical models of PWS such as Snord116 and Ndn9,58 will be needed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy. Admittedly, Ex4 gene therapy is not based on the fundamental mechanism underlying imprinting phenomena and therefore is not a cure for PWS. However, Ex4 gene therapy may offer a therapeutic option to manage metabolic symptoms in individuals with PWS. In addition, Schaaf-Yang syndrome (SYS) is a rare genetic disease driven by loss-of-function of MAGEL2. SYS shares clinical overlap with PWS in the early stages of life while less consistence regarding hyperphagia and obesity.59,60,61 However, SYS manifests distinct severe neurological defects in childhood and adolescence, indicating more underlying defects in SYS.5,62 It remains to be seen whether the Rec2-Ex4 could exert metabolic benefit in SYS. As Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy is not a replacement of mutant MAGEL2, it is unlikely to address neurological defects in SYS.

One new finding of the present study is revealing genotype-driven gene expression signature in the adipose tissue. In the visceral WAT, loss of Magel2-induced downregulation of Adrb3, Adipoq, Cfd, Vegfa, and upregulation of Lep, resembling gene expression signature associated with decreased sympathetic tone to the WAT. Importantly, Rec2-Ex4 treatment reversed this genotype-driven gene expression signature (Figure 4A). In the BAT, a cluster of thermogenic genes were downregulated in the Magel2-null mice, which was restored by Rec2-Ex4 treatment (Figure 4B). Glp1r expression in BAT and WAT was extremely low or undetectable by RT-qPCR (data not shown). As such, the effect of Ex4 on adipose gene expression is less likely due to direct effect on adipose tissue but rather possibly mediated by central mechanisms. It is reported that GLP-1RA stimulates BAT thermogenesis independent of nutrient intake through hypothalamic mechanisms.63 Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy increased BDNF receptor TrkB FL expression in the hypothalamus (Figure 5A). As aberrant hypothalamic leptin-POMC pathway underlies metabolic dysregulation in the Magel2-null model6,7,8,64,65 and BDNF acts downstream of this circuit regulating food intake and energy balance,66 data of present study raises the possibility of addressing some root cause of metabolic abnormalities in Magel2-null model through a systemic Ex4 gene therapy. Admittedly, analysis of selected genes in block dissected hypothalamus was a limitation. Future work is needed to provide an unbiased profiling of transcriptomic-wide changes in discrete nuclei of hypothalamus of Magel2-null mice following Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy. We speculate that Ex4 secreted from liver and visceral adipose tissue could alter systemic metabolism via direct actions on metabolic organs in the periphery, which might feedback to the brain. Alternatively, circulating Ex4 might cross blood brain barrier to act on hypothalamic circuits and thereby modulating systemic metabolism (Figure S10). The present study focused on assessing therapeutic efficacy but lacked mechanistic interrogation. Future research should fill in this gap.

Scarce data regarding hepatic phenotypes in the Magel2-null model has been reported. In this study, genotype-driven changes in the liver were unremarkable. However, Rec2-Ex4 treatment resulted in a significant decrease in liver weight and downregulation of genes involved in de novo lipogenesis and hepatic glucose production. Whether and how hepatic regulation mediates the whole-body metabolic improvement following Rec2-Ex4 treatment requires further investigation.

In this proof-of-concept study, behavioral assessments were preliminary, limiting to the two tests that were altered in the Magel2-null mice following hypothalamic BDNF gene therapy.11 The behavioral effects of Rec2-Ex4 were unremarkable. In contrast to previous data in female Magel2-null mice, neither genotype-driven deficit nor treatment-induced changes were observed in novel object recognition in the male mice of the present study. The discrepancy may be attributed to sex-dimorphism regarding behavioral alteration in Magel2 deficiency, consistent with our previous environmental enrichment study using male Magel2-null mice.67 Another caveat is the numbers of mice were not sufficient to assess subtle behavioral alterations. Emerging evidence has revealed potential therapeutic effect of GLP-1RA on mental health and psychiatric diseases.68,69,70 Future research will expand preclinical assessments to female Magel2-null mice, increase the number of mice, and perform a broader behavioral testing battery in both sexes to investigate potential therapeutic benefits beyond metabolic improvement.

In addition to genetic obesity such as the PWS model of Magel2 deficiency, Rec2-Ex4 via i.p. administration was highly effective in the dietary obesity model—reversing excessive weight gain, decreasing food intake, reducing adiposity, improving glucose metabolism, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and preventing hepatic steatosis, at a low dose of 4 × 1010 vg per mouse. Given the potent therapeutic effects and high circulating levels of Ex4, we predict the effective dose can decrease further. We are planning a dose-finding study in DIO models prior to expanding investigations to additional preclinical models of diseases related to obesity. Previous research on AAV-mediated Ex4 gene therapy has demonstrated limited efficacy.71,72 AAV5-mediated Ex4 gene therapy targeting salivary glands improved insulin sensitivity but did not decrease food intake or body weight in DIO mice.71 Notably, i.p. delivery of Rec2-Ex4, at 6-folds lower dose, resulted in 50-folds higher circulating Ex4 level as compared to the AAV5-Ex4 targeting salivary glands.71 Further optimizing the AAV-Ex4 vector will improve translational potential.

Adding HA tag to the C terminus of Ex4 did not interfere with the actions of Ex4 transgene product in vivo. On the contrary, Rec2-Ex4-HA exerted better effects over Rec2-Ex4 in GTT, serum leptin level, and hepatic triglyceride level. Whether the HA tag alters the property of Ex4 peptide remains unknown. Ex4 is more resistant to degradation than GLP-1, which is thought to be due to the nine additional amino acid residues at C terminus.73 Adding Ex4 C terminus to native GLP-1 significantly reduces its clearance, indicating the important role of C terminus in the stability of Ex4-related peptides.74 Moreover, it is reported that hydrodynamic delivery of a fusion gene in which α-antitrypsin was fused to the C terminus of Ex4 was effective in DIO model.75 Our results raise the possibility that HA tag at C terminus of Ex4 might enhance therapeutic outcomes by altering pharmacokinetics (e.g., altered half-life, tissue distribution, and receptor affinity). Further engineering the C terminus of Ex4 might be worthy of consideration to improve therapeutic efficacy.

Development and optimization of AAV capsid vector and minimally invasive administration methods continue to be an area of focus on the path to clinical development. We have developed a Rec2 capsid mutant with three residue substitutions (named V7) that exclusively transduces adipose tissue while ablating liver tropism,76 thereby eliminating AAV sequestering in the liver and minimizing severe adverse events related to liver toxicity that has been a major challenge to the field of systemic gene therapies.77 Moreover, subcutaneous injection of V7 vector carrying human leptin gene (V7-LEP) restored leptin levels and normalized metabolic syndromes in the ob/ob mice at the dose of 4 × 1010 vg per mouse,78 equivalent to the i.p. delivery in the same animal model.76 In an ongoing experiment, one-time subcutaneous injection of V7-LEP to extremely obese ob/ob mice leads to stable circulating human leptin level and weight loss for at least 12 months.78 We suppose that adipose-targeting gene delivery may have advantages as compared to other peripheral tissues such as liver: (1) as human adipose tissue has a lifespan of 10 years79 in contrast to 3 years for liver, a one-time dosing of adipose-targeting AAV is expected to be effective 5–10 years; (2) accessible administration as subcutaneous drug delivery (e.g., GLP-1 drugs) in humans is in fact injected to subcutaneous fat; and (3) fat tissues can be easily removed if treatment cessation is needed. To leverage these new technological advancements, we will refine the Ex4 transgene design and develop an adipose-tropic AAV vector to deliver the therapeutic gene via subcutaneous administration in models of genetic and acquired metabolic diseases.

Admittedly, Rec2-Ex4 treatment, although likely long-lasting, is not permanent. It is reported that in vivo genome editing targeting liver led to elevation of Ex4 and blunted weight gain for 28 weeks in DIO mice.80 Of note, the circulating Ex4 levels in the Ex4 knockin mice were sharply reduced at 2-weeks post injection of Crispr-Cas9 plasmids-loaded lipid nano particles and stabilized at ∼0.1 ng/mL throughout the 28-weeks study,80 which was approximately 500-fold lower than levels achieved with Rec2-Ex4 treatment (49.5 ± 6.8 ng/mL). It would be interesting to investigate whether Rec2 vector could improve in vivo genome editing particularly targeting adipose tissue. A recent report demonstrates that subcutaneous delivery of Ex4 via a lipid nanoparticle-based DNA delivery system improved metabolic outcomes in DIO mice, suggesting a potential alternative to viral vectors.81 However, circulating Ex4 concentrations were not reported, and therapeutic efficacy was assessed only up to 8 weeks.81 Direct comparison of AAV-based and lipid nanoparticle-based platforms is warranted to determine relative efficacy, durability, and safety.

Furthermore, we are developing this vector system as a platform for delivery of therapeutic peptides, particularly those not derived from a native mammalian gene. For example, we are designing transgenes to express engineered BDNF peptide variants that might improve the unfavorable pharmacokinetics of native or recombinant BDNF protein as an alternative to hypothalamic AAV-BDNF gene therapy10,11 for Magel2-null mouse model of PWS.

In summary, we present preclinical data that suggest intraperitoneal administration of Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy at a low dose normalizes PWS-related metabolic abnormalities in Magel2-null mice. Furthermore, Rec2-Ex4 gene therapy is efficacious and safe for treatment of dietary obesity. These proof-of-concept data indicate Rec2-Ex4 as a potential molecular therapy for genetic and acquired forms of obesity.

Materials and methods

Animals

Magel2-null mice harbor a maternally inherited imprinted/silenced wild type allele and a paternally inherited Magel2-lacZ knockin allele that abolishes endogenous Magel2 gene function. Male mice containing the Magel2-lacZ allele (Jackson Labs #009062) were bred with female C57BL/6 mice to produce both WT mice and Magel2-null littermates. Mice were genotyped from ear notch biopsies. Identification of mutant offspring was performed by polymerase chain reaction genotyping with Magel2 and LacZ oligonucleotide primers (common forward, 5′-ATGGCTCCATCAGGAGAAC; Magel2 reverse, 5′-GATGGAAAGACCCTTGAGGT; and LacZ reverse, RW4237, 5′-GGGATAGGTCACGTTGGTGT). Genotype was further confirmed by RT-qPCR detecting Magel2 transcript in the hypothalamus (primers, see Table S1). For diet-induced obesity experiment, C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. All mice had ad libitium access to water and normal chow diet (11% fat, caloric density 3.4 kcal/g, Teklad) or HFD (60% kcal from fat; caloric density 5.2 kcal/g, Research Diets, Inc. #D12492) as specified in each experiment. Mice were housed in standard laboratory cages within temperature (22°C–23°C) and humidity (30%–70%) controlled rooms under a 12:12 light:dark cycle. All animal experiments were in accordance with the regulations of The Ohio State University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Ex4 transgene design and in vitro validation

Ex4 transgene was designed in which the exendin-4 sequence82 (encoding 39-amino acid) was linked to the 21-amino acid signal peptide sequence of mouse leptin gene with an extra valine (Figure 1A). This transgene Ex4 was then cloned to an AAV expression plasmid driven by a cytomegalovirus enhancer and a CBA promoter. In vitro transfection experiment was carried out in HEK293 cells by Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen).

AAV vector construction, packaging, and administration

Transgenes: Ex4, Ex4-HA, or GFP were inserted in the AAV expression cassette containing a hybrid cytomegalovirus–CBA promoter, WPRE, and bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal (BGH polyA) flanked by two AAV2-inverted terminal repeats (ITRs). Plasmids used for AAV packaging were prepared by using EndoFree plasmid Maxi and Mega Kit (QIAGEN). Human embryonic kidney 293 cells were co-transfected with three plasmids—AAV cis-plasmid containing transgene, AAV trans-plasmid encoding rep and Rec2 cap genes, and adenoviral helper pF Δ6—using standard CaPO4 transfection protocol. AAV vector was purified from the cell lysate by ultracentrifugation through an iodixanol density gradient (OptiPrep Density Gradient Medium, D1556, Sigma). Vector titers were determined by quantitative PCR as previously described.83 Some AAV vectors were packaged by VectorBuilder. AAV vectors were administered by i.p. injection in 150 μL AAV dilution buffer.

Magel2-null mice experiment

Pilot study: Magel2-null mice (4–5 months of age) were randomized to receive an i.p. injection of either Rec2-GFP or Rec2-Ex4 at the dose of 4 × 1010 vg per mouse and fed on HFD after AAV injection. Four female mice and one male mouse each group. The experiment was terminated 2 weeks after AAV injection due to extreme weight loss.

Study with lower dose: male Magel2-null mice (4–19 months of age) and sex- and age-matched WT littermates were randomized to receive an i.p. injection of either Rec2-GFP or Rec2-Ex4 at the dose of 2 × 1010 vg per mouse. Mice were maintained on normal chow throughout the experiment. Metabolic and behavioral assessments were conducted as indicated in Figure 1B.

DIO model

Long-term experiment: male C57BL/6 mice (19 weeks of age) were randomized to receive an i.p. injection of Rec2-Ex4 (4 × 1010 vg per mouse) or AAV buffer. Mice were fed on HFD throughout the 21-week experiment.

Short-term experiment: male C57BL/6 mice (5 weeks of age) were fed on HFD for 5 weeks and then randomized to receive an i.p. injection of Rec2-Ex4, Rec2-Ex4-HA, or Rec2-GFP at the dose of 4 × 1010 vg per mouse. Mice were maintained on HFD till euthanasia 9 weeks post AAV injection.

Body weight and food intake measurement

Body weight was recorded weekly. Food intake was measured at the cage level on a weekly basis. Food intake data collected in weeks during which invasive assessments occurred that disrupted normal feeding patterns (e.g., removal from home cages for indirect calorimetry monitoring and fasting periods for GTTs) were excluded. Average food intake was calculated on a per-mouse per-day basis.

Body composition assessment

Body composition analysis was performed with a 3-in-1 Analyzer (EchoMRI LLC, Houston, TX, USA) according to manufacturer instructions. Mice were subjected to a 5 gauss magnetic field and whole-body masses of fat, lean, free water, and total water were determined during separate cycles by manufacturer software comparison to a canola oil standard.

GTT

Mice were overnight fasted with water ad libitum. The baseline blood glucose level was measured via tail niche with a portable glucose meter using default manufacturer settings (Bayer Contour Next). Then, mice were i.p. injected with glucose solution at a dose of 1 mg glucose per gram body weight. Blood glucose concentrations were measured at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose injection. Following blood collection, styptic powder was used to stop bleeding.

Indirect calorimetry

Mice underwent indirect calorimetry using a Comprehensive Laboratory Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS, Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH, USA) at CLAMS core, Davis Heart and Lung Research Institute of The Ohio State University Medical Center. Mice were placed in the calorimetry chamber individually with an access to water and standard chow diet for Magel2-null study or HFD for DIO study. The various physiological and behavioral parameter (VO2, VCO2, RER, energy expenditure, and ambulation) were recorded for 48 h. The first 24-h period is considered as habituation time and therefore excluded from data analysis. Mice were returned to their home cages after indirect calorimetry was performed.

Open field test

Mice were individually placed into the center of an open square arena (60 cm × 60 cm, enclosed by walls of 48 cm). Each mouse was allowed to explore the arena for 10 min, during which time and locomotion—in the center and the periphery of the open field—was recorded and analyzed via TopScan (Clever Sys, Inc.) software. Between each trial, the arena was cleaned with 70% ethanol to remove odor cues.

Novel object recognition test

For the test habituation period, mice were placed in an open arena (30 cm × 30 cm, enclosed by walls of 50 cm) with no objects present and allowed to explore the empty arena for 5 min. The habituation period was typically conducted 24-h prior to the test. For the familiarization period, mice were placed in the open arena with two identical objects (either two Falcon tubes filled with water or two stacks of large legos). Mice were allowed to explore the identical objects for 10 min while being recorded with an overhead camera. Mice were returned to their home cage while the arena/objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol. During the novel object recognition test session, the two training objects were replaced with one matched item from the training session and a novel item. Mice were placed in the arena and allowed to explore both the novel and learned object for 10 min while being recorded with an overhead camera setup. Mice were returned to their home cages after 10 min and the arena/objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol. Video recording was analyzed using an open-source event-logging software https://huggingface.co/spaces/Daniel-F/mouse-behavioral-study. When scoring the familiarization and testing period recordings, we monitored the time spent interacting with each object until 20 s of cumulative exploration time was achieved between both objects, with a maximal time of 10 min to reach the criteria; those that did not meet the criteria were excluded from statistical analyses. Time spent exploring each respective object was recorded. Exploration activity was defined as “directing the nose toward the object at a distance less than or equal to 2 cm” and time spent climbing on or chewing on objects was not deemed exploration activity.84 The discrimination index was calculated by dividing the novel object exploration time by the total amount of object exploration during the test.

Tissue collection

Mice were euthanized following a 4-h fast. Mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane (1.0 L/min) and then decapitated to collect trunk blood. Hypothalamus was collected under a dissection microscope. Adipose tissues (BAT, iWAT, eWAT, and rWAT), liver, heart, pancreas, and gastrocnemius muscle were collected and weighed. Tissues to be used for mRNA and protein analyses were flash frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Serum harvest and analysis

Trunk blood was collected at euthanasia, clotted on ice, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The serum component was collected and stored at −20°C until further analysis. ELISAs were performed to assay serum leptin (R and D Systems Cat# DY498), serum adiponectin (R and D Systems Cat# DY1119), serum insulin (Alpco Diagnostics Cat# 80-INSMSU-E01), and serum exendin-4 (Phoenix Pharmaceuticals Cat#EK-070-94). Caymen Chemical colorimetric assay kit was used to assay glucose (#10009582) and triglycerides (#10010303). HOMA-IR index was calculated as (fasting serum glucose [mmol/L] × fasting serum insulin [pmol/L]/22.5). ALT and AST levels were determined by Comparative Pathology & Digital Imaging Shared Resource at Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center Department of Veterinary Biosciences.

Liver triglycerides assay

Hepatic lipid was extracted from 15 to 30 mg of wet liver tissues by chloroform/methanol (2:1 v/v), followed by rinse in 50 mM NaCl and CaCl2 (0.36 M)/methanol (1:1 v/v). An aliquot of the extract was mixed with Triton X-100 and cold acetone, then was dried up and redissolved in PBS. Hepatic triglyceride quantification was performed with a Caymen Chemical triglyceride assay kit (Caymen Chemical, #10010303).

Histology

Liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin. Paraffin embedding, sectioning, and H&E staining were conducted by Comparative Pathology & Digital Imaging Shared Resource at Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center Department of Veterinary Biosciences.

Quantitative RT-qPCR

Following tissue sonication, RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN #74804) with RNase-free DNase treatment. cDNA was reverse transcribed using Taqman Reverse Transcription Reagents (Applied Biosystems #N8080234). RT-qPCR was completed on the StepOnePlus real-time PCR system using Power SYBR green PCR master mix (Applied Biosystems #A25742). Primer sequences are available in Table S1. Data were calibrated to endogenous controls—Hprt1 for hypothalamus, Ppia for liver, and Actb for adipose tissue. The relative gene expression was quantified using the 2 −ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting

Tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Pierce #89901) containing 1 × PhosSTOP (Roche #4906845001) and protease inhibitor cocktail III (Calbiochem #539134). Tissue lysates were separated by gradient gel (4%–20%, Mini-PROTEAN TGX, Bio-Rad #4561096) and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad #1620115). Blots were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: GAPDH (Millipore Sigma Cat#CB1001-500UG, 1:1000), HA-tag (Cell Signaling Technology #3724, 1:1000), Exendin-4 (Bioss Antibodies Cat#bs-4121R, 1:500), β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology # 4970, 1:1000). Blots were rinsed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bio-Rad, 1:3000). Chemiluminescence signal was detected and visualized by Odyssey Fc imaging (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Quantification analysis was carried out with Image Studio software v.5.2 (LI-COR Biosciences).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Unpaired t test with Welch’s correction was utilized for comparisons between the two groups. One-way ANOVAs were used for comparisons between the three groups. Two-way ANOVAs were used for comparisons between four groups (genotype and Ex4 treatment as factors). Following ANOVAs, Tukey’s post hoc test was used to adjust pairwise comparisons of all groups. Time course data (body weights, GTT, and indirect calorimetry) were analyzed using two-way repeated measure (RM)-ANOVA followed by Sidak’s post hoc test to adjust multiple comparisons at each time point. Area under the curve calculations were performed where applicable and analyzed by one-way or two-way ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to adjust pairwise comparisons of all groups. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk method. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data are reported as means ± scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Sample sizes (n) can be found in each figure’s respective legend.
