Difficult airway registry – An essential step towards safer anaesthesia practices
Authors: Divya Jain, Rakesh Garg
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Airway management remains one of the critical aspects in the field of anaesthesiology, critical care, and emergency medicine. Failure to secure the airway can lead to life threatening complications, including hypoxia, cardiac arrest, or death.[1] Improvement in techniques, the implementation of guidelines and strategies for managing difficult airways has markedly decreased morbidity and mortality rates.[2]

AIRWAY MANAGEMENT GUIDELINES

In 2016, the All India Difficult Airway Association (AIDAA) formulated the first evidence based guidelines for difficult tracheal intubation in adults, paediatrics, and obstetrics, catering to the Indian context.[345] Since then, there has been a significant increase in the equipment and upgradation of techniques used for airway management. To keep pace with the evolving literature resources, AIDAA has published updated guidelines in this issue.[678] Despite this, amalgamating these guidelines into daily clinical practice is not without challenges. A survey among Indian anaesthesiologists revealed varying levels of awareness and adherence to AIDAA guidelines. Only 60–70% of the practising anaesthesiologists followed recommended protocols in practice. The authors identified limited resources, lack of awareness, and psychological challenges of transitioning from familiar international protocols as major barriers.[9] Despite AIDAA’s efforts to promote patient safety, inadequate documentation and lack of systematic reporting of adverse events continue to hinder the evaluation of its effectiveness in improving clinical outcomes.

What next?

Guidelines alone are insufficient unless supported by reliable data to track compliance and guide improvements. In India, an increasing number of surgeries are performed annually across diverse healthcare settings, from well-equipped tertiary care centres to resource limited non-teaching hospitals. The absence of a centralised registry for tracking perioperative difficult airway incidents represents a glaring gap in patient safety infrastructure.

Although comprehensive national data are lacking, existing global literature suggests that the incidence of difficult airways is 1–3% in elective cases and as high as 10–20% in urgent cases.[10] Airway management in India faces multifaceted challenges, compounded by the limited availability of advanced airway equipment and the lack of specialised training for healthcare providers, especially in emerging branches like emergency medicine. Non-anaesthetist residents often handle airways with inadequate preparation, resulting in complication rates as high as 21.7% for airway injuries compared to 6.1% done under the supervision of trained anaesthesiologists.[11]

NEED FOR PERIOPERATIVE AIRWAY MANAGEMENT REGISTRY

An airway registry is a systematic database that collects data on airway interventions. Despite the establishment of airway management registries in emergency departments in many countries,[12] there remains a paucity of standardised perioperative airway registries. To enhance communication regarding patients with difficult airways and thereby improve patient safety, the Difficult Airway Society (DAS) launched the National Difficult Airway Alert Card and Airway Database project in 2016. This initiative comprises an online database that enables clinicians to record details of airway management and outcomes in patients with difficult airways, ensuring that future providers are aware of the history of difficult airway, facilitating better preparedness.[13] Similarly, AIDAA also released its difficult airway alert card, but the registry was lacking in the Indian population.[3]

Core objectives of the registry

A national perioperative difficult airway registry could serve multiple pivotal functions. First, it would enable the collection of anonymised real-time patient data, including predictors (e.g., Mallampati scores, neck circumference, etc.), management techniques employed, outcomes, and complications, enabling clinicians to identify at-risk individuals and tailor the airway management techniques and strategies accordingly. The national registry would generate invaluable data for studies on predictors tailored to Indian demographics. The Paediatric Difficult Intubation Registry (PeDI) serves as a prime example of how rigorously collected multicenter data have translated into improved paediatric airway management protocols.[14] National Audit Project (NAP4) collected prospective data on major complications of airway management across all NHS hospitals, provided critical insights into airway-related deaths, and led to widespread improvements in training and equipment standards.[10]

Secondly, the registry could serve as a platform to foster accountability through audits and enhance quality improvement. Emergency surgical airway, although rare, carries a significant risk of adverse events. The emergency front of neck access (eFONA) registry, initiated by the Royal College of Anaesthesiologists (RCOA) and DAS, aims to collect data from the reported cohort of cases to identify significant themes of both optimal and inadequate practices, common to these cases, and thereby make recommendations that would allow clinicians working in anaesthesia, intensive care, and emergency medicine to perform the skill safely.[15]

By mandating reporting of difficult airway events (both anticipated and unanticipated), the registry could track compliance, identify training gaps, and support the development of training and simulation-based education programs. The INTUBATE COVID Registry provided evidence supporting the effectiveness of videolaryngoscopy and aerosol-mitigation strategies, which helped form standardised operating protocols (SOPs) for airway management in COVID-positive patients.[16]

Data from registries fuel research into new tools, techniques, and protocols for airway management. The integration of collaborative research paves the way for the formulation and updating of the guidelines with emerging safer techniques.

Additionally, the registry provides a clear record for medicolegal purposes, protecting both patients and providers by ensuring transparency and accountability. The benefits extend beyond clinical outcomes. Economically, reducing airway related complications could lower healthcare costs associated with prolonged hospital stays and litigation.

Challenges in implementing

Despite the undeniable need, implementing a nationwide airway registry in a vast and diverse healthcare system like India’s is fraught with logistical hurdles, including concerns over data privacy, potential for underreporting due to fear of litigation, and difficulties with integration of electronic health records (EHRs). The absence of interoperable EHR systems complicates centralised data aggregation, hindering real-time access to critical airway events.

Ensuring high quality data collection and ongoing monitoring is difficult in resource limited settings like ours. Lack of financial resources restricts the ability to hire trained personnel, procure data management systems, or sustain long-term registry operations, leading to incomplete or inconsistent data collection.[17] Furthermore, technical challenges like difficulty with connectivity in rural areas further impede consistent data entry. The diverse cultural and administrative landscape in India requires extensive collaboration across states and institutions, which is challenging to coordinate.

The way forward

Collaborative efforts from associations like the stakeholders involved in airway management and national professional bodies could initiate this, starting with pilot programs in tertiary academic institutions and expanding nationwide. This calls for the technological integration to implement automated EHRs for documentation of difficult airways, as seen in some global studies, to reduce manual data entry burdens.

By doing so, we can pave the way for safer airway practices, reducing morbidity and mortality. The evidence is clear: Registries are not just tools for data—they are catalysts for transformation in patient care.

CONCLUSION

The time is ripe for India to establish a registry for perioperative difficult airways. By systematically documenting and analysing these high-stakes events, we can move from reactive crisis management to proactive prevention, ensuring safe airway management in every patient.
