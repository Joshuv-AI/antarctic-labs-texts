Intra-articular synovial arteriovenous malformation: A case report study
Authors: Mohammed Almuhanna, Fahad Alhamad, Mohammed Almulhim, Moayad Almaimani, Ahmed Aldraihem, Aljoharah Aljabr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synovial arteriovenous malformation (SAVM) of the knee is an exceedingly rare vascular anomaly, often presenting a diagnostic challenge in pediatric populations due to its nonspecific symptoms of chronic pain and recurrent swelling. We present the case of a 10-year-old boy with a 15-month history of intermittent right knee pain and difficulty bearing weight, despite normal initial radiographs. Advanced multiplanar MRI revealed an ill-defined intra-articular lesion within Hoffa’s fat pad. The imaging features was not specific, and the differential diagnosis include AVM and Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor (TGCT). Following surgical excision, histopathological evaluation confirmed a synovial arteriovenous malformation. This case emphasizes that while SAVM mimics common conditions like juvenile idiopathic arthritis or TGCT.

Introduction

Arteriovenous malformation of the knee considered one of vascular malformations that rarely involves the synovial membrane [1,2]. Joint pain and swelling are common clinical symptoms that are often nonspecific and the SAVM may be associated with spontaneous hemarthrosis [3,4]. The SAVM develops gradually and may cause persistent synovial inflammation and consequent joint degeneration [1].

Case presentation

A 10-year-old boy presented to the emergency department with a 15-month history of recurrent right knee pain and intermittent swelling. His symptoms had exacerbated during the week prior to evaluation, resulting in impaired weight-bearing and a restricted range of motion.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. He was mildly overweight and demonstrated mild bilateral genu valgum. Inspection of the right knee revealed no swelling, skin discoloration, or effusion. The knee exhibited full, painless range of motion without joint line tenderness. Patellar tracking and lateral translation were normal, and the patellar apprehension test was negative.

A mild joint effusion was present. The menisci, articular cartilage, cruciate and collateral ligaments, and extensor mechanism were intact. No Baker’s cyst, bursitis, abnormal bone marrow signal, or surrounding muscular abnormality was identified.

Given the imaging appearance, a synovial‑based vascular lesion was suspected, and surgical excision was performed for definitive diagnosis.

Histopathological evaluation of the excised tissue confirmed a synovial arteriovenous malformation, correlating with the preoperative imaging findings.

Discussion

Synovial hemangioma is a benign vascular lesion that results from the proliferation of the blood vasculature. It is an important but rare entity in the young population [5,2]. It accounts for about 0.07% of soft-tissue tumors, with fewer than 200 reported in the literature. It occurs in many joints [3,4]. Histopathology can specify the predominant type of proliferation in synovial hemangioma, which includes capillary, cavernous, venous, or arteriovenous [5,3].

Clinically, the patient could present with recurrent joint swelling, restricted range of motion, spontaneous intra-articular bleeding, palpable mass, or intermittent arthralgia [5,3,4]. In our case, there were recurrent episodes of right knee pain and swelling over the past year and three months. Multiple differential diagnoses should be considered in children and adolescents presenting with knee swelling and pain. The symptoms could be related to synovial chondromatosis, Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor (TGCT), juvenile idiopathic arthritis, hemophilic arthropathy, synovial lipoma, and malignant tumors such as chondrosarcoma, synovial metastases, and synovial sarcoma [5,2,3]. In imaging, radiographs can have no abnormalities at the early stages. However, in later diagnosed cases, they may show amorphous calcifications, phleboliths, soft tissue swelling, or bony erosions [5,6]. In our case, there was normal knee alignment with no evidence of acute or chronic osseous pathology. Doppler ultrasound might reveal a lesion of internal vascularity with homogeneous hypoechogenicity [5]. In our case, an ultrasound was bypassed in favor of advanced cross-sectional imaging. Angiography could be indicated in patients with abnormal varicosities and cutaneous hemangiomas, which could reveal the feeder vessels to allow for embolization if needed [5]. The gold standard modality of diagnosis of synovial hemangioma is MRI. It will appear as an intra-articular lobulated mass. In T1-weighted images, it will usually appear as an intermediate or isointense signal with a corresponding high T2 signal intensity, with an associated enhancement after administration of contrast, confirming the vascular nature. The small intravenous calcifications(phleboliths) can be present and would appear as small signal voids in both T1 and T2 weighted images. Gradient-echo images might help in detecting prior bleeding. Moreover, MRI allows for assessment of the location, extension, size, exclusion of other differentials, and possible complications [5,[2], [3], [4]]. In our case, the MRI findings showed nearly the same features.

Although characteristic MRI features strongly suggest a diagnosis of synovial hemangioma, its clinical rarity necessitates careful differentiation from other soft-tissue masses, particularly tenosynovial giant cell tumor (TGCT) and synovial sarcoma. Unlike synovial hemangiomas, which are intrinsically intra-articular, synovial sarcomas predominantly arise in the juxta-articular soft tissues and only rarely manifest within the joint space itself [4]. In our case, the lesion was intra-articular in location, which in turn made synovial sarcoma of low probability. However, a definite diagnosis necessitates histopathology. TGCT is of intermediate signal on T1-weighted images with corresponding low T2 signal intensity, with areas of high signal intensity due to the synovium inflammation. There is variable enhancement upon contrast administration with hemorrhagic components which are bright on fat-saturated sequences. Hemosiderin deposits are possible in TGCT and Synovial hemangioma. Multiple treatment options are provided in the literature, including sclerotherapy, surgical excision (arthroscopic or open surgery), radiotherapy, arthroscopic ablation with laser, and selective embolization [5,2,4]. In our case, surgical excision was done for definitive diagnosis. Recurrence is possible in cases of diffuse synovial hemangioma, which necessitates long-term follow-up. Furthermore, early diagnosis is essential since recurrent hemarthrosis can lead to chronic inflammatory synovitis, osteoarthritis, and joint destruction [5,1].

In conclusion, synovial hemangioma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric patients presenting with chronic pain and recurrent knee swelling. Because the clinical presentation frequently overlaps with other intra-articular pathologies, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays an integral role in identifying the lesion, though definitive diagnosis ultimately requires histopathological confirmation. Early therapeutic intervention is paramount to halt disease progression and prevent debilitating complications, such as secondary osteoarthritis and permanent joint destruction.
