Biomarkers of oxidative damage as a tool to investigate frailty syndrome in older women
Authors: Magdalena Sepúlveda, Iván Palomo, Felipe Lagos, Ximena Pardo, Camilo López-Alarcón, Felipe Ávila, Eduardo Fuentes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Frailty syndrome (FS) is a critical factor associated with several oxidative stress and age-related diseases. In this work, we investigated the connection between frailty, defined at early stages using a new cut-off value of the Frailty Trait Scale (FTS-5), and plasma biomarkers of oxidative stress. Plasma samples of 76 older women were analyzed to determine free and protein-bounded tryptophan (Trp), kynurenine (Kyn), N-formylKynurenine (N-Kyn), advanced glycation end products, and protein carbonylation. A subgroup of 30 subjects was selected from the entire group to determine malondialdehyde (MDA), free methionine (Met), phenylalanine (Phe), Trp, and Kyn. In the whole group (N = 76), Kyn and the Kyn/Trp ratio were higher in women with a Romberg test altered (p-values = 0.0439, and 0.0472, respectively) and BMI < 25.3 kg/m2 (p-values = 0.0008, and 0.0011, respectively). Increased protein carbonylation was found for subjects with BMI ≥ 25.3 kg/m2. For the subgroup, an increase in the Kyn/Trp ratio was observed in subjects with an altered Romberg test, concomitant with decreased levels of Trp, Met, Phe, and MDA (p-values = 0.0255, 0.0427, 0.0459, and 0.0173, respectively) when data were grouped according to the modified FTS-5. These results highlight the relevance of oxidative biomarkers as a tool for an early diagnosis of FS and its related complications.

Introduction

Globally, the population is aging at an accelerated rate with estimations of people over 65 years of age by 2050 reaching 2 billion1. This trend arises as a consequence of the increase in life expectancy, which is a positive result worldwide related to improvements in health, nutrition, and socioeconomic conditions2. Despite such behavior represent good advances for the society, several problems associated with the extended of life expectancy, where the frailty syndrome is one of the most important, have been evidenced1. Frailty syndrome has emerged as one of the main risk factor that can lead to older population losing their self-worth with the consequent impairment in their quality of life and health3. Frailty corresponds to a clinically recognizable state in which older people lose their ability to cope with daily stressors, accompanied by an increase in vulnerability, which is caused by an impairment in multiple physiological systems4.

Frailty has been associated with increased oxidative stress and concomitant impair on specific organs, the latter being related to multisystem dysfunction5,6. The deleterious effects of the frailty-linked oxidative stress involve oxidative damage of macromolecules, with oxidation of proteins, and changes in their biological functions, being a principal process7. Oxidation of free amino acids in plasma is also possible, which can be monitored by the production of their respective products, with some of these species proposed as plausible biomarkers of frailty3.

Tryptophan (Trp) is one of the most susceptible amino acids to oxidation mediated by reactive species, but also by oxidative metabolism in the well-known kynurenine (Kyn) pathway. The content of free Trp in plasma is controlled by the diet (Trp is an essential amino acid8,9), its direct oxidation induced by reactive species, and the activity of the Kyn pathway, which accounts almost 95% of the metabolism of the ingested Trp10. Interestingly, Kyn, and N-formylKynurenine (N-Kyn) are common oxidation products of both routes11–13. In the Kyn pathway, where indolamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) is the rate-limiting enzyme14, N-Kyn is first generated and then Kyn, which is catabolized into several metabolites8. Consumption of Trp by the Kyn pathway or by direct oxidation triggered by reactive species would explain the decreased levels of Trp reported in the plasma of frail older people when compared with samples obtained from non-frail people (classified by a cognitive test, mobility, and a survey for daily activities)15. The metabolism of Trp through the Kyn pathway has been associated with the modulation of inflammation16, and the related onset of age-related diseases17, for which the Kyn/Trp ratio has been used as plausible biomarker. Increased Kyn/Trp ratio in plasma has been also connected with increased frailty18, reduced cognitive performance19, increased risk of cardiovascular diseases20, and mortality21. Determinations in serum and brain tissue have also shown increased levels of the Kyn/Trp ratio and Kyn in an age-dependent manner16,22, which have been linked to a higher activity of the isoform 1 of IDO (IDO-1) enzyme23. This has been linked with increased production of interleukins -6 (IL-6) and -1β (IL-1β), and interferon γ (IFN-γ)24, which are powerful activators of IDO-116. The utility of Trp and Kyn as biomarkers of frailty has been supported in various studies involving different cohorts of older people from different countries. An investigation developed in Spain in which 259 older subjects were enrolled (40 non-frail, 131 pre-frail, and 88 frail, according to the Fried Frailty Phenotype) showed significant differences (p < 0.001) of the Kyn/Trp ratio amongst the groups studied25. Similarly, changes in the Kyn/Trp ratio were observed in a study involving 73 patients from South Korea (17 non-frail, 44 pre-frail, and 12 frail, according to Fried’s criteria)26. In this investigation, significant differences were observed when Kyn levels were compared between frail and non-frail (p = 0.001), and also pre-frail (p = 0.01) groups26. The same findings have recently been found in a study carried out on 291 older people in Portugal (111 non-frail, 130 pre-frail, and 50 frail according to Fried’s criteria), with significant differences between the groups in the Kyn/Trp ratio, with a p value < 0.00127.

Additionally to Trp and Kyn, the total content of carbonyl groups in proteins, species generated by oxidation of several amino acids, and also by covalent addition of aldehyde or ketone species to protein chains, has also been associated with oxidative stress and frailty28. In the FRAILOMIC initiative, in a cohort of 1450 participants, higher levels of protein carbonyls in plasma were reported for frail and pre-frail groups compared to non-frail (p-value 0.006)29. Another study, including 100 older participants with type 2 diabetes, reported increased levels of protein-bound carbonyls in the plasma of frail subjects in comparison to pre-frail or non-frail patients30.

Frailty of older people is modulated by multiple factors (including the socio-cultural origin of people), making it difficult to diagnose and treat. For such reasons, despite the advances in the field, it is of high relevance to get new insights linking biomarkers of oxidative stress and early markers of frailty. In such scenario, aimed to improve the connection between the level of frailty of older subjects and biomarkers of oxidative modifications, we have previously explored how changes in the levels of oxylipins (species resulting from lipid oxidation) in plasma of women reflect early stages of frailty when a different cut-off is employed involving higher or equal than 19 points for FTS-5 (Frailty Trait Scale) is employed31. In the present work, we have extended such investigations by carrying out a clinical study to investigate the connection between frailty of older women in Chile, using the new scale of frailty (cut-off) recently reported31, and biomarkers of oxidative stress. The levels in plasma samples of 76 older women of free and protein-bound Kyn, N-Kyn, and advanced glycation end products (characterized by different fluorescence properties, AGEs1 and AGEs232), as well as protein carbonylation, were systematically determined and compared with frailty and other clinical parameters. In addition, to search new biomarkers of oxidative modifications and the convenience of their use for early diagnosis of frailty, samples coming from 30 subjects were selected from the entire group (76 women) to determine malondialdehyde (MDA, usually used as indicator of peroxidation of lipids33), free methionine (Met, a susceptible to oxidation amino acid), phenylalanine (Phe, a target of hydroxyl radicals34,), Trp, and Kyn.

Determination of total (free and protein-bound) Kyn, N-Kyn, and AGEs, and protein carbonyls in the whole group of subjects (N = 76)

As Fig. 1 shows, older people with a score ≥7.5 points determined by the Romberg test, showed higher levels of Kyn, and the Kyn/Trp ratio than subjects with scores <7.5 points, with p-values of 0.0439 and 0.0472, respectively. Similarly, subjects with a score ≥7.5 points showed higher levels of the fluorescent AGEs1/glycaemia ratio, however, such difference was not statistically significant (p-value 0.1626). When subjects were grouped considering the BMI, results obtained for older women with a BMI < 25.3 kg/m2 showed higher levels of Kyn, Kyn/Trp, N-Kyn, N-Kyn/Trp, and AGEs2, with p-values showing statistical differences (p-values 0.0008, 0.0011, 0.0375, 0.0465, and 0.0076, respectively). Higher levels of the AGEs2/glycaemia ratio were also determined, however, without statistical significance (p-value 0.0790). Discussion

It is well known that frailty is a syndrome that progressively affects multiple physiological systems, and once it reaches an advanced stage, individuals begin to exhibit the consequences of physical frailty. It is important to have some health programs and recommendations to avoid the development and progression of frailty, where its early detection, based on changes of some blood markers before the manifestation of physical frailty, is a pivotal point. For this reason, the identification and validation of biomarkers are essential to support the early diagnosis of frailty. Due to the connection between frailty and oxidative stress, and the limitation in biological samples to directly determine oxidants (they commonly have lifetimes in the nano- to millisecond range35–37), evaluation of the oxidative modifications of low-weight molecules and macromolecules in plasma could constitute a promising strategy to determine the oxidative damage connected to frailty. As biomarkers, we have selected carbonyl groups and MDA, which represent oxidation of plasma proteins and lipids, respectively. In addition, we have also assessed the changes in the fluorescence associated with Trp, Kyn, N-Kyn, and AGEs, in total plasma. Combined analysis of these biomarkers can be considered as a valuable tool to investigate the connection between oxidative stress and frailty, as well as the association with physiological and pathological events38. Increased values of carbonyls and MDA in older people (65 years and more) have been linked with frailty, according to Fried’s phenotype, suggesting that both biomarkers could be employed to predict frailty in geriatric people39. We have previously reported increased levels of thromboxane B2 in plasma samples of women with a cut-off higher or equal than 19 points for FTS-5 scale, in comparison with the group of women with less than 19 points of the same scale, evidencing the advantages of the use of this limit to early detection of frailty31. In this work, we analyzed plasma samples of a group of 76 older women, assessing total content of Trp, Kyn, and N-Kyn (free and protein-bound), and protein carbonylation. A subgroup of 30 subjects was selected in which Trp, Kyn, Met, Phe, and MDA were determined, according to a frailty cut-off of 19 points, which also includes a cut-off for grip strength (<22 kg), Romberg test (≥7.5 points), and BMI (≥25.3 kg/m2). These new cut-off values can be considered as early diagnosis criteria of the frailty syndrome. We determined the changes in the fluorescence associated to AGEs, which have been related to frailty and human disorders in older subjects40,41. In addition, we evaluated the levels of different markers of oxidative stress, including protein carbonyls, as well as the fluorescence associated with Trp, Kyn, N-Kyn, and two pairs of excitation/emission wavelengths attributed to AGEs1 and AGEs2.

We found high levels of Kyn and a high Kyn/Trp ratio in women with a score ≥7.5 points in Romberg test, p-values 0.0439 and 0.0472, respectively. The analysis of BMI showed higher levels of fluorescence associated with Kyn, Kyn/Trp ratio, N-formylKyn, N-formylKyn/Trp ratio, and AGEs2 (p-values 0.0008, 0.0011, 0.0375, 0.0465, and 0.0076, respectively) in women with a BMI < 25.3 kg/m2. On the other hand, higher levels of carbonyls (p-value 0.0278) were observed in women with a BMI ≥ 25.3 kg/m2.

Jang et al. evaluated the levels of Kyn of 73 participants and their relationship with frailty, which was evaluated using the Fried’s Phenotype and the Rockwood Frailty Index (FI)26. They found that the levels of Kyn and the Kyn/Trp ratio had a positive correlation with the FI and a negative correlation with grip strength and gait speed26. Another study carried out by Al Saedi et al., they analyzed the samples from 85 participants to evaluate the Kyn pathway24. They found that quinolinic acid and the quinolinic acid/Kyn ratio were associated with a high probability of frailty, and high levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) in people with high levels of Kyn24. Another point that is important to mention is that our results showed higher levels of uric acid in women with a score ≥19 points according to the new proposed score for FTS-5 (p-values 0.0068 and 0.0016). Li et al. developed a mouse model knockout (Ko) for the urate oxidase (Uox) gene, Uox-Ko mice, for study hyperuricemia, and they found that the Uox-Ko mice had higher levels of Kyn and xanthurenic acid in the Kyn pathway42. It could be a good point to study if frail women with hyperuricemia can present high levels of Kyn, considering our results and the previous data mentioned from another study.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α that activate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), induce the conversion of Trp to Kyn by the Kyn pathway43. Results obtained using a mouse model deficient of IL-10, which induced an increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α), showed a decreasing of Trp jointly with increasing of Kyn levels in middle-aged and old mice44. Interestingly, such association with age correlates with the comparison of the levels of Trp, Kyn and Kyn/Trp in young, non-frail and frail people, where lower levels of Trp and higher levels of Kyn and Kyn/Trp ratio were determined in frail people44.

We found that the concentration of Trp, Met, and Phe was lower in women with a low grip strength (<22 kg) (p-values 0.0311, 0.0352, and 0.0311, respectively) and with a score of Romberg test ≥7.5 points (p-values 0.0130, 0.0006, and 0.0005, respectively). Oxidation of Phe and Met can generate oxidation products such as o-Tyr and methionine sulfoxide, respectively45. The increase in the levels of carbonylated proteins, as well as Kyn, together with a decrease of free Met, and Phe, suggest that non-enzymatic reactions can also contribute to the frailty phenotype.

This work shows that, in the plasma samples of the older women here studied, there are significant differences in the levels of Trp and Kyn, which are associated with parameters of frailty, like body composition (BMI), nervous system (Romberg test), and grip strength. In the case of the FTS-5 scale, it is possible to find differences with the cut-off 19 points in the levels of oxidizable amino acids, including Trp, Met, and Phe, as well as malondialdehyde, a marker for oxidative stress. This study suggests that it is important to analyze the oxidative stress and its implications in the frailty syndrome, considering the discovery of some novel biomarkers being a useful tool for the early diagnosis of this syndrome.

Finally, the present work was based on determinations of biomarkers which are not (easily) available at public and private clinical centers. Despite we believe our work is contributing to the scientific investigation (at laboratory level), necessary to be developed before the translation to clinical routine determinations, the application of the here reported experimental approach to routine clinical practice is challenging goal.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of the University of Talca (protocol code 06-2021, and date of approval April 14, 2021), and all procedures complied with national regulations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Subjects signed an informed consent to participate in the study. Employed samples correspond to the plasma of 76 women previously collected in a clinical study, and stored at −80 °C. Then, from the initial 76 samples of women, we randomly selected 30 samples to measure the concentration of some amino acids. Inclusion criteria were to be an age of 65 years or more, and the exclusion criteria were people with a state of dependency or disability. The Fried and FTS-5 scales were applied to participants who met these criteria through a survey.

Data collection

The sociodemographic characteristics of participants, such as gender, age, years of schooling, and place of residence, were collected through an individual interview. Data such as weight and height, to calculate the Body Mass Index (BMI), were also collected. Additionally, waist circumference, which it was used to determine if the person had abdominal obesity, according to the ATP III (Adult Treatment Panel) criteria was also determined. It was considered abdominal obesity, a waist circumference greater than 88 cm46.

Frailty diagnosis

According to the Frailty Trait Scale (FTS-5), 5 dimensions were evaluated: energy balance or nutrition (represented by the BMI), physical activity (Physical Activity Scale score for the Elderly, PASE), nervous system (score obtained from the Romberg test, which evaluates the proprioception or the ability of the body to sense the position and movement47), strength (determined by the grip strength measured by a hand dynamometer) and gait speed (in 3 m)48. Each dimension was weighted based on the score obtained by each person in the study population, where “frail” was classified by a score over 25 points and “non-frail” for scores less than 25 points49. As previously mentioned, in the herein reported data a cut-off 19 points, previously reported31, was employed.

Sample preparation

Employed samples correspond to two blood samples each taken in independent sessions; one collected in vials containing anticoagulant sodium citrate (3.2%), and another collected in vials without anticoagulant. After taken, samples were centrifuged to 1570 g for 8 min to obtain platelet-poor plasma (PPP) and serum, respectively. Then, the PPP and serum were frozen at −80 °C until processing. When used, samples were thawed at room temperature. From the entire group (samples from 76 women), PPP samples from 30 subjects were selected (named N = 30 subgroup). These samples were submitted to additional processing to deplete the major plasma proteins, using a Multiple Affinity Removal Spin Cartridge column, Human-14, 0.45 mL (Agilent), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, to be concentrated in Vivaspin 10 kDa. These samples were used to assess free amino acids (Trp, Met, and Phe), as well as MDA (section 4.5).

Analysis of plasma samples

The reagents trichloroacetic acid, o-phtadialdehyde, β-mercaptoethanol, sodium acetate, methanol, tetrahydrofuran, amino acid standards, Kyn, and chloroform were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Pierce™ Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit was purchased from Thermo Scientific (Rockford, IL, USA).

For the determination of total (free and protein bounded) Kyn, N-Kyn, AGEs, and protein carbonyl (N = 76), first we quantified the total content of proteins in plasma samples through the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, using bovine serum albumin as the reference protein, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

After that, we determined the content of protein-bound carbonyl groups in plasma proteins according to Levine et al., with some modifications. 200 µL plasma (1 mg mL−1 protein concentration) was derivatized with 1 mL 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) (0.2% p/v). Subsequently, the proteins were precipitated with 1 mL of trichloroacetic acid (20%) and centrifuged at 1610 g and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with a cold mixture of ethanol and ethyl acetate 1:1 to remove the excess of DNPH. Then 1 mL of 6 M guanidine was added, and the resulting solutions were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. The absorbance was determined in a UV/vis spectrophotometer (Spectroquant® Pharo 300, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), reading at 370 nm. Carbonyl concentration was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 22000 M−1 cm−150. The results were expressed as nmol carbonyl groups mg−1 of protein.

The next step was determining the fluorescence of Trp, Kyn, N-Kyn, and AGEs in plasma samples. To perform it, plasma samples were diluted with 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) until reaching 1 mg mL−1 of total protein content ( ~ 2.5 mL final volume). Excitation (λEXC) and emission (λEM) wavelengths (λ EXC / λ EM), for Trp, Kyn, N-Kyn, AGEs1, and AGEs2 were set to: 295 nm / 360 nm; 365 nm / 480 nm; 325 nm / 434 nm; 325 nm / 440 nm; and 389 nm / 443 nm, respectively51,52. Fluorescence measurements were carried out in a fluorescence spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, LS55, Waltham, USA).

For the quantification of Trp, Met, Phe, and Kyn by HPLC (N = 30), we removed proteins with a molecular mass lower than 10 kDa. Depleted protein samples (50 μL) were additionally treated with 0.15 mM trichloroacetic acid (TCA, cold at 4 °C) followed by delipidation by addition of chloroform, according to Wróbel et al.53. The resulting mixture was shaken vigorously and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was extracted carefully. Then, the samples were frozen at −20 °C, and subsequently lyophilized and kept at −20 °C until analysis. The day of the analysis, samples were resuspended in 100 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, using a precolumn derivatization with ortho-phtaldehyde (OPA) according to Hawkins et al.54. Briefly, 230 µL of deproteinized (and resuspended) samples were mixed with 270 µL OPA previously activated with β-mercaptoethanol. Standard solutions were prepared by mixing a known volume of 0.5–5 µM Trp, Met, Phe, and Kyn with another volume of activated OPA. These solutions were injected onto an Inertsil ODS-3 RP-18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm particle size, GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan), in an HPLC system equipped with an autosampler and online DAD and fluorescence detectors (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). The flow rate was set to 1 mL min−1 and the temperature of the oven to 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of buffer A containing 400 mL methanol, 50 mL tetrahydrofuran, 1450 mL H2O, and 100 mL of 1 M sodium acetate, pH 5.0; buffer B contained 1600 mL methanol, 50 mL tetrahydrofuran, 250 mL H2O, and 100 mL of 1 M sodium acetate, pH 5.0. The gradient program was as follows: 5% B from 0 to 7 min; 5% to 25% B from 7 to 17 min; 25% to 50% B from 17 to 27 min; 50% B from 27 to 35 min; 50% to 100% B from 35 to 40 min; 100% B from 40 to 46 min; 100% to 5% B from 46 to 48 min, and 5% B from 48 to 60 min. The fluorescence detector was set to 340 nm and 440 nm as excitation (λEXC) and emission (λEM) wavelengths, respectively. Quantification of analytes was carried out by interpolation of the areas of each peak into the respective calibration curves.

Finally, for the quantification of malondialdehyde (MDA), an aliquot of each sample, a point of the curve, and distilled water (reagent blank) were taken, and 0.67% TBA (thiobarbituric acid), and 50% TCA (trichloroacetic acid) were added. They were incubated for 30 min at 90 °C, then centrifuged at 608 g for 15 min. An aliquot of the supernatant was taken, and the absorbance was read at 532 nm. A calibration curve (1–10 µM) was prepared using a standard of MDA. Finally, the calibration curve was prepared, and each point on the curve was intercepted to obtain the concentration expressed in nmol MDA/mL serum.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted employing GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or as median (95% confidence interval, CI). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages along with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Skewed variables were conveyed as medians accompanied by a 95% confidence interval. To assess categorical variable discrepancies between groups, the Chi-square test was employed. In evaluating distinctions in means and medians, Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney test were utilized, respectively. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test55. Outliers were identified and removed using the ROUT method, with a Q = 1% in GraphPad Prism 9 software. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
