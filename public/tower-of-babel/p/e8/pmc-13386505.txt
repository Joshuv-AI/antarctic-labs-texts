Emphasizing society's expectations enhances charitable giving in NGO advertisements
Authors: Toshiko Tanaka, Tsuyoshi Nihonsugi, Masahiko Haruno, Valerio Capraro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople are often prompted to donate to charities through subtle cues rather than purely noble intentions. While previous studies have identified various factors that promote prosocial behavior, these factors have not been fully tested in designing effective donation advertisements. Here, focusing on advertisements for the nongovernmental organization WaterAid Japan, we conducted an online nudge experiment (n = 6,232) that revealed emphasizing “society's expectation” is most effective at soliciting donations without adverse psychological effects. We then tested messages with this emphasis in real-world advertisements on a commercial online platform (Yahoo Ads), comparing them to alternative framings that emphasized “inequity aversion,” “sense of control,” or “warm glow.” The “society's expectation” message achieved the highest number of impressions and clicks to the final donation page, suggesting its strong effect in activating prosocial instincts in real-world social decision-making.Keywords: prosocial behavior, charitable giving, nudge, belief, society's expectation, guilt aversion

Charitable giving is often influenced by subtle social cues, yet the relative effectiveness of different message framings has not been systematically compared. By combining a large-scale online experiment (n = 6,232) with a field test using commercial online advertisements, we show that messages emphasizing society's expectation are most effective in engaging potential donors.

Introduction

In modern human society, charitable giving represents an important and distinct form of prosocial behavior (1, 2). Notably, individual donations account for more than half of all charitable contributions (3–5). Importantly, 15–20% of donors report having had no initial intention to donate (5, 6), suggesting that people can be nudged into giving through prosociality-promoting messages (7).

Previous studies in experimental psychology (2, 8, 9), behavioral economics (10–12), and social neuroscience (13, 14) have identified key motivations that underlie prosocial behavior. More specifically, recent models have incorporated prosociality-promoting factors into utility functions that guide decision-making such as altruism (ALT) (2, 15, 16), inequity aversion (INEQ) (10, 17–20), guilt aversion (ie aversion to violating other's or society's expectations; 21, 22), social conformity (CNF) (23), and reciprocity (24, 25).

However, no prior study has systematically tested the effectiveness of these prosocial motivations when framed as persuasive messages to increase charitable giving. The objective of this study is to investigate which prosociality-promoting factors most effectively increase the willingness to donate when delivered through nontargeted advertisements in the real world. In addition, since donation advertisements target a wide and diverse audience, they should do so without producing unintended negative psychological effects (26). In this article, we evaluated representative prosocial motivations framed as persuasive text messages encouraging donations to WaterAid Japan (https://www.wateraid.org/jp), an NGO working to provide clean water and sanitation worldwide (Figure 1).

More specifically, we conducted an online nudge experiment (27) to assess the effectiveness of messages that emphasize different prosocial motivations (study 1). We also collected participants’ personality scores to examine whether the impact of each message varied across individual differences, thereby testing the utility of indiscriminate messaging.

In subsequent study 2, we tested the potentially effective messages identified in study 1 in real-world advertisements for WaterAid Japan on Yahoo. Notably, our analysis was not based on actual donations. This was because the real donation amounts cannot be disclosed publicly, and the number of donations was not enough to derive reliable statistics. We therefore evaluated the effects of the nudge advertisements using three behavioral indices: impressions, click-through rate (CTR), and donation page transitions. Impressions represent the extent to which each message attracted interest during the distribution period. CTR indexes active engagement of people, which is defined as the ratio of first clicks (initial interaction to open the nudge advertisement) to impressions. Finally, the number of transitions to the donation page assesses donation-oriented action by clicks after the initial ad interaction. In line with prior research on charitable giving and online fundraising, we adopted these impression- and click-based behavioral indices as proxies for donation-oriented motivation (28).

Thus, by integrating both experimental and field data, this study demonstrates that messages emphasizing social expectation are highly effective in encouraging charitable donations.

Results of study 1

We designed an online experiment to evaluate the effectiveness of the following prosociality-motivating components in nudge messages (study 1; see Figure 1, Table 1 and Supplementary information; Methods for study 1): ALT, social expectation (SoEx), INEQ, CNF, and sense of control (SC) on willingness to donate. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the 13 nudge messages emphasizing at least one component in a between-subjects design, such that each participant read only one message. After reading the message, they rated their willingness to donate on a 7-point scale. For the analysis, each motivation component was coded as a binary variable (0 = absent, 1 = present) depending on whether the component was included in the message or not.

Before analyzing data of the willingness to donate, we tested whether people perceived the prosociality-motivating components we intended in each nudge message by asking a different group of participants (n = 11) to rate how much they felt that each message incorporated each component when they read the message (on a scale of 0 to 10). We considered a nudge to have elicited the intended component if the group-level mean rating for an item exceeded 5 (annotated as ● in each component of Table 1). Thus, we confirmed that the messages convey the prosociality-motivating components as intended (Table 1).

We then evaluated the significance and the effect size of the contribution of each motivation component on the willingness to donate. We adopted a stepwise variable selection procedure combined with cross-validation for this purpose. More specifically, starting with the full model including all main effects and all their two-way interaction terms, we applied stepwise variable selection for removal to the training data in each cross-validation fold based on the AIC (29) and continued model improvement until no further reduction in the AIC was achieved. We regarded variables that were retained in all five cross-validation folds as stable predictors and used them to construct the final model. The final model was subsequently refitted using the full dataset (Fig. 2A and Table S1; see also Supplementary information) and showed an R2 of 0.032 (with the minimum AIC: 21612), indicating its modest but meaningful explanatory power.

We also assessed multicollinearity among (prosociality) motivation regressors using variance inflation factors (VIFs). We found that all adjusted VIF values (GVIF) were below 3, indicating that the regression coefficients were identifiable. As a robustness check, we additionally included a binary variable representing whether a message contained multiple components (ie message complexity) in the regression analysis. This variable did not replace the motivation (dummy) variables and was included as an additional control. Its inclusion did not alter the main results.

Crucially, our regression analysis demonstrated significant positive main effects of SoEx (P = 7.9 × 10−3) and the use of photographs depicting unhappy children (PhotoUC; P = 7.0 × 10−3), as well as a significant negative main effect of CNF (P = 1.2 × 10−3). Significant interaction effects were also identified between SoEx and SC (P = 1.3 × 10−4) and between CNF and income (P = 5.8 × 10−3). Consistent with a previous study (30), we also identified the main effect of gender (P = 1.1 × 10−4). Significant interactions of gender and age (P = 0.024), gender and income (P = 4.6 × 10−7), and age and income (P = 1.6 × 10−3) were also found. These results are visualized with a P-value of <0.05 (Fig. 2A). Whereas SoEx and SC interacted synergistically, CNF demonstrated a negative effect among individuals with higher income levels.

We also examined whether the effect of nudge messages depends on characteristics of the recipients of the messages by analyzing personality trait scores. This analysis helped us evaluate the usefulness of individually customized nudges. For each participants’ group who received a message containing each of the component ALT, SoEx, INEQ, SC, and CNF, we performed a LASSO regression to predict the willingness to donate (1–7) using personality trait scores, age, gender, and income as regressors. LASSO regression was suitable for this analysis since the input dimension including interactions was large (see also Supplementary information).

Figure 2B displays the LASSO coefficients. The top panel shows the coefficients for each personality trait score when all participants (all nudges: “ALL”) were included in the analysis. The subsequent panels illustrate the results separately for participants who received nudges containing the following components: ALT, SoEx, INEQ, SC, and CNF. Personality traits were color-coded based on coefficients in the ALL condition, with traits showing coefficients larger than 0.2 highlighted in red and those between 0.1 and 0.2 highlighted in blue.

Agreeableness (Big Five), prosocial orientation (SVO), personal distress (IRI), and happiness (SHS) were positively associated with a willingness to donate across nudge component groups. Importantly, no personality traits showed large negative coefficients or reversals in coefficient sign across groups. These observations suggest that no personality trait was specifically detrimental to a given nudge component, indicating that the added value of personality-targeted nudge messages was modest in the present task (see Supplementary information for details on personality measures).

To further test whether personality characteristics differentially impacted motivations, we included interactions between personality measures and nudge motivations (0/1 dummy variables, eg ALT vs. non-ALT) in a separate LASSO regression (Fig. 2C). We observed modest component-specific patterns: agreeableness was positively associated with SoEx, INEQ, and CNF; SVO prosociality with ALT, SC, and CNF; and IRI_PD (personal distress) with ALT, SoEx, and SC. These results suggest structured heterogeneity with limited effect sizes, whereby certain personality traits align with specific motivational messages.

Finally, indiscriminate advertising should not offend the recipients. We therefore asked the participants to rate the strength of their negative impressions about the nudge message they received (5-point scale). The results suggested that no nudges gave negative impressions: ie pressure to conform, repulsive feeling, and sense of discomfort (Fig. S1).

In summary, study 1 revealed that SoEx promoted the willingness to donate and CNF reduced it among individuals with higher income levels. We also confirmed that the nudge messages we prepared were not offensive to the recipients. It is notable that the effect of visual information (i.e. photos) was too large to ignore and that the emphasis on ALT and INEQ had small impacts, despite charitable giving being one of the most typical altruistic behaviors and a direct measure for alleviating inequity.

Results of study 2

In study 2, we designed actual advertisements for WaterAid Japan, which only consisted of texts on Yahoo, and conducted two separate measurements (Table 2). We quantified the effectiveness of each message as an advertisement by comparing the number of impressions, CTR (clicks/impressions), and the number of donation page transitions. Impression counts reflect the level of people's interest in each nudge message, whereas CTR reflects active engagement of people into the nudge message. The number of transitions to the donation page assesses donation-oriented action by counting clicks after the initial ad interaction.

Because the advertising platform algorithm tends to allocate more impressions to messages with higher engagement, messages that attract greater interest tend to receive more impressions. The high number of impressions for the SoEx nudge therefore indicates that the SoEx nudge elicited high interest.

Next, we examined the effectiveness of each nudge component using CTR, which normalizes clicks by the number of impressions, providing a measure of active engagement (Fig. 3B). The proportion test showed significant differences across nudges (χ2 = 36.1, P < 0.001). SoEx and WG showed the highest CTR (Table S2), while ALT and INEQ elicited no clicks, yielding the following ranking: SoEx = WG > SC > INEQ = ALT. Pairwise comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (31).

Finally, consistent with study 1, SoEx produced the largest number of behaviors toward donation. Specifically, we compared the number of transitions to the donation page (Table S2; Fig. 3C). The SoEx nudge yielded the highest number of page transitions, whereas no transitions were observed for ALT or INEQ. The cyan bar in Fig. 3C indicates messages that triggered an actual donation, showing that only the nudge message highlighting SoEx led to completed donations.

Thus, SoEx was the most effective component for motivating donations, followed by WG and SC, whereas INEQ and ALT showed the smallest effects. Based on these observations, we focused on SoEx, SC, and WG in the second measurement and further tested the robustness of the effectiveness of SoEx. Figure 3D–F shows the number of impressions, CTR, and number of page transitions for each message. Consistent with the first measurement, SoEx showed the strongest effects across impressions and CTR (Fig. 3D and E and Table S3). More specifically, proportion tests showed significant differences among messages for impressions and CTR (χ2 = 1.4 × 10−5, P < 0.001, and χ2 = 58.3, P < 0.001, respectively), and pairwise comparisons revealed the same ranking: SoEx > WG > SC (Table S3). Although page transitions also followed the same ranking pattern, one comparison did not reach a statistical significance (the difference between WG and SC was not significant; Fig. 3F and Table S3), likely due to the small number of transitions observed.

Taken together, our results demonstrate that SoEx was most effective in attracting interest as an advertisement, promoting engagement, and encouraging donation-oriented actions. Notably, in both the first and second measurements, actual donations were observed for the SoEx message (cyan bars in Fig. 3C and F).

General discussion

This study evaluated prosociality-promoting motivations for enhancing the willingness to donate to charities by using WaterAid Japan's real donation advertisements. Our online (simulation) experiment (study 1), which compared nudge texts including (intrinsic) motivational components, revealed not only that emphasizing SoEx is most effective at promoting the motivation to donate but also that SoEx does not create negative reactions such as discomfort.

We next validated the effectiveness of SoEx by designing real WaterAid Japan's donation ads (Yahoo listing ads). We evaluated the effects of nudges at three levels: interest, active engagement, and donation-oriented action. Interest reflects the diffusion of information and awareness of the need for donation, whereas active engagement and donation-oriented action capture the elicitation of behavioral responses that may ultimately translate into donation. These processes are closely related, as the dissemination of information is often tightly coupled with proactive action (32–34).

Donation motivations have been studied in psychology, behavioral economics, and neuroscience (12, 16, 35–38), and recent reports indicated that focusing on self-image concerns increases the willingness to donate (39). Also relevant is a recent behavioral study (40) showing that a simple moral nudge—prompting people to reflect on what is morally right—enhances subsequent altruistic and cooperative behaviors, including donation, although its application to advertising may not be straightforward. We demonstrated throughout this study that SoEx is most effective for motivating donations. This finding suggests that appealing to the “desire to act in a manner consistent with an image of the self that meets social expectations” can increase donation motivation more strongly than impressing other intrinsic motivations.

Why does emphasizing SoEx have such a strong effect on real-world prosocial behavior? First, previous research has repeatedly demonstrated that the human brain is sensitive to reward prediction errors not only for the self (41–43) but also for others (44). Building on this line of work, previous behavioral and theoretical studies showed that humans tend to avoid actions that would disappoint others or their expectations, a phenomenon termed as “guilt aversion” (21, 22, 45–47).

Second, Dufwenberg et al. (48) showed that framing can shift beliefs and thereby influence motivation and choice. In this sense, SoEx messages may function as belief-relevant framing devices in the donation context. Although our design did not directly measure belief updating, the results are consistent with the view that belief-dependent mechanisms identified in laboratory studies extend to real-world donation decisions.

Relating our findings to the literature on communication and guilt aversion may also provide a useful interpretative framework. Charness and Dufwenberg (45) showed that communication in strategic interactions can shift beliefs about others’ expectations and thereby affect guilt-averse behavior. In contrast, the present study examined messages from a nudging source rather than a strategic partner, yet the effectiveness of SoEx messages suggests that generalized social expectations can also translate belief-based motivations into real-world prosocial actions.

It is also important to consider whether the effectiveness of SoEx nudges is specific to collectivistic cultural contexts or generalizes more broadly. The present study was conducted in Japan, which is often characterized as a relatively collectivistic society. However, previous research on guilt aversion that considered expectations of others or society has reported comparable behavioral patterns across Japan, South Korea, and the United Kingdom (47). These findings suggest that the psychological mechanisms underlying SoEx nudges may not be limited to collectivistic cultures. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the effects of SoEx nudges generalize beyond the cultural context examined here. Future studies directly comparing individualistic and collectivistic societies are important for testing this possibility.

While SoEx showed the strongest and most consistent effects, examining the relative effectiveness of other nudge components also provides useful insights into the mechanisms underlying donation motivation. Compared with SoEx, warm glow (WG) nudges appear to operate through a related, but weaker, belief-dependent mechanism. Although WG framing does not explicitly refer to others’ expectations, it may implicitly evoke beliefs about social evaluation or one's self-image in relation to others. Such beliefs may generate affective signals resembling prediction errors, which may explain why WG showed relatively robust effects compared with purely altruistic framing. Notably, despite prior concerns that WG nudges emphasizing personal benefit might be less effective in Japan, our results indicate that WG is effective in the Japanese context and does not produce adverse effects.

Inequality-based (INEQ) nudges may engage belief-dependent processes even more indirectly. By highlighting disparities, these messages can activate social comparison and fairness norms, potentially eliciting beliefs about what constitutes acceptable or expected behavior in society. However, because such beliefs are not explicitly specified, the impact of INEQ framing appears more variable and sensitive to individual differences. Consistent with this interpretation, the effect of INEQ was modest in both studies and appeared to interact with demographic factors such as age and gender.

In contrast, ALT nudges largely represent a belief-independent form of motivation, grounded in stable preferences for others’ welfare rather than expectations or beliefs about others. As such, messages emphasizing ALT are less likely to influence behavior through belief updating. This interpretation is consistent with previous empirical findings showing limited or mixed effects of altruistic appeals in fundraising contexts (49), as well as with the relatively weak and inconsistent effects observed in the present study.

Taken together, the relatively strong effects of SoEx, WG, and SC may suggest that positively framed messages are effective in promoting donation intentions. Such messages may facilitate the formation or updating of belief-based expectations, from which simulated prediction errors are automatically computed.

Notably, although modest differences in responsiveness across personality traits were observed, these effects were limited in magnitude. The limited effects suggest that the effectiveness of SoEx and related belief-relevant framings was broadly distributed rather than confined to specific personality profiles.

This study has several limitations. First, it focused on intrinsic motivations, without examining the contribution of extrinsic motivation such as financial incentives, praise, and tax exemptions. Second, the interaction between visual and textual components was not explored in the real ads condition. With the rise of video ads, future research should investigate the combination of visual and verbal elements. In addition, the real Yahoo verification used its own delivery algorithm, resulting in an unusually high number of SoEx impressions. It might be necessary to validate the results using delivery algorithms of other media. Despite these limitations, the results of the present study demonstrated that belief-related framing that invokes generalized social expectations represents a scalable and robust strategy for promoting real-world prosocial behavior.

Materials and methods

All research methods were in accordance with the Code of Ethics of Japanese Psychological Association (third) and Ethics Checklist of Japanese Behavioral Sciences Team. All experimental procedures in this study were approved by the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (NICT) ethics committee on 2021 January 22. The handling of the personal data was approved by NICT (c-19400_160711_01). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. When a participant was under the age of 18, informed consent was obtained from his/her parents or legal guardians.

We conducted an online experiment (n = 6,232) testing nudge messages for charitable donation emphasizing society's expectation, inequity aversion, SC, or warm glow. Participants were randomly assigned to message conditions and indicated their willingness to donate. Details of the study 1 survey questionnaire and its order are provided in the Supplementary information. We then conducted and evaluated a field test using real online advertisements on Yahoo Ads. Detailed procedures and statistical analyses are provided in the Supplementary information.
