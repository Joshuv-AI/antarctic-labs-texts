Ureteral wall thickness as an imaging-based predictor of difficult ureter during ureteroscopic lithotripsy: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Ye Chen, Jun Zhang, Linbo Li, Yuanjing Shen, Xinwu Guo, Gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the value of ureteral wall thickness (UWT) obtained from non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) for predicting difficult ureter (DU) among patients receiving ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URS).MethodsPatients with unilateral ureteral stones managed by URS were retrospectively reviewed. According to intraoperative findings, they were classified into DU or non-DU group. UWT was measured at the level of the stone on preoperative NCCT. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff value of UWT for predicting DU.ResultsA total of 271 patients managed with URS were enrolled in the study, with 67 (24.72%) in the DU group and 204 (75.28%) in the non-DU group. The mean UWT in the DU group was 3.77 ± 1.24 mm, which was significantly higher than that in the non-DU group. Multivariate logistic regression revealed that UWT was an independent risk factor for DU, with an OR of 2.139 (95% CI: 1.634–2.79). The ROC curve demonstrated that a UWT cutoff of 2.9 mm provided the best threshold for predicting DU, with sensitivity and specificity levels of 77.6% and 71.6%, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.786. Moreover, a UWT greater than 2.9 mm was associated with abnormal endoscopic findings during surgery, prolonged operative time, and a reduced stone-free rate.ConclusionUWT is an independent risk factor for DU. Preoperative measurement of UWT holds significant value in predicting DU. These findings may assist in surgical planning before URS.Keywords: Ureteral calculus, Ureteral wall thickness, Difficult ureter, Ureteroscopic lithotripsy

Introduction

Ureteral calculi are a common urological condition that can cause pain and hematuria. Obstruction or infection may further exacerbate the disease and significantly impair patients’ quality of life [1]. Ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URS) is a minimally invasive treatment, and it is particularly beneficial for small distal stones and serves as a reliable alternative when extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) has failed [2]. However, urologists sometimes encounter difficult ureters (DU) [3–6]. Ureteral narrowing or tortuosity may prevent the ureteroscope from reaching the stone, thus making lithotripsy impossible. Although some of these cases can be managed with balloon dilation or by using a smaller-caliber ureteroscope, such approaches often prolong operative time, increase the risk of complications including hematuria, fever, acute kidney injury and complete ureteral avulsion [7]. Therefore, predicting difficult ureters based on patients’ preoperative characteristics is crucial for evaluating surgical risk and guiding optimal treatment strategies. Previous studies have identified several factors such as younger age, smaller stone size, presence of hydronephrosis, and narrower ureteral diameter directly increase the risk of DU [3–5]. However, these factors alone are insufficient for accurate preoperative prediction. Further studies are still needed to improve the preoperative prediction of DU.

Ureteral wall thickness (UWT) is considered an imaging surrogate for tissue edema and focal ischemia of the ureteral mucosa [8]. It can be obtained easily via non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT), which is an essential preoperative examination before URS. Recent research suggests that UWT serves as a dependable indicator for spontaneous stone passage (SPP), ESWL outcomes, and impacted stones [9–12]. However, research on the relationship between UWT and DU remains limited. This study examined the predictive performance of UWT for DU using preoperative clinical and imaging data to support surgical risk assessment and strategy planning.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

This study employed a retrospective design and was carried out at Nanyang Second General Hospital. A cohort of 312 patients managed with URS for unilateral ureteral calculi from January 2024 to May 2025 was retrospectively analyzed. Exclusion criteria included bilateral stones, ureteral tumors, congenital anomalies, previously diagnosed ureteral stricture, prior interventions such as double-J stenting, and lack of preoperative NCCT imaging. Following the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 19 patients were excluded due to incomplete NCCT images, and 12 patients were excluded because of severe infection requiring double-J stent placement. Clinical characteristics of the remained 271 patients were collected from medical records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, history of hypertension and diabetes, time from diagnosis confirmed by NCCT to surgery, history of ESWL, previous ureteral surgery, white blood cell count (WBC) and preoperative urinary tract infection.

Operative procedure and postoperative management

All URS procedures were performed by two experienced urologists. After induction of anesthesia, each patient was positioned in the dorsal lithotomy position, and standard sterile preparation and draping were carried out. An 8/9.8-Fr Wolf rigid ureteroscope (Richard Wolf, Knittlingen, Germany) was inserted into the bladder through the urethra to visualize the ureteral orifice. A hydrophilic guidewire was then advanced into the ureteral orifice, along which the ureteroscope was introduced until the stone site. Holmium laser lithotripsy was performed at an energy setting of 0.5–2.0 J and a frequency of 5 Hz, and stone fragments were removed via endoscopic grasping forceps. A double-J stent was subsequently placed.

Intraoperative ureteroscopic findings, including ureteral edema, polyps, and strictures, were recorded. Operative time was documented at the end of the procedure. Residual stones were assessed via plain abdominal radiography (KUB, kidney-ureter-bladder) at 2–4 weeks postoperatively. Stone-free status was defined as the presence of residual stone fragments < 4 mm on postoperative KUB imaging. For residual fragments, if spontaneous passage did not occur, secondary intervention was scheduled.

In accordance with previous studies [3, 13], we defined DU as a condition in which the ureteroscope cannot be advanced smoothly during surgery due to ureteral tightness, requiring one or more of the following interventions:

Using a smaller-caliber ureteroscope;

Using a smaller-caliber ureteroscope;

Performing ureteral dilation, including balloon dilation;

Performing ureteral dilation, including balloon dilation;

Placing a preoperative stent when entry cannot be achieved successfully.

Placing a preoperative stent when entry cannot be achieved successfully.

When DU was encountered, surgeons adopted different strategies based on experience, including ureteral balloon dilation or switching to a smaller 4.5/6.5-Fr ureteroscope. If the stone site could be reached after these measures, the procedure continued as planned; otherwise, a double-J stent was placed, and surgery was rescheduled 2–4 weeks later. The incidence of DU was 26.2% for Surgeon A and 23.0% for Surgeon B, with no significant difference between the two surgeons (P > 0.05).

Radiological evaluation

Preoperative NCCT was performed for all patients with 1.5 mm axial slices, and images were managed using the Vision Picture Archiving Communication System. The following describes the measurement methods of imaging parameters, as shown in Fig. 1.

Measurement of stone-related parameters on non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT). A Ureteral wall thickness (UWT) measured on axial NCCT image. B Ureter attenuation above the stone (UAAS) measured at the level of 1 cm above to the stone on the axial CT image. C Ureter diameter above the stone (UDAS) measured at the level of 1 cm above to the stone on the axial CT image. D Anterior–posterior diameter of renal pelvis (APDRP) measurement on the axial CT image

Stone size was defined as the largest diameter observed across axial, coronal, or sagittal planes.

Stone laterality was classified as right or left ureter.

Stone location was recorded as proximal, middle, or distal ureter based on anatomical landmarks.

Stone density was measured in Hounsfield units (HU) by placing a region of interest (ROI) over the stone on the axial image.

UWT was measured at the site of greatest soft-tissue thickness around the stone, reflecting the combined thickness of the ureteral wall and adjacent periureteral inflammatory changes [8].

Ureter diameter above the stone (UDAS) and Ureter diameter below the stone (UDBS) were measured 1 cm above and below the stone, respectively. At each location, a manually defined ROI covering more than two-thirds of the ureter was used, recorded as Ureter attenuation above the stone (UAAS) and Ureter attenuation below the stone (UABS) [14].

Anterior–posterior diameter of renal pelvis (APDRP) was defined as the maximal anterior–posterior dimension of the renal pelvis on axial NCCT images.

To evaluate the reproducibility of UWT measurements, imaging data from 30 randomly selected patients were remeasured. Observer 1, a urologist repeated the measurements of the same cases one month after the initial assessment to evaluate intra-observer reliability; observer 2, another independent urologist performed measurements on the same cases to assess inter-observer reliability. All measurements were performed in a blinded manner, using standardized imaging parameters and measurement criteria. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using SPSS version 27 (IBM, USA) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement definition. The interpretation of ICC values followed the criteria proposed by Koo and Li [15]. The intra-observer ICC for UWT was 0.969 (95% CI 0.931–0.985), and the inter-observer ICC was 0.943 (95% CI 0.884–0.972), indicating excellent reproducibility. Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables with approximately normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Student’s t-test. Variables with non-normal distribution are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (n, %) and compared using chi-squared analysis or Fisher’s exact test. Potential determinants of DU were examined through univariate analysis, followed by multivariate logistic regression to identify independent risk factors. The predictive ability of UWT was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM, USA). Two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics of the study population

A total of 271 patients managed with URS were included in the study and were divided into the DU group (n = 67) and the non-DU group (n = 204) based on intraoperative findings. Among the 67 patients in the DU group, 31 successfully underwent lithotripsy after intraoperative ureteral dilation or by switching to a smaller ureteroscope, while the remaining 36 patients required double-J stent insertion to complete the procedure.

In the DU group, the mean UWT was 3.77 ± 1.24 mm, markedly greater than that observed in the non-DU group (2.55 ± 1.14 mm, P < 0.001). Similarly, UABS values were elevated in patients with DU group (P < 0.001). Moreover, individuals in the DU group tended to be younger and had a lower prevalence of hypertension compared with those in non-DU group (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups regarding sex, BMI, waist circumference, stone features, APDRP, time since diagnosis confirmed by NCCT to surgery, history of ESWL, previous surgical interventions, or WBC (P > 0.05). Identification of independent predictors of DU via logistic regression

Predictive performance of UWT for difficult ureter using ROC analysis

The ROC curve for predicting DU shows that UWT has the largest area under the curve, with an AUC of 0.786 (95% CI: 0.726–0.846, P < 0.001). Based on this analysis, a threshold of 2.90 mm was determined as optimal, corresponding to 77.6% sensitivity and 71.6% specificity, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Comparison of intraoperative findings and surgical outcomes across different UWT levels

A total of 235 patients who achieved successful lithotripsy (31 DU and 204 non-DU) were divided into two subgroups according to the optimal UWT cutoff: low UWT (≤ 2.90 mm) and high UWT (> 2.90 mm). Abnormal endoscopic findings, such as ureteral edema, polyps, and strictures, were detected significantly more often in the high UWT group than in the low UWT group (40.51% vs. 17.31%, P < 0.001). The duration of surgery was prolonged in the high UWT group relative to the low UWT group (55.06 ± 30.38 vs. 43.96 ± 27.30 min, P < 0.01). Postoperative stone-free rate was significantly lower in the high UWT group (82.28% vs. 94.87%, P < 0.01). Detailed data are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that preoperative UWT, measured on NCCT, was significantly associated with DU during URS and serves as an independent predictor after adjustment for clinical and radiologic confounders. A UWT threshold of > 2.90 mm yielded high predictive value for DU. In addition, increased UWT was associated with abnormal endoscopic findings during surgery, prolonged operative time, and a lower stone-free rate, indicating its relevance to surgical complexity. These findings support the value of UWT as a practical and reproducible imaging biomarker that may strengthen preoperative risk assessment for ureteroscopy and optimize surgical planning.

Our work extends current knowledge on DU prediction. The assessment of DU relies on intraoperative judgment and is therefore inevitably influenced by surgeon-related factors, including experience, patience, and individual risk tolerance. In the present study, ureteral difficulty was defined by the requirement for any supplementary interventions, including ureteral dilation, use of a smaller-caliber ureteroscope, or temporary double-J stent placement when ureteroscope advancement was not feasible. This intervention-based definition provides a more standardized and clinically meaningful framework for DU assessment and helps to reduce operator-dependent variability. According to prior research, the reported incidence of DU ranges from 7.50% to 37.00% across different patient populations [3–6, 13, 16]. The observed incidence in our study cohort was 24.72%, consistent with previous studies. DU complicates ureteroscopy and increases the risk of complications, including ureteral wall injury, bleeding, and perforation [2, 17], and even severe adverse events such as sepsis or complete ureteral avulsion [18, 19]. Prior studies have explored radiological parameters such as ureteral diameter, the negative traceability of ureter and degree of hydronephrosis as potential predictors of DU [3, 5, 13]. However, some of these indicators are either qualitative, poorly standardized, or subject to substantial interobserver variability. Only limited research has specifically focused on ureteral wall morphology, despite its pathophysiological relevance. Yoshida et al. established the pioneering and widely recognized method for measuring UWT [8]. Although termed ureteral wall thickness, this measurement reflects maximal peri-stone soft-tissue thickness on CT and should be interpreted as a composite imaging marker rather than a histological wall measurement. Normal ureteral walls measure approximately 0.95–2 mm in thickness [9].

Stones can induce substantial pathological injury to the ureter, triggering an inflammatory response that leads to mucosal edema, muscular hypertrophy, and progressive fibrosis [20]. These inflammatory changes contribute to the increase in UWT. Previous studies have shown a close association between UWT and stone impaction [8], and it has been demonstrated to predict SSP, medical expulsive therapy (MET), and outcomes of ESWL [9, 11, 21]. Compared with conventional radiographic features, UWT provides a direct, quantitative assessment of ureteral wall status and demonstrated excellent reproducibility in our study.

The association between UWT and DU may be explained by several pathophysiological mechanisms. First, increased UWT often reflects ureteral strictures, polyps, inflammation, and edema, any of which can hinder the passage of the ureteroscope [22]. These changes are largely localized to the ureteral wall and may not be reflected by systemic inflammatory markers. In our cohort, preoperative WBC counts were comparable between the DU and non-DU groups, further supporting the notion that UWT primarily captures local ureteral pathology relevant to DU. Second, thickened ureteral walls are closely related to stone impaction, which cause severe hydronephrosis and ureteral bending, further complicating ureteroscopic access [23]. Finally, elevated UWT may reflect reduced ureteral compliance and alterations in the direction and frequency of ureteral peristalsis, both of which can impede successful ureteroscope insertion [24]. Collectively, UWT may serve as an imaging indicator of ureteral pathological changes, providing a more accurate representation of the underlying pathophysiology than traditional radiographic measures.

We also analyzed operative and postoperative outcomes to further explore the clinical relevance of UWT. Consistent with previous studies, patients with UWT > 2.9 mm more frequently exhibited intraoperative ureteral polyps, edema, and strictures [12]. Patients with increased UWT experienced longer procedures and lower stone-free rates, likely mediated by higher rates of DU rather than a direct effect of wall thickness itself. DU often complicates scope advancement, reduces working space, and may necessitate repeated attempts, all of which can impair lithotripsy efficiency and visualization. DU may also require higher irrigation pressure during surgery to maintain visibility, which could increase the risk of stone escape. Identification of high-risk patients may therefore enable surgeons to adopt alternative strategies, including preoperative stenting, early dilation, or selection of a smaller ureteroscope, potentially reducing intraoperative trauma and postoperative complications [25].

This study has several strengths. UWT measurement followed a standardized and previously validated methodological approach, and interobserver agreement exceeded conventional acceptability thresholds. The inclusion of real-world, unselected clinical data enhances the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, our multivariate model accounted for major clinical and imaging confounders, increasing confidence that UWT is an independent predictor of DU.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective single-center study may be subject to selection bias, and our conclusions require validation in external, multicenter cohorts. Second, although reproducibility was acceptable, UWT measurement still relies on manual assessment and may be influenced by CT slice thickness or equipment variation. Future studies should evaluate standardized measurement protocols across different imaging platforms and explore automated tools to reduce subjectivity. Third, information regarding preoperative alpha-blocker use was unavailable in this retrospective cohort and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Given the potential effect of alpha-blockers on ureteral smooth muscle tone, this factor may represent a source of unmeasured confounding and should be addressed in future prospective studies. Moreover, although preoperative WBC were included in the analysis, other inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein or cytokine-related indicators, were not routinely available and therefore could not be evaluated. Incorporating a broader panel of inflammatory parameters may further strengthen future DU prediction models. Prospective studies combining UWT with complementary imaging and clinical variables could facilitate the development of robust predictive nomograms or scoring systems.

In conclusion, UWT is a practical, quantitative, and highly reproducible imaging parameter that independently predicts DU in patients undergoing ureteroscopy. Incorporating UWT into preoperative evaluation may improve risk stratification, guide operative planning, and potentially reduce procedure-related morbidity. Future prospective and multicenter research is warranted to validate these findings and explore integrated prediction models for clinical application.

Conclusion

This research highlights UWT as an independent determinant of DU. UWT can be easily obtained via preoperative NCCT and may serve as a useful parameter for assessing surgical difficulty prior to URS. Patients with higher UWT are more likely to have intraoperative ureteral edema, strictures, or polyps, resulting in longer operative times and lower stone-free rates. Our findings provide a powerful predictive factor for preoperative identification of DU and optimization of surgical strategies.
