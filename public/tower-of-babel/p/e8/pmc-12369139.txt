Development and verification of a convolutional neural network-based model for automatic mandibular canal localization on multicenter CBCT images
Authors: Xiao Pan, Chengtao Wang, Xuhui Luo, Qi Dong, Haiyang Sun, Wentao Zhang, Hongyan Qu, Runzhi Deng, Zitong Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDevelopment and verification of a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based deep learning (DL) model for mandibular canal (MC) localization on multicenter cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images.MethodsIn this study, a total 1056 CBCT scans in multiple centers were collected. Of these, 836 CBCT scans of one manufacturer were used for development of CNN model (training set: validation set: internal testing set = 640:360:36) and an external testing dataset of 220 CBCT scans from other four manufacturers were tested. The convolution module was built using a stack of Conv + InstanceNorm + LeakyReLU. Average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) and symmetric mean curve distance (SMCD) were used for quantitative evaluation of this model for both internal testing data and partial external testing data. Visual scoring (1–5 points) were performed to evaluate the accuracy and generalizability of MC localization for all external testing data. The differences of ASSD, SMCD and visual scores among the four manufacturers were compared for external testing dataset. The time of manual and automatic MC localization were recorded.ResultsFor the internal testing dataset, the average ASSD and SMCD was 0.486 mm and 0.298 mm respectively. For the external testing dataset, 86.8% CBCT scans’ visual scores ≥ 4 points; the average ASSD and SMCD of 40 CBCT scans with visual scores ≥ 4 points were 0.438 mm and 0.185 mm respectively; there were significant differences among the four manufacturers for ASSD, SMCD and visual scores (p < 0.05). And the time for bilateral automatic MC localization was 8.52s (± 0.97s).ConclusionsIn this study, a CNN model was developed for automatic MC localization, and external testing of large sample on multicenter CBCT images showed its excellent clinical application potential.Keywords: Cone beam computed tomography, Mandibular canal, Convolutional neural network, Localization

Introduction

Dental implants have gained widespread use due to their high success rates. Accurate assessment of alveolar bone volume in the edentulous region is essential for formulating surgical strategies and selecting appropriate implants [1]. In mandibular premolar and molar implant surgery, injury to the inferior alveolar nerve is the most critical complication. In clinic practice, the incidence of dental surgeries resulting in inferior alveolar nerve injury ranges from approximately 0.4–8.4% [2, 3]. Of them, implant procedures account for approximately 3% of inferior alveolar nerve injuries. Permanent symptoms—including numbness, hypersensitivity, and neuropathic pain in the mandibular region—occur in up to 12% among these patients, adversely affect patient’s quality of life [4–9]. So, localization mandibular canal (MC) and evaluation the distance from the alveolar bone crest to MC is fundamental for implant planning.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), as a three-dimensional radiographic technique, has been widely used in pre-operative treatment planning. Compared to conventional two-dimensional imaging, CBCT facilitates preoperative planning and risk avoidance, e.g. assessing the optimal position and depth of mandibular implants to avoid injury to inferior alveolar nerve. Therefore, localization of MC on CBCT images is a critical preoperative procedure, to ensure safe implant positioning in posterior mandibular regions. However, accurate MC localization on CBCT images relies on image quality and the experience of clinician, which can be quite time-consuming in some cases [10, 11].

Artificial intelligence (AI), especially deep learning (DL), stands as a transformative auxiliary in the medical diagnosis, offering significant time-saving benefits [5, 12–18]. Integration of AI with CBCT facilitates superior, objective and reproducible radiological evaluations, thereby streamlining and speeding up dentists’ workflow [12]. Recently, several studies have explored the potential of convolutional neural networks (CNN) models in automatic segmentation of MC on CBCT images and shown promising results. However, to perform high-precision segmentation of MCs on CBCT images, numerous CBCT images need be manually segmented to train CNN models. And the manual three-dimensional segmentation of MC is quite labor-intensive, segmentation MC from the mandibular foramen to the mental foramen could spend nearly 2 h [11]. Therefore, previous studies have small sample sizes and lack external testing on CBCT images from various manufacturers [5, 19–21], or only segment MC in the third molar regions [11]. Moreover, according to Ni [11] et al.’s study, it takes approximately 25 s to accurately segment the MCs in the third molar regions using a CNN model, and segmentation of the whole MC would take more time. It’s known that accurate MC segmentation is crucial in certain clinical situations. For instance, when the MC is in close contact with the mandibular third molar, precise segmentation is essential. However, for implant planning, precise MC segmentation is not so necessary since the risk of nerve injury can be effectively minimized by ensuring a safe distance of at least 2 mm from the alveolar crest to the superior wall of MC. For many implant cases, the bone height from the alveolar crest to MC is not so borderline, so the clinicians just need a quick localization of MC rather than a slow voxel-wise segmentation. Thus, in this study, we developed a CNN model for MC localization on CBCT images, and internal and external testing from different manufacturers were performed to validate it future clinic applicable potential.

The innovation of this study is that: (1) By employing a stacked module structure of Conv + InstanceNorm + LeakyReLU, the network effectively handles various types of CBCT scans, providing stable feature extraction and interpretation even for low-quality scan images. Combining Dice Loss and Cross-Entropy Loss as the loss function enhances localization accuracy. (2) External testing was conducted using a large-scale cohort of CBCT scans from diverse manufacturers. Crucially, these scans were excluded from the AI model’s training dataset to assess generalization capability of the model. (3) The clinic diversity such as CBCT images with artifact or ambiguous MC of osteoporosis patients was fully tested. All of this provides a basis for its wide clinical application for implant planning in future.

Materials and methods

CNN model development

Data collection

A total of 836 CBCT scans from 836 patients were collected and archived at the Institute of Medical Imaging (Centre A) for CNN model development. All these CBCT scans were captured using Bondream 3D-1020 or Bondream 3D-1030 devices (Bondent, Shanghai, China). The inclusion criteria of CBCT images were: (1) the voxel sizes were 0.25 mm–0.2 mm; (2) absence of significant artifacts and noise interference; (3) bilateral MCs were completely presented on the images; (4) the patients’ age ranged from 10 to 80 years, the gender distribution was randomized, and the CBCT scans unrestricted in terms of pathology types. 836 CBCT scans were divided into training, validation, and internal testing dataset (training set: validation set: internal testing set = 640:360:36).

To protect patient privacy, we strictly adhere to data privacy and protection laws and regulations. All patient data underwent rigorous anonymization during collection, with all personally identifiable information irreversibly removed prior to analysis.

Ground truth labeling

The ground truth labels were meticulously annotated by two experienced radiologists using BonDream3D post-processing software. On each CBCT scan slice, the radiologists manually delineated the location of the mandibular nerve canal. To ensure consistency in annotations, cross-validation was performed by both radiologists to minimize inter-observer and intra-observer variability, and consensus was reached on ambiguous cases.

Network model structure

We employed a 3D U-Net-like network architecture. This network consists of a symmetric feature extractor and feature interpreter, as shown in Fig. 1.

Feature Extractor (Encoder)

Feature Extractor (Encoder)

The feature extractor was located on the left half of the network and comprises five layers. Each layer took the output of the previous layer as input, with the first layer directly receiving the segmented image blocks as input. Each layer contained two modules: a downsampling module and a convolution module. ①Downsampling Module: Reduced the size of the image feature maps via max pooling. ②Convolution Module: Extracted feature information from the feature maps. The convolution module was built from a stack of Conv + InstanceNorm + LeakyReLU. The feature extractor not only past feature information to the feature interpreter through the bottom layer but also transfered its output to the corresponding layer in the feature interpreter via skip connections.

2)Feature Interpreter (Decoder)

Feature Interpreter (Decoder)

The feature interpreter was located on the right half of the network and consists of five layers. Each layer included an upsampling module and a convolution module. ①Upsampling Module: Scaled up the feature maps using nearest-neighbor interpolation with a scaling factor of 2. ②Convolution Module: Similar to the convolution module in the feature extractor, it consisted of Conv + InstanceNorm + LeakyReLU. Each layer’s input included both the output feature map from the previous layer and the feature map transferred from the corresponding layer in the feature extractor via skip connections.

Data validation and internal testing

We employed five-fold cross-validation to validate the model. We randomly divided the 21,600 image blocks of size 80 × 96 × 128 into five equal parts, each containing 4,320 image blocks. During the model training process, four out of the five parts were used to train the model, while the remaining part was used to validate the model’s performance. This process was repeated five times, with a different part being used for validation each time. In this way, each part was used as the validation set once, ensuring comprehensive and unbiased evaluation of the model’s performance.

Finally, we validated the model using the testing set consisting of 36 CBCT scans. The testing data underwent the same preprocessing as the training data and were split into 27 image blocks of size 80 × 96 × 128. These image blocks were fed into the model to obtain the localization results for each block. The localization results of all image blocks were then stitched together to form a complete localized image, which was compared with the corresponding labels to calculate localization accuracy. The average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) and symmetric mean curve distance (SMCD) were used to evaluate the localization performance of the model.

External testing

Sample collection

For external testing, CBCT data were collected from January 2023 to June 2024 at 3 centers, namely Nanjing Stomatological Hospital (Centre B), the Third Outpatient Department of Nanjing Stomatological Hospital (Centre C) and Stomatology Department of Danyang Third People’s Hospital (Centre D), with a total of 220 scans (B: C: D = 128:53:39). The CBCT brands were KaVo 3D eXam (KaVo Sybron, Biberach, USA) and NewTom VGi (Qualitative Radiology SRL Co.,Ltd, Verona, Italy) at Centre B, Meyer CK (Meyer Optoelectronic Technology Inc., Hefei, China) at Centre C, and Vatech PHT-35LHS (Vatech, Seong-Nam, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) at Centre D (Fig. 2). All CBCT scans were exported in Digital Imaging Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. Prior to conducting this retrospective study, approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Stomatological Hospital, Medical School of Nanjing University [Ethics Approval: No. NJSH-2024NL-065]. To protect patient privacy, we strictly comply with data privacy and protection laws and regulations, and have undergone strict anonymization of all patient data during the collection process. Statistical analyses

SPSS23.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analysis. For the quantitative data, values were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Chi-square test, Kruskal Wallis test and one-way ANOVA test were used for analysis of the differences among the four manufacturers. The significance level of all statistical tests is set as α = 0.05, that is, when the p value is less than 0.05, the difference is considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have achieved significant success in the field of computer vision due to their capability for input-image feature extraction and prediction [13, 22, 23]. Recently, several studies have used CNNs for mandibular canal localization/segmentation on CBCT images. Jindanil [5] et al. collected 200 CBCT datasets from three different devices at the same center and developed a 3D-Unet CNN mode (training: validation: test = 160:20:20), the CNN model automatically segmented MC with an average dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.873, intersection over union (IoU) of 0.775. In Lim [20]’s study, a semi-supervised segmentation model was developed on 98 CBCT datasets from a single center, and the model was tested with 40 CBCT from two centers, resulting in DSC values being 0.55 ± 0.11 and 0.43 ± 0.13, respectively. Lahoud [21] et al. reported that 235 CBCT datasets (training: validation: test = 166:39:30) were utilized for segmentation model development, the outcomes demonstrated the mean IoU was 0.636 (± 0.081), and the mean DSC was 0.774 (± 0.062). In Jaskari [24]’s study, automatic MC localization by applying fully convolutional neural network segmentation on a dataset of 637 CBCT volumes, and a dataset of 15 volumes showed the MCD of 0.56 mm and ASSD of 0.45 mm. Although the previous works have shown promising results, these studies have small sample of datasets or did not perform external test of other manufacturers’ CBCT images.

Recently, Ni [11] et al. developed a CNN model based on a larger sample of CBCT scans (training: validation: test = 376: 80: 80) and conducted an external testing on 89 CBCT dataset from 3 centers. These 89 external datasets were obtained using other CBCT manufacturers which were completely untrained. The external sets demonstrated high accuracy in mandibular canal segmentation (DSC: 0.960; IoU: 0.924; ASSD: 0.040 mm). Since voxel-level manual annotation of the MC is fundamental for training but highly time-consuming (about 120 min), this study focused on partial segmentation—specifically in the third molar region.

In this study, for implantation planning, different from evaluation of relationship between third molar with MC, the criterion for implant placement is to leave a 2-millimeter safety margin above the canal when formulating the implant plan. And it is generally known MC to be a single structure with a diameter of 2–2.4 mm bilaterally [25]. To efficiently localize and annotate MC from the mandibular foramen to the mental foramen, the annotation was performed with a fixed 3 mm diameter round dot [19] (Fig. 3). Given that our model was a localization model, we did not used the DSC and IoU but used ASSD and SMCD as quantification metrics. In this study, the average ASSD and SMCD of 36 internal testing data was 0.486 mm and 0.298 mm, and the average ASSD and SMCD of 40 CBCT scans with visual scores ≥ 4 points were 0.438 mm and 0.185 mm respectively, which is not so satisfactory compared with previous segmentation studies. In clinic practice, most patients have enough alveolar volume in the implant region, for these patients, a 1 voxel (0.4 mm) error will not influence implant planning. For some patients who have critical bone volume, a more precise localization of MC is needed. However, the safety distance above the mandibular canal is 2 mm, and the smallest interval of different implant size is 1 mm, so generally ≤ 0.4 mm error also will not influence implant size selection [25]. For few patients whose alveolar bone volume is critical(the alveolar height from the alveolar bone crest to the superior wall of MC is around 6–8 mm), if automatic localization of MC with error ≥ 0.4 mm, manual adjustment is needed to ensure more appropriate implant selecting.

To assess real-world applicability, this study included a comprehensive external test across multi-center datasets. A total of 220 CBCT scans were collected from 3 centers of 4 different manufacturers, providing advantages in terms of sample size and manufacturer diversity. These CBCT images were entirely excluded from the CNN model’s training set. Moreover, the external test set exhibited a wider age distribution than the training cohort. These out-of-distribution tests demonstrated the model’s generalizability. In our study, 86.8% of the 220 CBCT scans achieved visual scores of 4 or 5 (according to standardized evaluation criteria), providing evidence of the model’s robustness and generalization capability. However, statistical tests found significant performance variations across device brands (p < 0.01), likely attributable to manufacturer-specific image processing algorithms.

Moreover, CBCT images presented with motion artifacts, metallic artifacts and difficult bone structure because of osteoporosis in elderly patients were tested in this study. The results demonstrated that >50% CBCT scans with motion artifacts or metallic artifacts had visual scores ≥ 4 points, however, only 15% CBCT scans with osteoporosis had visual scores ≥ 4 points (Table 6). Further training on challenging cases, particularly osteoporotic ones in future, might improve the model’s performance.

In clinic practice, MC localization is of great importance in mandibular implant planning. However, manual localization of MC is tedious and laborious. In our study, manual localization of bilateral MCs took 10.61 min(± 1.20 min), while the time of automatic localization of bilateral MCs was 8.52s (± 0.97s), our model achieves 74.7-fold acceleration. And it’s far faster than manual segmentation of MC (nearly 120 min) [11]. Moreover, localization speed can be further improved with medical imaging workstation optimization. The high efficiency enables our model to operate effectively in clinical workflows, thereby enhancing diagnosis and treatment efficiency.

In this study, the resolution is downscaling to 0.4 mm to enhance the efficiency of the model. Although a 0.3 mm resolution is theoretically superior, it offers accuracy enhancement of only 0.1 mm. It’s known that the smallest interval of different implant size is 1 mm, so 0.1 mm accuracy enhancement will not influence implant size selection. Moreover, given the volume of CBCT data is large (usually about 200-600 M), using 0.3 mm resolution instead of 0.4 mm would significantly compromise clinical efficiency. Additionally, we processed the localization results through skeletonization to extract the center points of the nerve canal, and then connected these points to generate a neural canal with a radius of 1.5 mm. This method effectively avoided discontinuities caused by the inability to recognize parts of the neural canal in some unclear slices, ensuring the continuity and completeness of localization results.

There are still some limitations of our AI model. Firstly, the localization performance for certain brands and for some challenging cases is suboptimal, indicating that further expanding training data diversity is still necessary. Secondly, in the future, domain adaptation AI models—leveraging minimal target data and self-supervised, label-efficient learning—will enable robust AI deployment [26–28]. Thirdly, uncertainty quantification techniques like Monte Carlo Dropout or confidence calibration can identify the neural tube while outputting confidence metrics and highlighting regions of high certainty/uncertainty are needed to be further developed [29–34]. And ethical and regulatory implications of deploying such tools in real-world clinics should be considered.

Conclusion

In summary, a novel, rapid and robust CNN model was developed for automatic localization of the MCs. We conducted a comprehensive external testing on a large sample size across multicenter CBCT images, demonstrating its excellent clinical application potential.
