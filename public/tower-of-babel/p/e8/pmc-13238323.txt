Investigation of Contrast Retention in PCOM Aneurysms as a Possible Marker for Instability and Rupture
Authors: Laurel M. M. Marsh, Fernando Mut, Alireza Chitsaz, Rainald Lohner, Anne M. Robertson, Naoki Kaneko, Juan R. Cebral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIdentification of cerebral aneurysms at risk of destabilization and rupture remains challenging. Although flow stagnation has been recognized as a risk factor, the underlying mechanisms and its predictive power are poorly understood. The purpose was to investigate possible associations between flow stagnation determined from computational fluid dynamics (CFD)‐based virtual angiograms and aneurysm instability and rupture. A total of 312 aneurysms at the posterior communicating (PCOM) artery were analyzed with image‐based CFD. Virtual angiograms were constructed by simulating contrast injections, and contrast retention metrics were calculated from time density curves of the aneurysm and their parent artery. These metrics were compared between 23 stable and 18 unstable aneurysms from a longitudinal dataset and between 129 unruptured and 142 ruptured aneurysms from a cross‐sectional dataset. Aneurysmal contrast retention was significantly larger (p < 0.05) in ruptured compared to unruptured aneurysms as well as when considering only small aneurysms (size < 7 mm). Similar associations were obtained when comparing stable and growing aneurysms. Multivariate analysis showed that aneurysm instability can be forecasted from flow stagnation with good accuracy (AUC = 0.88), while aneurysm rupture with only moderate accuracy (AUC = 0.72) or marginal accuracy for small aneurysms (AUC = 0.66). Mean residence time was identified as a good predictor of aneurysm instability and rupture. Flow stagnation, determined by contrast retention in angiographies (virtual or real), is associated with PCOM aneurysm instability (growth or symptoms development) and can be used to identify aneurysms prone to further growth and potentially rupture. However, aneurysms can also rupture without exhibiting signs of flow stagnation.

Introduction

The identification of intracranial aneurysms (IAs) at risk of destabilization and rupture remains challenging [1]. On the one hand, it is difficult to collect and analyze aneurysms longitudinally followed without treatment because many are prophylactically treated to avoid the devastating consequences of a possible rupture. On the other hand, competing multi‐factorial mechanisms seem to be acting simultaneously, and aneurysms may rupture for a variety of different reasons [2, 3, 4]. Previous clinical work has focused on developing scoring scales to evaluate risk of rupture or of growth, but these scales do not consider many aneurysm‐specific factors, only size, location, and presence of blebs [5, 6, 7], and as such are not very precise. Other approaches have focused on geometric and hemodynamic factors derived from image‐based computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models [8, 9, 10], but there is still no consensus about which parameters or flow conditions predispose aneurysms for destabilization and rupture.

Stagnation has been recognized as a potential risk factor for quite some time [11, 12]. Although areas of partial blood retention have been observed at locations with atherosclerotic changes on the aneurysm wall [13], the underlying mechanisms as well as the predictive power of flow stagnation are still not well known. Previous work has shown that synthetic or virtual angiograms based on image‐based CFD models of cerebral aneurysms can reproduce the salient features observed in digital subtraction angiography (DSA) sequences [14, 15], and in particular can detect flow stagnation as assessed by expert observers [16].

The objective of the current study was to investigate possible associations between flow stagnation metrics determined from patient‐specific CFD‐based virtual angiograms and aneurysm instability and rupture. For this purpose, the study compared stagnation parameters of ruptured and unruptured as well as stable and growing aneurysms at a single location, the posterior communicating (PCOM) artery, to control for location specific characteristics. This is an important cohort [17] because, among other reasons, PCOM aneurysms have a relatively large rupture risk, and patients can develop severe symptoms related to cerebral nerve III (CNIII) palsy which can be considered a sign of aneurysm instability.

Methods

Datasets

In this work, all PCOM aneurysms previously modeled with image‐based CFD were selected from our database [10, 18]. This resulted in two subsets: (a) cross‐sectional dataset with a total of 271 PCOM aneurysms including 129 unruptured (U) aneurysms and 142 ruptured (R) aneurysms, and (b) a longitudinal dataset with a total of 41 PCOM aneurysms, including 23 stable (S) aneurysms that showed no changes in serial imaging, and 13 aneurysms that exhibited enlargement in follow‐up images along with 5 aneurysms that developed CNIII palsy which were combined into a growing (G) aneurysm group with 18 aneurysms. These cross‐sectional and longitudinal datasets were used to investigate possible associations between aneurysm rupture and instability with intra‐aneurysmal flow stagnation quantified from virtual angiograms created from the CFD simulations as illustrated in Table 1 and detailed below.

Flow Models

Patient‐specific vascular models were previously constructed by segmentation of 3D rotational angiography (3DRA) images and corresponding CFD simulations were carried out by numerically solving the Navier–Stokes equations under pulsatile flow conditions [19, 20]. Inflow boundary conditions were derived from population averaged waveforms scaled with the patient‐specific inflow vessel area using an empirical relationship [21, 22]. Flow solutions were obtained with a finite element solver [23] on unstructured grids with a minimum resolution of 200 μm [24]. The simulation data consisted of flow velocity fields stored at the mesh points at 100 instants of time during the cardiac cycle. The process is illustrated in the top row of Figure 1.

Virtual Angiograms

Simulations of the transport of a contrast agent injected at the inlet (i.e., virtual angiogram) were carried out by numerically solving the advection equation using an explicit finite volume approach previously described [16]. The convective velocity field was linearly interpolated in time from the flow data stored at 100 instants during the cardiac cycle and it was assumed to be periodic from cycle to cycle. A stepwise contrast injection with a duration of 1 s was prescribed at the model inlet: C0(t)=1t≤1sec0t>1sec where C0 is the inlet contrast concentration (in arbitrary units). The transport equation was advanced in time until the maximum contrast concentration over the entire vascular model fell below a specified threshold. The threshold was prescribed at 0.1%, of the maximum concentration which typically required less than 5 s (i.e., 5 cardiac cycles) to wash out the entire vascular model. This threshold was determined arbitrarily and worked well in our analysis. For application to experimental/clinical data the value of this threshold should be further analyzed. The contrast concentration field was saved at 0.05 s intervals (equivalent to a 20 fps angiogram). Visualizations of the contrast field were created by rendering the mesh elements with transparency and gray values modulated by the contrast concentration. The virtual angiogram visualization is illustrated in the second row of Figure 1. In our numerical simulations, only convection was incorporated into the models. The typical diffusivity of contrast agents (iodine) is around 1.36 × 10−9 m2/s which for a 10 mm aneurysm with a mean flow velocity of approximately 3.0 cm/s gives a Peclet number of Pe ≈ 2.2 × 105 indicating a convection dominated process. It is possible that in some aneurysms with lower flow velocities this number can be smaller and diffusion becomes more relevant, an interesting topic to investigate in future studies.

Regional Time Density Curves

To quantify the contrast retention of the aneurysm with respect to the parent artery, time density curves were constructed by computing the average contrast concentration in corresponding regions. The aneurysm region was identified by computing the path distance between tetrahedral mesh elements and the aneurysm neck surface (i.e., oriented surface triangulation through the aneurysm orifice) [25]. The proximal parent artery region was automatically identified with the following procedure: (1) build the skeleton of the vascular model, (2) identify the path from the aneurysm neck to the model inlet (by finding the skeleton points closest to the neck centroid and the inflow boundary), (3) march 1 cm along this path from the aneurysm neck toward the inflow, (4) label mesh elements contained within a sphere with twice the vessel radius. Once the aneurysm (ane) and proximal parent artery (ppa) regions were identified and labeled, the corresponding time density curves were computed by averaging the contrast concentration over the region. The process is illustrated in the third row of Figure 1.

Aneurysm Stagnation Quantification

For each region (i=ane,ppa), the time of the maximum concentration (tmaxi) was found, and the arrival time (tarri) and washout time (twshi) were identified as the instants when the contrast crossed a threshold of 0.1% of the maximum concentration (see Figure 1 bottom panel). From these values the following variables were calculated: transit‐time: TTi=twshi−tarri, time‐to‐peak: TPi=tmaxi−tarri, time‐to‐washout: TWi=twshi−tmaxi, skewness: SKi=TPi/TWi. In addition, the mean‐residence‐time was computed as: MRTi=∫tC‾i(t)dt/∫C‾i(t)dt, with Ci‾ the spatial average over region i. The difference and ratio of each variable between the this threaneurysm and parent artery were respectively computed as: D(x)=xane−xppa, and R(x)=xane/xppa and used to quantify the contrast retention in the aneurysm with respect to the proximal parent artery, and provide quantitative assessments of the intuitive notion of aneurysm flow stagnation [16].

Data Analysis

The average contrast retention metrics were statistically compared between unruptured and ruptured aneurysms from the cross‐sectional dataset, as well as between stable and growing aneurysms from the longitudinal dataset, using the nonparametric Wilcoxon (Mann–Whitney) test. The unruptured versus ruptured comparisons were repeated for small aneurysms (i.e., maximum aneurysm size smaller than 7 mm). Differences with a p‐value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Univariate logistic regression (LR) models were fitted for each metric and the corresponding odds‐ratio (OR) and area under the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves (AUC) were calculated.

Associations between flow stagnation and aneurysm rupture and instability were further analyzed by first classifying aneurysms flows into two categories corresponding to stagnation and no‐stagnation. Aneurysms with D(TT)>0.72 or D(TW)>0.68 were classified as having stagnation. The values of these thresholds corresponded to the optimal values that best divided a previously studied dataset of 48 aneurysms (22 with stagnation, 26 without stagnation) where flow stagnation was manually determined by inspection of DSA sequences [16]. It was shown that the classifications based on these thresholds coincided with the manual classification with an accuracy above 90% (AUCs of 0.91 and 0.90, respectively). Once categorized, flow stagnation was compared with aneurysm rupture and instability using 2 × 2 contingency table analysis and the Fisher exact test.

Finally, multivariate logistic regression models were fitted to the datasets. All contrast retention metrics were included in the initial models and stepwise variable selection based on the Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) was carried out and the AUC and the cross‐validation error based on the leave‐one‐out method were calculated for the final models. All statistics were performed with scripts written in R.

Results

Associations Between Contrast Retention and IA Rupture and Instability

Comparisons of the difference (D) and ratio (R) of contrast retention metrics (TT,TP,TW,SK,MRT) between the aneurysm and parent artery regions for ruptured and unruptured aneurysms (all and smaller than 7 mm) as well as stable and growing aneurysms are presented in Table 1. Values in each group are listed as mean ± standard deviation. The p‐values correspond to the Mann–Whitney test, and the odds ratios with their 95% confidence intervals and the AUCs were obtained from univariate logistic regressions. Statistically significant differences (95% confidence) are indicated with a “*.”

Several metrics indicative of longer retention in the aneurysm compared to the parent artery (e.g., D(TT), R(TT),D(TW), R(TW),R(MRT)) were significantly larger in ruptured compared to unruptured aneurysms from the cross‐sectional dataset, however their discriminatory power as quantified by the AUC was only moderate with the largest values between 0.61 and 0.64. Similar results, with slightly larger p‐values, were obtained when restricting the analysis to aneurysms smaller than 7 mm. On the other hand, stronger associations with larger odds ratios and good discriminatory power (max AUC between 0.70 and 0.77) were obtained when comparing stable and growing aneurysms from the longitudinal dataset.

Associations Between Flow Stagnation and IA Rupture and Instability

After classifying aneurysms into flow stagnation categories (stagnation = Yes, stagnation = No) by thresholding the aneurysm‐artery difference in transit‐time (TT) and washout‐time (TW), which were shown to reproduce manual categorization from DSAs with over 90% accuracy, the number of ruptured/unruptured and stable/growing aneurysms in each category were counted and compared. The results (Table 2) show that flow stagnation is more prevalent in ruptured and growing aneurysms compared to unruptured or stable aneurysms, respectively. However, due to the smaller sample size of the longitudinal dataset, the association between flow stagnation and aneurysm instability only reached marginal significance (94% confidence).

Predictive Models of IA Rupture and Instability Based on Contrast Retention Metrics

The performance of multivariate logistic regression models of aneurysm rupture (all and smaller than 7 mm) and instability following the stepwise variable selection based on the AIC are presented in Table 3 and Figure 2. These results indicate that aneurysm instability can be forecasted from aneurysm flow stagnation with approximately 88% accuracy, while aneurysm rupture with approximately 72% accuracy, and this latter performance was reduced to about 66% for aneurysms smaller than 7 mm. The aneurysm‐artery difference and ratio of the mean‐residence‐time (MRT) was retained in all models, indicating that it is a good predictor of aneurysm instability and rupture.

Stagnation and IA Geometry

In order to investigate what aneurysm geometries favor flow stagnation, correlations between each of the contrast retention metrics and several aneurysm geometric parameters [10, 25, 26] were investigated using all the data from the cross‐sectional dataset. For this purpose, the Spearman correlation coefficient, indicative of a monotonic association between variable ranks, were computed for each variable pair. The results presented in Figure 3 show that aneurysm aspect ratio (AR), volume‐to‐ostium ratio (VOR) and bottleneck factor (BF) had the strongest associations with contrast retention variables (correlations ≿0.70). Additionally, aneurysm depth and size ratio also had some influence on contrast retention but with smaller correlation coefficients. Additionally, the largest linear regression correlation coefficients (R2) were between 0.5 and 0.6 and corresponded to AR and BF (see Figure 3 right panels), while aneurysm size metrics (Asize, SizeR) had poorer linear correlations with R2<0.3.

Discussion

The results of the current study suggest that PCOM aneurysm growth and/or development of symptoms related to cranial nerve palsy are associated with or favored by hemodynamic environments characterized by slow and stagnant flows. Furthermore, such flow environments can be recognized by the retention of contrast in angiographic images, either from real X‐ray DSA sequences or CFD‐based virtual angiograms. Thus, contrast retention can be considered a marker for aneurysm instability (growth and/or symptoms development), which is a recognized risk factor for rupture.

An example of a stable aneurysm with no flow stagnation (contrast retention) is presented in Figure 4. This aneurysm had a strong inflow stream that impacts the aneurysm dome, creating a region of elevated wall shear stress with a divergence critical point (node source), and a vortex that produces a swirling critical point (focus) on the aneurysm body. The corresponding virtual angiogram and time‐density curves show that the aneurysm fills slightly after the proximal parent artery region and washes out in roughly the same amount of time, thus not exhibiting any significant contrast retention.

In contrast, an example of an unstable (growing) aneurysm with significant contrast retention is presented in Figure 5. In this case, the inflow stream impacts the distal part of the neck and enters the aneurysm with much slower velocity, creating a vortex structure and a low velocity and low WSS zone toward the dome. A node critical point can be observed in the outflow zone near the neck. Contrast retention can be clearly observed especially toward the distal part of the dome well after the proximal parent artery is washed out. It is interesting to note that this aneurysm exhibited contrast retention even though it was quite small (3 mm).

Our study also found associations between contrast retention and aneurysm rupture. However, it further showed that rupture can also occur under high flow conditions without flow stagnation and contrast retention, especially if considering only smaller aneurysms.

Examples of ruptured aneurysms with and without contrast retention are presented in Figures 6 and 7, respectively. In both cases, there was a strong inflow jet that impacted the wall, creating a region of elevated WSS and vortices ending at the wall with swirling critical points. In the first case, there was substantial contrast retention, especially at the wall opposite to the flow impingement, which was subjected to low WSS. In contrast, in the second case, the flow stream was strong throughout the aneurysm and able to quickly wash out the contrast without significant retention. These findings and observations provide support to the idea of at least two underlying mechanisms responsible for aneurysm wall degeneration, weakening, and subsequent rupture. One related to high flow effects and another related to low and stagnant flow effects. Furthermore, these two kinds of local hemodynamic conditions can co‐exist simultaneously in a single aneurysm, resulting in a heterogeneous wall and arguably a more vulnerable wall structure [27].

Although flow stagnation was weakly related to size, it was found to be more dependent on aneurysm shape, in particular to geometric parameters related to its elongation (AR) or inflation (BF or VOR) with respect to its neck. In other words, larger/wider aneurysms with smaller necks are likely to produce stagnant flows that favor further growth (and perhaps also wall thickening [4, 27]) and future rupture. Although, in general, stable aneurysms had less flow stagnation, the absence of contrast retention should not be considered an indication of low rupture risk since many ruptured aneurysms did not exhibit significant contrast retention. In the absence of contrast retention, other flow conditions and rupture risk factors (e.g., strong impingements, vortex flows, complex and unstable flow patterns, etc.) should be further analyzed to assess the likelihood of aneurysm rupture without stagnation.

Low flow conditions have been associated with increased endothelium permeability and dysfunction as well as local thickening of the wall and inflammation [28, 29]. Presumably, this combination of effects on the aneurysm wall favors further wall weakening and remodeling, and subsequent aneurysm growth.

The current study has several limitations. The sample size, especially the number of longitudinally followed aneurysms (stable or unstable), was small. Whether unruptured aneurysms in the cross‐sectional database will remain stable or rupture was unknown. While restricting the analysis to a single location ensured that the differences observed between stable and unstable or between ruptured and unruptured aneurysms were due to their distinct hemodynamic environments, the results cannot be generalized to other locations without further study. Additionally, the CFD models have several previously discussed limitations [30]. In particular, inflow boundary conditions were derived from population‐averaged waveforms personalized by scaling with the inlet area instead of directly from patient‐specific flow measurements. However, previous work showed that this approach resulted in virtual angiograms that reproduced the salient features of actual DSA sequences and were able to reliably identify flow stagnation [16]. Although flow stagnation can be identified by analyzing contrast retention directly in DSA sequences (without CFD analysis), this approach also has limitations. For instance, angiographic sequences are acquired in 2D projections, which can be affected by the aneurysm overlapping the parent artery or distal vessels. DSA sequences are typically acquired at low and nonuniform framerates (e.g., 3 frames per second for the first 3 s, and 1 frame per second for the following 2 s in a 5 s acquisition). This makes the quantification of the filling and washout of the aneurysm and its contrast retention more challenging. In contrast, despite the limitations mentioned above, CFD‐based virtual angiograms allow extraction of time‐density curves more precisely and objectively while specifying arbitrary frame rates, similar injections for all cases, and total duration of the contrast simulation.

Conclusions

Aneurysm flow stagnation, which can be identified by contrast retention during angiography (virtual or real), is associated with PCOM aneurysm instability (growth or symptoms development) and can be used to identify aneurysms prone to further growth and potentially rupture. However, not all aneurysms that rupture exhibit flow stagnation. In the absence of contrast retention (flow stasis), other concurrent factors should be further analyzed to assess the likelihood of aneurysm rupture or stabilization.
