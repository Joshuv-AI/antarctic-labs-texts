Global, regional, and temporal trends in aortic aneurysm burden, 1990–2021: insights from the Global Burden of Disease study
Authors: Zhuohang Jiang, Ziyun Fu, Jinxing Peng, Jiayu Song, Jie Liu, Tucheng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic aneurysm (AA) is a serious cardiovascular condition with high mortality. This study evaluated the global burden, temporal trends, projections, and regional disparities of AA, focusing on variations across Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) regions.MethodsBased on publicly available Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 estimates, we analyzed the global and regional burden of AA from 1990 to 2021. Pearson’s correlation assessed the association between age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) and age-standardized disability-adjusted life years (ASDR) for AA and 2021 SDI values. A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model projected deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) to 2030. An age-period-cohort (APC) model assessed effects across SDI regions. A preliminary evaluation of risk factors was also conducted.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, global absolute AA-related deaths and DALYs increased by 74.1% and 64.9%, respectively, with further increases projected by 2030. In contrast, ASMR and ASDR declined and are projected to continue decreasing. At the global level, ASMR and ASDR demonstrated a modest but significant positive correlation with SDI values (P<0.001). Stratified analyses revealed distinct regional patterns. Notably, period and cohort effects in high and high-middle SDI regions were opposite to those in middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions.ConclusionsTailored intervention strategies are essential to address the heterogeneous burden of AA across SDI regions. While sustained attention is needed in high and high-middle SDI regions, greater vigilance is required in low-middle SDI regions, where the potential burden remains substantial.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Global absolute deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from aortic aneurysm (AA) increased by 74.1% and 64.9%, respectively, from 1990 to 2021, with further increases projected by 2030.

• Age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rates (ASDR) declined globally during this period and are expected to continue decreasing.

• A significant positive correlation was found between Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) and both ASMR and ASDR (P<0.001).

What is known and what is new?

• AA remains a significant cardiovascular health challenge with increasing absolute burden due to population aging. Previous studies have documented declining age-standardized rates (ASRs) but persistent regional disparities associated with SDI.

• This study reveals heterogeneous trends across five SDI quintiles and opposing period/cohort effects; and highlights emerging concerning trends in low-middle SDI regions and provides detailed risk factor attribution by age and gender.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Population aging will continue to drive increased absolute AA burden, particularly in low-middle SDI regions. Healthcare access, screening availability, and risk factor management are critical determinants of regional disparities.

• High-SDI regions: maintain existing screening programs and strengthen smoking cessation efforts, particularly targeting middle-aged males and rising female risk.

• Low-middle and low-SDI regions: urgently improve diagnostic and treatment capacity, implement cost-effective screening strategies, and enhance control of hypertension and smoking.

• Global health policies: develop differentiated public health strategies based on SDI levels, prioritize risk factor management, and promote research on AA subtypes and subnational disparities.

Introduction

Aortic aneurysm (AA) is defined as a localized dilation of the aorta exceeding 50% of normal diameter and represents a serious cardiovascular condition characterized by structural compromise of the aortic wall (1). Despite advances in clinical management, AA continues to be associated with alarmingly high mortality, ranging from 48% to 56% (2). The global annual mortality burden of AA is comparable to that of several major malignancies, highlighting its significant public health impact (3). Although surgical repair improves short-term survival, long-term outcomes remain controversial (4,5), and currently no surgical or pharmacological therapies can effectively halt disease progression (6). Therefore, a thorough understanding of AA epidemiology and the implementation of robust prevention strategies are urgently needed.

In 2019, more than 170,000 deaths worldwide were attributed to AA, representing an 82.1% increase since 1990 (7). With global population aging and the rising prevalence of behavioral and metabolic risk factors (8), the burden of AA is expected to continue escalating. Characterizing the epidemiology of AA may be essential for guiding effective interventions and mitigating its growing impact on global health.

Comprehensive assessments of disease burden, accounting for variations by cause, demographics, region, and time, are fundamental to contemporary public health research. Over the past three decades, the availability and quality of global health data have markedly improved (9). The Global Burden of Disease (GBD), Injury, and Risk Factor Study is widely recognized as the most authoritative source of such estimates (10,11). Previous analyses based on GBD 2019 (7,12) and GBD 2021 (13-15) consistently reported increasing absolute numbers of AA-related deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) since 1990, despite declining age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rates (ASDR). Interestingly, among individuals aged 15–39 years, Wang et al. (16) documented an opposite pattern, with increasing ASMR and ASDR. This earlier work has offered only limited comparisons across Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) regions and included few projections into the future. The underlying reasons for these divergent trends remain poorly understood. Furthermore, prior studies have seldom conducted detailed comparative analyses across SDI regions.

In this study, we sought to provide a comprehensive assessment of global and regional epidemiological transitions of AA from 1990 to 2021 using GBD 2021 data. Specifically, we examined temporal changes in deaths and DALYs, explored associations with SDI and demographic factors, analyzed attributable risk factors, and forecasted future trends up to 2030. Our findings aim to inform global strategies for the prevention and control of AA. We present this article in accordance with the GATHER reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2069/rc).

Methods

Data sources

The GBD 2021 project estimated the disease burden associated with 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 (11). In this study, based on publicly available GBD 2021 estimates, we estimated deaths and DALYs attributable to AA, and examined related risk factors, including smoking, high systolic blood pressure, high body mass index (BMI), low fruit intake, low vegetable intake, high sodium intake, and lead exposure. Our analysis focused on the occurrence of AA among individuals aged 15 to 95 years, classified into distinct categories: young, middle-age, elderly, super-elderly, and working age (the latter encompassing both young and middle-aged groups). Detailed methodologies for estimating the disease burden of AA are available in the accompanying GBD 2021 publications (10,11). All estimates and methods used in this study are publicly accessible through the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation website, including the GBD Compare tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-compare/) and the GBD Results Tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Disease definition

AA was defined as a localized dilation of the aorta, exceeding at least 50% of its normal diameter, representing a major cardiovascular event characterized by compromised structural integrity of the aortic wall (1). In GBD 2021, causes of diseases were categorized into four hierarchical levels, with AA categorized as a level 3 cause under cardiovascular diseases. AA was identified in hospital records using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes 441-441.9 or ICD-10 codes I71.1-I71.9. Countries and territories were grouped into the five SDI quintiles, including high, high-middle, middle, low-middle and low SDI regions, based on the GBD 2021 SDI values (12).

Glossary of terms

DALYs provide a comprehensive measure of total health loss, calculated as sum of years of live lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs). YLLs for fatal causes were estimated by subtracting the observed age at death from the reference standard life expectancy. YLDs were calculated by multiplying prevalence by disability weights assigned to each severity level. However, in GBD 2021, DALYs are equivalent to YLLs, likely due to unavailable prevalence data. The reference standard life expectancy was derived from the GBD standard life table.

ASMRs and ASDRs were adopted to allow comparisons between populations with different age structures or within the same population over time. Both metrics were reported per 100,000 population and were pre-calculated and made available through GBD 2021 online resources.

Age-standardized rates (ASRs) were quantified using a regression model applied to the natural logarithm of the ASR (y = α + βχ + ε, where y = ln (ASR) and χ = calendar year). Estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was calculated as 100 × (eβ − 1), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived from the regression model (17). An increasing trend was defined when both the EAPC estimate and the lower CI limit were positive. A decreasing trend was defined when both the estimate and the upper CI limit were negative. If the CIs overlapped zero, the ASR was considered stable.

The age-period-cohort (APC) model is an advanced research methodology that surpasses traditional analyses in health and socio-economic studies. It enables the estimation of net drift and local drift, reflecting overall and specific time trends, respectively, while assessing the independent effects of three fundamental temporal dimensions: age, period, and birth cohort (13-15). In this study, the APC model was applied to examine trends in deaths attributed to AA across different age groups, time periods, and birth cohorts. The APC analysis provides several key insights. First, it helps elucidate trends in the number of deaths and identifies shifts in disease patterns across age groups, periods, or cohorts. Second, by disentangling age, period, and cohort effects, the model allows hypotheses regarding how changes in lifestyle, medical advancements, or environmental conditions may influence mortality. Third, it offers information on epidemiological transitions of AA and examines how socioeconomic factors impact AA mortality across different cohorts and time periods. Overall, the APC model deepens understanding of AA epidemiology and aids in identifying gaps in disease prevention, control, and treatment strategies. In APC analyses, age intervals are typically aligned with period intervals. Accordingly, this study used 5-year age groups paired with 5-year periods, incorporating data from the GBD 2021 database on AA deaths over the previous 30 years (1992–2021) along with corresponding population data by location. The age range of 15 to 95 years was divided into sixteen groups for analysis: 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 74–79, 80–84, 85–89, and 90–95 years. Subsequently, the study period from 1992 to 2021 was divided into six 5-year intervals: 1992–1996, 1997–2001, 2002–2006, 2007–2011, 2012–2016, and 2017–2021. This framework allowed for the analysis of 12 partially overlapping 10-year birth cohorts, ranging from 1942–1951 to 1997–2006. The APC model estimated both the overall temporal trend and age-specific trends in deaths. The overall trend is expressed as the annual percentage change in deaths, representing the net drift (% per year), influenced by calendar time and continuous birth cohorts. Age-specific trends are expressed as local drift (% per year), reflecting the annual percentage change in deaths by age. Even small variations in drift values can produce substantial changes in model fitting over a 30-year period. The significance of annual percentage change trends was assessed using the Wald χ2 test. Within the APC model, the age effect is represented by age-specific rates across birth cohorts, while period and cohort effects are expressed as the relative risk of prevalence, calculated by comparing age-specific rates for each period or cohort to a reference period or cohort. The choice of reference period or cohort is arbitrary and does not affect the result interpretation.

The GBD study provides the SDI, a composite measure of regional development status. SDI is calculated as the weighted geometric mean of three rescaled components: per capita income, average educational attainment among those aged 15 years or older, and total fertility rates among individuals under 25 years of age. SDI scores range from 0 to 1, with 0 representing the theoretical minimum level of development (lowest income, fewest years of schooling, highest fertility) and 1 representing the theoretical maximum level of development (highest income, most years of schooling, lowest fertility) (10,11).

Statistical analysis

Projection analyses were performed using Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) models, with a particular focus on forecasting. BAPC models utilize integrated nested Laplace approximations (INLA) to enable comprehensive Bayesian inference and can generate age-standardized projected rates. When the predictive distribution is of interest, Poisson noise is inherently incorporated (18). Using this approach, the anticipated disease burden for the period 2022–2030 was estimated.

Correlations between SDI and regions or countries were assessed using Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted in R Studio (version 4.4.1) using the BAPC package (version 0.0.36), INLA (version 23.09.09), and ggplot2 (version 3.5.1). A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Global disease burden of AA

From 1990–2001, the ASDR for AA showed a consistent decline, with an EAPC of −1.28 (95% CI: −1.38 to −1.18). A similar trend was observed for the ASMR, with an EAPC of −1.23 (95% CI: −1.33 to −1.13). Specifically, the ASMR decreased from 2.5 per 100,000 population [95% uncertainty interval (UI): 2.3–2.7] in 1990 to 1.9 per 100,000 (95% UI: 1.7–2.0) in 2021, reflecting a 26.7% reduction. Likewise, the ASDR declined from 48.8 per 100,000 (95% UI: 46.0–51.8) in 1990 to 36.5 per 100,000 (95% UI: 33.5–39.5) in 2021, corresponding to a 25.1% decrease (Tables 1,2).

Similarly, DALYs increased from 1.88 million (95% CI: 1.78–2.01) in 1990 to 3.02 million (95% CI: 2.86–3.35), indicating a rise of 69.4%.

BAPC modeling further revealed that both deaths and DALYs were consistently higher in males compared with females, in agreement with the ASMR and ASDR patterns. Projections suggest that by 2030, the absolute numbers of deaths and DALYs will continue to increase in both sexes, whereas ASMR and ASDR are expected to remain stable or decline slightly (Figure 1A-1D). Specifically, by 2030, deaths from AA are projected to increase by 31.49% in men, compared with 27.97% in women. DALYs are expected to rise by 30.57% in men and 26.28% in women. ASMR is projected to increase by 13.62% in men versus 10.36% in women, while ASDR is expected to rise by 17.78% in men compared with 11.90% in women. These findings highlight the role of demographic dynamics in shaping the global AA burden.

Disease burden of AA across five SDI regions

We evaluated trends in AA-related deaths and DALYs across five SDI regions from 1990 to 2021. In both 1990 and 2021, the absolute number of deaths (53,900 in 1990 and 67,200 in 2021) and DALYs (1.03 million in 1990 and 1.12 million in 2021) were highest in high-SDI regions, substantially exceeding those in other regions and the global average. Consistently, the ASMR (4.8 per 100,000 in 1990; 2.9 per 100,000 in 2021) and ASDR (92.1 per 100,000 in 1990; 54.5 per 100,000 in 2021) were also greatest in high SDI regions. Importantly, the largest relative declines were likewise observed in these regions, with EAPCs of −1.98 (95% CI: −2.11 to −1.85) for ASMR and −2.04 (95% CI: −2.17 to −1.91) for ASDR (Tables 1,2). Collectively, these findings suggest that although high-SDI regions historically carried the greatest burden of AA, they have also experienced the most substantial improvements over the past three decades. This indicates a significant relationship between AA burden and SDI level.

Following this, we analyzed ASMR and ASDR across the five SDI regions over the 30-year period (Figure 1E,1F). From 1990 to 2021, both indicators declined markedly in high- and high-middle-SDI regions, consistent with the global trend. By contrast, in the middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions, both ASMR and ASDR either increased slightly or remained relatively stable (Tables 1,2). Notably, the steepest reduction occurred in high SDI regions, with EAPCs of −1.98 (95% CI: −2.11 to −1.85) for ASMR and −2.04 (95% CI: −2.17 to −1.91) for ASDR. In contrast, the steepest increases were observed in low-middle SDI regions, with EAPCs of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.21–1.33) for ASMR and 1.20 (95% CI: 1.15–1.25) for ASDR. Notably, declines in ASMR and ASDR were more pronounced before 2014 across both global and regional levels, after which the trends appeared to plateau (Figure 1E,1F). These findings suggest that, beyond demographic influences, temporal effects may also play a role in shaping the global burden of AA.

Relationship between SDI and the disease burden of AA

Analysis of data from 1990 to 2021 revealed marked shifts in the regions with the highest AA-related deaths and DALYs (Tables 1,2). In 1990, Western Europe and High-income North America accounted for the greatest burden. By 2021, however, the highest numbers were observed in Western Europe and the high-income Asia Pacific region. Notably, high-income Asia Pacific experienced a dramatic increase of 386.8% in deaths and 261% in DALYs over three decades, whereas Western Europe and high-income North America showed substantial declines. In 2021, other regions reporting more than 10,000 deaths and 200,000 DALYs included high-income North America, Tropical Latin America, Eastern Europe, South Asia, and East Asia. Notably, many of these regions exhibited rising ASMR and ASDR trends, as indicated by EAPC estimates from 1990 to 2021. Among all 21 GBD regions, Australasia demonstrated the most rapid decline, with an ASMR EAPC of −4.11 (95% CI: −4.27 to −3.96) and an ASDR EAPC of −4.28 (95% CI: −4.44 to −4.13).

To further examine the association between socio-demographic status and AA burden, we assessed correlations between SDI and both ASMR and ASDR for AA in 2021. Across the 21 GBD regions, we observed moderate positive correlations (ASMR: R=0.47, P<0.001; ASDR: R=0.44, P<0.001) (Figure 3A,3B). Extending the analysis to 204 countries and territories, the correlations were weaker but remained statistically significant (ASMR: R=0.32, P<0.001; ASDR: R=0.28, P<0.001) (Figure 3C,3D). These results indicate that SDI is moderately associated with AA burden, with higher SDI values corresponding to higher ASRs.

Disease burden of AA in 204 countries and territories

In 2021, AA-related deaths and DALYs varied substantially across countries and territories (Figure 4A-4E, Figure S1). Japan reported the highest absolute numbers, with 23,815 deaths and 344,492 DALYs. Other countries or territories exceeding 5,000 deaths or over 200,000 DALYs included the United Kingdom, China, Brazil, the Russian Federation, the United States, and India (Figure S1). EAPC analyses from 1990 to 2021 revealed divergent trends. According to these findings, the fastest declines were observed in Australia (ASMR EAPC: −4.27; 95% CI: −4.43 to −4.10; ASDR EAPC: −4.43; 95% CI: −4.59 to −4.26), while the steepest increases occurred in Georgia (ASMR EAPC: 7.38; 95% CI: 6.07–8.70; ASDR EAPC: 7.16; 95% CI: 5.86–8.47). Many countries or territories in Asia, Africa, and South America exhibited increasing ASMR and ASDR (Figure 4C,4D), and several of these nations also had relatively higher SDI values (Figure 4E). By contrast, most countries or territories in North America, Europe, and Oceania, despite also having high-SDI values, demonstrated pronounced declines (Figure 4C,4D). For example, the United Kingdom (ASMR EAPC: −3.41; 95% CI: −3.78 to −3.05; ASDR EAPC: −3.77; 95% CI: −4.12 to −3.42), the United States (ASMR EAPC: −3.61; 95% CI: −3.86 to −3.36; ASDR EAPC: −3.25; 95% CI: −3.50 to −3.00), and Australia (ASMR EAPC: −4.27; 95% CI: −4.43 to −4.10; ASDR EAPC: −4.43; 95% CI: −4.59 to −4.26) all showed significant reductions in disease burden. Conversely, some high- and high-middle SDI countries or territories in Asia reported rising trends. Armenia demonstrated increasing ASMR (EAPC: 2.57; 95% CI: 2.21–2.94) and ASDR (EAPC: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.77–2.52), while Saudi Arabia exhibited even steeper increases in both ASMR (EAPC: 3.95; 95% CI: 3.54–4.36) and ASDR (EAPC: 4.22; 95% CI: 3.78–4.67). EAPC increased primarily in Asia and the Middle East, while Europe and Oceania experienced steep declines. A complete list of country-specific EAPC estimates is provided in Table S1.

Temporal trends in AA deaths across different age groups

To assess global temporal patterns in AA mortality, we applied the APC model using data from 1992 to 2021. Figure 5A illustrates the annual percentage change in deaths across age groups, ranging from 15–20 years to ≥95 years. Analysis of local drift (reflecting age-specific cohort effects) showed a global decline in AA deaths among middle-aged and older adults, whereas trends were less favorable in 15–39-year age groups and those older than 90 years. The downward trajectories observed in high- and high-middle SDI regions were consistent with global patterns, although the magnitude varied. In global, high-, and high-middle-SDI regions, females experienced a slightly steeper decline before age 55 years, while males demonstrated more rapid reductions after age 55 years. The most pronounced decrease occurred around age 75 years. In contrast, middle-, low-middle-, and low-SDI regions did not exhibit obvious downward patterns.

Net drift (reflecting overall cohort effects across all ages) further highlighted these disparities, with a 2.86% annual divergence in trends between high-SDI and middle- to low-SDI regions. Globally, AA deaths decreased by −0.97% per year (95% CI: −0.15 to −0.89). The steepest decline was seen in high-SDI regions (−1.77% per year; 95% CI: −2.04 to −1.51), followed by high-middle-SDI regions (−0.71% per year; 95% CI: −0.8 to −0.61) (Figure S2). By contrast, deaths modestly increased in middle SDI regions (0.20% per year; 95% CI: 0.10–0.30) and rose more substantially in low-middle-SDI regions (1.09% per year; 95% CI: 0.95–1.23), with males exhibiting a steeper upward trend. In low-SDI regions, overall mortality remained relatively stable (0.15% per year; 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.36), though sex-specific patterns diverged: deaths increased among males (0.68% per year; 95% CI: 0.4–0.96) but declined among females (−0.54% per year; 95% CI: −0.85 to −0.23). High-SDI regions show consistent declines, whereas middle- and low-SDI regions experience stagnation or increases, particularly among men. Age, period, and cohort effects on AA deaths across five SDI regions

Figure 5B-5D present the APC estimates across the five SDI regions. Longitudinal age curves revealed broadly consistent patterns across regions (Figure 5B). Mortality risk increased steadily with advancing age, peaking around 95 years. Globally, nearly all age groups demonstrated higher risks among males. In high-SDI regions, deaths were substantially higher across nearly all age groups compared with other regions, with males showing an elevated risk up to age 90 years, after which risk increased more prominently among females. In high-middle- and middle-SDI regions, mortality was lower overall, though male excess risk persisted across most age groups. In low-middle- and low-SDI regions, mortality remained lower across age groups; however, males over age 30 faced a higher risk. For those under 30, deaths were substantially fewer, with no obvious sex differences in low-middle-SDI regions, whereas in low-SDI regions, females exhibited a higher risk.

Period rate ratios, reflecting temporal shifts in mortality relative to the earliest reference period, showed striking heterogeneity across SDI regions (Figure 5C). Globally, period effects demonstrated a steady annual decline, with minimal sex differences. In high-SDI regions, the decline was more rapid, particularly among males, who experienced the sharpest reductions in recent years. In high-middle-SDI regions, period risk also declined, though at a slower pace; females experienced a pronounced decline between 2000 and 2005. In middle-SDI regions, period risk was largely stable, with a modest increase observed among males. By contrast, in low-middle-SDI regions, period risk rose steeply, with males experiencing substantially sharper increases than females. In low-SDI regions, risk declined until around 2010, followed by a reversal with increasing risk in more recent years; males again showed slower declines or faster increases compared with females. Importantly, after ~2014, declines in period risk plateaued in global, high-SDI, and high-middle-SDI regions, while the rate of increase accelerated in middle-, low-middle-, and low-SDI regions.

Cohort rate ratios, reflecting mortality risk across successive birth cohorts, demonstrated marked heterogeneity across SDI regions (Figure 5D). Overall, high- and high-middle-SDI regions exhibited trends opposite to those observed in middle-, low-middle-, and low-SDI regions. Globally, individuals born before 1957 faced elevated cohort risks, whereas subsequent cohorts showed relatively stable risk trajectories. In high-SDI regions, older cohorts experienced substantially higher risks, followed by a moderate downward trend among younger cohorts. Similarly, in high-middle-SDI regions, older cohorts had moderately elevated risks, while younger cohorts showed a slight decline. By contrast, middle- and low-middle-SDI regions demonstrated increasing risk from older to younger cohorts. Specifically, in middle-SDI regions, risks were stable among older cohorts but rose modestly in more recent cohorts. In low-middle-SDI regions, older cohorts exhibited lower risks, while younger cohorts experienced a moderate upward trend. In low-SDI regions, cohort risk remained largely flat across all birth cohorts. Sex-specific patterns were also evident. In high- and high-middle-SDI regions, older cohorts exhibited higher mortality among males. In high-middle, middle, and low-middle-SDI regions, however, younger cohorts showed a stronger male excess in mortality. Notably, in low-SDI regions, older cohorts demonstrated higher female mortality, whereas younger cohorts exhibited greater male mortality.

Risk factors

The 2021 GBD study evaluated seven risk factors contributing to DALYs associated with AA (Figure 6A-6D). Globally, the leading contributors were smoking (37.3%), high systolic blood pressure (17.1%), high BMI (8%), diet low in fruit (3.8%), diet low in vegetables (2.9%), diet high in sodium (1%), and lead exposure (0.7%). Comparative analyses of smoking, high systolic blood pressure, and high BMI across the five SDI regions revealed only modest differences. Smoking emerged as the predominant risk factor, with its attributable DALY proportion slightly higher in high-, high-middle-, and middle-SDI regions. Age-stratified analyses showed that the smoking-attributable burden was disproportionately concentrated among younger and middle-aged individuals, peaking at 70.2% in the 35–39-year age group (Figure 6E). Across all SDI regions, smoking accounted for a substantially greater share of DALYs among males. However, in high-, high-middle-, and middle-SDI regions, elevated proportions were also observed among younger and middle-aged females. It is noteworthy that smoking peaks in younger and middle-aged groups, which may contribute to rising mortality among younger cohorts in certain SDI regions.

Discussion

Drawing on findings from the 2021 GBD study, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of global and SDI-specific trends in AA-related deaths and DALYs from 1990 to 2021, as well as their quantitative association with SDI values. To characterize temporal dynamics and generate projections, we applied the BAPC, EAPC, and conventional APC models. Although deaths declined markedly in high- and high-middle-SDI regions, low-middle-SDI regions displayed a worrisome upward trajectory. APC modeling further demonstrated opposing period and cohort effects between high-/high-middle-SDI regions and middle- or low-SDI regions. In addition, we performed a preliminary evaluation of risk factor contributions across SDI strata.

In line with previous GBD 2019 (7,12) and 2021 (13-15) reports, our analysis showed that the global ASMR and ASDR decreased annually by approximately 1.28% and 1.23%, respectively, over 1990–2021. By contrast, the absolute numbers of deaths and DALYs increased by 74.1% and 64.9%, respectively, during the same period. Wang et al. (16) further reported increasing ASMR and ASDR among adolescents and young adults (15–39 years), highlighting the influence of age effects. Consistent with earlier studies, our results suggest that high-SDI regions simultaneously exhibit lower ASMR/ASDR and a stronger impact of population aging on deaths and DALYs (19,20). BAPC-based projections indicate that, globally, ASMR and ASDR will remain stable or decline slightly between 2022 and 2030, whereas absolute deaths and DALYs will continue to rise. This finding aligns with forecasts by Tian et al. (13). Their findings indicate a continued decline in ASRs despite an increasing absolute burden, alongside worsening absolute inequalities across SDI levels. The apparent paradox, declining ASMR despite increasing deaths, likely reflects demographic aging combined with expanded access to screening, treatment, and preventive interventions. Furthermore, health policies may reduce mortality rates but exhibit lagged effects in lowering absolute case numbers.

The contrasting temporal patterns of AA burden across SDI strata highlight the importance of socioeconomic development in shaping disease outcomes. Extending our analysis to 21 regions and 204 countries and territories, we identified weak but significant positive correlations between ASMR and SDI (P<0.001) and between ASDR and SDI (P<0.001), consistent with findings from the GBD 2017 study (21). These results highlight the need to account for SDI when interpreting the global distribution of AA burden. Unexpectedly, however, the distribution of EAPC values did not align with SDI in high-income regions such as North America, Europe, and Oceania, where higher SDI levels were not associated with elevated AA burden. By contrast, in Asia, Africa, and South America, EAPC values appeared to partially colocalize with higher SDI values. These discrepancies likely reflect complex interactions, including the implementation of cost-effective screening initiatives (22), but are primarily attributable to unequal levels of economic and healthcare development across continents, resulting in disparities in access to advanced medical interventions.

To date, reports about AA disease-burden heterogeneity across SDI quintiles remain limited. In our study, we analysed and projected ASDR and ASMR for different SDI regions and employed an APC model to decompose mortality trends. Contrary to the age effect, period and cohort effects appear to operate in opposite directions across different SDI regions. Specifically, advancing age raised death rates in all regions (Figure 5B), whereas period and cohort effects reduced mortality in high- and high-middle SDI regions but increased it in low-middle and middle SDI areas, especially in the low-middle quintile (Figure 5C,5D). Coupled with the upward trajectories of ASDR and ASMR projected by the BAPC model for low-middle SDI regions, our findings suggest that AA prevention and intervention efforts should prioritise this population. The APC model further clarified the contributions of period and cohort effects to global AA mortality patterns across SDI regions (Figure 5C,5D). The overall declining mortality trends are likely driven by improvements in public health, advances in surgical and endovascular technologies, wider adoption of preventive measures, and enhanced treatment efficacy (23-25). Nonetheless, the growing size and proportion of the elderly population remain key drivers of the rising absolute numbers of AA deaths and DALYs (7,12). Age effects were consistent across all SDI regions, with mortality rates increasing steadily with advancing age. However, period and cohort effects diverged across SDI strata, reflecting differences in healthcare capacity.

For period effects, mortality rates have declined continuously in high- and high-middle-SDI regions, supported by stronger health systems and economic investment. In contrast, middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions demonstrated upward mortality trends, partly attributable to improved registry systems that have increased detection of AA, but also to limited capacity to manage complex surgical and endovascular interventions. Cohort effects revealed a pivotal inflection point around 1957. In high- and high-middle-SDI regions, individuals born after 1957 experienced declining AA-related mortality compared to earlier cohorts, coinciding with major surgical advances. The first open thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) repair by Rosenblum et al. (26) in 1955, followed by Crawford’s (27) refinements in 1978 that significantly reduced perioperative complications, heralded a turning point in aneurysm management. Coupled with parallel advances in diagnostic imaging, these innovations substantially lowered mortality in high-SDI regions. By contrast, in middle, low-middle and low SDI regions, post-1957 birth cohorts experienced rising mortality risks, reflecting limited dissemination of advanced surgical techniques and slower adoption of modern diagnostic tools. Despite substantial progress over the past three decades, disparities in AA-related mortality have widened across the five SDI regions. In high and high-middle SDI regions, the number of deaths has shown a persistent downward trend, whereas in low-middle SDI regions, mortality has continued to rise, regardless of whether trends were evaluated by net drift, period effects, or cohort effects. In low SDI regions, mortality patterns were more heterogeneous, displaying complex year- and sex-specific variations.

Across five SDI regions, the values for net drift and the EAPC of ASMR were largely consistent. Although the observed increase in AA mortality may be partially attributable to the wider adoption of CT imaging (12,17,28), it is equally critical to recognize the insufficient management of AA in low-middle SDI regions. This gap highlights the urgent need for targeted interventions, including enhanced public education and screening, strengthened preventive strategies, and improvements in diagnostic and therapeutic capacity.

Despite notable advances in mitigating the global burden of AA, projections indicate that this burden will continue to rise in the coming decades, highlighting the necessity for sustained and proactive public health preparedness. Prior studies have demonstrated that higher Health Access and Quality Index (HAQI) scores are associated with reduced mortality and YLLs due to AA (19). In contrast to YLLs, DALYs may offer a more comprehensive measure of the AA disease burden, as not all cases result in death (29). Our analysis further highlights the pivotal role of demographic structure in shaping the number of deaths and DALYs attributable to AA. Consistent with earlier findings (7), AA primarily affects individuals aged 15 years and older, with the highest mortality and DALY burden concentrated in those aged 65–75 years. Both ASMR and ASDR increased steadily with age. With respect to sex-specific patterns, our results corroborate prior GBD analyses, demonstrating that AA-related deaths, DALYs, ASMR, and ASDR are consistently higher in males compared to females (12-15,18).

In our analysis, smoking, high systolic blood pressure, and high BMI emerged as the leading contributors to DALYs associated with AA. Although smoking and high systolic blood pressure are well-established risk factors, the role of BMI in AA remains debated (30-32). Consistent with prior studies (7,12,16), our findings highlight the urgent need to promote smoking cessation, particularly among young and middle-aged men in higher SDI regions, where the proportion of DALYs attributable to smoking reached as high as 77.3% (Figure 6F). Attention should also be directed toward women in higher SDI regions, given the notable disease burden observed in this group. Furthermore, beyond conventional tobacco use, the potential impact of e-cigarettes on AA incidence warrants close monitoring (33). Contrary to results from the 2019 GBD study (7), our investigation did not identify significant sex-related differences in the DALYs attributed to high systolic blood pressure.

Whether the observed changes in disease burden reflect genuine epidemiological shifts or simply improved access to AA screening also requires careful consideration. A useful analytical approach is to examine the ratio of AA incidence to mortality: if the increase in incidence markedly exceeds that in mortality, the rise is more likely attributable to better detection and correct death certification; conversely, if mortality increases more than incidence, true epidemiological change is probably driving the trend. Unfortunately, the GBD 2021 database does not provide incidence estimates for AA. From an APC perspective, mortality increases stemming from enhanced screening would primarily manifest as period effects. During 1990–2021—the timeframe of our data—overall healthcare capacity in low-middle- and low-SDI regions improved only modestly, making substantial gains in AA detection unlikely (34,35). Yet, we observe a pronounced upward trend in AA mortality cohort effects in these same regions (Figure 5C). We therefore contend that, at least within low-middle- and low-SDI settings, the observed rise in AA-related mortality is more likely to reflect genuine epidemiological change rather than detection bias.

Amid population ageing, the age effects remain a major driver of the rising disease burden of AA. We believe that age-specific AA screening for older adults, coupled with tobacco control and blood-pressure reduction, can effectively lessen the disability and mortality caused by AA. At the same time, the surprisingly high DALYs attributable to AA among young and middle-aged smokers (Figure 6E) call for stronger tobacco-control policies targeting young and middle-aged men, especially in high- and high-middle-SDI regions. Differentiated, SDI-based public-health strategies for AA should therefore be considered. Given the declining burden in high- and high-middle-SDI regions and the upward trend in low-middle- and low-SDI regions, priority should be given to the latter. From a global resource-allocation perspective, resources from higher-SDI countries or territories could be shared—via wireless telemedicine guidance, international surgical missions, or exchange programmes—to boost AA screening and treatment capacity in lower-SDI areas, while simultaneously promoting research on AA subtypes and subnational disparities.

This study has several limitations. First, the GBD 2021 dataset does not include prevalence or incidence estimates for AA, restricting our ability to conduct a comprehensive epidemiological assessment. Secondly, AA-related mortality data are primarily derived from death certificates, which may underestimate the actual burden, particularly in resource-limited settings where autopsies are infrequently performed. Third, the dataset does not differentiate between AA subtypes, namely TAAAs, despite their distinct epidemiological characteristics and treatment approaches (4,5). Fourth, subnational variations were not assessed, as the GBD 2021 dataset does not provide sufficiently granular data; such analyses could help reveal region-specific trends. Fifth, there is potential ascertainment bias, as higher CT utilization in high-SDI regions may lead to inflated detection rates, while underdiagnosis persists in low-SDI settings. Finally, there is a risk of ecological fallacy, because SDI is an aggregate measure that can mask substantial within-country disparities. Despite these limitations, our study utilizes the most recent data available and provides valuable insights into the global burden of AA.

Conclusions

Although substantial progress has been achieved in reducing the burden of AA over the past three decades, sustained and tailored interventions are necessary. Notably, high- and high-middle-SDI regions may primarily need to maintain existing screening programs, whereas middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions must prioritize strengthening diagnostic and treatment capacity. Low-middle SDI regions, in particular, warrant urgent attention, given the concerning upward trajectory of the AA burden observed in these populations.
