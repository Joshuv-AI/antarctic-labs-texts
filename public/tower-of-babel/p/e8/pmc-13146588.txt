Okinawa’s distinctive hepatitis B epidemiology reflected in 11-year seroprevalence trends among elderly long-term care residents in Japan
Authors: Norifumi Kudeken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Hepatitis B surface antigen prevalence remained low in Okinawan long-term care facility residents over 11 years.•Anti-hepatitis B surface antibody prevalence stayed stable at about one-third to one-half.•No hepatitis B virus (HBV)–related flares, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, or reactivation were observed.•Most recorded deaths were due to senility, not HBV-related liver disease.•Findings support region-sensitive HBV surveillance in elderly care.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) epidemiology in Japan has changed substantially over recent decades, but marked regional heterogeneity remains. Okinawa has historically shown high exposure to HBV and lower mortality from HBV-related cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma than might be expected from its carrier prevalence. Previous studies suggest that this pattern is linked to the predominance of genotype B in Okinawa, whereas genotype C predominates in most mainland areas and is more often associated with progressive liver disease [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

The present elderly population in Okinawa was born decades before selective prevention of mother-to-child transmission, and routine infant HBV vaccination could influence their birth cohorts. Historical studies from Okinawa documented high prevalences of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), anti-hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs), and anti-hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) in adults, followed by declining infection rates in younger birth cohorts over time and a changing balance between intrafamilial and horizontal transmission [2,3,6,7].

Long-term care facilities (LTCFs) bring together frail older adults with prolonged stays, repeated clinical procedures, and frequent contact with health care staff. Although HBV transmission in LTCFs has been reported, long-term facility-level serological data from Okinawa are scarce [8].

We, therefore, examined 11-year trends in HBsAg and anti-HBs among residents of a long-term care welfare facility in Okinawa and reviewed HBV-related clinical events and causes of death during follow-up. We aimed to determine whether the serological profile of this institutionalized elderly cohort reflected the distinctive HBV background of Okinawa.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study analyzed annual HBsAg and anti-HBs screening results from a long-term care welfare facility (Tokuyo) in Okinawa, Japan, between 2015 and 2025. The facility has a maximum capacity of 134 residents and primarily serves older adults with high care needs.

During the study period, the mean age of residents was 85.6 years and women accounted for 62.6% of the facility population. The annual number of admissions and discharges was approximately 20-30, with no major change in the overall resident mix during the 11-year period.

Data collection

HBsAg and anti-HBs testing was performed as part of routine annual health assessments conducted by the facility’s on-site clinical team. For each year, seroprevalence was calculated as the proportion of residents who tested positive for HBsAg or anti-HBs. Results were summarized overall and stratified by sex and residential ward.

Facility medical records were also reviewed for documented HBV-related liver events, including hepatitis flares, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and HBV reactivation. Recorded causes of death during the study period were reviewed descriptively.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed descriptively. Ward-level differences in anti-HBs positivity were compared using the chi-square test. Because only annual aggregate data were available, individual longitudinal seroconversion analyses were not performed.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kubagawa Medical Clinic (KM-2025-01). The serological tests were performed as part of routine clinical care, and the retrospective analysis used de-identified data. The requirement for study-specific informed consent was, therefore, waived.

Results

Across 2015-2025, HBsAg prevalence remained low, ranging from 0.8% to 3.6%, and never exceeded 5%. Anti-HBs prevalence remained relatively stable, ranging from 32.1% to 47.8%. Discussion

In this LTCF-based elderly cohort in Okinawa, HBsAg prevalence remained consistently low, whereas anti-HBs prevalence remained stable at approximately one-third to one-half of residents over 11 years. These findings support the view that the current institutionalized elderly population in Okinawa still reflects the prefecture’s distinctive HBV background, characterized by high past exposure but limited progression to advanced liver disease [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

The genotype interpretation in this study is indirect. Previous studies have shown that genotype B is relatively common in Okinawa compared with mainland Japan and that genotype B infection in Japan is associated with lower hepatitis B e antigen positivity, lower HBV DNA levels, and better long-term prognosis than genotype C. In addition, genotype B in Japan includes at least two major subtypes, Bj/B1 and Ba/B2-related strains, which may differ virologically [[4], [5], [6]]. However, we did not perform HBV DNA testing or genotype/subgenotype analysis in individual residents. Accordingly, the present data should not be interpreted as direct evidence of genotype-specific effects; rather, they should be understood as seroepidemiologic findings from Okinawa, a region where genotype B has historically predominated [4,5].

The high and stable anti-HBs prevalence also suggests that many residents had remote exposure to HBV before entering the facility. Given the combination of low HBsAg prevalence and relatively high anti-HBs prevalence, the serological pattern is compatible with remote horizontal HBV exposure acquired after early childhood in many residents rather than persistent chronic infection after mother-to-child transmission, although individual routes cannot be determined from the available data. Because this cohort was born decades before selective prevention of mother-to-child transmission and long before routine infant HBV vaccination could affect their birth cohorts, naturally acquired immunity likely explains much of the observed anti-HBs positivity. Historical studies from Okinawa reported high adult exposure rates and declining infection among younger birth cohorts, supporting the idea that the current elderly population retains the serological imprint of earlier transmission eras [[2], [3], [7], [9]]. At the same time, because vaccination histories, anti-HBc, and hepatitis B e antigen status were unavailable, we cannot determine the exact route or age at infection for individual residents.

Another notable finding was the absence of HBV-related liver events and HBV-related deaths during follow-up. Most deaths in this facility were due to senility, and a small number were due to aspiration pneumonia. This pattern is consistent with a very old and frail LTCF population in which non-hepatic causes of death predominate. We, therefore, revised the interpretation to avoid overstating the absence of liver-related deaths as proof of negligible HBV risk; rather, the data suggest that, within this facility, HBV contributed little to observed morbidity and mortality during the study period.

The lack of ward clustering and the persistently low HBsAg prevalence also argue against substantial intra-facility HBV transmission. This is clinically important because LTCFs are recognized settings in which blood-borne virus transmission can occur if infection control practices are inadequate [8]. In the present facility, stable serological patterns despite annual resident turnover suggest that routine care practices were sufficient to prevent detectable spread.

These findings have practical implications for public health and facility management. In LTCFs that serve older residents from historically high-exposure regions, HBV serostatus can inform admission assessment, identification of susceptible residents, vaccination when appropriate, staff safety education, and risk assessment before immunosuppressive therapy or chemotherapy. Region-specific surveillance may, therefore, be more informative than a uniform national assumption of risk.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single facility, which limits generalizability. Second, vaccination history, anti-HBc, HBV DNA, and genotype/subgenotype data were unavailable. Third, the data set was aggregated annually, precluding resident-level longitudinal analysis of serostatus changes. Fourth, the causes of death were obtained from facility records and not independently adjudicated. Despite these limitations, the study provides uncommon long-term serological data from an elderly institutionalized population in Okinawa and helps contextualize current LTCF findings within the region’s distinctive HBV history.

Conclusion

Among elderly residents of a long-term care facility in Okinawa, HBsAg prevalence remained rare and anti-HBs prevalence remained stable over 11 years. No HBV-related liver events or HBV-related deaths were identified, and most recorded deaths were due to senility or aspiration pneumonia. Although direct genotype data were unavailable, these findings are consistent with the long-recognized HBV epidemiology of Okinawa and support region-sensitive HBV surveillance in long-term care settings.
