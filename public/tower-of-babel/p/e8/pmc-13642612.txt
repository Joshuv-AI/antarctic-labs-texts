Topological Transitions at Optical Interfaces Near Reflection Singularities
Authors: Yawei Tan, Xiaohui Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTopological transitions of structured light at optical interfaces, particularly near singularities in reflection coefficients, hold significant potential for advanced light field manipulation. However, these phenomena remain largely unexplored beyond classical Brewster‐angle configurations. In this work, we establish a unified theoretical framework to describe topological transitions—from an ideal vortex to a deformed off‐axis vortex—near reflection singularities and provide experimental validation of the predictions. Through a full‐wave model, we demonstrate that the transition is governed by three independent degrees of freedom: the incident angle, the incident polarization state, and the reflected polarization state. This triple‐control mechanism enables precise manipulation of vortex deformation and lateral shifting. Furthermore, we extend this approach to multilayer systems by designing a PT‐symmetric bilayer structure satisfying r s = 0. This structure enables vortex generation and topological transition under a quasi‐Brewster condition. Our findings pave the way for enhanced topological control of structured light and offer promising prospects for advanced photonic applications.

Introduction

Propelled by rapid advancements in singular optics and topological photonics, the study of geometric and topological properties of optical fields has attracted considerable attention [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In particular, structured light beams host singular configurations such as optical vortices defined by phase singularities, offering not only intricate spatial control but also high sensitivity to system parameters, including polarization, incident angle, refractive index, and geometric dimension [3, 7, 8]. Variations in these parameters can trigger significant topological transitions among vortex generation, spin splitting, and beam shifts [9, 10, 11]. Importantly, such topological effects establish a new physical basis for emerging applications such as optical differential imaging [12, 13, 14, 15] and precision metrology [16, 17, 18, 19].

The interaction of light with interfaces can generate a variety of topological effects [10, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Recent studies have demonstrated that under normal incidence of a circularly polarized beam, the reflected (refracted) light exhibit an anomalous mode carrying a vortex phase with a topological charge of ± 2. Under oblique incidence, this mode undergoes a continuous topological transition from vortex generation to spin‐Hall effect [10, 24]. More intriguingly, near Fresnel coefficient singularities—such as in vicinity of the Brewster angle—an elliptically polarized incident beam can yield a reflected vortex phase with a topological charges of ± 1 through precise control of the reflected polarization state [14, 25]. Further adjustment of interface parameters or the ellipticity of the incident polarization enables a topological transition from a vortex state to a beam shift. These effects show promising potential for applications in optical differentiation, edge imaging, and sensing [12, 13, 14, 15, 25, 26, 27]. However, topological transitions near reflection singularities and their underlying mechanisms remain obscure. Current investigations are largely restricted to single‐interface scenarios near the Brewster angle [14, 25, 26, 27, 28] leaving a gap in generalizing these phenomena to multilayer systems. It is also unclear whether analogous topological transitions can occur near other types of Fresnel coefficient singularities. Since realistic optical systems often incorporate multilayer configurations and complex reflection coefficients, a unified theoretical framework capable of accommodating complex structures and diverse polarization states is crucial for the multidimensional manipulation of structured light fields [29, 30, 31].

In this work, we address these issues by systematically investigating topological transitions—specifically from an ideal vortex to a deformed off‐axis vortex—near reflection coefficient singularities at optical interfaces (Figure 1) both theoretically and experimentally. We first develop a full‐wave theory to model the dynamical behavior of light beams during interface reflection near the Brewster angle (r p = 0). We then demonstrate that this topological transition can be controlled via three degrees of freedom: the incident angle, which induces vertical beam deformation and a shift of the beam centroid; the reflected polarization angle, which governs off‐axis displacement or lateral shift; and the incident polarization state, which drives the transition from a vortex to a two‐lobe intensity distribution. Furthermore, we extend this framework beyond the conventional Brewster angle by exploring topological transitions near engineered singularities where the s‐polarized reflection coefficient vanishes (r s = 0), thereby establishing a more universal quasi‐Brewster condition. This study establishes a unified framework for understanding topological transitions in complex optical systems, providing new insights for structured light manipulation, high‐precision sensing, and advanced photonic device design.

Topological Transition of Reflected Light Near the Brewster Angle at a Single Interface

We theoretically simulated the evolution of the vortex in the reflected field at an air–dielectric interface (Figure 2) to study how its topological structure evolves near the singularity. The dielectric medium has a refractive index of 1.5 corresponding to a Brewster angle of θ B = 56.31°. Since vortex generation at the Brewster angle requires specific polarization conditions, numerical calculations (see the method in Supporting Information S1: S3) indicate that an ideal vortex is obtained when α = 66.9°, θ q = 0°, and β = 0°. With the incident polarization angle fixed at α = 66.9°, a slight change of the incident angle θ i from θ B induces a notable shift of the beam centroid along the y‐direction, whereas variation in the analyzer angle β, leads to a pronounced beam shift along the x‐direction. Simultaneously adjusting both θ i and β displaces the beam centroid along ± 45° and ± 135° directions relative to the x‐axis, demonstrating that beam shifts can occur independently along both transverse directions. This highlights the high sensitivity of the topological structure to changes in the incident angle and analyzer angle (Figure 2a).

When the analyzer angle is set to β = 0°, changing the incident polarization angle α causes the generated vortex, which initially has a phase singularity, to split into a two‐lobe structure. For α < 66.9°, the beam evolves into a horizontally oriented bilobed profile, whereas for α > 66.9°, a vertical two‐lobe structure emerges (Figure 2b). Different combinations of (α, θ i ) drive the topology of the reflected beam through a series of transitions: from a doughnut shape to horizontal or vertical two‐lobe structures and eventually to a y‐direction off‐axis displacement or a centroid shift accompanied by intensity splitting. Furthermore, at a fixed incidence angle of θ i = 56.31°, varying (α, β) prompts the reflected beam to evolve from an x‐direction off‐axis displacement to an x‐direction intensity splitting (Figure 2c).

Figure 3a illustrates the topological transition induced by the incident angle θ i under fixed polarization conditions (α = 66.9° and β = 0°). As θ i approaches the Brewster angle θ B = 56.31°, the intensity profile evolves from a Gaussian distribution into a y‐direction off‐axis vortex culminating in an ideal vortex at precisely θ B . Mode decomposition indicates that this transition is governed by the competition between the fundamental mode (m = 0) and the high‐order m = +1 mode. Near θ B , the weight of the m = 0 mode is nearly completely suppressed, whereas the m = +1 mode becomes overwhelmingly dominant. These results confirm that the incident angle serves as a control parameter that modulates the relative modal weights, thereby governing both the beam displacement and the emergence of the vortex phase singularity.

Figure 3b depicts the evolution of the reflected field as a function of the incident polarization angle α with θ i fixed at the Brewster angle and the analyzer angle maintained at β = 0°. In this scenario, the evolution governed by α no longer involves competition with the m = 0 mode, but instead results from the interplay between the m = +1 and m = −1 modes, which exhibit opposite chirality. As α approaches 0° or 90°—corresponding to linear polarization states—the modal weights of the m = +1 and m = −1 become comparable. Their interference results in the breakup of the vortex into a two‐lobe structure.

Figure 3c illustrates the scenario where θ i is fixed at the Brewster angle and α = 75°, whereas the reflected polarization angle β is tuned. At β = 0°, the reflected field exhibits a distorted doughnut‐like profile arising from the interference between the m = +1 and m = −1 modes with the m = 0 mode fully suppressed. As β deviates slightly from 0°, the m = 0 mode emerges and coexists with the m = +1 and m = −1 components. The competition among these three modes lead to deformation and displacement of the intensity profile.

Figure 3d illustrates the scenario with a fixed incident angle of θᵢ = 56.31°, a constant incident polarization angle of α = 66.9°, and a fixed reflected polarization angle of β = 0°, whereas the QWP angle θ q is varied. Mode analysis reveals that the reflected field in this configuration is predominantly characterized by the m = +1 mode across the entire range of θ q , whereas contributions from the m = −1 modes remain strongly suppressed and the m = 0 mode is completely suppressed. Consequently, the reflected intensity maintains a robust vortex profile, although minor deformation and rotation of the intensity distribution occur as θ q varies. The persistence of the dominant m = +1 component underscores the vortex's stability against polarization‐phase modulation introduced by the QWP.

Experimental Verification of Topological Transition at the Air–Glass Interface

To experimentally validate the topological transitions predicted by our theory, we constructed the experimental setup depicted in Figure 4a. A Gaussian beam from a He–Ne laser first passes through a half‐wave plate (HWP) and a Glan‐Laser polarizer (GLP1) for intensity control. The orientation of GLP1 is precisely adjusted to set the incident linear polarization angle α. A quarter‐wave plate (QWP) with its fast axis at 0° then converts the linearly polarized light (set to α = 66.9°) into the desired elliptical polarization. Subsequently, the beam is focused by a lens and incident at an angle θ i upon the surface of a right‐angle prism, which is mounted on a precision rotation stage for accurate control of θ i . The reflected light passes through a second polarizer (GLP2) serving as an analyzer, whose transmission axis defines the reflection polarization angle β. Finally, the intensity distribution of the reflected beam was captured by a CCD camera.

The experimental results, presented in Figure 4b–d, show excellent agreement with the theoretical predictions shown in Figure 2. Figure 4b verifies the topological control of the vortex via the incident angle θ i and the analyzer angle β. In the central row (β = 0°), as θ i is swept across the experimentally determined Brewster angle (θ B = 56.31°), the beam clearly evolves from a vertically oriented off‐axis vortex into an ideal vortex, and then into an off‐axis vortex with reversed orientation. Moreover, introducing a small offset in β leads to a distinct horizontal displacement of the vortex, the direction of which depends on the sign of β. This behavior is fully consistent with the theoretical predictions illustrated in Figure 2a.

Figure 4c presents the experimentally measured evolution of the reflected beam when the incident angle θ i and the incident polarization angle α are varied simultaneously, whereas the analyzer angle β is fixed at 0°. At Brewster condition (θ i = θ B and α = 66.9°), an ideal vortex is observed. When α < 66.9°, a horizontally oriented two‐lobe structure is observed, whereas for α > 66.9°, the reflected beam evolves into a vertically oriented two‐lobe intensity distribution. For specific combinations of (θ i , α), the vortex exhibits a centroid displacement along the y‐direction resulting in a displaced two‐lobe intensity profile. These experimental observations are in excellent agreement with the theoretical predictions shown in Figure 2b, confirming that the topological morphology of the reflected beam is jointly governed by θ i and α.

Figure 4d shows the experimental evolution of the reflected beam when the incident angle is fixed at θ i = 56.31°, whereas the incident polarization angle α and the analyzer angle β are varied. Under these conditions, the reflected field evolves from an x‐direction off‐axis vortex to an x‐direction intensity‐split structure as the polarization parameters are tuned. This behavior reproduces the theoretical predictions in Figure 2c and demonstrates that, at a fixed incidence angle, the interplay between α and β governs the transition between centroid displacement and intensity splitting along the x‐direction.

Overall, the experimental results provide robust validation of our theoretical framework. They comprehensively demonstrate that independent tuning of the incident angle, incident polarization state, and reflected polarization state enables deterministic and controllable topological transitions of the reflected beam—spanning ideal vortex generation, vertical and horizontal displacement, and intensity splitting.

Extension and Discussion

Topological Transition for s‐Polarized Beam at Quasi–Brewster Angle

The vortex generation and topological transition near the Brewster angle are intrinsically constrained by the vanishing condition of the p‐polarized reflection coefficient (r p →0). To assess the generality of this topological behavior beyond the conventional Brewster scenario, we design a parity–time (PT)‐symmetric bilayer system that circumvents this limitation. PT‐symmetric systems comprise balanced gain and loss media whose refractive indices satisfy spatial inversion symmetry n1=n2∗ [32, 33, 34, 35]. As illustrated in Figure 5a, a key advantage of this system is the simultaneous presence of reflection zeros for both p‐ and s‐polarized waves at specific incident angles. Specifically, the reflection coefficient for s‐polarized light, r s , vanishes at θ i = 29.994°, while that for p‐polarized light, r p , vanishes at θ i = 65.556°. Here, we introduce the term quasi‐Brewster condition to refer to reflection minima in engineered multilayer systems where r s→0 or r p →0 is achieved through interference rather than the natural Brewster effect at a single interface.

A perfect reflection zero is achieved for s‐polarization (|r s | = 0) at θ i = 29.994° and for p‐waves (|r p | = 0) at θ i = 65.556°. The PT‐symmetric system is composed of a bilayer with a thickness of d = 0.2λ for each layer. We subsequently investigate the topological evolution of the optical field around this s‐polarized reflection zero. In this regime, the s‐polarized component is suppressed rendering the orthogonal p‐polarization the dominant contribution to the reflected beam. Accordingly, the analyzer angle β was set near 90° to isolate the phase singularity associated with the anomalous s‐wave component. Figure 5b illustrates the control of the vortex evolution by θ i and β. When the s‐wave reflection vanishes and the analyzer is aligned to suppress the p‐wave (θ i = 29.994°, β = 90°), an ideal vortex is observed. With θ i fixed and varying β, the vortex undergoes a horizontal displacement from on‐axis to off‐axis resulting from interference introduced by a residual p‐wave component transmitted through the analyzer. In contrast, when β is fixed at 90° and θ i is varied near the reflection zero, the beam exhibits a vertical displacement due to interference with the reappearing nonzero s‐wave component. These results suggest that the vortex generation and topological transition are not limited to the conventional Brewster angle at a single interface but can also be realized under quasi‐Brewster conditions characterized by r s = 0 in engineered multilayer structure. Therefore, our theoretical framework—centered on the singular condition of a vanishing reflection coefficient (r p = 0 or r s = 0)—provides a unified description of topological transitions extending from the classical Brewster angle to engineered quasi‐Brewster conditions in complex systems.

Ideal Vortex Beam Generation From Single Interface to Multi‐Interface System

At a single dielectric interface, ideal optical vortices can be generated by tuning the incident polarization (P1) to a specific condition. However, this approach fails for more general multilayer structures, such as a dielectric slab, where the reflection coefficient is a complex with a nontrivial phase. This complexity invalidates simple polarization‐only control schemes. This added complexity renders polarization‐only control insufficient. To overcome this limitation and demonstrate the universality of our multiparameter control framework, we apply it to three typical systems: a conventional air–dielectric interface, a dielectric slab, and the previously described PT‐symmetric structure.

Although an ideal vortex is easily achieved at the air–dielectric interface with the QWP fixed at θ q = 0°, the same configuration applied to the slab system yields a distorted intensity profile (Slab [Config. 1]). A pure vortex is recovered only when the fast axis of the QWP is rotated to a specific angle (Slab [Config. 2]). Similarly, in the PT‐symmetric system operating at the s‐wave reflection zero, generating a doughnut‐shaped vortex requires careful tuning of both polarizer P1 and the QWP. This behavior stems from the additional phase imparted by the slab or PT‐symmetric bilayer—a mechanism generalizable to other multilayer systems, such as photonic crystals [36, 37, 38].

Vortex mode decomposition, shown in Figure 6b, offers quantitative confirmation. For the air–glass interface and the properly configured slab and PT‐symmetric systems, the mode power is predominantly concentrated in the m = +1 order. By contrast, the slab under fixed QWP conditions (Slab [Config. 1]) exhibits a significant m = −1 component, indicating mode mixing and reduced purity.

These results underscore a general principle: generating pure vortices in systems with complex reflection coefficients requires compensating for the phase acquired upon reflection. This can be achieved through coordinated control of the incident polarization and the QWP orientation. A fixed QWP angle (e.g., θ q = 0°) is generally insufficient for multilayer platforms. In fact, multiple combinations of P1 and QWP settings can yield an ideal vortex, highlighting the need for additional degrees of freedom in the generation and control of optical singularities in such systems.

Conclusion

We have systematically investigated the topological transitions of optical vortices near reflection singularities and experimentally validated the proposed theoretical framework. It is demonstrated that the incident angle controls vertical shifts of the beam centroid, whereas the reflection polarization governs horizontal displacements—thereby establishing a direct connection between vortex generation and beam shift phenomena. Furthermore, variation of the incident polarization has been shown to drive a transition from a vortex state to a two‐lobe intensity profile. Extending the approach to multilayer systems has revealed that stable vortex generation requires coordinated control of both polarization and phase delay. Importantly, by designing a PT‐symmetric bilayer structure with a vanishing reflection coefficient for s‐polarization, we have successfully generalized the vortex generation and topological control from the traditional Brewster condition (r p = 0) to a more universal quasi‐Brewster condition (r s = 0). These results generalize vortex physics beyond the conventional Brewster condition and establish a foundational framework for the topological control of structured light with promising applications in precision metrology and integrated photonics [39, 40, 41, 42, 43].
