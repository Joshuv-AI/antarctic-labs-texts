A comparison between intensive care unit and surgical ward in clinical educators-related factors that influence clinical nursing competencies of new graduate nurses
Authors: Toru Miyake, Masato Yoshioka, Midori Kaga, Hideaki Andoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: This study aimed to identify the degree of influence of clinical educators for improving clinical nursing competencies of new graduate nurses assigned in intensive care unit (ICU) and also to suggest ways to educate new graduate nurses in the ICU.Materials and Methods: A self-administered, unmarked web-based questionnaire survey method was employed. The quality of clinical educators was measured using the Clinical Educator Quality Scale, and the clinical nursing competency of new graduate nurses was measured using the Clinical Nursing Competence Self-Assessment Scale (CNCSS). Multiple regression analysis was performed with the four CNCSS factors as dependent variables and clinical educator quality and personal and organizational background as independent variables.Results: A total of 125 participants responded to the survey; 57 (45.6%) were admitted to the ICU, while 68 (54.4%) to the surgical ward. In the ICUs, the “competence in basic nursing practice”, “competence in coordinating the care environment and team system”, and “competence in studying in nursing practice” influenced the attitude toward new graduate nurses. In the surgical wards, ‘sex and leadership’ influenced “competence in basic nursing practice”, and “competence in developing nursing care”. A supervisor who monitors their daily work and supports their job affected “competence in coordinating the care environment and team system” and “competence in studying nursing practice”.Conclusion: We identified distinct factors that affected clinical nursing competence in ICUs and surgical wards. Therefore, new graduate nurses must be educated with different emphases, particularly in the ICUs.Keywords: new graduate nurses, clinical nursing competencies, clinical educators, intensive care units (ICUs), surgical wards

Introduction

In recent years, intensive care units (ICUs) have advanced medical equipment that can aid in performing therapeutic procedures such as organ transplantation; thereby improving quality of life among aging patients in the presence of a severe illness. Therefore, it is imperative for ICU nurses to possess advanced experiential clinical nursing competencies. However, the duration of basic nursing courses for ICU nursing education is extremely limited, making it challenging to ensure uniform proficiency among students in comprehending ICU nursing concepts. New graduate nurses hired in critical care settings are likely to encounter additional challenges as they transition into their nursing roles, as they require specialized training and knowledge to practice safely1). In response to evolving demands in clinical practice, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare published the Examples of Technical Training for new graduate nurses2). While these guidelines outline specific content and educational objectives for nursing skills required in general wards, such as hygiene and waste elimination, they lack specific provisions for comprehending and managing medical electronic equipment, such as ventilators, central venous pressure measurement, and blood gas analysis required in critical areas, including ICUs, or ensuring precise dosage and administration. However, determining whether education should encompass techniques associated with infusion management, including curating accurate dosages and administration, remains challenging.

To overcome this situation, several facilities are devising and developing continuing nursing education programs to supplement specialized knowledge and skills for ICU care. Clinical education is a continuing nursing education program introduced by many hospitals. In clinical education, novice nurses are paired with experienced staff to facilitate role transition and foster workplace3). Moreover, they discovered that several hospitals have introduced clinical education to facilitate nursing practice in highly advanced medical care. Clinical educators and clinical education can also have a significant effect on the competence of new graduate nurses by supporting the development of their confidence and competence during the transition period4, 5). However, the details of educational policies and contents are determined by each facility. Specific educational content is created by educators and clinical educators at each facility, tailoring content to suit individual circumstances. In ICU, where advanced expertise and nursing practice are required, the involvement of clinical educator adjusted on the basis of clinical nursing competencies is important for new ICU nurses to acquire essential clinical nursing skills. However, guiding factors for clinical educators in facilitate the acquisition and enhancement of clinical nursing competencies of new ICU nurses remain unclear.

Thus, a comparative study on the qualities required by clinical educators of ICU and surgical ward nurses was conducted by evaluating the qualifications of clinical educators and their clinical nursing competencies. Additionally, a comprehensive analysis was conducted to elucidate factors influencing clinical nursing competencies of new nurse graduates, including personal and organizational backgrounds and characteristics of their clinical educators. The primary aim was to identify features of clinical educators essential for enhancing the clinical nursing competence of new graduate nurses practicing in ICU. The findings of this study offer valuable suggestions for future education policies tailored to new graduate nurses.

Materials and Methods

Definition of terms

This study defines new nurses who have just graduated from school and obtained nursing licenses as “new graduate nurses”, senior nurses who provide preceptor education as “clinical educators”, and experienced nurses who supervise clinical educators as “associates”.

Study design

A self-administered, unmarked questionnaire, using a web-based platform was employed in this study. This survey form with a QR code was sent to 624 new graduate nurses; who indicated their intent to participate in the survey at 54 facilities; they were instructed to complete the questionnaire online. The survey was conducted from December 1, 2021, to March 31, 2022.

Target facilities and participants

Among the intensive care specialist training facilities accredited nationwide by the Japanese Society of Intensive Care Medicine, 300 ICU were selected, excluding pediatric and gynecological facilities. New graduate nurses in general ICUs and surgical wards (e.g., neurosurgery, cardiovascular surgery, digestive surgery, respiratory surgery, and orthopedics) were included in this study.

Survey contents

Background of the participants

Personal background included age, sex, educational attainment, hospital ward, years of clinical educator experience, number of discussions with clinical educators, presence of associates, and number of team discussions on educational progress.

Measurement of clinical educators’ qualifications

The quality of the clinical educators was measured using the Clinical Educator Quality Scale developed by Sugiura and Kogo6). This scale was based on a survey conducted among new graduate nurses and clinical educators to evaluate the competencies of clinical educators to support new graduate nurses. A 5-point Likert scale (5, true; 4, somewhat true; 3, neither true nor false; 2, not true, and 1, not true) for 25 items encompassing four factors, namely “overall support”, “leadership”, “support for duties”, and “attitude toward new graduate nurses” in the clinical educator quality scale were used to measure the qualities of clinical educators. A permission from the creator of the Clinical Educator Quality Scale was obtained to use the scale.

Measurement of clinical nursing competence

Clinical nursing competency was measured using the Clinical Nursing Competence Self-Assessment Scale (CNCSS)7). The CNCSS consists of four concepts of clinical nursing competence, namely “competence in basic nursing practice”, “competence in developing nursing care”, “competence in coordinating the care environment and team system”, and “competence in studying in nursing practice”. The 64 items encompassing these four factors were surveyed and quantified using a 5-point Likert scale (true, 5 points; somewhat true, 4 points; neither true nor false, 3 points; not sufficiently true, 2 points; and not true, 1 point). The current nursing practice abilities of new graduate nurses were quantified and evaluated using this method. A self-assessment scale was used, with permission from the creator.

Statistical analysis

Basic statistics for each scale and attributes were calculated. Cronbach’s α reliability coefficients were calculated for both scales to confirm reliability through internal consistency. The nominal and interval variables were reclassified into two groups: age, “20s” and “30s and 40s”; educational background, “bachelor’s degree” and “vocational school diploma”; ward affiliation, “ICU” and “surgical ward”; years of experience of the clinical educator in charge, “3–5 years” and “>6 years”; number of discussions with clinical educator and with the team, “0–3 times” and “>4 times”; type of people who join the clinical educator in his or her daily work and study sessions, “middle management” and “ward nurses”; and existence of associates, “exists” and “does not exist”.

Subsequently, we compared the differences between the qualifications and nursing competencies of the clinical educators serving in ICU and surgical wards using an unpaired t-test. Moreover, a multiple regression analysis (stepwise method) was performed by transforming the nominal variables into dummy variables, with each of the four factors of the CNCSS serving as the dependent variable, and the other four factors of the basic background and clinical educator quality scale serving as independent variables. Statistically significance level was set at 5%. The absence of multicollinearity of the independent variables in the multiple regression analysis was confirmed using variance inflation factor values. SPSS version 28 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA) was used for all the analyses.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was granted approval by the Ethics Committee of Akita University Graduate School of Medicine (Approval No. 2739). The developers of the scales used in the survey granted the tool use. The purpose, methods, and safety of the survey information were explained in writing to the directors of the participating nursing departments. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Results

Background of the participants

Table 1 presents the participants’ backgrounds. Of the 624 new graduate nurses at 54 facilities, 125 responded to the survey, with a response rate of 20%. Overall, 109 respondents (87.2%) were females. The majority of participants (90.4%) were in their 20s, while eight (6.4%) in their 30s and four (3.2%) in their 40s. Regarding educational attainment, 61 participants (48.8%) had a bachelor’s degree and 64 participants (51.2%) had a vocational school diploma. 57 participants (45.6%) were stationed in the ICU and 68 (54.4%) were in the surgical ward. Regarding the career of clinical educators, 89 participants (71.0%) had a work experience of 3–5 years, and 36 participants (29.0%) had >6 years. Seventy-one participants (56.8%) had 0–3 meetings per week with their clinical educators and 54 participants (43.2%) had >4 meetings with their clinical educators. Discussion among clinical educators, new nurse graduates, and educational manager was conducted to track education progress. 88 participants (70.4%) had an educational manager, whereas 37 participants (29.6%) did not. Discussion frequency with educational manager was 0–3 meetings per week for 93 participants (74.4%), and >4 times for 32 participants (25.6%). Daily study sessions were joined by 7 middle managers (5.5%) and 118 ward nurses (94.4%).

Comparison of clinical educator qualities

Cronbach’s α for the Clinical Educator Quality Scale was 0.8. The Clinical Educator Quality Scale was used to score the clinical educator qualities of new graduate nurses in the ICU and surgical wards using a 5-point Likert scale and the results of the two groups were compared using unpaired t-tests. The mean score ± standard deviation (SD) for overall support was 53.6 ± 9.0 for the ICU and 55.6 ± 12.0 for the surgical ward (P-value=0.33); for leadership 25.5 ± 3.6 for the ICU and 26.5 ± 5.4 for the surgical ward (P-value=0.23) for job support 16.9 ± 2.3 for the ICU and 17.4 ± 3.7 for the surgical ward (P-value=0.35), and for attitude toward new graduate nurses 7.7 ± 2.2 in the ICU and 8.5 ± 2.3 in the surgical ward (P-value=0.07). No significant differences were found in any of the items (Table 2).

Comparison of nursing practice competencies

Cronbach’s α for the CNCSS was 0.8. The CNCSS was used to score the clinical nursing competencies of new graduate nurses in ICU and surgical wards on a 5-point Likert scale. The results of the two groups were compared using unpaired t-tests. The mean score ± SD for each factor “competence in basic nursing practice” was 63.8 ± 7.5 for the ICU and 64.1 ± 11.9 for the surgical ward (P-value=0.89) for “competence in developing nursing care” 96.2 ± 16.4 for the ICU and 99.2 ± 21.0 for the surgical ward (P-value=0.39), for “competence in coordinating the care environment and team system” 40.5 ± 7.6 for the ICU and 42.9 ± 6.9 for the surgical ward (P=0.06), and for “competence in studying in nursing practice” 37.6 ± 7.4 for the ICU and 40.1 ± 8.2 for the surgical ward (P=0.08). No significant differences were observed between the items (Table 3).

Effects on clinical nursing competencies

Concerning the impact on practical nursing ability in the ICU, multiple regression analysis (stepwise method) was conducted using the CNCSS as the dependent variable and the Clinical Educators Qualifications Scale and Basic Background as the independent variable. The results showed a multiple correlation coefficient R of 0.306, with an adjusted coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.076 for “competence in basic nursing practice”. The standard partial regression coefficient (β) of attitudes toward new graduate nurses was 0.306 (P<0.05). “Competence in developing nursing care” did not have any significant predictor. The “competence in coordinating the care environment and team system” had an R value of 0.292 an R2 value of 0.068, and had “the attitude toward new graduate nurses” as a significant predictor (β=0.292, P<0.05). The “competence in studying in nursing practice” had an R value of 0.277, an R2 value of 0.059, and “attitude toward new graduate nurses” as a significant predictor (β=0.277, P<0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the impact of clinical educators in the clinical nursing competencies of new graduate nurses in ICU. No difference in the clinical educator qualities—evaluated using the four items of the clinical educator’s qualifications scale (overall support, leadership, support for duties, and attitude toward new graduate nurses)—between the ICUs and surgical wards was found. O’Shea et al. reported that new graduate nurses perceive a deficit in clinical skills upon qualification. Similarly, Patterson et al., reported that broad skills are required for special areas and rapid pace may be overwhelming for new graduate nurses. Although conducted outside critical care, these studies suggest that competencies required in the ICU and wards differ, necessitating context-based education8, 9). Under these circumstances, the roles and abilities that new graduate nurses require from clinical educators may differ.

In the CNCSS, the ICU and surgical ward were compared in terms of four competencies: basic nursing practice, developing nursing care, coordinating the care environment and team system, and studying nursing practice. No significant differences were observed in the four competencies of the CNCSS between the ICU and the surgical wards. Similarly, no significant differences in the qualities of clinical educators between ICU and surgical ward. These findings suggest that the students were able to acquire the necessary clinical nursing competencies after graduation from the educational institution, even though the place and environment for nursing practice differed according to each department.

Regarding the impact on clinical nursing competencies in ICU and surgical wards, only attitudes toward new graduate nurses had a significant effect on clinical nursing competencies. Emotional support and social talk, which facilitate workplace relationships, have a positive impact on the commitment of novice nurses commitment and their willingness to stay in their role10). Based on the results of the present and previous studies, psychological support from clinical educators contributes to the improvement of new graduate nurses’ abilities to practice nursing in ICU. For new graduate nurses to adapt to ICU environment and improve their nursing competencies, supportive and positive attitudes, such as feedback and recognition of their efforts and growth, from clinical educators are essential. While teaching daily tasks and providing medical and nursing knowledge are the main tasks of clinical educators, creating a stable and comfortable environment for new graduate nurses feel mentally stable and comfortable is also important. Since attitudes toward new graduate nurses were the sole factor of clinical educators that affected nursing competencies, this suggests that new graduate nurses in ICU mainly sought psychological support from their clinical educators.

Competence in developing nursing care in ICU did not have any predictors. Regarding the influence of senior nurses, including clinical educators, on new graduate nurses, support from peer nurses was associated with nearly all items on the CNCSS, whereas support from senior nurses was limited to ethical practice, risk management, and continuous learning11). This suggests peer support had a stronger influence on the clinical competence of new graduate nurses. Previous research highlights the value of managerial and senior support12). Therefore, an environment where new graduate nurses can interact and mutually support their peers—improving their clinical nursing competence and capacity to provide assistance—is essential.

The results showed that the clinical educator’s attitude toward new graduate nurses influenced the improvement of clinical nursing competencies in the ICU, and that male sex, availability to follow up with ward nurses, and the clinical educator’s leadership qualities and assistance with duties are major predictors for increased nursing competencies in the surgical ward. These results suggest that new graduate nurses in ICU and surgical wards have different needs and clinical educators should focus on meeting those needs to improve clinical nursing competencies of new graduate nurses in these two departments. In most hospitals in Japan, educational content for new graduate nurses is standardized, regardless of the ward where they work at. Therefore, when clinical educators provide on-the-job training, they should tailor their training material to cater to the needs of new graduate nurses in different ward, thereby improving their clinical nursing competencies.

In many respects, the transition of new graduate nurses to critical care is fundamentally different from the transition to general nursing practice13). New graduate nurses go through the phases of doing, knowing, and being iteratively, transitioning from a task-oriented practitioner to someone with a solidified professional identity and able to provide whole-person care. The setting causes high moral distress due to the unpredictable nature of their work, even for experienced nurses14). For new graduate nurses, these challenges are compounded by complex ethical issues for which they are not yet prepared, such as medical futility, withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment, and medical aid in dying, although they are expected to manage all these issues15, 16). In high-acuity settings such as the ICU, securing the necessary knowledge and skills without the support of clinical educators and other staff can be challenging. The literature indicates that new graduate nurses struggle with communication skills, conflict resolution skills, organization, prioritization and time management, critical thinking skills, clinical decision-making, and stress management17, 18).

Omer et al. indicated that new graduates should serve as models of clinical judgment and support their peers when encountering difficulties in nursing practice19). Clinical educators are the main sources of support for new graduate nurses in ICUs. When providing support to new graduate nurses in ICU, clinical educators need to help them become accustomed to the new environment while providing a rationale for nursing practice. Stewart highlighted that a system, in which clinical educators and others provide social support for new ICU nurses, is essential to stimulate learning of new graduate nurses20). Therefore, not only clinical educators but also other senior nurses and managers need to work together to provide emotional support that positively influence the ability of new ICU nurses.

AI-Adawi et al. identified five competencies required for competent clinical educators: 1) have ability to teach professional communication skills, 2) have appropriate teaching strategies, 3) have time management skills, 4) can create a learning atmosphere, and 5) have teaching critical thinking skills. In addition, they indicated that improvement and the use of problem-solving skills of new graduate nurses are important for their development21). Therefore, new graduate nurses must improve their communication skills, ethical practice, time management, and other skills apart from performing their usual duties and acquiring medicine and nursing knowledge. Clinical educators need not only to teach them their duties and knowledge but also to create an environment where they can easily support and communicate with new graduate nurses to ensure that they can solve problems through their own trial-and-error experiences.

Limitations of this study and future issues

This study included new graduate nurses working in ICUs and surgical wards (e.g., neurosurgery, cardiovascular surgery, gastroenterology, respiratory surgery, and orthopedics) across 300 ICUs, excluding pediatrics and gynecology, who were accredited by the Japanese Society of Intensive Care Medicine. Fifty-four (18%) facilities participated in the survey, comprising 624 new graduate nurses. Of these, 125 nurses responded, setting the response rate only at 20% thus, limiting generalizability of the results nationwide. This may explain why R2 for “attitude toward new graduate nurses”, an item that influences the ability of new graduate nurses to practice nursing in the ICU, was low. Data collection occurred during the COVID-19, which likely may have influenced the response rates, particularly in ICUs and surgical wards that were burdened by infection control demands. As this survey was conducted only with new graduate nurses, their clinical educator qualities may be higher if their clinical nursing competence is higher. Therefore, a nationwide survey of new graduate nurses and their clinical educators may reveal more accurate influencing factors. Given the demographic transition in Japan by 2040, advancing nursing education is indispensable. Therefore, it is necessary to contribute to the medical field by clarifying the mechanisms that improve the clinical nursing competencies of new graduate nurses.

Conclusion

No significant difference in the clinical educators’ quality scale and CNCSS was observed between ICU and surgical ward. The only factor influencing the practical nursing ability of new graduate nurses in the ICU was “attitude toward new graduate nurses”, and no factor influenced the “competence in developing nursing care” was found. When clinical nursing competencies between ICU and surgical ward was compared, no differences in the factors affecting the clinical nursing competencies of new nurse graduate nurses were found. This finding underscores the importance of tailoring teaching strategies to improve the clinical nursing competencies of new graduates based on the department to which they are assigned. Emotional support provided by clinical educators, senior nurses, and managers help new ICU nurses improve their practical nursing abilities.
