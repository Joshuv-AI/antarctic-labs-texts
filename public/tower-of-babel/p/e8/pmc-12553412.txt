Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing in Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy: Refining a Proven Tool for Better Risk Prognostication
Authors: Onyedika J Ilonze, Sula Mazimba
Published: 2025

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a common heritable cardiac condition, characterized by variable clinical expression/trajectory with a manifold constellation of clinical symptoms. The clinical profile of HCM ranges from an asymptomatic phenotype to debilitating functional intolerance, overt heart failure, stroke, intractable arrythmias, and in the worst clinical scenario, sudden cardiac death. Although many patients with HCM are asymptomatic and attain relatively normal life expectancy, 30% to 40% of patients exhibit symptoms. 1 , 2 The pathophysiological mechanisms undergirding the wide spectrum of HCM symptoms is complex 3 (Figure), posing challenges in risk stratification and prognostic ascertainment. In this sense, cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), a valuable noninvasive diagnostic modality, provides an integrated assessment of cardiac, pulmonary, hemodynamic, musculoskeletal, and cellular oxidative pathways. CPET has traditionally been used to enhance risk stratification and prognostication in patients with HF but is increasingly being used and has an expanding role in HCM with endorsements by both the American and European consensus guidelines for the management of HCM. 4 , 5 The most commonly used CPET metrics include peak O2 uptake (pVO2), minute ventilation (VE) to CO2 production (VCO2) (VE/VCO2) slope, and abnormal blood pressure response to exercise.

Landsteiner and colleagues must therefore be congratulated for their well‐done systematic review and meta‐analysis in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA). They explore the usefulness of CPET variables in the risk assessment and prognostic ascertainment of adverse outcomes in HCM. 6 The meta‐analysis included a systematic search of eligible studies of CPET and HCM in databases through 2023 comprising 60 full‐length articles and 12 914 patients (predominantly men; mean age, 48–50 years). The authors then assessed the primary end point of mortality, defined as all‐cause mortality or sudden cardiac death, and the secondary end point that was a composite of cardiovascular‐related adverse events. These composite outcomes included combinations of sudden cardiac death, heart failure hospitalization, appropriate implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator therapy, stroke, and septal reduction therapy. Univariable Cox proportional hazards models were then used to assess the prognostic value of CPET‐derived parameters for the primary or secondary outcomes. Definitions and characteristics of commonly used CPET parameters are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The hazard ratios (HRs) and pooled effect estimates were analyzed using fixed‐effects modeling.

The authors reported that pVO2, a metric that is highly dependent on cardiac output and arteriovenous O2 content difference, was the most measured CPET variable. There are multiple predictive equations that are used to derive pVO2 (Wasserman/Hansen, Wasserman‐ideal body weight (IBW), Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database (FRIEND), and Neder) which may translate to significant differences in the measured pVO2. 7 This fact alone underscores the importance of emphasizing future efforts to standardize CPET protocols to enable interstudy comparisons for research purposes. Even so, pVO2 was lower in the HCM cohort versus control subjects (22.5 versus 27.6–37.7 mL/kg per minute), with a further observable gradient between obstructive versus nonobstructive HCM (19.5 [95% CI, 19.1–19.8] mL/kg per minute versus 22.4 [95% CI, 21.5–23.2] mL/kg per minute). A low pVO2 was associated with adverse events; HR per 1 mL/kg per minute difference in peak VO2 was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.82–0.90; P<0.001). These findings further reinforce the relevance of pVO2, even among patients with asymptomatic HCM in whom functional capacity may be overestimated by an over reliance on the New York Heart Association function classification system that has wide adoption in routine clinical practice and is highly subjective. 8 Prior studies have demonstrated poor correlation of New York Heart Association class (especially lower classes) with pVO2. 9 A mean pVO2 of 22.5 mL/kg per minute, as noted in the overall HCM cohort, represented a reduction in exercise capacity of at least 20% from what would be expected in the general population with similar age. 7 , 10 The relationship of other CPET parameters (VE/VCO2) and abnormal blood pressure response to exercise, defined as failure to increase systolic blood pressure by ≥20 mm Hg or a drop in systolic blood pressure >20 mm Hg, were similarly predictive of mortality (HR for mortality per 1‐unit difference with HR for VE/VCO2 slope was 1.09 [95% CI, 1.05–1.13]; P<0.001); and HR for abnormal blood pressure response to exercise was 2.29 [95% CI: 1.13–4.64]; P=0.02). VE/VCO2 as a CPET metric, is associated with increased exercise‐induced pulmonary ventilation/perfusion mismatch, 11 left ventricular diastolic efficiency/filling pressures, and pulmonary hypertension, 12 which are all highly correlated with an advanced disease state. 13 Although abnormal blood pressure response to exercise is associated with sudden cardiac death, especially in the younger patients with HCM (aged 18–40 years), its usefulness is limited due to heterogeneities in exercise protocols along with challenges in its reproducibility. 14

Beyond the use of CPET as a risk stratification and prognostication tool in HCM, other nuanced and emerging applications are highlighted (Table 3). Cardiac myosin inhibitors (the newest therapeutic class for obstructive HCM) have shown demonstrable improvements in CPET parameters, namely an increase in pVO2 among patients with obstructive HCM from randomized controlled trials. 15 The association between β‐blocker use and pVO2 has however shown mixed results. 16 , 17 Invasive therapies for obstructive HCM, both septal myectomy 18 , 19 and septal ablation, 19 , 20 were associated with an increase in pVO2, though the evidence is limited to observational studies. This meta‐analysis supports existing data that CPET variables may record objective data on exercise intolerance that may better predict prognosis in patients with HCM.

Limitations of the meta‐analysis by Landsteiner and colleagues include (1) potential publication bias of studies with positive outcomes, (2) outcome reporting of composite end points rather than individual patient‐level data (clinicians are often interested in the prediction of discrete patient risk profiles, such as arrythmia and sudden cardiac death with the end goal of mitigating these risks as well as informing shared decision making), and (3) understandably (due to recency of data), the study does not include emerging CPET parameters such as pVO2 recovery kinetics, which when prolonged would portend worse prognosis. 21 For example, VO2 recovery delay, defined as time until postexercise VO2 falls permanently below peak VO2 signals impaired CO augmentation during exercise and predicts outcomes in HF and HCM. Furthermore, the emerging role of CPET imaging incorporating essential structural data on diastology, left ventricular outflow dynamics, mitral regurgitation, right ventricular reserve, and pulmonary hypertension during CPET, was not included in this meta‐analysis. CPET imaging has the potential for providing a wholistic integration of the systemic, hemodynamic, pulmonary, valvular and dynamic physiological data with the traditional exercise parameters.

In summary, this meta‐analysis reinforces the centrality of CPET as an objective and reliable but underutilized tool for assessing (1) exercise capacity, (2) risk stratification and prognosis, and (3) adjudication of the effectiveness of therapeutic/interventional modalities in HCM. Future studies of CPET in HCM may benefit from an integration of CPET with invasive hemodynamics, cardiovascular imaging, and harmonization of outcome definitions to enhance comparability, support objective clinical interpretation and incorporate longer‐term data of follow‐up. Additionally, the adoption of CPET variables as end points in future HCM clinical trials would bolster robust outcome adjudication as well as cement the primacy of CPET metrics in HCM prognostication in routine clinical practice.
