Comparative Analysis of Isavuconazole DDIs With Other Azole Antifungal Drugs and PBPK Model‐Informed Dosing Recommendations for Anticancer Drugs
Authors: Theunis C. Goosen, Xiaofeng Wu, Jian Lin, Narayan Cheruvu, Susan R. Raber, Maria Lavinea Novis de Figueiredo, Manthena V. S. Varma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIsavuconazole is a broad‐spectrum triazole approved for the treatment of invasive aspergillosis or mucormycosis in adults and children aged ≥ 1 year. Current prescribing information lacks guidance regarding the co‐administration of isavuconazole with anticancer drugs–limited by the availability of clinical drug–drug interaction (DDI) data in the patient population. This study utilized physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling to evaluate the DDI risk of isavuconazole compared with other azoles and provide dosing recommendations when co‐administered with anticancer drugs (ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin). PBPK models were developed in the Simcyp simulator using physiochemical properties, in vitro, and clinical pharmacokinetic data. The model well‐predicted isavuconazole pharmacokinetic changes with cytochrome‐P450 3A (CYP3A) modulators (itraconazole and rifampicin), and recovered midazolam DDI with isavuconazole as a CYP3A inhibitor. PBPK models for ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin were developed and validated by comparing simulated and observed pharmacokinetic parameters with and without the CYP3A inhibitor, ketoconazole. The PBPK model predicted area under the plasma concentration–time curve ratios of 2.1, 1.1, and 2.1 for ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin, respectively, when co‐administered with isavuconazole at clinically relevant doses. The findings suggest that isavuconazole can be safely co‐administered following appropriate dose adjustments with ibrutinib (50% of normal dose), venetoclax (50–100% of normal dose), or midostaurin (50% of normal dose). Other azoles, posaconazole and voriconazole, showed larger CYP3A‐mediated DDIs and consequently require 3–6‐fold lower doses of the substrate drugs. In conclusion, this model‐informed PK‐based dose optimization can enable treatment management in these untested scenarios.

Introduction

Isavuconazole, a broad‐spectrum triazole, was first approved by the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2015 [1]. In the US, isavuconazole is approved for the treatment of invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis in patients aged ≥ 1 year (injection) and in those aged ≥ 6 years weighing at least 16 kg (capsules) [2]. In European Union, isavuconazole is approved in patients aged ≥ 1 year for invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis for whom amphotericin B is inappropriate [3]. [Correction added on 06 December 2025, after first online publication: The subsequent paragraph has been updated in this version.] Pediatric approval was granted by the FDA in December 2023 and the EMA in August 2024. Similar to other triazoles, isavuconazole works by inhibiting cytochrome P450 (CYP)‐dependent lanosterol 14α‐demethylase; this disruption leads to fungal cell death [4]. The unique structure of isavuconazole (Figure S1), which includes an N‐(3‐acetoxypropyl)‐N‐methylamino‐carboxymethyl group side arm that allows the molecule to engage with the fungal lanosterol 14α‐demethylase protein (CYP51), provides a broader antifungal activity compared with other azoles [5].

Isavuconazole is dosed in the form of the water‐soluble prodrug isavuconazonium sulfate [6]. In healthy individuals, after oral intake, peak plasma concentrations of isavuconazole are typically achieved within 2–3 h [2]. The oral bioavailability of isavuconazole is 98% and its absorption is not influenced by food consumption [1]. The metabolism of isavuconazole involves CYP enzymes, specifically CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 [2], and thus it should not be co‐administered with drugs that are strong inhibitors or inducers of CYP3A4 [2]. Ketoconazole and ritonavir, strong inhibitors of CYP3A4, have been shown to significantly elevate isavuconazole levels, while rifampin, a strong CYP3A4 inducer, reduces its exposure. Moreover, isavuconazole moderately inhibits CYP3A4, and its simultaneous use with CYP3A4 substrates such as midazolam, tacrolimus, sirolimus, or cyclosporine can increase their systemic exposure [7, 8]. There is a lack of drug–drug interaction (DDI) data for newer targeted therapies for hematologic malignancies, where antifungal prophylaxis and treatment are frequently required.

The utilization of physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling in drug development is increasing, with application in dosage information in drug labels [9]. In 2019, over 40% of drugs for humans approved by the FDA utilized PBPK modeling [10], and PBPK modeling is now a standard component in new chemical entity submissions to regulators. PBPK models may be used in lieu of some clinical DDI studies, especially in cases involving metabolism by CYP enzymes [11].

In this study, PBPK modeling was employed to quantitate isavuconazole DDIs and thus offer dosing recommendations in untested clinical scenarios. The objectives of this study were (1) to describe the clinical DDI risk of isavuconazole in comparison to other azole antifungal agents, specifically posaconazole and voriconazole, and (2) to evaluate the clinical risk when isavuconazole was co‐administered with the anti‐cancer agents ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin, in order to provide dosing recommendations. These objectives were designed to enhance the understanding of isavuconazole interactions with other drugs and to guide safe and effective dosing practices.

Methods

Modeling and Simulation Strategy

All modeling and simulations were conducted using Simcyp PBPK Simulator Version 22, release 1 (hereafter “Simcyp”; Certara, Radnor, PA, USA). The simulations were performed in a virtual population library of healthy volunteers within Simcyp, using a design of 10 trials with 10 individuals aged between 20 and 50 years, maintaining a 1:1 male to female ratio. The PBPK models for inhibitors and substrates were developed using measured physiochemical properties, along with existing pharmacokinetic and in vitro data. These models were then verified using observed outcomes from studies involving midazolam and ketoconazole. The verified models were utilized to simulate the DDI after the co‐administration of each inhibitor (isavuconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole) with each substrate (ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin).

Model Development and Verification

A PBPK model was developed for isavuconazole, based on derived physiochemical and pharmacokinetic data. Isavuconazole is orally dosed in the form of its prodrug, isavuconazonium sulfate. However, for the PBPK model, the prodrug was not considered as it was found to be rapidly converted in the human plasma or presystemically and therefore its systemic exposure is negligible [12]. Due to its rapid conversion, the prodrug contribution to DDIs was assumed to be small, if any. The final parameters for isavuconazole (active) are presented in Table S1. An ADAM model was adopted to capture the absorption and intestinal disposition of isavuconazole. CYP3A‐based metabolism is the primary clearance pathway of isavuconazole and is accounted for in the enzyme kinetics. Isavuconazole as a substrate drug was verified following simulations with CYP3A4 modulators including ketoconazole (inhibitor) and rifampicin (inducer; Figure S2). PBPK models of ketoconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole, and rifampicin were adopted from default files with minimal changes (Table S3). The models' accuracy of inhibitor drugs was confirmed by comparing simulated pharmacokinetic outcomes, both with and without midazolam co‐administration, to the existing clinical data. PBPK models were also constructed for each substrate (ibrutinib, venetoclax, midostaurin, and midazolam), and the final parameters are presented in Table S4. Best practices were followed to develop/verify the models in healthy populations for each substrate drug [13, 14]. All substrate models were verified in two steps before simulating DDI with isavuconazole. Firstly, model‐predicted plasma exposures including C max and AUClast in healthy or control volunteers were compared with observed values. The substrate models were considered acceptable if the simulated data were within 2‐fold of observed values in the healthy population. Secondly, DDI with ketoconazole was predicted to ascertain model recovery of the quantitative contribution of CYP3A‐mediated clearance in the healthy population. The model acceptance criterion was initially set based on AUCR prediction within 0.80‐ to 1.25‐fold of the observed values.

Model Application

The simulations involved the co‐administration of isavuconazole at steady‐state (200 mg QD for 10 days) with a single dosage of ibrutinib (420 mg), venetoclax (400 mg), or midostaurin (50 mg) administered on Day 7. Similarly, the co‐administration of posaconazole (300 mg QD for 10 days) and voriconazole (200 mg BID for 10 days), both at steady‐state, with the aforementioned substrates was also simulated. AUClast and C max were compared with the observed data. The fold‐increase in the AUCinf ratio and AUCR was determined from the ratios of simulated values in the treatment groups relative to the control groups.

Results

DDI Potential of Isavuconazole in Comparison to Other Azoles

To understand the potential for DDIs among azoles, we extracted clinical data on the interactions between isavuconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole, ketoconazole, and itraconazole with various substrate drugs using the Drug Interaction Database (DIDB) (Certara, Radnor, PA, USA) (Figure 1). Generally, isavuconazole produced a limited change in the pharmacokinetics of these substrate drugs compared with other azoles. For example, the area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to the time of the last measurable concentration (AUClast) change for the CYP3A probe drug midazolam was only about 2‐fold, whereas CYP3A index inhibitors (ketoconazole and itraconazole) could produce a > 10‐fold change. Posaconazole and voriconazole also perpetrated an approximately 5.5–9‐fold effect on midazolam pharmacokinetics. Similar observations were noted with other CYP3A probe substrates, wherein isavuconazole produced relatively smaller interactions with tacrolimus (AUClast ratio [AUCR]: 2.25) and sirolimus (AUCR: 1.84). The DDIs of posaconazole and voriconazole on tacrolimus were somewhat similar to those of ketoconazole and itraconazole on tacrolimus, indicating strong CYP3A inhibition. Isavuconazole also showed limited effects on CYP2D6 (dextromethorphan, AUCR: 1.19), CYP2C9 ([R]‐warfarin, AUCR: 1.20), CYP2C19 (omeprazole, AUCR: 0.90), CYP1A2 (caffeine, AUCR: 1.06), and drug transporters including organic anion transporter polypeptide 1B (atorvastatin, AUCR: 1.40; repaglinide, AUCR: 0.92), P‐glycoprotein (digoxin, AUCR: 1.25; apixaban, AUCR: 1.33), and organic anion transporters (OATS; methotrexate, AUCR: 0.97).

DDI Predictions With Isavuconazole, Posaconazole, and Voriconazole

The PBPK model predicted AUCR values of 2.1, 1.1, and 2.1 for ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin, respectively, when co‐administered with isavuconazole at steady‐state (Table 3). These findings suggest that a 50% dose reduction of ibrutinib would be necessary when isavuconazole and ibrutinib are co‐administered. In addition, based on the PBPK simulations, a 50% dose reduction of midostaurin would be needed when co‐administered with isavuconazole, and 50%–100% of the normal dose is recommended for the co‐administration of isavuconazole and venetoclax (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study aimed to quantitate the CYP3A‐based DDI risk of isavuconazole and compare it with other azole antifungals. We initially analyzed available clinical data on the interactions between isavuconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole, ketoconazole, and itraconazole with various drugs. The findings suggest that isavuconazole has a relatively lower impact on the pharmacokinetics of CYP3A substrate drugs compared with other azoles. Additionally, the effect of isavuconazole on other enzyme and transporter mechanisms such as CYP2C19 (omeprazole) and P‐glycoprotein (edoxaban) is relatively small. To further quantitate the CYP3A‐based DDIs, PBPK modeling and simulations were employed. The isavuconazole model, accounting for oral absorption via the ADAM model, and elimination primarily via metabolism, accurately described the plasma exposure data after multiple doses relevant to the therapeutic dosage regimen. Moreover, considering measured CYP3A inhibition potency, the model well‐recovered its DDI with midazolam, a CYP3A‐sensitive probe. PBPK simulations using verified models demonstrated a weak‐to‐moderate CYP3A inhibition potency of isavuconazole and a lower DDI risk with substrate drugs, relative to posaconazole and voriconazole. Finally, PBPK modeling was employed to offer dosing recommendations for isavuconazole in patients receiving the anticancer agents ibrutinib, midostaurin, and venetoclax.

Isavuconazole inhibits several CYPs in vitro; however, its potency against CYP3A is > 4‐fold higher than for other CYPs. This is reflected in positive DDIs with CYP3A substrates like tacrolimus and midazolam [7, 8], but no measurable effect on probes of other enzymes including CYP2C9 (S‐warfarin), CYP2C19 (omeprazole), CYP1A2 (caffeine), and CYP2D6 (dextromethorphan) [1, 21, 22]. Therefore, we focused on the quantitative assessment of CYP3A‐based interactions via mechanistic modeling. A PBPK model accounting for the hepatic and intestinal CYP3A components of midazolam and CYP3A reversible inhibition by isavuconazole well recovered the observed moderate DDI. A DDI matrix approach was adopted to verify substrate and inhibitor models for the intended use [13]. The substrate models were verified to recover CYP3A‐based interactions with ketoconazole, an index inhibitor. The previously verified ketoconazole model well recovered pharmacokinetic changes of ibrutinib, midostaurin, and venetoclax. Similarly, models of azole antifungals including isavuconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole well described midazolam pharmacokinetic changes associated with CYP3A inhibition. The DDI matrix thus offered qualification for subsequent simulation of clinically untested DDIs.

The three targeted oncology agents indicated for hematologic malignancies assessed here, ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin, are primarily cleared by CYP3A, with gut extraction and hepatic metabolism contributing, in varying degrees, to their pharmacokinetics [23, 24, 25]. Consistently, the CYP3A index inhibitors ketoconazole and itraconazole perpetuate strong DDIs (AUCR ~6 to 24‐fold) [19, 26, 27, 28, 29]. PBPK models account for the large CYP3A phenotype (high fmCYP3A) and are able to adequately predict DDIs between ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin and the index inhibitors, ketoconazole and itraconazole [29, 30, 31]. All azole antifungals evaluated here are known to be reversible inhibitors of CYP3A, with little to no time‐dependent inhibition or CYP induction reported, suggesting similar interaction mechanisms across the inhibitors [32, 33]. The varying magnitude of DDIs between isavuconazole and other azoles is primarily driven by in vivo inhibition potency, which is a function of the inhibitory constant (K i) and unbound concentrations at the interaction sites (intestine and liver). The study results confirm that dosage modifications are necessary when ibrutinib is co‐administered with isavuconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole due to their inhibitory effects on CYP3A. For patients with B‐cell malignancies, when ibrutinib is co‐administered with voriconazole (200 mg twice daily [BID]) or posaconazole (100 mg QD suspension, 100 mg QD, 100 mg BID, or 200 mg BID), the recommended ibrutinib dosage is 140 mg QD [26, 34], which is similar to the dose modification we found with isavuconazole (50% of dose, 280 mg QD). However, if posaconazole is administered at higher doses (200 mg three times daily suspension, 400 mg BID, 300 mg intravenously QD, or modified‐release tablets 300 mg QD), the recommended ibrutinib dosage is reduced to 70 mg QD [26].

Similarly, dosage modifications for venetoclax are necessary when co‐administered with a strong or moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Concomitant use with these inhibitors increases venetoclax C max and AUCinf, which may increase venetoclax toxicities, including the risk of tumor lysis syndrome [35]. In patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia or small lymphocytic lymphoma, concomitant use of venetoclax with a strong CYP3A inhibitor at initiation and during the ramp‐up phase is contraindicated [35]. After discontinuation of the inhibitor, the venetoclax dosage that was used prior to concomitant use with a strong or moderate CYP3A inhibitor can be resumed 2–3 days later. Given the weak interaction potential of isavuconazole, any dose reduction should be approached with caution, and a 50%–100% venetoclax dose may be considered when co‐administered. The DDIs that affect midostaurin are primarily related to its metabolism by CYP3A [28]. Co‐administration of midostaurin with strong CYP3A inhibitors may increase midostaurin concentrations, potentially leading to increased toxicity. This effect may be particularly pronounced if strong CYP3A inhibitors are administered during the first week of midostaurin administration [28]. To manage this interaction, the FDA recommends considering alternative therapies that do not strongly inhibit CYP3A activity. Based on our analysis, a two‐fold lower dose of midostaurin may be tolerated when co‐administered with isavuconazole as an alternative to other azoles.

Finally, there are some limitations to the work presented here. The PBPK model simulations reflect a healthy population and may not represent the clinical impact of the DDI between isavuconazole and co‐administered therapies in cancer populations. For example, the observed midostaurin AUC0‐120h ratio following ketoconazole co‐administration in healthy subjects was 6.1, or extrapolated to infinity, a 10.4‐fold increase in midostraurin exposure [20]. However, co‐administration of another strong CYP3A inhibitor, itraconazole, in patients with acute myeloid leukemia resulted in a significantly lower DDI (AUCR = 1.6) while the current product label still suggests using alternatives when co‐administered with strong CYP3A inhibitors [28]. In the case of the itraconazole DDI, the midostaurin exposure increase may have been underestimated since exposure in the absence of itraconazole was not determined at steady state but was measured a week earlier when the analyte concentrations are expected to be higher than at steady state. The ibrutinib‐ketoconazole DDI (AUCR 23.9) was studied in healthy volunteers [18] while the ibrutinib‐posaconazole DDI (AUCR 9.4–10.3) in healthy volunteers [36] or ibrutinib‐voriconazole DDI (AUCR 5.7) in patients with B‐cell malignancies [37] are in good agreement with the healthy subject simulations presented in this study. Venetoclax interactions with ketoconazole (AUCR 6.4 or 7.9) were from patients with non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma [19] while posaconazole DDI (AUCR 7.9–9.7) were studied in patients with acute myeloid leukemia [38]. Therefore, cancer patient‐specific differences would require continued adverse effect monitoring based on the severity of disease, risk–benefit analysis with the addition of antifungal treatment, or confirmation of DDI effect size in clinical studies.

In summary, our study provides a comprehensive understanding of the DDI risk of isavuconazole in comparison with other azole antifungal agents, posaconazole and voriconazole. The results indicate that isavuconazole has a minimal impact on the pharmacokinetics of co‐administered drugs compared with other azoles, suggesting isavuconazole is a safer option than other azoles when considering potential DDIs. This study projects that isavuconazole may have a relatively lower impact on the pharmacokinetics of anticancer agents, including ibrutinib, midostaurin, and venetoclax. The PBPK models developed in this study accurately represented the plasma exposure data for isavuconazole and other azoles, providing a reliable tool for predicting potential DDIs. These models can be used to guide safe and effective dosing practices when co‐administering these drugs. Overall, this study enhances our understanding of isavuconazole's interaction with other drugs and provides valuable insights to help clinicians make informed decisions regarding the use of isavuconazole in patients receiving anticancer drugs such as ibrutinib, venetoclax, and midostaurin. Future research should focus on validating these findings in clinical settings and exploring strategies to mitigate the DDI risk associated with the use of these drugs.
