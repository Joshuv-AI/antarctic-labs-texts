Experiments on flow over obstacle arrays: a database for turbulent flow and wave transformation modeling
Authors: Francisco Nicolás Cantero-Chinchilla, Oscar Castro-Orgaz, Sk Zeeshan Ali, Subhasish Dey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This data descriptor presents a detailed dataset of open-channel flow interacting with arrays of obstacles, originating from a laboratory experimental campaign involving Gaussian- and log-normal-shaped obstacles arranged sequentially in a flume, as well as a solitary dune profile. Six different array configurations were tested. Both steady and unsteady flow conditions were implemented resulting in a wide range of experiments. Data on free surface flow level, flume bed level, and head pressure data were systematically extracted using a methodology specifically designed for this purpose. The dataset is supplemented with schematic representations of the data and panoramas images of the experiments, including characteristics of turbulence and wave transformation phenomena. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first database in the literature to comprehensively examine the interaction of flow with various obstacle arrays under both steady and unsteady flow conditions. Combined with the detailed description of the employed methodology, this data descriptor is expected to promote significant usage of the dataset, benefiting the open-channel flow modeling community.

Background & Summary

Curved flows in open channels are inherently present in flow-control hydraulic structures and during sudden transient flow scenarios, under both steady and unsteady flow conditions. These flows often develop in conjunction with other hydraulic phenomena, such as turbulence, which further complicates their study1. The link between the occurrence of flows over uneven beds and extreme floods, largely attributed to global climate change, is evident. As a result, the severity and frequency of curved flows are being exacerbated by the effects of global climate change2,3. Extreme weather events, such as intense rainfall, are occurring more frequently worldwide. These events lead to the rapid generation of sharp hydrographs in river basins, characterized by short times to peak discharge, which significantly affect river discharge regimes. Under unsteady conditions, water waves can be generated that travel in both upstream and downstream directions, posing a high risk of damage to hydraulic structures, property, and human life4. Therefore, for the effective management of watersheds, hydraulic structures, and open channel systems, the accurate modeling of curved flows, under both steady and unsteady flow conditions, including wave transformation, is essential. To achieve this, experimental databases capturing steady and unsteady curved flows over various hydraulic structures involving uneven beds are necessary. These databases should include detailed characterizations of water wave transformations. In hydraulic engineering, the development of experimental databases is a standard practice for a wide range of applications5–10.

The study and simulation of flows over uneven beds in open channels are integral components of hydro-environmental studies in river basins or watersheds, where analyzing fluvial dynamics in a specific river reach is essential. Additionally, investigating the generation and propagation of non-hydrostatic flows in open-channel irrigation systems is crucial to ensure that downstream users, such as farmers, receive water under the required and arranged conditions. These tasks are typically addressed using depth-averaged flow models within the shallow water framework, which offer a practical balance between computational efficiency and result accuracy11–13. The shallow water equations (SWE) are commonly employed in this field, as they have been extensively studied in the literature and are supported by efficient numerical methods suited for river environments. The SWE are derived based on fundamental flow assumptions, including hydrostatic pressure and uniform streamwise velocity distributions, which simplify both derivation and solution13. However, in the case of curved flows, like those occurring over uneven beds, these assumptions break down: the pressure distribution becomes locally non-hydrostatic, and the streamwise velocity distribution assumption fails to accurately represent the velocity field12. This leads to inaccuracies in simulating wave transformation processes. Furthermore, turbulence closure in SWE models is often overly simplified to maintain computational efficiency, which limits their ability to simulate turbulence phenomena in both steady and unsteady curved flows, such as hydraulic jumps. Accurate modeling of these processes requires the integration of more advanced turbulence closure models, such as the zero-equation, one-equation, or two-equation k-ε models, considering the turbulence production linked to nonuniform velocity profiles, where k is the turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) and ε is the TKE dissipation rate1,14. Therefore, the development and validation of depth-averaged hydrodynamic models that incorporate these essential features of curved flows require the creation and analysis of comprehensive experimental datasets involving flows over uneven beds.

The transformation of water waves in fluvial streams is partly due to the interaction of the flow with uneven bathymetry. In canals, changes in bathymetry are mostly associated with the presence of hydraulic structures used for flow control, such as weirs, gates, check dams, or plates. Flow through these hydraulic structures in open channels often deviates from the assumptions of the classical SWE12. Among other reasons, in the vicinity of hydraulic structures, the flow undergoes rapid vertical accelerations and decelerations, which modify both the velocity field and the pressure distribution. When multiple hydraulic structures are present, their interaction leads to complex transformations of water waves, resulting in unsteady, wavy flows characterized by frequency dispersion. These scenarios are common in open-channel flows, as hydraulic structures rarely operate in isolation along a given reach. Although complex, studying these flow patterns is essential for a precise understanding of flow physics and for the development of robust hydraulic models for flow simulation15. In rivers, bedform development and migration create a natural uneven bathymetry, which is conceptually equivalent to introducing arrays of obstacles in the stream. However, creating experimental databases on flow interactions with arrays of obstacles is challenging. It requires long flumes and significant financial investment in hydraulic structures and instrumentation. Consequently, despite their prevalence in real-world conditions, such flows are rarely studied experimentally in the existing literature.

The objective of this study is to develop a comprehensive database of flow interactions with arrays of obstacles in a canal, under both steady and unsteady flow conditions. This database will support the development and validation of depth-averaged flow models suitable for flow over uneven bathymetry scenarios. To this end, an extensive laboratory experimental campaign has been designed to examine steady and unsteady flow conditions over various obstacle arrangements, using Gaussian and log-normal bedform profiles, different flume inclinations, and three sluice gates to generate water waves and control flow conditions at the flume boundaries. The flume is equipped with instrumentation that enables characterization of the free surface and measurement of bed pressure around the central bedform (only one window allows the installation of piezometers for pressure measurements). A monitoring system is used to capture images of the experiments. This flume setup, as reported in this study, has previously been used to generate flow data for the development and validation of a third-order variational flow model by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.16 All post-processed data generated in the present study is available in the Zenodo repository17, thus facilitating its use by researchers and hydraulicians.

The present study introduces a comprehensive dataset derived from six flume configurations, incorporating one, two, or three weirs under both steady and unsteady flow conditions. These setups were designed to investigate the generation and transformation of hydraulic jumps and water waves, resulting in a total of 38 steady and 30 unsteady flow experiments. Additionally, data from a steady flow experiment simulating a realistic river dune shape is included. An image capturing system was employed to extract free surface flow data in both steady and unsteady experiments. For the unsteady flow experiments, 6 to 7 time instants were analyzed per case. In the steady flow experiments, bed pressure readings were recorded at the central bedform of each flume configuration using 16 piezometers installed within the central inspection window of the flume. All images used for data extraction in each experiment are available in a Zenodo repository, enabling independent verification and reproducibility by researchers and hydraulicians. This dataset is unique in the literature, as it combines flow interaction over arrays of obstacles, flume inclination, hydraulic jumps, and wave transformation. As such, it is expected to make a significant contribution to the hydraulics research community, particularly in the field of river flow modeling. By closely mimicking real-life flow phenomena, the dataset supports improvements in depth-averaged modeling approaches and enhances both the understanding and simulation accuracy of complex flow behaviors.

Methods

Physical model description

Experiments involving six different flume bed setups were conducted in the hydraulic laboratory at the University of Córdoba (UCO) using the large hydraulic flume. This flume is 15 m long, 0.4 m wide, and 1 m deep, and it includes eight lateral windows for experimental inspection. Figure 1 shows a schematic view of the flume. Each window measures 1.875 m in length and 1 m in height (Fig. 2a). While the total width of the flume is 1 m, it was reduced to 0.4 m to nominally increase the available discharge per unit width in the recirculation system. Note that this nominal width of 0.4 m differs slightly from the 0.405 m reported in the previous study conducted in the same laboratory6. This difference is due to rehabilitation work carried out on the flume between studies to improve several structural aspects (Fig. 2b,c). Structurally, the flume retained its cantilever configuration, but improvements in horizontality were achieved, resulting in a reduced residual slope of 0.0002 at rest, compared to the 0.0015 slope in the earlier study by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6 Consequently, the vertical coordinates of the window corners, used in the subsequent data digitization process, were updated as shown in Table 1. All weirs installed in the six setups were constructed from stainless steel, following design specifications to produce Gaussian weirs with crest heights of 5, 10, and 15 cm, a log-normal weir with a crest height of 20 cm, and a dune-shaped weir with a crest height of 12.51 cm. However, due to construction and installation processes, manufacturing deviations occurred, necessitating the digitalization of all weirs after installation to fine-tune the match with the original design functions. This process involved photographing each weir as perpendicularly as possible, correcting image distortion using Panorama Corrector 2.2 by Altostorm, and then digitizing the weir contours using Golden Software Grapher 19.2.305. The digitized data was used to refine the design formulas of the weirs. Given that the process was conducted manually, a margin of error of ±1 mm was considered acceptable. This level of error was found to have no significant impact on flow simulation results, as confirmed by numerical experimentation conducted by the authors. Note that the error scale relative to the flow sheet thickness is of the order of 2.5 × 10−³. As a result, the weirs after installation are described by Eq. (1)−(6). The flume bottom roughness was characterized using Manning roughness coefficient n = 0.01 s/m1/3, which was obtained by calibrating the variational hydrodynamic model16 against the experiments conducted. This value of n is consistent with those for a hydraulically smooth bed.

Both steady and unsteady flow experiments were conducted in setups 1–5, while setup 6 was used only for a steady flow experiment resembling the dune design conditions. For the steady flow experiments, the recirculation system of the flume was used, which includes a centrifugal pump with an adjustable frequency drive (AFD) capable of delivering a maximum discharge of 0.078 m³/s. A SIEMENS SITRANS FM MAG/5000 flowmeter, with an accuracy of ±0.4%, was used for discharge measurements. Additionally, the recirculation system comprises an inlet water tank, flow tranquilizers, a tailwater tank, and a tailgate to control downstream conditions. For the unsteady flow experiments, no external discharge was applied to the flume. Instead, a high-speed release sluice gate, driven by a pneumatic system capable of opening 0.5 m in 0.3193 s, was used to generate a dam-break-like flow from water initially at rest. This pneumatic gate was located at x = 9.275 m in the flume (Fig. 1). By adjusting the flume inlet and tailgate to different openings, the water waves generated by the sudden release transformed as they traveled through the flume, interacting with arrays of obstacles and reflected bores at the end gates. All these devices and flume components were used as described in the study by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6; further details can be found in the referenced paper.

An inlet sluice gate was newly installed for the present study and was therefore not available during the previous experimental study reported by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6 The purpose of this new component in the flume setup is to generate controlled flow conditions in the upstream section, including the production of supercritical flows upstream of the log-normal weir, the central hydraulic structure in setups 1−5. Notably, in the inclined configurations of setup 5, supercritical flow throughout the upstream reach of the flume was achievable only by using the inlet sluice gate with a precisely adjusted small opening. This allowed the experimental campaign to include a wider range of flow conditions, encompassing not only naturally occurring subcritical flow in the upstream flume reach but also supercritical flow interacting with the installed weirs. Figure 5 shows photographs of the newly installed inlet sluice gate. The gate’s precise opening control mechanism includes a worm screw and a reduction gearbox, which convert large handwheel rotations into small vertical displacements of the gate. In the central lateral window, numbered four when counting from the flume inlet, 17 piezometer tubes are installed. These tubes are connected at one end to the weir and at the other end to a piezometric panel, which consists of vertical tubes. The system is pre-filled with water to eliminate air bubbles. This setup allows steady-state flow pressure to be transmitted through the water column, with the pressure level displayed as a corresponding vertical height in the piezometric panel. However, due to the specific construction characteristics of the log-normal weir [Eq. (1)], only 15 or 16 of the piezometer tubes could be connected and operational during the steady flow experiments. It is important to note that piezometer tubes are not adequate for capturing transient fluid pressure at the bed. Their response to flow perturbations is not instantaneous, and there is a noticeable lag, which is influenced by the length and diameter of the tubes. Further details on the piezometric tubes, as used in a previous study, can be found in Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6.

Tests procedure

To enable an accurate description and analysis of open-channel flow interactions with arrays of obstacles or weirs, capturing a wide range of real-world scenarios, the experimental campaign included tests under both steady and unsteady flow conditions for setups 1–5. For setup 6, a single steady flow experiment was conducted, aimed at studying the flow field variables under the conditions defined by the dune design.

The time origin for the analyzed flow instants is set at the moment of the pneumatic gate’s release. The selected time instants for flow characterization vary between experiments, as priority is given to capturing the most representative instants of key wave transformations. For example, the final analyzed instant in setup 4 was 15 s, while in setup 5 it was 11 s. This is because the 15 s mark in setup 5 did not correspond to a significant wave transformation or interaction with the array of weirs.

In all 30 unsteady flow experiments, the execution sequence was as follows: (i) prescription of the boundary conditions at the end gate, (ii) closure of the pneumatic sluice gate, (iii) filling of the upstream section of the flume using the recirculation pump until the prescribed flow level was reached, (iv) filling of the downstream section of the flume until the prescribed flow level was reached, unless the experiment was designed without water in the downstream section, (v) monitoring of the flow level until the flow could be considered at rest with no free surface disturbances, (vi) activation of the monitoring camera system to record the entire length of the flume, (vii) release of the pneumatic gate, and (v) termination of the recording once the designed total experiment time was reached. During the experiments, the transformation of waves often led to the formation of unsteady hydraulic jumps. These are also characterized in the database. While steady and unsteady hydraulic jumps were previously studied by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6, the hydraulic jumps observed in this study interact with arrays of obstacles, representing a novel feature. It is important to note that the experimental procedure described above is the same as that used by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6, and thus, detailed procedural descriptions are not repeated here. Free surface level measurements

The process of measurement and characterizing the free surface level in all steady and unsteady flow experiments involves a multistep sequence comprising the following:Image acquisition. The monitoring camera system was used to record videos of the experiments for both steady and unsteady flow conditions. For the unsteady flow experiments, video recordings were essential to capture key moments that best represent wave transformation, enabling more effective analysis, postprocessing, and data extraction. For the steady flow experiments, videos were also recorded, rather than relying solely on still images, to provide greater flexibility in identifying the most representative moment of flow steadiness. The videos recorded during the unsteady flow experiments lasted 30 s, while those for the steady flow experiments lasted 6 s. The monitoring camera system, described in detail by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6, comprised eight Basler Ace acA1920-40uc cameras (one per lateral inspection window), each operating at a maximum resolution of 1920 × 1200 pixels and 96 ppi. This configuration resulted in an approximate pixel width of 0.00132 m, based on an average camera-to-window distance of 1.4 m. The cameras were interconnected via cable to ensure perfect synchronization. Camera drivers, settings, and synchronization were managed using the Basler Pylon software, while Streams7 software controlled the recordings. The frame rate was set to 25 fps, out of a maximum of 35 fps, to avoid blinking artifacts between frames6. This provided a time resolution of 0.04 s per frame, allowing accurate analysis of wave transformation processes.Image postprocessing. Once the video records were available for each experiment, the time instants for data extraction were selected, and the corresponding frames were then extracted. The selected time instants for each experiment can be found in Table 5. Due to the 6 mm focal length of the camera lens, the extracted frames exhibited notable image distortion, which needed to be corrected prior to the digitalization process for data extraction. MATLAB was used for frame extraction, while Panorama Corrector 2.2 by Altostorm was employed to correct the image distortion. The correction followed a pseudo-automatic procedure: (i) the lateral and top/bottom metal beams in each window were used to manually adjust 3-point arcs in the first frame of each series, and (ii) the image distortion correction settings from the first frame were saved and directly applied to the rest of the frames in the same series (i.e., from the same camera, since the camera position remained fixed across all frames, making the same correction settings applicable). Finally, brightness, contrast, and levels were adjusted for all corrected frames to improve their quality prior to the digitalization of the free surface flow level. Full details of the image postprocessing can be found in Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6.Data production. The corrected and improved frames extracted in step 2 were used for data extraction and processing, leading to the final production of the database. Note that the database contains both free surface flow levels and bed pressure readings along the surface of the central weir/obstacle. However, the data production process described here applied only to the free surface flow levels. The software Grapher 19.2.305 (Golden Software LLC) was used for the digitalization, following the sequence bellow: (i) a corrected frame is imported into the software, (ii) the coordinates system is defined by setting the coordinates of two opposite points in the corrected frame, using the values from Table 1, (iii) the free surface of the flow is manually digitalized, with an associated error of ±0.00132 m, and (iv) the resulting free surface data is stored in a collection file for each time instant in the experiments.

Pressure measurements in steady flow

The bed pressure reading system installed in the flume at UCO does not allow for the registration of instantaneous bed pressure. Instead, piezometric tubes transmit the flow pressure of the water column through water continuity (i.e., without air bubbles), using a piezometric panel composed of vertical pipes at the ends of the piezometric tubes for readings. For this reason, bed pressure was measured only during steady flow experiments in this campaign. As previously noted, a total of 17 piezometers were installed in the fourth lateral inspection window of the flume, allowing connection to the log-normal and dune bedforms (with 15 to 16 of them connected, depending on the form). These connections were made using stainless steel welding (Fig. 10), providing a solid and reliable connection to the piezometric tubes, minimizing local head losses as much as possible, and ensuring alignment normal to the form surface vector. The pressure measurement process was as following: (i) first, the flume was filled with at least 50 cm of still water to purge the piezometric tubes, (ii) second, once purged, the water was carefully removed from the flume to avoid disturbing the water-filled piezometric tubes, (iii) third, the piezometric pressure rest, as indicated on the piezometric panel, was recorded as the reference level, (iv) fourth, the piezometric levels were recorded during each steady flow experiment after the steadiness criterion was met, and (v) finally, the reference level was subtracted to each piezometric reading to obtain the piezometric pressure at the position of the tubes along the forms. The accuracy of this process was ±0.5 mm, given that the piezometric levels were read manually using a millimeter-scale background board behind the vertical pipes. Data Records

The dataset described in this manuscript is available in the Zenodo repository17. It is provided as a single compressed file named “ROLLER_ExpDataSeries.zip” and is organized into two main folders corresponding to the steady and unsteady experiments. Each of these folders contains, for each selected experiment (or time instant, in the case of the unsteady tests): (i) the digitalized data in a XLSX file, (ii) a plot showing the data from a lateral view of the flume, and (iii) a panoramic image of the experiments corresponding to the digitalized data. This structure enables users to visually inspect the experimental data (via the plots) and verify the experimental conditions (via the panoramas) within the same folder. The name ROLLER in the filename is the acronym of the research project under which the experimental campaign was conducted: TuRbulence MOdeLing for Non-Hydrostatic FLows in Environmental HydRaulics (PID2020-114688RB-I00), funded by the Spanish Ministry of Science and Innovation. Figure 11 illustrates the structure of the dataset and its contents. The naming convention for the files is as follows: SXEYBZs, where S = setup, X = 1−6 (indicating the setup), Y = 1−6 (indicating the experiment), B = S or U (indicating steady or unsteady experiment, respectively), Z = time instant (only for unsteady experiments), and s = seconds (only for unsteady experiments). For example, S1E2S refers to experiment 2 in setup 1 from the steady flow series, and S2E3U1s refers to the 1 s time instant of the experiment 3 in setup 2 from the unsteady flow series. Note that this naming convention was not applied to the steady flow experiments in setup 1, in order to enhance accessibility. Free surface level, bed level, and bed pressure head readings

Each experiment provides its corresponding data in a single XLSX file. For the steady flow experiments, the XLSX file includes measurements of the free surface elevation (zs), flume bed elevation (zb), and bed pressure head (pb) along the central log-normal weir (setups 1–5) and the dune profile (setup 6). In the unsteady flow experiments, the same data is included, except for the bed pressure head (pb), which is omitted due to the inability of the measurement devices to capture dynamic pressures accurately. The flume bed elevation remained unchanged throughout the experiments for each setup, unless for those in which different flume inclinations were used. Although it can be calculated using Eqs. (1–9), it is also included in the XLSX files to facilitate ease of use. All data in the XLSX files have been vertically corrected, allowing for direct plotting. The resulting figures are provided in the corresponding subfolders17.

Additionally, key information is included in text boxes within each plot, such as the unit discharge and AFD prescription for steady flow tests, the time instant analyzed for unsteady flow tests, and the experiment number and setup corresponding to each plot. Although repetitive, the legend is included in all plots to facilitate their standalone use. Technical Validation

Free surface data

As previously described in Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6, the digitalized experimental data for the free surface flow level were validated using readings from manually operated point gauges (accuracy ±0.1 mm) at various positions along the flume in each setup, although only during steady-state experiments. Due to fluctuations in flow level in highly turbulent regions, this method was directly applied for validation in the subcritical flow region upstream of the central log-normal weir. In contrast, average readings were used for validation in the turbulent zones. Overall, this approach yielded a high correlation, with a determination coefficient of R2 = 0.97. Additionally, free surface level was measured using eight SIEMENS ECHOMAX XRS-5 ultrasonic transducers, calibrated through still-water tests in the flume. A known limitation of these transducers is their inability to accurately measure in regions of high surface curvature, as they operate within a 10° cone. These measurements, with an accuracy of ±0.2 mm, were also used to validate the digitalized free surface data. These error margins fall within accepted thresholds for hydro-environmental studies. The digitalization technique used has been successfully applied in other hydrodynamic modeling studies14,16,20. Furthermore, to avoid introducing camera-related errors, the camera positions were kept constant throughout all experiments in the campaign. For further details on the validation methods employed, refer to Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6, as the procedures remain unchanged and are not repeated here for the sake of clarity.

Pressure data

The bed pressure head along the log-normal and dune surfaces was measured using a piezometric panel. A still water test was conducted to validate the piezometers using readings from two instruments: (i) point gauges and (ii) piezometric vertical pipes. The validation process involved measuring water head data with point gauges at the locations of the piezometric connections to the bedforms and comparing it to the pressure readings from the piezometric panel. For the latter, water levels were measured along the central axis of the vertical pipes to avoid meniscus effects caused by surface tension. Readings were taken using a millimeter scale located at the back of the vertical pipes at the ends of the piezometers, with a measurement error of ±0.5 mm. Bed pressure readings were obtained by subtracting the reference levels from the piezometric pipe readings. The validation technique yielded a very strong correlation, with R2 = 0.96. This method has been successfully and routinely applied in hydro-environmental modeling studies14,16,20. Full methodological details are available in Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6 and are therefore not repeated here for brevity. The bed pressure data from the steady flow experiments are also included in the database plots, as shown in Fig. 12a, represented by empty rhombuses.

Data Usage

The novelty introduced by the laboratory experimental campaign presented in this manuscript lies in the detailed characterization of both steady and unsteady flows interacting with arrays of obstacles in a flume—a topic that remains scarcely addressed in the literature. This includes the transformation of breaking waves. While such studies are critically important from both scientific and engineering perspectives, conducting these experiments in the field is typically infeasible due to budget constraints. Therefore, the laboratory approach is the most suitable choice in this context. Importantly, the experiments closely mimic real-world flow scenarios, such as irrigation canal operations and riverine flows during floods or steady-state conditions. In such situations, flow often interacts with multiple obstacles in sequence. A common example of obstacles in open-channel flows is weirs, which are used to control water levels and discharge rates. The high-quality dataset presented in this manuscript is thus highly relevant for risk-assessment studies and for the design and modeling of efficient control algorithms in canals. It serves as a key resource for the development and validation of hydrodynamic codes11,13,16.

In the dataset, accurate measurements of free surface levels, flume bed levels, and bed pressure head readings are provided for both steady and unsteady flows in a laboratory setting. These experiments were conducted under various setups, involving combinations of multiple weirs and different flume tilting configurations. In the steady flow experiments, a sluice gate installed at the inlet section of the flume was used to generate supercritical flow upstream—a novel aspect of this dataset. To enable researchers to replicate this experimental campaign or to develop similarly detailed datasets, the methodology for data acquisition is thoroughly documented. This includes a multi-step procedure for digitizing free surface levels and capturing bed pressure readings. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this dataset is unique in the literature. It introduces significant novelties, such as the interaction of flow with arrays of obstacles, that build upon the related earlier dataset by Cantero-Chinchilla et al.6 As such, this dataset represents a valuable resource for the open-channel flow research and engineering communities in following contexts:Shallow water flow modeling: For the development and validation of higher-order shallow water models, the free surface data as well as the bed pressure head measurements at the central obstacle are particularly valuable.
