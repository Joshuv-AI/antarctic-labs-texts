Characteristics of mental health needs following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake: Comparison with the 2016 Kumamoto Earthquake
Authors: Yoshifumi Takagi, Sho Takahashi, Tatsuhiko Kubo, Yasuhisa Fukuo, Sayaka Gomei, Chie Yaguchi, Tetsuaki Arai, Hirokazu Tachikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study characterized Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Team (DPAT)‐recorded consultations following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake (NPE) by comparing Japan Surveillance in Post Extreme Emergencies and Disasters (J‐SPEED) records with Disaster Mental Health Information Support System (DMHISS) records from the 2016 Kumamoto Earthquake (KE), with particular attention to population aging, peninsula geography, geographic isolation, and infrastructure disruption.MethodsThis descriptive epidemiological study analyzed J‐SPEED data from the NPE (January 2 to May 26, 2024) and DMHISS data for the KE (April 14 to June 30, 2016). Age group, sex, consultation period, and mental health events were compared. Temporal comparisons were restricted to the common 78‐day DPAT activity window. p values for 15 comparable event categories were adjusted using the Holm–Bonferroni method.ResultsConsultations were fewer after the NPE (n = 488) than after the KE (n = 2,125), but among consultations with recorded age data, the proportion of older adults (65+ years) was higher after the NPE (58.4% vs. 41.4%). Anxiety (42.4%) and insomnia (32.8%) were the most frequent symptoms. After Holm–Bonferroni adjustment, depression, suicidal ideation, flashbacks, anger, excitement, incontinent speech, wandering, and verbal/physical aggression were more frequent after the NPE, whereas somatic symptoms were more frequent after the KE. Consultation timing within 78 days did not differ significantly, and consultations and deployments on or after Day 79 occurred only after the NPE.ConclusionThe NPE was characterized by a higher proportion of older adults and more frequent depression, suicidal ideation, flashbacks, and behavioral problems. Records on or after Day 79 indicate DPAT‐involved consultations in a later post‐disaster phase, although persistent needs and delayed reach of support could not be distinguished.Keywords: 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake, Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Team, J‐SPEED, mental health, older adults

INTRODUCTION

On January 1, 2024, the Noto Peninsula Earthquake (NPE)—a magnitude 7.6 event with a maximum Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) seismic intensity of 7—struck the Noto Peninsula of Ishikawa Prefecture, Japan. It resulted in 634 deaths, 1398 injuries, and a peak of approximately 52,000 evacuees—the largest human toll since the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake (Table 1). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The Noto Peninsula is a promontory surrounded on three sides by the sea with limited flat land (Figure 1) 8 , 9 . At the time of the earthquake, the region already had a very large aging population of 30.5%. In Suzu City, the hardest hit municipality, the proportion of adults aged 65 years or older was 51.7% 7 —the highest in the prefecture—and older‑adult households accounted for more than 40% of all households. Because public transportation is limited, access to medical and welfare services had been recognized as difficult even before the disaster. 10 Under these circumstances, the earthquake caused damage to infrastructure and lifelines, including road blockages and water outages, producing 33 isolated districts with 3345 residents at its peak. Immediately after the event, up to 93 road sections were closed, leaving the entire Oku‑Noto region effectively inaccessible by land routes. 3 , 11

To address survivors' mental health needs, the Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Team (DPAT), established in 2013 after the Great East Japan Earthquake, began support from the earliest phase, including deployments from outside Ishikawa Prefecture. 12 A total of 944 DPAT units were dispatched from within and outside the prefecture. Nationwide, full‑scale DPAT operations were mounted for the first time for the 2016 Kumamoto Earthquake (KE), 13 and the NPE constituted the second instance of nationwide deployment. The KE sequence severely affected the Kumamoto metropolitan area in central Kyushu; during the main shock (April 16, 2016), a JMA seismic intensity of 7 was observed in Mashiki Town and Nishihara Village, and a seismic intensity of 6 upper was observed in Kumamoto City and adjacent municipalities (Minamiaso, Kikuchi, Uto, Ozu, Kashima, Uki, and Koshi). 4 In Kumamoto City, the proportion of residents aged ≥65 years was 24.2% according to the 2015 Population Census. 14 In contrast, the NPE occurred in a rural area characterized by a large elderly population and a high risk of isolation. However, detailed data and analytical reports on post‑disaster mental health needs in such rural settings with greatly aging populations are scarce.

Accordingly, this study aimed to descriptively examine the characteristics of mental health needs recorded after the NPE by comparing DPAT clinical records in Japan Surveillance in Post Extreme Emergencies and Disasters (J‐SPEED) with those from the KE in DMHISS, taking into account regional contextual differences such as population aging, peninsula geography and resulting geographic isolation, and disruptions to essential services.

METHODS

Study type

This descriptive epidemiological study analyzed mental health events recorded by DPAT over approximately 5 months following the NPE. Using the mental health version of the Disaster Medical and Public Health record within the J‑SPEED system, 12 , 15 we examined the characteristics of mental health needs—focusing on age, sex, consultation period, and psychiatric symptoms. We also conducted comparative analyses with data from the Disaster Mental Health Information Support System (DMHISS) used up to and including the KE. 13 , 16

Data collection

The primary dataset comprised anonymized DPAT activity records entered into J‑SPEED for the NPE (January 2 to May 26, 2024). For comparison, we used DMHISS records collected by DPAT after the KE (April 14 to June 30, 2016). Data on the number of DPAT team deployments were provided by the DPAT Secretariat as part of a Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare‑commissioned project.

Since 2018, DPAT has centralized disaster medical support data using J‑SPEED as a standardized tool. J‑SPEED was introduced in 2015 as a clinical recording system aligned with the World Health Organization Emergency Medical Team Minimum Data Set (WHO EMT MDS). 12 , 15 Patient information and support activities at the scene are entered via tablet or smartphone applications, enabling real‑time situational awareness and analysis. 17 , 18 However, because DMHISS was a complex research‐oriented database, on‐site data entry was time‐consuming, and it may include delayed or retrospective entries. 16 Therefore, the timing and context of data recording were not necessarily identical between the two systems.

Both J‑SPEED and DMHISS specify items for the demographic characteristics of survivors with mental health problems supported by DPAT, as well as primary and related secondary health events. The J‑SPEED variables used for the present analyses included age group (0–14, 15–64, and ≥65 years), sex, consultation period, and mental health events (insomnia, anxiety, flashbacks, depression, somatic symptoms, suicidal ideation, paranoid symptoms, amnesia, disorganized speech, anger, excitement, incontinent speech, mutism, wandering, self‐injury, suicide attempt, verbal/physical aggression, and inability to stop drinking alcohol).

The DMHISS variables 13 used included age group (0–14, 15–64, and ≥65 years), sex, consultation period, and mental health events (insomnia, anxiety, flashbacks, depression, somatic symptoms, suicidal ideation, paranoid symptoms, anger, excitement, incontinent speech, wandering, self‐injury, suicide attempt, verbal/physical aggression, and inability to stop drinking alcohol).

Following prior literature, 19 the consultation period (time since the event to consultation) was categorized into a total of five groups. Three groups were classified as having ultra‑acute/acute phases: (1) within 48 h, (2) 3–14 days, and (3) 15–30 days; and two groups as having medium‑to‑long‑term phases: (4) 31–78 days and (5) more than 79 days. The 78‐day cutoff was chosen because it corresponds to the duration of DPAT activity after the KE and therefore defines the longest common time window for between‐disaster comparison of both consultation timing and deployment timing. Accordingly, statistical analyses were restricted to data from the first 78 days of each event, whereas observations from on or after Day 79 are presented descriptively for the NPE only.

Data analysis

We first calculated descriptive statistics for the consultation period, age, and sex. We then performed cross‑tabulations to compare frequency distributions of age group, sex, consultation period, and mental health events between the NPE and the KE. Finally, we cross‑tabulated the number of DPAT team deployments by period for the NPE and the KE. For these period‑specific comparisons, the five consultation‑period groups were aligned to the end date of DPAT activity for each event.

Group differences were tested using chi‑square tests; when significant overall differences were observed, adjusted standardized residuals were examined, with absolute values > 1.96 interpreted as indicative of cell‑level deviation. If any expected cell count was <5, Fisher's exact test was applied. In addition, to control the Type I error associated with multiple comparisons, p values for the 15 mental health events (insomnia, anxiety, flashbacks, depression, somatic symptoms, suicidal ideation, paranoid symptoms, anger, excitement, incontinent speech, wandering, self‐injury, suicide attempt, verbal/physical aggression, and inability to stop drinking alcohol) were adjusted using the Holm–Bonferroni sequential procedure. Effect size was evaluated using the following thresholds: ≥0.40 (strong association), 0.25–0.39 (moderate), 0.10–0.24 (weak), and <0.10 (negligible). 20 Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Accordingly, age‐group percentages were calculated from 488 consultations in the NPE and 1845 consultations in the KE, whereas sex percentages were calculated from 482 consultations in the NPE and 2074 consultations in the KE. In addition, to examine the possible impact of the 280 missing age values in the KE, we calculated the proportion of older adults under two boundary assumptions: first, treating all missing age values as <65 years, and second, treating all missing age values as ≥65 years.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted with the approval of the Hiroshima University Epidemiological Research Ethics Review Committee (Approval No. E‐2059) and the Ethics Committee of the Japan Psychiatric Hospitals Association (Approval No. 161110‐01).

Among consultations with recorded age, the proportion of adults aged ≥65 years was higher in the NPE [285/488, 58.4%] than in the KE [763/1845, 41.4%]. Consultations occurring beyond Day 78 were recorded only after the NPE and are therefore presented descriptively rather than as part of an inferential between‐disaster comparison.

Among these, somatic symptoms were more frequent after the KE, whereas the other eight categories were more frequent after the NPE. In contrast, self‐injury and inability to stop drinking alcohol were significant in unadjusted analyses but were no longer significant after multiplicity adjustment. The effect sizes for these category‐level differences were generally small to weak, indicating that the findings should be interpreted mainly as differences in pattern rather than large‐magnitude separations. Items recorded under “Other” were reported in the table as recorded counts; however, because the specific symptom or behavioral content could not be retrospectively verified, these items were interpreted descriptively only.

DISCUSSION

This study is, to our knowledge, the first to report on disaster mental health following the 2024 NPE. By comparing DPAT records from the NPE with those from the 2016 KE, we identified characteristicsof survivors' mental health needs and the temporal patterns of consultations and deployments. Few studies have continuously analyzed post‑disaster mental health needs from the early phase in regions with greatly aging populations; therefore, our findings may provide useful evidence to design medical responses for future disasters with similar regional profiles.

Summarizing the results, first, more than half of the survivors presenting with mental symptoms after the NPE were ≥65 years old, indicating a marked concentration of older adults. Second, while anxiety and insomnia were the most frequent symptoms overall, significant between‐disaster differences after Holm–Bonferroni correction remained for depression, suicidal ideation, and flashbacks. In particular, wandering, excitement, anger, verbal/physical aggression, and incontinent speech were more frequent after the NPE, whereas somatic symptoms were more frequent after the KE. Third, the distribution of consultation timing within 78 days did not differ significantly between the two disasters, whereas consultations and deployments beyond Day 79 were recorded only after the NPE. These findings are discussed below.

Challenges in post‑disaster mental health support in regions with large aging populations

Areas with a high proportion of older adults have been reported to experience greater deterioration in mental health after disasters. Because older adults often have poor physical function and weaker social support systems even in normal times, their risks of post‑traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression increase after disasters. 21 , 22 Following the 2010 Haiti earthquake, older adults and women of all ages were more likely to have persistent psychological distress, and PTSD symptoms did not sufficiently abate over time. 23 Four years after the KE, psychological distress correlated with limited social interaction, particularly among older women. 24 Even when older survivors experience severe distress—such as anxiety, depression, or suicidal ideation—they may be less likely to self‑report or proactively seek help. 24 , 25 Moreover, the loss of family and community networks can precipitate social isolation, leading to further deterioration in physical and mental health. 26

On the Noto Peninsula, the geography and fragile public transportation system were compounded by road collapses and severances as well as prolonged lifeline outages; some areas still lacked running water 1 month after the event. 11 In such settings with many older adults, mental health should be positioned as a priority for roughly the first month after the disaster. Response systems should incorporate early, outreach‑oriented, proactive approaches centered on older adults to prevent isolation and loneliness. Our findings suggest that DPATs and public health authorities should strengthen specialized, sustained approaches for older adults from the earliest phase, calibrated to local characteristics.

Characteristics of mental health events after the NPE compared with the KE

After the NPE, among the consultations recorded by DPAT, depression, suicidal ideation, flashbacks, and behavioral problems (e.g., wandering, excitement, and anger) occurred at higher proportions than after the KE. Depression and PTSD symptoms commonly emerge after disasters, and higher fatality rates are associated with higher frequencies of these symptoms, including bereavement reactions. 23 , 27 , 28 , 29 As shown in Table 1, although the number of evacuees after the NPE was roughly one quarter of that after the KE, the death toll was about double, which may have contributed to the higher proportions of depression and trauma‐related symptoms, including flashbacks, observed after the NPE. The higher prevalence of behavioral symptoms after the NPE may also partly reflect the older age structure of the affected population. In particular, wandering, excitement, anger, and verbal/physical aggression may have been related, at least in part, to coexisting cognitive impairment or dementia‐related symptoms among older survivors. In addition, interruption of psychiatric treatment and abrupt changes in living environment may have made such behavioral symptoms more overt.

In severely affected areas of Noto, psychiatric outpatient treatment interruptions reportedly increased. 30 Treatment interruption can exacerbate depression and suicidal ideation; among older adults, it may also worsen behavioral problems via cognitive decline. While prior studies of earthquake survivors have often reported anxiety, sleep disturbance, and depression in the acute phase, 18 , 31 , 32 few have quantified the rates of behavioral problems. 31 With the NPE, a confluence of factors—including the high concentration of older adults and disaster‑related care interruption—may have made mental health events relatively more salient. In disasters striking regions with greatly aged populations, clinicians should anticipate a broader and more persistent spectrum of symptom expression than in younger populations.

Regional characteristics and the temporal pattern of DPAT support

Comparing DPAT deployments, the NPE was followed by a relatively higher proportion of deployments at 3–14 days and a lower proportion at 31–78 days than the KE, while deployments were also recorded on or after Day 79 (Table 4). Regarding consultation timing, the distribution within 78 days did not differ significantly between the NPE and KE (Table 2). Therefore, the distinctive feature of the NPE was not the within‐window consultation‐period distribution itself, but rather the fact that DPAT‐involved consultations and deployments were still recorded on or after Day 79. Prior literature notes that mental health consultations tend to concentrate within about the first month after a disaster. 32 , 33 Accordingly, the continued recording of consultations and deployments on or after Day 79 after the NPE indicates that DPAT‐involved consultations were still being identified in a later post‐disaster phase. However, the present data do not allow us to determine whether this finding reflects persistent mental health needs among the affected population or delayed reach of support due to peninsula geography, road disruption, isolated settlements, and lifeline failure.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, selection bias is possible. Our data comprise cases assessed and recorded by DPAT—a mental health specialty team—so survivors who directly accessed other medical facilities or did not seek consultation may be underrepresented. The present study directly assessed only consultations recorded by DPAT, that is, needs that were expressed and reached the team. Therefore, the presence or magnitude of unmet or unvoiced mental health needs cannot be determined based on the present dataset. Consultation counts are also influenced by multiple contextual factors (e.g., road conditions, shelter environments), warranting caution in simple temporal comparisons. Second, because the analytic window was limited to about 5 months, we could not capture medium‑ to long‑term trajectories in health and mental health. Given reports of long‑term PTSD after large‑scale disasters, longer follow‑up is needed. Third, this study compared two systems with different recording structures and operational contexts, namely J‐SPEED and DMHISS. J‐SPEED is designed for structured, real‐time data entry in the field, whereas DMHISS may include delayed or retrospective entry, meaning that the timing and context of symptom identification and recording may have differed between the two systems. Accordingly, items that are more sensitive to observation conditions or recording timing, such as subjective symptoms and behavioral problems, may have been affected by differences in detection or documentation. In contrast, relatively objective variables such as age group, sex, and consultation period are likely to have been less affected by these differences. In addition, for items recorded under “Other” in Table 3, the available dataset allowed confirmation that an “Other” option had been selected, but did not permit retrospective identification of the specific symptom or behavioral content. Accordingly, these items could not be interpreted in further detail. Fourth, the KE dataset included 280 consultations with missing age data (13.2%). If missing age data were more common among older adults, the proportion of older adults in the KE may have been underestimated. However, when we examined the possible range by treating all missing age values as either <65 years or ≥65 years, the proportion of older adults in the KE remained between 35.9% and 49.1%, and did not exceed the 58.4% observed in the NPE. Thus, the direction of the age‐group difference remained unchanged, although the magnitude of the difference should be interpreted with caution.

CONCLUSION

We conducted a descriptive epidemiological comparison of DPAT records in J‑SPEED from the NPE with DMHISS records from the KE to characterize mental health needs during disasters. The main findings were as follows: (1) after the NPE, a majority of consultations involved adults aged ≥65 years; (2) while anxiety and insomnia were the most commonly reported symptoms, after Holm–Bonferroni correction, flashbacks, depression, suicidal ideation, anger, excitement, incontinent speech, wandering, and verbal/physical aggression remained more frequent after the NPE, whereas somatic symptoms remained more frequent after the KE; and (3) consultations and deployments on or after Day 79 were recorded only after the NPE, indicating that DPAT‐involved mental health consultations continued into a later post‐disaster phase. These results indicate that, despite fewer consultations than after the KE, the population structure (older‑adult concentration) of the Noto region, the peninsula geography, and severe disruption of essential services and lifelines may be associated with both the content of consultations and the timing with which support reached affected communities. However, this descriptive study cannot determine whether the later‐phase consultations reflected more persistent underlying needs, delayed access to support, or both.

Amid ongoing aging and depopulation in many regions, these findings provide practical evidence to inform future disaster medicine and public health preparedness and response.
