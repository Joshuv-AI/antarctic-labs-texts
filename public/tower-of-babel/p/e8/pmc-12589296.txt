Soil geochemistry and manganese contamination risk in the abandoned mining area – Eastern Carpathians
Authors: Doina Smaranda Sirbu-Radasanu, Ramona Huzum, Delia-Georgeta Dumitraş, Cristina Oana Stan, Ştefan Marincea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The investigated area belongs to the Mn metamorphosed ore chain along the Bistriţa Mountains in Eastern Carpathians. Over time, mining waste accumulated and underwent physico-chemical changes. A particular situation of sampling area consists of ore-roasting furnace tailings, abandoned without extensive reclamation. The study included the control soil and Technosol associated with tailings. XRD and XRF methods were applied for mineralogical and geochemical characterization of the two soil categories. The rare minerals of Mn (sonolite, hodgkinsonite, bustamite, braunite, ardenite, nimite and pyrosmalite) were identified. Pedogenetic effects were reflected by the presence of clay minerals (illite-montmorillonite; chlorite-montmorilonite; montmorillonite). The geochemical indices and mass balance were applied for soil maturity degree evaluation. The average CIA (76.60) and MIA (70.29) in control soil and CIA (73.12) and MIA (67.79) respectively for Technosol denoted that the soil achieved an intermediate to advance state of evolution. Mass balance also inferred these weathering state of soil due to the depletion of Ca > Mg > Na > K. The compositional peculiarity was determined by Mn high abundance (averages of: 1,894 mg/kg in control soil; 23,421 mg/kg in Technosol). The Mn risk of contamination in Technosol evaluated by the means of metal enrichment index (MEI) was very high (10.28–21.71),but the ecological risk (Ei) was low (< 40). The quality of control soil in relation with Mn enrichment did not require any monitoring program. Only Technosol could induce ecological risk over time.

Introduction

Soil is one of the Earth's most invaluable resources, and its generation and evolution are the consequence of parental material weathering and subsequent pedogenic processes. Under humid and cool climatic conditions typical of alpine areas, chemical weathering could rapidly proceed (Dahms et al., 2012; Egli et al., 2017) being responsible for the development of a thin soil layer on slopes or a thicker one on plateau zones. Once the biota interaction occurs, the weathering processes interfere with pedogenic processes and soil evolves. The main compositional features of soil are closely related to parent material mineralogy, while the pedogenesis could facilitate the pH change, the accumulation of trace elements in the root zone or the precipitation of secondary minerals able to adsorb trace elements (e.g. Fe and Mn oxyhydroxides, clay minerals) (Hardy & Cornu, 2006). The soil composition developed on rocks bearing ore-deposits is generally atypical, being enriched in minor and trace elements, possibly representing a source of contamination. In the mining areas, the waste material accumulates as barren rocks dumps or tailings dumps from ore roasting furnaces or flotation separation. These materials can serve as a skeletal matrix for the new forming soil which can be classified as Technogenic soil (briefly Technosol) (Tarnawczyk et al., 2021; Uzarowicz et al., 2020, 2024; WRB, 2015).

Since the level of minor and trace elements assessment in waste dumps was not required by law prior to the closure of the mining fields, the changes in environmental conditions could bring to the contamination of soils and waters in the former mining areas (Dey et al., 2023; Fernández-Martínez et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2018; Uzarowicz et al., 2024). The risk of contamination must therefore be considered a priority in soil quality assessment for viable regional development in the context of European legislation (Alvarenga et al., 2022). The contamination can cause toxic effects on plants and other biota component when the nutrition requirement is exceeded (Dey et al., 2023; Kabata-Pendias & Szteke, 2015; Uzarowicz et al., 2024). The extent of toxicity mainly depends on the nature of the parent material, the physical–chemical soil features and its evolution stage (Kabata-Pendias & Szteke, 2015).

In this paper several investigation methods were combined to evaluate the degree of soil maturity.

Thus, particle size was considered a primary indicator of soil evolution, as it is determined by the processes of weathering and transformation to which the parent material is subjected once the soil formation process proceeded (Bonnet et al., 2022; Stromsoe & Paasche et al.2011).

Then, mineralogical study was applied to identify the gradual transformations of minerals under environmental conditions. The mineral weathering leads to the release of ions with different behavior (Buggle et al., 2011; Churchman & Velde, 2019), which could be involved in the generation of new phases like clay minerals.

All changes from parent material to soil are preserved by geochemical composition of the soil and therefore weathering indicators (Nesbitt et al., 1989; Babechuk et al., 2014; Perri, 2020) that reflect the relationship between ions with different mobility and tendencies were used to assess the current state of soil maturation. Moreover, the mass balance (Brimhall & Dietrich, 1987; Chadwick et al., 1990; Holbrook, 2019; Musielok et al., 2021; Waroszewski, 2016) allowed that the intensity of the loss or gain of elements during soil generation to be determined, while the correlation between elements indicated their common sources in connection with soil mineralogy.

Materials and methods

Study area

The geological setting of the investigated area is related to the Crystalline—Mesozoic Zone of Eastern Carpathians. The pre-Alpine metamorphic basement and the Mesozoic sediments cover are divided into three Alpine tectonic nappes (Balintoni, 1997; Balintoni & Balica, 2013), having a complex structure. Only Bucovinian and Subbucovinian nappes comprise all Variscan units, consisting of all metamorphic groups (Balintoni, 2010; Balintoni & Balica, 2013; Munteanu et al., 2004) separated by pre-Alpine thrust faults.

The metamorphic terranes are represented by: 1) the Bretila group mainly with gneisses and amphibolitic rocks; 2) the Tulgheṣ group with quartz-mica (sericite) or chlorite-mica schists, graphite schists, white and black quartzites, meta-rhyolites, carbonate schist; 3) the Negriṣoara group with para and ortogneisses; 4) the Rebra group with mica-schist, metamorphic carbonate rocks and amphibolites (Fig. 1) (Balintoni, 2010; Balintoni & Balica, 2013; Munteanu et al., 2004; Stumbea et al., 2019).Fig. 1Geological map of Crystalline – Mesozoic Zone of Eastern Carpathians (metamorphic terrane) and simplified geological map of the area hosting Mn- ore deposit in Dealu Rusului (modified after Balintoni, 2010; Iancu & Popa, 2010; Kräutner & Bindea, 2002; Stumbea et al., 2019). The Tulgheṣ group is divided into other four formations, each of them bearing metamorphosed ore deposits. The lithological features are quite homogenous (e.g. mica schists, chlorite mica schist) with some variation related to the presence of carbonate metamorphosed rocks in Tulgheṣ 2 (Tg2) or meta-rhyolitic volcanic material in Tulgheṣ 3 (Tg3) (Balintoni, 2010; Balintoni & Balica, 2013; Munteanu et al., 2004).

The syngenetic Mn ore in metamorphosed carbonate rocks is associated with black quartzites and is found in Tg2 formation and occurs along the Bistriţa Mountains in more than 20 deposits (Hȋrtopanu, 2020). The mineral polymorphism within Mn bearing mineralization and rocks from Tg2, the variation within solid solution of minerals or the mineral associations supports the retrograde metamorphism (Hȋrtopanu & Scott, 2004). There are typical polymorphic minerals such as rhodonite and pyroxmangite, in distinct association, or bustamite and johannsenite in the same association reflecting high pressure and low temperature conditions (Hȋrtopanu, 2020). The garnet solid solution ranges from grossulare to spessartine and from calderite to spessartine being a classical indicator of pressure and temperature variation responsible for retromorphic alternation (Hȋrtopanu, 2020). The parent material of the soil in Dealu Rusului (Fig. 1) belongs to the Tg2 formation and is represented by both mica-schist host rocks and Mn-bearing rocks. The alpine climatic conditions at about 1300 m altitude imply in average 670 mm/year for precipitations and 5° C for temperature. The frost occurs in September and ends in May, favoring the mechanical degradation of the rocks.

Soil sampling and preparation

Two soil categories were selected: Technosol and control soil. Samples collected from the flat top site of the tailing dump and from the dump's base represented the first category (11 samples). The soil from the upper site of dump was added in the past by mechanical means and its thickness is lower than 20 cm depth. The short grass unevenly distributed was observed. The soil at the lower part of the dump represents a mixture between natural soil and man-made material.

The natural soil consisted of washed upper soil and the underground soil from hills of ants or other small species (e.g. moles). The anthropogenic material that provided part of the skeletal support of the soil resulted from the tailings of the ore-roasting furnaces and thus the soil was considered as belonging to the Technosol category according to (Secu et al., 2021; Tarnawczyk et al., 2021; Uzarowicz et al., 2020, 2024; WRB, 2015). The vegetation was sparse with herbaceous species, shrubs and young spruce tree.

The second category was selected as control soil (21 samples), for which the samples were collected approximately 2 km far from tailings dumps in order to minimize the risk of anthropogenic impact. The soil profiles consist of a very narrow blackish (2-max 10 cm) layer rich in vegetal debris, followed by a larger brownish horizon developed by weathering of parent material represented by frequent variable size angular rock fragments and also by pedogenic processes associated to the biota presence. The upper part (~ 10–30 cm) of this low-differentiated horizon was sampled in order to be representative of natural soil. The vegetation consisted of high grass and spruce trees with juvenile age. The soil was air-dried at room temperature and then passed through a 2 mm sieve. The fraction higher than 2 mm consist of angular mica-schist and black quartzites fragments typical of the Tulghes metamorphic unit.

Soil analyses

The particle size distribution was determined for ten representative samples by means of pipette method after preliminary treatments for organic matter (30% H2O2) and carbonates (0.2 N HCl).

The samples were selected on the basis of preliminary observations resulting from sieving, in order to save operational time and resources. The soil pH was determined by potentiometric method on a soil:water suspension of 1:5 ratio (ISO 10,390; 2005), using an EDGE HI 2020 pH meter.

The mineralogy of bulk soil, sand fraction and silt + clay fraction was studied by the XRD method.

To enhance the distinction of mineralogical weathering among soil fractions, six samples were used: bulk for general mineralogical observations; sand fraction for improved identification of minerals less susceptible to alteration; and fraction < 63 μm to identify newly formed minerals.

For a more comprehensive assessment of soil evolution, a total of 32 samples measuring < 63 μm were subjected to XRD analysis.

The diffraction patterns were generated by Bruker D8 Advance automated diffractometer at 40 kV and 40 mA using Cu Kα radiation source with Ni filter. The random oriented samples were scanned in 2θ range from 4–90°, with a step size of 0.04° 2θ and 1 s/step. The qualitative phase analyses were performed using the dedicated DIFRAC.EVA 3.1 software and the International Center for Diffraction Data (Database, 2013). The semi-quantitative phase analyses were performed using the DQUANT program.

The assessment of major and minor element concentrations was achieved by means of ED-XRF analyses (Epsilon 5, PANalytical). Technical characteristics of ED-XRF Epsilon 5 consist of: Gd anode X-ray tube, Be side window (300 μm), rating 25–100 kV, 0.5–24 mA, 600 W; Ge-X-ray detector, 30 mm2 collecting area, 5 mm thick, Be window (8 μm), resolution ≤ 140 eV, polarizing optics with 3-dimensional design. The sample preparation required the mechanical mixture of soil and blind powder (5:1 ratio) for 20 min at 180 rpm in agate mortar, followed by hydraulically pressing (20 t/cm2) to give rise to 9 g pellets. Each pellet was exposed two times and the final results represented the average of both counts. Certified reference materials (SO1-4, RT, RTH, GSD and LKSD) were used for the quality assurance and quality control of the results.

Weathering indices and mass balance

In order to gain more information about the soil development state, the geochemical indices can be applied. The chemical index of alteration CIA proposed by Nesbitt & Young (Nesbitt & Young, 1989) and mafic index of alteration MIA proposed by Babechuk et al. (Babechuk et al., 2014) were considered. The CIA presumes that most mobile elements underwent depletion in relation to aluminum, whose immobile behavior brings to its accumulation in weathered material. The mineralogical observations and pH values were used for a better evaluation of CaO distribution among phosphate, carbonate and silicate phases. Thus, for samples with pH lower than 6.5, CaO was not attributed to carbonate and the rest of CaO* after apatite removal was included in silicates. For samples with pH higher than 6.5, the carbonates (most possibly as secondary phases since the XRD analyses did not identify primery carbonate) was taken into account and CaO* was equal to Na2O content (Ding et al., 2016; Mclennan, 1993).

Herein, the mole values of each ion (mono-cationic moles) were used instead of the mole values of element oxides, following the model proposed by Meunier et al. (2013). The contribution of each ion to the CIA value is improved. The mafic index of alteration MIAO (Babechuk et al., 2014) is commonly used for mafic minerals bearing rocks and their associated soil, including Fe(III) near Al(III) as immobile elements which trend to accumulate by weathering in oxidative environment. Since Mn in the soils of Dealu Rusului, due to its higher concentration, can be classified as both a minor and a major element (Lee, 2018) and because its geochemical behavior is quite similar to that of Fe, this element was included in the original formula. The mono-cationic values were used and the formula became:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{MIAo }} = \frac{{100 \times \left( {{\text{Al}} + {\text{Fe}} + {\text{Mn}}} \right)}}{{\left( {{\text{Al}} + {\text{Fe}} + {\text{Mn}} + {\text{Mg}} + {\text{Ca*}} + {\text{Na}} + {\text{K}}} \right)}}$$\end{document}MIAo=100×Al+Fe+MnAl+Fe+Mn+Mg+Ca*+Na+K

CIA and MIA values denote the weathering stage of the investigated soil, allowing the assessment of soil evolution degree. There are three main ranges used to describe the weathering intensity: 50–60 for weak weathering; 60 ~ 80 for moderate or intermediate weathering; > 80–100 intensive or strong weathering (Babechuk et al., 2014; Négrel et al., 2023). The MIA upper limit for moderate weathering lowers to 70 (Babechuk et al., 2014) due to the possible Mg retention within newly generated clay minerals.

The weathering proceeds and the mobile elements are released from the mineral structure. The mass balance method was used as a geochemical tool for the assessment of element depletion or enrichment in soil in relation to their initial concentration in parent material and a reference immobile element in parent material and soil respectively (Brimhall & Dietrich, 1987; Chadwick et al., 1990; Holbrook, 2019; Musielok et al., 2021; Waroszewski, 2016). In Eq. 3, the notations denote: i—for immobile element (e.g. Ti, Zr), j—for studied element, p—for parent material, w—for weathered material, c—concentrations. The use of Ti as immobile element is recommended by its higher concentration and low variability comparing with other immobile elements like Zr (Bergstrom et al., 2019; Musielok et al., 2021).

When τ = 0—no change in concentration of mobile element occurred from parent material to soil; τ > 0—element concentration increased in weathered material; τ = − 1—total depletion of mobile element is found in weathered material.

Contamination degree and potential ecological risk

The determination of the geochemical background and characteristic thresholds for a single element are a mandatory step when the risk of contamination and its ecological impact could emerge in a certain area. The geochemical data variability is generally reduced by log transformation and then the normal distribution is checked in order to apply the background assessment using the formula developed by Reimann et al. (Reimann et al., 2005, 2018):4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Background}} = {\text{median }} \pm 2{\text{MAD }}$$\end{document}Background=median±2MADwhere MAD represents median absolute deviation and thus the outlier effects are minimalized. The upper and lower (inverse transformed) values are the thresholds that serve as reference values for contamination screening.

Metal enrichment index (MEI) (Devahi et al., 2023; Meybeck et al., 2004) can provide the degree of single element contamination on the basis of reference value (background), following the relation:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{MEI}} = \frac{{C_{i } - C_{b} }}{{C_{b} }}$$\end{document}MEI=Ci-CbCbwhere: Ci is the concentration of element i in soil and Cb is the background value of element i. Low contamination is detected when MEI is lower than 5; moderate contamination is found when MEI ranges between 5 and 10; high contamination occurs above 10.

The ecological risk for a single element can be calculated according to the Hakanson model (Hakanson, 1980):6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{i} = {\text{Cf}}_{i} \times T_{i}$$\end{document}Ei=Cfi×Tiwhere the Cfi is the contamination factor for a single element (i) and represent the ratio between element concentration in the sample and its background; Ti is the biological toxic factor of element i whose values were determined by Hakanson (Hakanson, 1980) and reevaluated (e.g. Cao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2008). The ecological risks less than 40 suggest a low ecological risk, whereas moderate and considerable ecological risk occurs when Ei takes values between 40–80 and 80–160 respectively. Statistical analysis and graphical representations were carried out using Origin Pro 2024 software.

The proportions of particle size fractions denoted that the sandy fraction was the main one (Fig. 2a) significantly exceeding the sum of the other two size fractions. Silt fraction, which varies between 16.08 to 39.08%, was the next one in order of abundance, being significantly higher than the clay fraction in all the samples (Fig. 2a). Clay fraction contribution is very low, ranging from 3.04–12.12% (Fig. 2a). Soil geochemistry

The soil reactivity described by the pH variation in both soil categories was noted, ranging from circum-neutral to acidic. Control soils were predominantly acidic, while Technosols were mostly circum-neutral (Table 3). The pH increase in Technosol could be related to the tailings composition which mainly resulted by roasting of Mn-bearing carbonates ore. Gradually, compounds such as CaO or other Ca–Mg oxides formed during Mn carbonate ore-roasting undergo hydrolysis and lead to the generation of Ca2+ and OH− groups which raise the pH value (Petitjean et al., 2021; Riley & Mayes, 2015). In control soil, the larger pH variation (4.5–7.06) could be related to the heterogeneous composition of parent material and the implication of pedogenetic processes (e.g. organic matter accumulation). Mass balance

The mass balance is one of the geochemical methods developed for the quantification of the loss or gain of elements in geological materials, being largely used for soils as well (Brimhall & Dietrich, 1987; Chadwick et al., 1990; Holbrook et al., 2019; Musielok et al., 2021; Waroszewski, 2016). The cation mobility was validated by the values of mass fluxes (τ- tau) for Ca, Mg, Na and K in control soil.

Calcium was the only cation exclusively affected by depletion, the loss varying from low to intensive, reaching a very high level as well (Fig. 6). Sodium loss was less extensive comparing with Ca loss. There were samples where Na gain attained a significant level (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Mass fluxes (τ) for Mg, Ca, Na and K in control soil

The Mg loss was quantified by mass fluxes and varied from low to moderate and intense (Fig. 6). The Mg gain was subordinately found, bringing to low and moderate enrichment. The K loss in soil was low to moderate (Fig. 6) but also gain covering the range from low to moderate within almost half of the samples. The mass flux in control soil denoted that Mn mobility was variable (Fig. 6). The Mn enrichment occurred only for a few samples. The Technosol Mn gain was very high, exceeding the upper limit of mass balance (> 1; τ = 3.93–21.74).

Discussions

Weathering intensity and soil evolution

The soil development involves a series of processes (Egli et al., 2014), including chemical alteration and pedogenetic generation of clay minerals, which coexist to a greater or lesser extent alongside their precursors. The weathering indices, mass balance, mineralogical composition and non-lastly grain size frequently served for the evaluation of soil maturity (Egli et al., 2017; Mohanty et al., 2016).

The effects of weathering upon soil composition were recorded by CIA and MIA variation among sampled sites. These two indices reflect weathering of aluminosilicates (CIA) and mafic minerals (MIA) respectively, regardless of the parent material nature (Nesbitt and Young, 1982).

The weathering statement of soil quantified by CIA and MIAo results (Table 4) were closed to the CIA range presented for European or Central India soils (Negrel et al. 2023; Mohanty et al. 2016) and lower than those from Cameroon (Onana et al., 2016) in soil developed on roughly the same lithology. The climatic conditions control the rate of weathering, and leaching solution and temperature in soil enhance the mineral–water interaction. Under alpine conditions the chemical weathering and erosion could rapidly be initiated (Egli et al., 2014). The younger soils are more reactive, while in older soil the weathering rate decreases but the enrichment in weakly and poorly crystallized phases pedogenetically progress (Dahms et al., 2012, Egli et al., 2014). The CIA and MIA increase with relative abundance of clay minerals (Fig. 4b) could confirm that soils are not of the same age and are in different mature stage respectively.

The association of illite with illite-montmorillonite and ± kaolinite (Table 1, 2) suggested that illite started to undergo transformation processes that lead to the soil maturation. The Al released from illite, once the montmorillonite started to form, enabled the kaolinite development (Velde & Meunier, 2008). Moreover, in sample where kaolinite was detected, only albite was found. An intense leaching typical of mountain slop could promote the plagioclase weathering and the kaolinite occurrence due to Ca depletion from soil (Velde & Meunier, 2008; Waroszevki et al., 2016). Since kaolinite was not found in most of the (< 63 μm) samples, the above mention scenario had limited application. The development of clay minerals on the expanse of plagioclase weather could be inferred by the good relation between CIA and Al/Na ratio (Negrel et al. 2023), since the Al is retained by new formed phases (Fig. 4d).

The presence of montmorillonite, exclusively in the < 63 μm fraction, under both circum-neutral and acidic conditions, denoted that the change of H+ ion concentration in the soil solution proceeded over time. Generally, montmorillonite occurs under slightly alkaline conditions and with Si and Mg input from the soil solution (Churchman & Velde, 2019; Velde & Meunier, 2008). The variation of pH from circum-neutral to acidic could not have been favorable for montmorillonite generation, just for its transformation. Thus, the coexistence of montmorillonite and kaolinite in the present control soil supported the idea of pH change once the soil solution received more components through mineral weathering.

The presence of muscovite among illite and other clay minerals that possibly formed at the expanse of muscovite transformation could infer that the parent material accumulation took place in more than one stage.

The higher or lower contribution of mafic minerals in soil (Fig. 5a) was reflected by MIA lower or higher values. The rise in MIAO highlights compositional changes which, this time, were attributed to the partially loss and conservation of Mg (in new formed clay minerals) or to the apparent enrichment of Fe and Mn in the soil through the steadily depletion in mobile elements by the mineral weathering. The preservation of the mafic phases was related to slight acidic to circum-neutral pH conditions (Fig. 5c), which nevertheless could favor the dissolution of more sensitive mineral, especially Ca-bearing pyroxene and amphiboles, and even plagioclases (Sheldon&Tabor, 2009). However, mafic phases could serve as indicator for a relatively younger soil or as an evidence of more stages parent material supply due to slop erosion.

The minor differences between the CIA and MIAO indices were noted for both soil categories. In the control soil, the MIAO index denoted a slightly higher degree of soil evolution than the CIA index. Besides plagioclase weathering effect on CIA, this index recorded the contribution of additional Ca and Na sources from other silicates as well. Even in this context, the CIA did not entirely reflect the maturity state of the soil comparing with MIAO index. The Technosol was found in a higher degree of evolution according to the MIAO index (Table 4). This feature is related to the particularities of the parent material consisting of natural soil and roasting tailings affected by advanced transformation due to anthropogenic impact. Consequently, the maturation of Technosol was mainly controlled by the parent material weathering state and subordinated by the involvement of pedogenetic processes.

Mass-balance was extensively used for the evaluation of soil degree of evolution and consequently to understand pedogenesis (Sheldon&Tabor, 2009). In contrast to geochemical indices, mass balance evaluates soil evolution in relation to the composition of the parent material. Thus, ion gain or loss is determined in the composition of soil that has been subject to long-term alteration processes (Hayes et al, 2020; Waroszevski et al., 2016). Comparing with loss or gain for Ca, Mg, Na and K detected within soil developed on schist parent material (Negrel et al., 2023), the trends in soil from Dealu Rusului were differently. Within European soil the sequence of the average element losses was Mg > Ca > K > Na, while for the investigated control soil the general trend was Ca > Mg > Na > K. These differences could be related to the mineralogical particularities and ages of the soil.

According to mass balance, Ca mobility was higher than Na mobility showing that the soil generation was mainly controlled by intensive weathering of Ca-rich plagioclase coupled with that of Ca-rich mafic silicate, while the Na plagioclase and other Na-bearing silicates (e.g. riebekite, richterite) weathering was subordinated. Even though Ca loss was moderate to intense, Ca-bearing silicates represented by mafic phases (e.g., diopside, hornblende) (Table 1) were still presented and inferred a moderate degree of evolution of the control soil. This feature was also supported by the reduced horizons differentiation. Most of Mg was also lost from soil (Fig. 6) but there were samples enriched in this element. As previous was mentioned and now tau values confirmed, most of the Mg loss was related to chlorite weathering (Fig. 7), more sensitive to acidic conditions than other Mg-bearing silicates (e.g. hornblende, cummingtonite, richterite, augite, dravite) (Chetty, 2018).Fig. 7The Mg depletion in relation with chlorite abundance in soil

The Mg depletion in relation with chlorite abundance in soil

The loss of K was connected with the weathering of K-feldspars, which were identified by XRD and showed a limited contribution to soil mineralogy (Tables 1, 2). Their incongruent dissolution under acidic conditions could have been responsible for the K mobility increase. The K gain ranged from low to intensive, being ascribed to an increase in the abundance of muscovite and illite in the soil (Fig. 6), whereas the other silicates were subjected to more intense weathering. In oxic environment the Mn loss was amplified by pH decrease, which favored the reduction of less mobile Mn (IV) to more mobile Mn (II) (Bernardini et al., 2021; Sinha & Purcell, 2019). The Mn enrichment could be explained by mineralogical observation which confirmed the presence of Mn minerals (tephroite, rhodonite, pyrosmalite, nimite, sonolite) near Mn bearing minerals (hornblende, richterite) in both sand and clay + silt fraction (Tables 1, 2). In Technosol a special enrichment was noted for Mn. The tailings from mineralized rocks had a significant contribution to the Technosol Mn budget and circum-neutral conditions favored its accumulation as less mobile Mn (IV) (Zahoransky et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025).

In soil, the Mn speciation (MnII, MnIII, MnIV) depends on the pH and Eh conditions and the positive correlation between pH and Mn(IV) could develop (Zahoransky et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). However, under oxic and acidic environment in upper soil layers, reduction of Mn(IV) to Mn(II) could occur (Bernardini et al., 2021; Sinha & Purcell, 2019). In the investigated soil the presence of positive correlation of total Mn with pH (r = 0.55) denoted that Mn was mainly represented by Mn(IV) speciation, being attributed to newly formed oxyhydroxides such as H-birnessite. The contribution of Mn(II, III) species to the total Mn budget had not to be omitted, since un-weathered Mn-bearing minerals were still present. Moreover, the correlation between Fe and Mn (r = 0.52) supported the involvement of Fe (III) oxyhydroxides in Mn(II) adsorption and Mn(III) retention (Zahoransky et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). H-birnessite (δ MnO2) possibly acted in the same manner (Ling et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2018), leading to Mn(II) and Mn(III) temporary preservation in soil.

Environmental impact

Contamination and pollution reflect the enrichment of the environment with chemical elements (mainly those initially included in the category of trace elements) above certain concentration limits which require surveillance and sometimes intervention through reclamation. The manganese, one of the potential toxic elements, was selected according to its high variability and concentration (Table 3) in the investigated soils.

In order to detect abnormal Mn content, that exceeded the natural background, the geochemical thresholds evaluation was completed according to the method proposed by Reimann (Reimann et al., 2005, 2018). The application was performed on control soil. Thus, all other values above the upper thresholds were considered to represent anthropogenic contamination, which was particularly followed for Technosol samples.

In the case of Mn, the most representative and abundant trace elements supplied by the parent material, the lower threshold was 400 and the upper one was 4454 mg/kg Mn. The lower threshold was close to the median value of Mn (445 mg/kg) in European soil (Reimann et al., 2018) revealing the high natural enrichment of Dealu Rusului soil. The perennial grass growing in soil from the vicinity of investigated area accumulated manganese up to 260 mg/kg (Culicov et al., 2024), showing that the bioavailability of Mn in soil was low. This confirmed that, in control soil, the toxicity risk was also low even though the tolerability level (500 mg/kg) for most of the plants was exceeded (Kabata-Pendias & Szteke, 2015).

The contamination of Technosol with Mn was very high since the upper threshold was exceeded at list 2 times till almost 10th times (Fig. 8a). This high degree of contamination was also determined using the metal enrichment index (MEI) (Devahi et al., 2023) (Fig. 8b). Even if a high degree of contamination was evaluated, the impact on soil quality reflected by ecological effects on plant growth and biota diversity could or not could occur. In order to assess the negative response, induce by contamination, the potential ecological risk proposed by Hakanson method (Hakanson, 1980) and remodeled by Jiang et al. (Jiang et al., 2014) and Cao et al. (Cao et al., 2023) was determined. Low ecological risk was found for Mn in Technosol, when the lower risk limit was settled to 40 (Hakanson, 1980; Kowalska et al., 2018). The bioavailability of Mn in soil under circum-neutral pH is typically low and consequently the ecological risk significantly decreases. Thus, the high Mn content of Technosol should not affect the quality of developing soil as long as the pH does not lower in acidic domain due to organic matter enrichment or mineral weathering intensification.

Conclusions

The main feature of both soil categories in Dealu Rusului consisted of their high Mn content (above mean values in world soils) and the atypical mineralogy which imply the presence of ‘exotic’ Mn-bearing silicates (such as sonolite/jerrygibbsite, hodgkinsonite, bustamite/johannsenite, braunite, ardenite, nimite, pyrosmalite) among soil forming minerals.

The CIA and MIAO indices variation recorded for the control soil inferred that the weathering intensity within parent material was not homogenous. This natural soil developed on slopes and with time the erosion was able to generate a mixture of rock fragments from the Tg2 formation (mica-schist and Mn-bearing mineralized rock), which accumulated in different stages. The weathering processes successively occurred and differentially acted in connection with the mineralogy of the mixed parent material. The CIA and MIA variation in a narrower range for Technosol showed that the soil development evolved more homogenous than for control soil.

Comparing with CIA index, the MIAO index, which taken into account the contribution of Mn to the less mobile ions bugged of soil, better reflected the state of control soil maturation. The higher degree of transformation of tailings controlled the advance state of maturity on Technosol reflected by MIAO as well. Generally, when the mineralogy of parent material included both plagioclase and Fe and Mn bearing-mafic minerals, MIAO index provided more reliable information about soil development.

The compositional changes from parent rocks to soil were supported by mass balance results (τ). The Ca, Mg and Na loss intensities in conjunction with the preservation of their mafic silicates (dioside, hornblende, chlorite, cummingtonite, richterite, augite) confirmed the intermediary through the advance state of evolution for soil. The Mn mass balance and the presence of low proportion of ‘exotic’ Mn (II)-bearing silicates also supported the intermediate to high evolution of control soil.

The Technosol, the second soil category in Dealu Rusului, was enriched with Mn up to the specific concentration of the major element in soil. The mass balance confirmed a very high level of Mn gain for this soil, which presumes that the ecological risk can occur. The contamination exists in Technosol, but the potential ecological risk for Mn was reduced. As long as the pH does not shift into the acidic range, a limited ecological risk could be considered. The Mn enrichment in control soil was related to its natural abundance in parent material. Since Mn (IV) speciation is dominant according to total Mn correlation with pH values, the mobility is low and there is no ecological risk. Currently, the quality of the control soil did not raise any concern, while Technosol can be considered a soil containing contaminants and can be subjected to a surveillance program.

The level of Mn content in Technosol recommends this material as alternative raw material, if a proper recovery method is applied.

Further, the control soil by its mineralogical and geochemical composition (e.g. typical Mn-bearing minerals and high Mn content) can serve as geochemical tools in ore exploration.
