Synergistic efficacy of ZnO quantum dots, Ag NPs, and nitazoxanide composite against multidrug-resistant human pathogens as new trend of revolutionizing antimicrobial treatment
Authors: Islam Gomaa, Ghadah Aleid, Shahira H. EL-Moslamy, Anoud AlShammari, Sumayyah Al-Marshedy, Freah Alshammary, Jouza Gharkan, Reda Abdel-Hameed, Elbadawy A. Kamoun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic resistance is currently becoming a more serious threat to global health, especially in severe nosocomial infections treatment by multidrug-resistant bacteria. This research provides a new way of synergizing green-synthesis for zinc oxide quantum dots (ZnO-QDs with hexagonal crystals) that are 7 nm in diameter and zero-valent Ag cubic crystals that are 67 nm in size embedded with nitazoxanide substrate (NAZ). Instrumental characterization like SEM, TEM, EDAX, and FT-IR and comprehensive antimicrobial studies were conducted to study the incorporation behavior of composites based on Ag NPs/ZnO QDs/NAZ. This combination has not been hitherto addressed anywhere else in the published literature, as well as commercial viability. In this context, we have precisely tuned nanoparticle to nitazoxanide ratio for designing the formulation demonstrating potent activity against MDR infections. By employing nitazoxanide as a scaffold and careful decoration thereof antimicrobial potency has been unlocked overriding conventional therapies. In addition, Ag NPs/ZnO-QDs/nitazoxanide (G6) formula exhibited a therapeutic efficacy span of 96.15 ± 1.68% to 99.57 ± 0.20% against MDR human infections post 48 h incubation; a breakthrough in therapeutic efficacy levels has been achieved by our method. Introduction

The discovery of antibiotics stands as a monumental testament to scientific ingenuity in the history of human achievement. These miraculous pharmaceuticals have safeguarded humanity against microbial proliferation for over a century, dramatically reducing morbidity and mortality rates from infectious diseases [1]. A disconcerting reality that surfaces amidst apparent triumphs is the relentless ascent of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Envision a world where a simple injury portends a fatal outcome, and a child's otitis media signals an inevitable descent into untreatable septicemia [2]. Once limited to the pages of science fiction, this frightening possibility is becoming more real with the emergence of extensively drug-resistant (EDR) and multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms [3, 4]. According to a 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) assessment, AMR might kill an extra 10 million people worldwide each year by 2050, more than even cancer [5]. This unsettling possibility highlights the pressing need for a multifaceted, adaptable strategy that includes the creation of new antimicrobials, careful antibiotic stewardship procedures, and strong surveillance systems. Antimicrobial resistance and the COVID-19 virus continue to be major health tragedies with significant death rates [6]. In addition, infections associated with multi-resistant human pathogens have been related to a marked rise in infant fatalities in recent years [7]. Furthermore, patients' regular use of antimicrobial medications was consistently linked to the likelihood of nosocomial infections and the development of multidrug-resistant bacteria in the hospital setting [8]. The scientific community has noticed a worrying drop in the effectiveness of once-reliable medicines against a growing number of bacteria. The overuse and misuse of antibiotics in human medicine, agriculture, and animal husbandry have caused this problem, allowing bacteria to develop advanced defenses against these life-saving drugs [9]. Unchecked AMR has grave repercussions [10]. Zinc oxide NPs have become a type of nanomaterial, with a range of uses, including catalysis, solar energy conversion and importantly in combating microbial resistance [11]. There are many ways to produce ZnO NPs categorized broadly as physical, chemical and biological methods. Physical techniques like sol gel and spray pyrolysis offer control over the size and shape of the particles [12, 13]. Often high temperatures and harsh chemicals [14]. Chemical methods such as precipitation and hydrothermal synthesis are simpler to execute. May use substances in the process and produce harmful byproducts [15, 16]. On the hand biological synthesis involves using plant extracts, bacteria or fungi for an environmentally friendly approach. Plant based synthesis relies on biomolecules found in extracts to reduce metal salts and stabilize the resulting nanoparticles [17]. ZnO NPs exhibit potent antimicrobial activity against a wide range of bacteria, including multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. The exact mechanism remains under investigation, but several factors contribute to their efficacy. The high surface area of ZnO NPs allows for intimate interaction with the bacterial cell wall, leading to membrane disruption and leakage of cytoplasmic contents [18]. Additionally, ZnO NPs can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon exposure to light, further damaging bacterial membranes and inducing oxidative stress [6]. The release of Zn2+ ions from ZnO NPs can also disrupt essential cellular processes within bacteria [19]. The antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs is dependent on their physicochemical properties such as size, shape, surface charge and surface functionalization. Smaller nanoparticles have higher antimicrobial activity due to larger surface area for interaction with bacterial cells. Surface modification with biocompatible polymers can further improve the targeting and efficacy of ZnO NPs against specific pathogens [20]. Silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) and their use as antimicrobial agents have been studied extensively due to their properties and applications in medicine, agriculture and water treatment [21]. Ag NPs have unique physicochemical properties such as high surface area to volume ratio and mechanism of action which makes them more efficient antimicrobial. Several methods have been developed for the synthesis of Ag NPs, broadly classified into physical, chemical and biological approaches. Among this chemical reduction is the most widely used method due to its simplicity and ability to produce nanoparticles of controlled size and shape. In this method silver ions are reduced to silver atoms using reducing agents like sodium borohydride (NaBH4) or ascorbic acid in the presence of stabilizing agents like polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) or citrate to prevent agglomeration [22]. Other approaches like green synthesis using plant extracts have gained popularity due to their eco-friendly nature and ability to produce biocompatible Ag NPs with enhanced antimicrobial properties. Ag NPs antimicrobial activity is due to several mechanisms. Firstly, Ag NPs release silver ions (Ag+) which interact with microbial cell membrane, causing structural damage and increased permeability. This disrupts cellular processes and ultimately leads to cell death [23]. Secondly Ag NPs generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) which induce oxidative stress within microbial cells further contributing to their antimicrobial activity [24]. Thirdly Ag NPs can enter microbial cells and interact with intracellular components like proteins and DNA and inhibit cell function and replication [25]. Many studies have shown the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of Ag NPs against various pathogens including bacteria, fungi and viruses [26]. For example, Ag NPs have shown activity against antibiotic resistant bacteria like methicillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [27], and multidrug resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa [28]. They are also effective against fungal pathogens like Candida albicans and viruses like HIV and influenza [29]. The ability to combat resistant strains makes them a potential alternative antimicrobial agent in an era where antibiotic resistance is a global health threat. Despite their antimicrobial properties Ag NPs are not without challenges due to careful consideration of exposure levels during synthesis. Recent efforts have been focused on optimizing the synthesis process to make Ag NPs biocompatible and targeted. New antimicrobial drugs are needed to treat nosocomial infections that are severe and caused by multi-drug resistant bacteria [30]. An increasing number of infections and deaths worldwide are being attributed to microbial resistance to widely used antibiotics. Biofilm generation is a major pathogenicity factor in many bacterial infections, as significantly increases the resistance of microbes to both innate host defenses and drugs [31]. A multitude of physical, chemical, and biological processes are involved in the complicated process of wound healing. Nanomaterials have lately gained attention as possible therapeutic agents for wound healing due to their extraordinary properties and capacities [32, 33]. Numerous nanomaterial-based wound-healing therapies have been developed and implemented in clinical practice [34]. As a potential substitute for traditional antibiotics, numerous efficient wound-healing treatments based on nanomaterials have been developed in an effort to treat serious wound infections [35]. The development of novel antimicrobial medicines has become more challenging due to the threat posed by multi-drug-resistant pathogens in global healthcare systems. The characterization of nanomaterials' modes of action can reveal a variety of different mechanisms of action, including membrane pore formation, replication inhibition, and immune response modulation [36]. Nitazoxanide, a prototype for the novel class of drugs known as thiazolides, has emerged as a broad-spectrum antiviral and antibacterial agent of considerable interest due to its distinctive and unconventional mechanism of action [37]. Unlike conventional antibiotics that commonly target cell wall synthesis, protein synthesis, or nucleic acid synthesis, nitazoxanide exerts its antimicrobial effects by inhibiting the enzyme pyruvate oxidoreductase (PFOR) [38]. This enzyme is pivotal for anaerobic energy metabolism in a wide range of pathogens, rendering it an attractive target for disrupting essential metabolic pathways crucial for pathogen survival and proliferation. Moreover, the inhibition of PFOR by nitazoxanide presents a lower risk of inducing cross-resistance, as this mechanism differs significantly from those affected by other antimicrobial agents [39]. On the other hand, certain metal nanoparticles may have extra potent modes of action when combined with antibiotics due to their synergistic effects. The strong antimicrobial properties of nitazoxanide, ZnO NPs [40], and Ag NPs [41], make them particularly appealing antimicrobial agents against a variety of human pathogens. Thus, we designed a wound-healing formula employing nitazoxanide, ZnO NPs, and Ag NPs at various dosages. Multidrug-resistant human pathogens were described in earlier research as those that showed resistance to three or more distinct classes of antibiotics [42–44]. Nitazoxanide considered as well-established oral antidiarrheal treatment [45]. Previous study showed that nitazoxanide (NAZ) core structural change under consideration revealed excellent antibacterial activity against nosocomial infections better than nitazoxanide and ciprofloxacin [10]. Moreover, the efficacy of ZnO loaded-nitazoxanide in treatment of cryptosporidiosis has been studied. It was found that nitazoxanide has the highest reduction in cryptosporidium oocyst shedding, 81.5% [46, 47]. This groundbreaking study unveils a novel triple therapy of silver nanoparticles, zinc oxide quantum dots, and nitazoxanide, synthesized using a benign method. The research provides a detailed characterization of the resulting nanocomposite, suggesting its potential as ultimate lifeline treatment against multi-resistant human pathogens.

Materials and methods

Materials

Nitazoxanide (commercial API ≥ 97%, Unidrug Innovative Pharma Technologies Limited, Indore, India), Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn (NO3)2·6H2O. ≥ 98%, Merck, Germany) and silver nitrate (AgNO3 ≥ 99%, Merck, Germany), Ethanol (commercial C2H5OH ≥ 99%, Alpha Chemical, Egypt) and potassium hydroxide pellets (KOH ≥ 98%, Merck, Germany). The deionized (DI) Milli-Q water was used during this experiment.

Synthesis of ZnO-quantum dots (QDs)/Ag nanoparticles NPs

Separately, 1 g of each metal nitrate precursor—for example zinc nitrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) and silver nitrate (AgNO3)—were dissolved in suitable solvents at 60 °°C for 5 h under powerful magnetic stirring. The process of complete dissolution was monitored by the clarity of the solution. Furthermore, these solutions went through pH adjustments that were done under strict control. The solution turned turbid during addition of 1M KOH drop by drop until a pH 11 was achieved which often led to notable turbidity as an indication of initial nucleation of metal precursors [48]. An additional 2 h of vigorous stirring was done to the solutions for homogeneous mixing and efficient delivery of precursors. The reaction mixtures were then subjected to probe sonication for about 30 min using a pulsed mode sonicate operating at 20 kHz, which was used to enhance nucleation and growth control. This enables localized high-energy conditions that trigger production of well-defined nanostructures. The synthesized nanoparticles were eventually separated through centrifugation. Centrifugation involved three consecutive washes of the precipitated particles with Milli-Q water at 9000 rpm each lasting for approximately ten minutes. It is at this point that any residual reaction byproducts and unreacted precursors are eliminated successfully. The isolated metal precursors were dried in a vacuum oven at 200 °C for 12 h. Finally, the dry powders obtained were confirmed as Ag NPs (grey powder) and ZnO-QDs (white powder) denoted as G1 and G2; respectively by appropriate techniques.

Integrated synthesis of ZnO QDs and Ag NPs on nitazoxanide substrates

10 mg of each nanoparticle powder (i.e. ZnO QDs and AgNPs) were accurately weighed and individually mixed with 90 mg of nitazoxanide using a mortar and pestle. This mechanical grinding ensured homogeneous dispersion of NPs throughout nitazoxanide support material. The resulting composite mixtures were then dispersed in Milli-Q water with a pre-adjusted pH 5.5. This step facilitated the interaction between NPs and nitazoxanide surface. The dispersions were sonicated for 5 min using a bath sonicator. Sonication promotes particle de-aggregation and facilitates their uniform distribution on the support material. Following sonication, the dispersions were centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 10 min to isolate the nitazoxanide-supported nanoparticles. The isolated composites were then dried in an oven at 70°C for 6h to remove any residual solvent. The dried composites were Ag-NPs/nitazoxanide(G3) and ZnO-QDs/nitazoxanide (G4). Subsequently, equal portions 50 mg of each of Ag/nitazoxanide and ZnO/nitazoxanide were physically mixed using a mortar and pestle. This step resulted in the formation of a composite containing both Ag-NPs and ZnO-QDs supported on the nitazoxanide scaffold. The final composite was designated as G6 for ease of reference.

Antimicrobial efficacy bioassays

Antimicrobial activity of synthesized formulations that were built up of different combinations of Ag NPs, ZnO QDs, and nitazoxanide was determined using a variety of techniques. These synthesized formulations were categorized as mentioned before to G3: Ag NPs/nitazoxanide, G4: ZnO QDs/nitazoxanide, and G6: Ag NPs/ZnO QDs/nitazoxanide. Furthermore, the antimicrobial ability of each formulation constituent was examined as controls coded as G1: Ag NPs, G2: ZnO QDs, and G5: nitazoxanide.

The investigated human pathogens were tested for antibiotic susceptibility using agar-disc-diffusion method, according to the CLSI standard [49]. Initially, McFarland-scale microbial cell suspensions were prepared (108 CFU/mL) for each of the studied human pathogens separately. The surface of the nutrient agar plate (0.5% peptone, 0.3% yeast extract, 0.2% beef extract, 0.5 NaCl, and 1.5% agar) was then uniformly covered with these cell suspensions (100 µL) of each pathogen. On these plates, antibiotic discs were placed once the surfaces had dried. When the zones of inhibition were observed after a 24 h incubation period at 37 °C, the examined pathogens were classified as either sensitive (S, inhibitory zones > 5 mm) or resistant (R, clear zone < 5 mm). Finally, the following method was employed to assess the antimicrobial properties of our formulations against recognized multi-drug-resistant human pathogens:

Agar-well diffusion bioassay

To accomplish 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards, the examined human pathogens were grown in a nutrient broth medium. With sterile cotton swabs, 100 µL of microbial cultures were spread out onto nutrient agar plates. Subsequently, 50 µL of the tested formulations were placed into each well after they had been drilled using a sterile cork-borer with a 6 mm diameter. The agar plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24h. The inhibitory zone, which is a clean zone surrounding each well in millimeters (mm), was measured using a ruler [43].

Antibiofilm activity

The possibility of tested formulations for inhibiting the growth of pathogens was investigated using a biofilm inhibition assay via micro-dilution technique [50]. The pre-inoculums were generated by cultivating each pathogen separately in nutrient broth medium at 37 °C and 200 rpm. Then, to obtain 2 × 105 CFU/mL, fresh nutrient broth was individually inoculated with each pathogen then incubated at 37 °C and 200 rpm. The inherent microbe's exponential phase was determined via measurement of optical density (OD) at 600 nm over a 6h incubation period. To obtain treated cultures, 100 µL of each formulation was aseptically added to 900 µL of these planktonic cultures. A control group of pathogens under investigation was also created using untreated (formula-free) cultures. The cultures were then incubated for a further 16 h period at 200 rpm and 37 °C. The inhibitory effects of the tested compound were assessed spectrometrically by measuring the microbial turbidity. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The broth microdilution method was employed in accordance with CLSI guidelines to determine the MIC and MBC of the tested formulations [51]. For calculating MIC of tested formulations against pathogens, the broth microdilution method was employed in accordance with CLSI standards. The microbial cultures were diluted overnight in fresh Luria Bertani (LB) broth to reach a final microbial density of 1 × 105 CFU/mL. The cultures were subsequently inserted into wells of a 96-well polypropylene microtiter plate, which contained the formulation at different concentrations ranging from 0 to 180 µg/mL (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 µg/mL). The plate was incubated at 37 °C for a period of 24 h. The MIC was determined by measuring the lowest concentration at which no appreciable growth developed. Moreover, the MBC was determined by identifying the lowest dose that destroyed at least 99.9% of the initial microbial cells [52]. The MBC was determined by spreading this MIC sample on LB agar plates and incubating for 24 h at 37 °C. Finally, a colony count analysis was used to determine the MBC [53].

Time-kill assay

A pathogen that showed the strongest antimicrobial effect in each microbial group was chosen for further investigation. The macro-broth dilution method was used in this experiment [54, 55]. To reach the early logarithmic stage, each of these pathogens was individually injected into nutrient broth medium and cultured for 6 h at 37 °C with 200 rpm agitation. A subsequent step consisted of transferring inoculum from each microbial culture (5 × 108 CFU/mL) to 9 ml of newly prepared nutrient broth medium. After that, one milliliter of the chosen formula was added. For every pathogen, a control growth system was developed without adding the tested formulation. After that, these tubes were constantly shaken at 200 rpm at 37 °C during the incubation period. At several time intervals (0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, and 48 h), the samples were regularly removed in aseptic conditions. These samples were diluted with 0.9% NaCl sterile saline and subsequently swabbed (100 µL) onto nutrient agar plates. The number of visible colonies was counted and reported as CFU/mL during the incubation periods. With each time interval, the logarithm of the counted colonies (Log10 CFU/mL) was used to calculate the percentage of biofilm reduction of the pathogen's cells exposed to the tested formulae with each control. The percentage of cell viability in treated and untreated samples for each period was finally compared using a stacked bar plot.

Statistical analyses

Three rounds of antimicrobial efficacy tests were carried out. The mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) was computed using Microsoft Excel 2019 to determine the study's findings. Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test was utilized in the Minitab 19 program (MINITAB version 19.1) to compute a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and demonstrate statistical significance. We used a 95% confidence interval for statistical significance (p value ≤ 0.05).

Instrumental characterization

The phase composition and crystal structure of samples were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) model (Malvern Panalytical Empyrean diffractometer, France). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of samples were obtained using (Joel-JEM-2100 TEM, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of samples were collected using (Vertex 70-Bruker FTIR spectrometer (Germany)) in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 3 cm−1. The morphology and topology of samples were characterized using Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM, Quattro S, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Two potential elucidations for the observed feeble interaction can be delineated; Firstly, the elevated surface energy inherent to ZnO QDs emerges as a compelling factor. The diminutive dimensions coupled with an extensive surface area to volume ratio confer upon ZnO QDs a pronounced surface energy. Such heightened energy levels instigate spontaneous aggregation amongst QDs, driven by the imperative to minimize overall surface energy. Secondly, the phenomenon of Ostwald ripening emerges as an alternative explanation. Ostwald ripening delineates a process wherein larger particles undergo growth at the expense of smaller counterparts. In the present context, extant aggregates of ZnO QDs might serve as nucleation sites, facilitating the accretion of smaller QDs while impeding their adherence to the nitazoxanide substrate. Thus, this phenomenon presents an additional mechanism hindering the desired interaction between QDs and substrate. The bonding might be attributed to Van der Waals forces or physical adsorption of ZnO onto the nitazoxanide surface. This possibility aligns with the relatively short sonication time and frequency used for the preparation process. Each element exhibits a high degree of purity and a well-distributed presence throughout the sample. Notably, the zinc element is visualized by yellow dots, indicating a relatively low concentration compared to other elements. This observation reflects the addition of a comparatively small quantity of ZnO (10%) during the composite formation process.

FT-IR analysis

Figure 7 shows FTIR spectrum of Nitazoxanide unveils a characteristic array of absorption bands that offer a symphony of information regarding its molecular architecture. A particularly prominent peak materializes at a wavenumber at ν 3357 cm−1, signifying the characteristic stretching vibration attributable to N–H moiety. Furthermore, distinct peaks are demonstrably observed at ν 1770 cm−1 and 1660 cm−1; respectively assigned to carbonyl stretching vibration of ester functional group and amide I band associated with amide linkage. It is germane to note that the latter peak at ν 1660 cm−1 might exhibit partial spectral overlap with the band corresponding to symmetric stretching vibration of nitro group, typically observed around ν 1520 cm−1. This combined band, designated as amide II band, offers complementary structural insights into amide functionality. Additionally, a noteworthy absorption peak adorns spectrum at ν 1355 cm−1, specifically attributable to asymmetric stretching vibration (ν NO₂) of nitro group [61]. Interestingly, a band around this region can also arise from presence of an alkene (C=C) functional group. Finally, a sharp peak grace spectrum at ν 1160 cm−1, ascribed to in-plane deformation vibration of C-H bond. FT-IR spectrum of Ag NPs displays bands at ν 433 cm−1 and 505 cm−1 hint at presence of metallic Ag NPs and might be silver oxide (Ag₂O), due adsorbed oxygen and Ag–Ag stretching vibrations. Additionally, a peak at ν 1374 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibrations of methyl (CH3) groups as residual carbon moiety. The presence of bands at ν 2300 cm−1 and 2685 cm−1 suggests contributions from surface-adsorbed carbon monoxide (CO) and atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂); respectively. Spectra of ZnO exhibits distinct absorption bands at ν 415–612 cm−1, characteristic of ZnO nanoparticles [20]. These bands correspond to stretching vibrations of Zn–O bonds and lattice vibrations within ZnO crystal lattice. The FT-IR spectra of the Ag NPs/ZnO QDs/nitazoxanide composite reveal the absence of significant alterations in the surface functional groups of nitazoxanide. This indicates that the essential chemical structure of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) is preserved, suggesting that the attachment mechanism is predominantly governed by weak interactions. The retention of the structural integrity of nitazoxanide within the composite is noteworthy, as it underscores the remarkable stability and effectiveness of the formulation. This stability is advantageous, as it minimizes the risk of chemical modifications or mutations that could potentially compromise the therapeutic efficacy of nitazoxanide. G6 formula's optimal concentration is determined using MIC method. The log10CFU/mL factor is used to determine the optimal dose of G6 formulation. In this assay, various G6 formulation dosages (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 µg/mL) are employed against all tested pathogens, relying on microbial turbidity analysis. The positive controls contained uninoculated broth with G6 formulation doses. Additionally, the negative controls have microbial broth without G6 formula (0 µg/mL). The turbidity values of treated pathogens that received G6 formula dosages varied according to pathogen type, as indicated in Table 5. The estimated biofilm reduction (%) for each treated pathogen with varying G6 formula dosages are then displayed in Fig. 11a. As shown in Fig. 11ai, Bacillus cereus (96.13%) shows the highest percentages of biofilm reduction when treated with the 180 µg/mL dose of G6 formula, followed by Candida krusei (93.30%) and Escherichia coli (83.30%). The box plot graph in Fig. 11aii further shows the data collection of anti-biofilms (%) for all tested dosages of G6 formula in a statistically categorized pattern. Compared to other doses studied, the 180 µg/mL dose result of G6 formula shows a statistically significant difference. The results indicate that the highest anti-biofilm efficacy of G6 formula is statistically significant at 180 µg/mL. Then, in order to determine MIC values—the lowest dosage at which there is no microbial turbidity [65] (≥ 98%); G6 formulations are employed to treat each tested pathogens at various doses of 210, 240, 270, and 300 µg/mL. In our work, the MBC is also calculated as the lowest G6 formula concentration needed to reduce the initial microbial inoculum's viability by ≥ 99.9%. Therefore, it can be measured through MIC testing at a broth dilution and then sub-cultured onto nutrient agar plates without adding G6 formula. An MBC endpoint is reached at the lowest dose of G6 formula when 99.9% of the microbial population is destroyed based on the colony count assay [66]. The pathogen's species influences the measured ranges of MIC and MBC after a 24 h incubation period at 37 °C. For both yeast cells and Gram-negative bacteria, G6 formula's MIC and MBC varies from 270 to 300 μg/mL; respectively. As compared to comparable untreated cells, the percentage of cell viability in all treated pathogens decreased with the length of culturing period (Fig. 11b). Bacillus cereus (99.29 ± 0.37%), Candida krusei (97.49 ± 2.58%), and Escherichia coli (95.04 ± 0.81%) had the highest growth reduction percentages after the pathogens are cultured for 36 h with G6 formula. After the incubation period of 48h, these reduction percentages considerably increase (Table 6). The period of G6 treatment needed to completely eradicate the pathogens' biofilm is also determined using time-kill kinetics test. After 96 h, biofilms of treated Escherichia coli and Candida krusei show 0% CFU/mL; however, Bacillus cereus needed 72h to destroy the biofilms. In conclusion, G6 mix consisting of Ag NPs, ZnO QDs, and nitazoxanide exhibits promising antimicrobial abilities associated with microbial infections caused by multidrug-resistant human pathogens. The intracellular metabolic processes are impacted by the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which is encouraged by our tested formula. These modifications make it possible for recently produced ROS to interact with and disrupt DNA, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids [67].

Conclusions

This study introduces a novel formulation that integrates green-synthesized zinc oxide quantum dots (ZnO-QDs) approximately 7 nm in size, silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) around 67 nm, and nitazoxanide using ultrasound waves, showcasing significant antimicrobial efficacy against multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens. Comprehensive characterization through SEM, TEM, EDAX, and FT-IR confirmed the composite's structural integrity and functional properties. The nanocomposite demonstrated remarkable therapeutic efficacy, achieving antimicrobial activity ranging from 96.15 ± 1.68% to 99.57 ± 0.20% after 48 h of incubation. These results highlight the potential of this unique composite material as a highly effective alternative to conventional antibiotics, providing a promising strategy for addressing severe nosocomial infections caused by MDR bacteria. The innovative nature and superior antimicrobial properties of the formulation suggest strong potential for patenting, making it a valuable candidate for future therapeutic applications.
