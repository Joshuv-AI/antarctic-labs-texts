Concerns on classification, statistical methods, and interpretation in NMA on acupuncture for endometriosis
Authors: Wenrui Huang, Xuelian Du
Published: 2025

To the Editor-in-Chief,

We read with considerable interest and concern the article by Su et al. (2025) titled "Efficacy and safety of acupuncture-related therapies in symptomatic endometriosis: a systematic review and network meta-analysis" published in Archives of Gynecology and Obstetrics [1]. While acknowledging the importance of investigating complementary therapies for endometriosis, we identified several critical methodological inconsistencies, significant analytical errors, and profound misinterpretations that compromise the validity and reliability of their conclusions.

Firstly, the inclusion criteria presented by the authors conflict fundamentally with their stated research objectives. The abstract clearly describes the interventions under investigation as "acupuncture therapies combined with medication." Contrarily, their inclusion criteria broadly encompass "various acupuncture or moxibustion therapies," and their control group mixes placebo, sham acupuncture, routine treatment, traditional medicine, and no treatment without adequate differentiation. Such a discrepancy undermines the coherence and validity of their systematic review. Moreover, throughout the manuscript, there exist considerable ambiguities and inconsistencies in defining and classifying interventions.

For example, Dongfang et al. listed “abdominal acupuncture,” but it is unclear whether this was categorized as standard body acupuncture (A) or treated as a distinct modality. Liu et al. used “A-M” for “acupuncture and medicine,” while Jianfang et al. listed “M” without clarification—“M” does not appear in the abbreviation list, making its meaning ambiguous. In Qiaozhang et al., the intervention “abdominal acupuncture + infrared” is unclear, especially as the control group was labeled simply “abdominal.” Dongdong et al. described a combined therapy of “gestrinone + acupoint catgut,” but the pharmacological component was not separately analyzed. Likewise, Meirong et al. combined electroacupuncture with Tongbining granules, yet this was omitted from network diagrams and league tables—while NWA + MNF, another combined therapy, was separately reported.

This inconsistent and poorly explained categorization—mixing monotherapies and combination therapies without clear definitions—undermines the internal logic of the network structure and raises serious concerns about how interventions were grouped, labeled, and analyzed across the study.

Secondly, the authors’ decision to pool vastly different interventions into a single "routine therapy" control group is methodologically unsound. Grouping diverse treatments—including GnRH agonists, traditional medicine, and placebo—into one category introduces substantial heterogeneity, distorting comparisons and undermining the validity of conclusions. They neither justified this categorization adequately nor conducted sensitivity or subgroup analyses to mitigate potential bias from such grouping.

Thirdly, we observed significant deficiencies in their literature search strategy. Despite submitting their manuscript in June 2024, their database search concluded in May 2023, thus omitting relevant recent studies. In stark contrast, similar systematic reviews, notably by Li et al. (J Pain Res. 2024), included updated literature searches up to April 2024, incorporating substantially more relevant studies. Consequently, Su et al. appear to have overlooked significant evidence that could critically influence the outcomes and interpretations of their analysis.

Additionally, numerous substantial statistical errors severely compromise their analytical framework. Most notably, the authors inappropriately applied Odds Ratios (OR) instead of Relative Risks (RR) to analyze the clinical effectiveness rate (CER), a binary outcome typically associated with high event rates. Such practice contravenes established recommendations by the Cochrane Handbook and likely exaggerates their reported intervention effects. Furthermore, the authors incorrectly applied conventional meta-analytic techniques, such as RevMan-generated forest plots, in interpreting network meta-analysis results, disregarding methodological norms that necessitate specialized statistical methods and dedicated network analysis tools.

Moreover, the authors inadequately assessed heterogeneity and inconsistency in their network analysis. They relied solely on traditional I2 and Q statistics suitable for pairwise meta-analyses, ignoring essential NMA-specific parameters such as tau-squared (τ2) for evaluating global heterogeneity [2]. They also omitted critical node-splitting analyses to assess network inconsistency explicitly, despite encountering signals of significant inconsistency (e.g., NWM + MNF intervention). Additionally, the absence of explicitly promised analyses, like contribution plots, further compromises transparency and reliability.

Finally, severe interpretative inaccuracies critically undermine their findings. The authors incorrectly employed Mean Difference (MD) for binary CER outcomes, misrepresenting significance thresholds and misleading readers by presenting non-significant comparisons as significant improvements.

The interpretation of VAS scores is particularly problematic. Su et al. report acupuncture treatments significantly reduced VAS compared to routine therapy (MD = − 0.73, 95% CI [− 0.93, − 0.54]), yet contradictorily assert routine therapy (SUCRA = 99.4%) as the most effective based on SUCRA values. This paradox suggests a critical methodological error—likely failing to reverse SUCRA calculation direction for a negative continuous outcome, thus producing contradictory results. Their conclusion that routine therapy was superior to acupuncture-based interventions for reducing pain defies clinical logic and undermines the credibility of their statistical methodology entirely.

In summary, the pervasive methodological flaws, fundamental statistical misapplications, and significant misinterpretations noted in Su et al.’s review necessitate extreme caution. We strongly recommend rigorous re-analysis, methodological refinement, and careful reconsideration of their data to provide scientifically accurate and clinically valuable insights.
