What is the impact of salutogenic design elements on human well-being in microhousing? A systematic review protocol
Authors: Narges Asadi, Mohsen Saffar Dezfuli, Ali Bonyadi Naeini, Hassan Sadeghi Naeini, Seyed Hashem Mosaddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRapid urbanisation, increasing housing density and rising costs have intensified pressure on urban housing systems, limiting access to health-promoting residential environments. Microhousing (compact units typically ranging from 15 to 40 m²) has emerged as a strategy to address these challenges. While microhousing improves affordability and spatial efficiency, it may also pose psychological and well-being challenges for residents. Salutogenic design, grounded in the sense of coherence framework, emphasises environmental resources that promote mental health and resilience. However, evidence on the operationalisation of salutogenic principles in compact residential environments remains fragmented. This systematic review aims to identify and synthesise empirical studies, including quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods research, examining salutogenic design strategies in microhousing and their potential impact on human well-being.Methods and analysisThis systematic review protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols guidelines. A comprehensive literature search will be conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies published in English from 2005 onward will be included. Title and abstract screening, full-text assessment and data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and, if necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer. Data will be synthesised using a narrative approach to identify key salutogenic design elements, outcome measures and contextual factors.Ethics and disseminationThis review will be based exclusively on previously published studies and will not involve the collection of primary data from human participants; therefore, ethical approval is not required. The findings of this review will be disseminated through presentations at scientific conferences and publication in a peer-reviewed journal. Any amendments to the protocol will be documented and reported in the final publication.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261281219.

Introduction

Background

Rapid urban population growth, increasing housing density and rising housing costs are creating unprecedented pressure on urban housing systems worldwide, limiting access to adequate and health-promoting living environments.1 2 Housing quality and residential design are widely recognised as key determinants of both physical and mental health outcomes.1 2 The WHO identifies the physical environment as a core social determinant of health, highlighting that housing conditions, including stability, quality and safety, affect psychological well-being and stress-related outcomes across populations.2 Urbanisation trends are expected to continue, with more people spending a significant portion of their lives within confined residential spaces, underscoring the importance of housing design as a public health concern.3 The scale of this challenge is reflected in global urbanisation projections. According to the 2018 Revision of World Urbanization Prospects, 55% of the world’s population lived in urban areas in 2018, and this proportion is projected to increase to 68% by 2050, adding approximately 2.5 billion people to urban areas, with nearly 90% of this increase expected to occur in Asia and Africa.4 From a public health perspective, the WHO Housing and Health Guidelines emphasise that housing conditions are increasingly important to health in the context of urban growth and identify inadequate living space and crowding as housing-related health concerns requiring policy attention.2 Empirical evidence also suggests that very small dwelling size may be associated with poorer mental health outcomes. In a mixed-methods study of 413 female caregivers living in subdivided units in Hong Kong, Chan et al5 reported that a total floor area smaller than 13.0 m² was associated with an increased likelihood of anxiety and depression and reduced health-related quality of life. These findings highlight the need to examine how design strategies in compact and microhousing environments can support mental health, psychological well-being and quality of life. In response to land scarcity and affordability challenges, microhousing, compact residential units often ranging from approximately 15–40 m2, has emerged as a strategy to accommodate growing urban populations efficiently.6 7 While microhousing provides opportunities3 for affordable and high-density urban living, compact residential settings can pose unique psychological challenges. Research has shown associations between smaller dwelling sizes and adverse mental health outcomes, including increased stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms and reduced subjective well-being, particularly where residents lack control over their space or perceive their environment as crowded or restrictive.5 8 The quality of the physical environment, often conceptualised through indoor environmental quality (IEQ) parameters, plays a pivotal role in mediating these effects.9 10 Access to natural light, adequate ventilation, thermal and acoustic comfort and coherent spatial organisation have been positively associated with mental health and perceived quality of life in residential settings.3 9 Beyond these physical attributes, environmental psychology and design research emphasise the importance of elements that support psychological processes such as perceived control, autonomy, social interaction opportunities and restorative experiences.11 12 Design strategies that incorporate access to natural features, views of landscapes and opportunities for meaningful engagement with the environment align with frameworks such as the biophilia hypothesis, which proposes that reconnecting people with nature can reduce stress and enhance cognitive functioning and emotional well-being.13 Salutogenic design, originating from Aaron Antonovsky’s work on the sense of coherence, captures the shift from focusing solely on risk reduction to identifying environmental resources that actively promote health.14 This framework emphasises comprehensibility (clarity and legibility of space), manageability (perceived control and environmental mastery) and meaningfulness (connection to place and purpose), which together contribute to residents’ ability to cope with environmental stressors.14 Although salutogenic principles have been applied in therapeutic spaces and eldercare settings, evidence on their operationalisation in densely populated housing contexts, including microhousing, remains fragmented.15 16

Rationale

Several gaps in the current literature underscore the need for a systematic review focusing on salutogenic design in microhousing. First, most research on microhousing has concentrated on spatial efficiency, cost-effectiveness and minimalism, with limited integration of psychological outcomes related to design attributes.6 7 Second, while some studies examine aspects such as thermal comfort or indoor air quality independently, few adopt a holistic perspective that integrates environmental quality with psychological experiences.3 9 Third, the fragmented nature of existing evidence reflects disciplinary silos. Architecture, environmental psychology, urban planning and public health each contribute relevant insights, yet interdisciplinary integration remains limited.11 12 Fourth, although salutogenic and biophilic design principles are increasingly discussed conceptually, there is no established analytical framework that systematically classifies these principles in compact residential environments and links them to mental health outcomes.13 15 Finally, the literature often overlooks experiential and psychosocial factors such as perceived control, restoration and facilitation of social interaction that mediate the relationship between physical design and mental well-being.11 Addressing these gaps is essential for translating design theory into evidence-based practice and policy recommendations. The primary objective of this systematic review will be to identify, synthesise and critically analyse existing evidence on the impact of salutogenic design elements in the microhousing context on residents’ mental health, psychological well-being and related health-promoting outcomes.6 7 14 This review will systematically identify empirical and conceptual studies that examine architectural, environmental and experience-driven design elements in microhousing and report outcomes related to mental health, well-being, resilience or a sense of coherence.8 It will classify salutogenic design elements into functional categories such as IEQ, natural light, ventilation, thermal and acoustic comfort, spatial organisation, environmental control and biophilic features and compare how these elements are conceptualised and measured across studies.9 17 Furthermore, the review will explore associations between identified design elements and health-related outcomes, examine mechanisms and contextual factors through which salutogenic design may influence psychological well-being in compact residential environments and identify patterns that can guide design strategies.11 14 Finally, it will highlight gaps in the evidence base and provide recommendations for future research, policy and health-oriented design practice for microhousing development.7 15 By synthesising this evidence, the review aims to support the development of housing design strategies that optimise mental health and psychological resilience in increasingly dense urban spaces.2 13

Theoretical foundation

The theoretical foundation of this review is grounded in Antonovsky’s salutogenic framework (1996), which emphasises identifying and enhancing resources that promote health, rather than focusing solely on illness prevention. Central to this framework is the sense of coherence, comprising comprehensibility (clarity and legibility of the environment), manageability (perceived control and environmental mastery) and meaningfulness (connection to place and purpose). Together, these dimensions support psychological resilience and overall well-being. Within microhousing and compact living contexts, architectural and experience-driven design features can act as salutogenic resources. Elements such as access to natural light, adequate ventilation, thermal and acoustic comfort, coherent spatial organisation and biophilic strategies have been associated with enhanced perceived control, environmental coherence and satisfaction in residential settings.9 11 12 18 19 Table 1 summarises these relationships by linking design features to salutogenic resources and expected outcomes in small-scale residential settings. These design elements operate within a broader socioenvironmental context, where factors such as housing density, affordability and access to green spaces can further influence psychological well-being and quality of life.1 2 Additionally, individual and contextual moderators—including age, household composition, socioeconomic status, prior mental health and cultural preferences—can affect the impact of design interventions.3 7 10 By applying this framework, the review will systematically evaluate how salutogenic and experience-oriented design strategies in compact residential environments contribute to mental health, psychological well-being and resilience, as suggested by salutogenic architectural research linking design features to sense of coherence outcomes.14 Given the growing interest in compact residential settings and human-centred residential design, a systematic review provides a comprehensive approach to assess how architectural and design features affect residents’ mental health, psychological well-being and resilience.3 10 Both qualitative and quantitative studies will be considered to capture diverse perspectives and provide a comprehensive understanding of design features and their potential impacts on occupants.20 21 Consistent with prior research on health-promoting spaces, this review examines interventions and design features, including access to natural light, ventilation, spatial organisation, thermal and acoustic comfort, biophilic strategies and experience-oriented design elements.9 12 22 Incorporating interdisciplinary studies from architecture, industrial design, environmental psychology and health sciences ensures that the synthesis accurately represents the current scientific landscape and the potential effects of design on human well-being.13 14 Systematic reviews employ established methodologies that involve rigorous literature identification, critical appraisal and evidence synthesis, providing timely and reliable insights to support research and design decisions.23 24 This approach is particularly appropriate for emerging areas of research, such as health-promoting design in microhousing, where the current evidence base is still developing. The theoretical and conceptual framework for this review is informed by well-established principles of biophilic and positive design.11 18 The systematic review outlined in this protocol will critically appraise and synthesise future empirical studies to provide updated and reliable insights.

Objectives

Addressing these gaps is essential for translating design theory into evidence-based practice and policy recommendations. The primary objective of this systematic review will be to identify, synthesise and critically analyse existing evidence on the impact of salutogenic design elements in microscale housing environments on residents’ mental health, psychological well-being and related health-promoting outcomes. Previous studies have highlighted the relevance of these design elements for mental health and well-being, but evidence specific to microhousing remains limited.6 7

This review will systematically identify empirical and conceptual studies that examine architectural, environmental and experience-driven design elements in microhousing and report outcomes related to mental health, well-being, resilience or sense of coherence. The review will categorise salutogenic design elements into functional domains, including IEQ, natural light, ventilation, thermal and acoustic comfort, spatial organisation, environmental control and biophilic features and will examine how these elements are conceptualised and measured across studies.9 10

In addition, this systematic review will investigate the relationships between the identified design elements and health-related outcomes, examine the mechanisms and contextual factors through which salutogenic design may affect psychological well-being in compact residential settings and identify patterns that can inform evidence-based design strategies. The theoretical and conceptual framework for this review is informed by well-established principles of biophilic and positive design and by the Salutogenic Model as a guiding theory for health-promoting design.11 14 Finally, the review will seek to identify gaps in the existing evidence and provide guidance for future research, policy development and health-oriented design practice in microhousing.15 16 By synthesising the current literature, the review will aim to inform housing design strategies that have the potential to support mental health and foster psychological resilience in increasingly dense urban settings.2 13

Methods

This systematic review will evaluate both existing and emerging evidence regarding salutogenic and experience-driven design interventions in microhousing and other compact living contexts. The review will follow established systematic review methodologies to ensure rigorous identification, appraisal and synthesis of the literature, thereby providing robust, evidence-based guidance for research, policy and design practice.23 24 Both qualitative and quantitative studies will be considered to capture diverse perspectives and provide a comprehensive understanding of design features and their potential impacts on occupants. Studies from architecture, industrial design, environmental psychology and health sciences will be included to ensure the synthesis accurately represents the current scientific landscape and the potential effects of design on human well-being.

Systematic review registration and reporting

The protocol has been registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) and the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (OSF registration DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/EK2A5 and PROSPERO registration ID: (CRD420261281219)) to enhance transparency and decrease the risk of non-reproducibility. The review will be conducted according to established methodological standards for systematic reviews to ensure rigorous synthesis. To promote comprehensive and transparent reporting, this protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement (see online supplemental material 1). The final systematic review will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. This review will systematically examine salutogenic and experience-driven design interventions in microhousing, focusing on their impact on residents’ mental health, psychological well-being and resilience. Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for this systematic review will be defined according to the PECO framework (see table 2) and summarised in table 3. This review will focus on peer-reviewed articles, case studies and official reports examining microhousing or small residential units, including tiny houses, compact dwellings and microapartments. The focus will be on human-centred residential architecture and salutogenic or experience-driven (emotion-focused) design interventions that aim to enhance mental health, psychological well-being or resilience. Studies may report outcomes such as occupant satisfaction, quality of life or other health-related measures. Studies will be included regardless of the presence or absence of a comparator group. Exclusion criteria will include studies focusing on commercial, office or industrial buildings; studies unrelated to human occupancy; purely theoretical studies without applied design elements; or studies that do not report outcomes related to residents’ well-being and experience. Only studies published in English from 2005 onward will be included. The year 2005 was selected as the starting point to capture contemporary research on compact and micro housing,5 25 which has expanded in response to rapid urbanisation and housing affordability challenges.5 26 There is also a growing interest in health-oriented design approaches such as salutogenic and biophilic design within the built environment.15 18 Restricting the review to this time frame allows the synthesis to focus on recent architectural practices and interdisciplinary evidence examining the relationship between the built environment and human well-being.6 10 17 In addition, the search will be limited to English-language publications due to feasibility considerations and to ensure accurate screening, data extraction and interpretation of findings. Although this restriction may introduce language bias, it is a commonly acknowledged methodological limitation in systematic reviews.24 This protocol will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-P 2015 statement to ensure transparent reporting of the review protocol.23 The final systematic review will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.24

Defining and identifying salutogenic design and related frameworks

To ensure consistency in study selection and synthesis, ‘salutogenic design’ is operationally defined as architectural and interior design strategies that facilitate the development and maintenance of a ‘sense of coherence’, comprising comprehensibility, manageability and meaningfulness, as conceptualised by Antonovsky.14 27 In the context of microhousing, we classify interventions as salutogenic if they explicitly engage ‘Generalized Resistance Resources’ (GRRs) through design elements that promote autonomy, social interaction and environmental mastery.28 29 Furthermore, this review recognises and includes complementary health-oriented frameworks. Specifically, it incorporates biophilic design, which consists of interventions aimed at restoring the human–nature connection to reduce physiological stress and enhance psychological restoration,13 30 as well as experience-driven (emotion-focused) design, which encompasses design strategies that prioritise the occupant’s emotional states, cognitive appraisal and subjective well-being within constrained spaces.31 These frameworks are identified in the literature through explicit mention of salutogenic and/or biophilic principles or through evidence of design features (eg, natural lighting, spatial flexibility, thermal comfort or acoustic quality) that have been linked to mental health and well-being outcomes in housing or small-scale dwelling contexts.26 32

Data source and search strategy

To identify relevant studies, a systematic search will be conducted in interdisciplinary and widely recognised databases, including PubMed/Medline, Scopus and Web of Science (WoS). The systematic review is planned to be conducted between July 2026 and October 2026, including literature searching, screening, data extraction and narrative synthesis according to the predefined protocol. These databases were selected to ensure comprehensive coverage of literature across industrial design, health-oriented architecture, the built environment and human well-being. PubMed/Medline was chosen for its extensive coverage of health-oriented research and studies addressing salutogenic approaches and environmental impacts on human health. Scopus, as the largest interdisciplinary database, includes a wide range of studies in industrial design, architecture, environmental psychology and social sciences, making it essential for reviews that integrate design and human health. The WoS ensures scientific quality, precise citation tracking and coverage of studies related to design and human behaviour. No data collection or analysis has been performed at this stage. This protocol outlines the planned methodology. The search process will be systematic. Initially, the titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies will be screened to identify potentially relevant articles. This will be followed by a full-text review for final inclusion. Duplicates will be removed before screening. The search strategies will be tailored for each database and the included controlled vocabulary (eg, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms in PubMed), Boolean operators and database-specific syntax. Citation tracking of included studies will also be conducted to ensure comprehensive coverage. Examples of the search strings developed for each database are provided in table 4. These search strings will be constructed based on the PECO framework (table 5) to ensure comprehensive identification of relevant studies. The search will be restricted to studies published in English from 2005 onward. This time frame was selected to focus on contemporary and interdisciplinary evidence that examines the interaction between the built environment, health-oriented design and human well-being. It also ensures alignment with theoretical frameworks and interdisciplinary paradigms.3 14 17 20 33 Limiting the search to English may exclude non-English studies; however, this restriction ensures access to high-quality, peer-reviewed literature and facilitates reproducibility.24 To comprehensively capture the concept of salutogenic design, the keyword Salutogen* will be used, covering ‘Salutogenesis’ and related derivatives. Wildcards (*) will be applied to maximise recall. For PubMed, searches will combine MeSH terms with title/abstract (TiAb) to capture health and well-being outcomes, even in design journals. WoS searches will use the topic field according to Clarivate recommendations for systematic reviews. In Scopus, title, abstract and keyword searches will be performed. Overly technical architectural terminology will be deliberately avoided to reduce false negatives. A Gold Set validation will be applied to increase the search sensitivity (online supplemental material 1). This will involve defining a reference set of key studies previously identified as core research in salutogenic design, microhousing and human well-being. Search strategies were iteratively refined to ensure that all studies in the reference set were retrieved. To minimise the risk of inadvertently excluding relevant studies, two independent reviewers will perform screening and data extraction (double screening). Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. The planned results management includes consolidating database outputs, removing duplicates, initial TiAb screening and full-text assessment. The review protocol will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-P guidelines23 (online supplemental material 1) to promote transparent reporting of the planned methodology. The results of the completed systematic review will be reported according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.24 The overall screening process is illustrated in figure 1 (PRISMA 2020 flow diagram).

Screening and data extraction

Two independent reviewers will conduct the study selection process, including title and abstract screening, full-text assessment and data extraction. All records retrieved from the selected databases (PubMed/Medline, Scopus and WoS) will be merged, and duplicate or highly similar entries will be identified and removed using Automated Systematic Search Deduplication before screening. The references will then be stored in Mendeley for management and access to full texts and subsequently imported into Rayyan (V.1.6.4) to support a systematic, transparent and standardised screening workflow. Within Rayyan, a pilot or calibration exercise will be conducted to ensure consistent application of the eligibility criteria by the reviewers. All screening and selection decisions will be made exclusively by human reviewers, and Rayyan will be used solely as a supportive tool for data organisation and decision tracking. In the first screening stage, titles and abstracts will be independently screened by two reviewers on the basis of the predefined eligibility criteria (table 3) and the PECO framework (table 2). Any disagreements between reviewers will be resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted to make the final decision. In the second stage, the full texts of studies identified as potentially eligible will be independently assessed by at least two reviewers to confirm final inclusion. The planned data items to be extracted include: author(s), year of publication, publication type, journal name, study design, organising entity, research context, design thinking models or frameworks, process stages and elements, tools and techniques, reported strengths and limitations and key findings relevant to comparative synthesis. To increase the sensitivity and completeness of the search strategy, a gold set validation approach will be applied via a predefined reference set of key studies (online supplemental material 2). In the final stage, a comprehensive verification of all included studies will be undertaken to prepare the extracted data for subsequent synthesis and analysis. The number of records at each stage of the screening and selection process will be prospectively documented. The entire screening and data extraction process will be conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-P guidelines for protocol development and the PRISMA 2020 recommendations for reporting the subsequent systematic review. Variations across studies in terms of methodological quality, study populations and operational definitions of key concepts will be examined to contextualise similarities and differences in findings. Patterns related to the influence of design elements will be explored by considering recurring findings, the range of reported effects and relevant contextual or environmental factors that may explain observed heterogeneity. Quality assessment

The methodological quality and scientific credibility of the included studies will be assessed using appropriate domain-specific critical appraisal instruments, alongside other appropriate appraisal instruments where necessary to accommodate qualitative, conceptual and methodological study designs. The methodological quality and scientific credibility of the included studies will be evaluated using a structured critical appraisal approach tailored to the characteristics of the included study designs. The appraisal framework will be applied in a manner consistent with the methodological characteristics of the included studies to ensure appropriate quality assessment across diverse research designs. The appraisal will focus on key methodological dimensions relevant to this review, including transparency of reporting, completeness and clarity of design and process descriptions, credibility of data sources, conceptual coherence and reproducibility or traceability of findings. Quality assessments will be conducted independently by two reviewers. Any discrepancies in appraisal judgments will be resolved through discussion, and where consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted. For conceptual and non-empirical studies, the appraisal approach will place particular emphasis on conceptual clarity, logical consistency, transparency of methodological reasoning and alignment between stated objectives and reported outcomes. All quality assessments will be conducted prospectively as part of the planned review process. The results of the quality appraisal will inform the narrative synthesis and comparative interpretation of included studies, rather than serving as a basis for exclusion solely on the grounds of quality. The overall confidence in the cumulative body of evidence will be assessed qualitatively. Formal evidence grading systems, such as Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE), are not considered appropriate given the non-interventional, design-oriented and often conceptual nature of the included studies. Instead, confidence will be informed by the methodological rigour of individual studies, the consistency of key concepts and findings across studies, the transparency of reporting and the scholarly credibility and impact of sources. This approach will support a nuanced interpretation of the synthesised evidence and enhance the robustness of the review findings.

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this systematic review will be among the first to comprehensively synthesise existing evidence on salutogenic and experience-driven design strategies in microhousing and their associations with residents’ mental health and psychological well-being. Although the relationship between the built environment and human health has been extensively explored in prior research,33 34 focused and theoretically grounded evidence on small-scale residential environments remains fragmented and conceptually underdeveloped. The concept of Salutogenesis, originally introduced by Antonovsky, emphasises health-promoting resources and the core components of comprehensibility, manageability and meaningfulness.14 While this framework has been widely applied in public health and psychology, its integration into architectural design and residential surroundings has gained momentum only in recent decades, largely through interdisciplinary approaches.3 28 This integration is particularly relevant in the context of microhousing, where spatial constraints, environmental conditions and design decisions may exert a disproportionate influence on residents’ psychological experiences and well-being.34 35 As microhousing continues to emerge as a response to urbanisation pressures and housing affordability challenges, existing evidence suggests that its well-being outcomes will be highly contingent on design quality, spatial flexibility and indoor environmental conditions.25 Health-oriented and salutogenic design approaches will therefore likely play a critical role in mitigating environmental stressors and enhancing residential satisfaction. However, how these design strategies are conceptualised and operationalised appears to vary considerably across cultural, social and economic contexts.17 This review is expected to make several key contributions. It will provide a structured synthesis of how salutogenic and experience-driven design principles have been defined, applied and evaluated within the microhousing literature. It will also identify recurring design strategies, outcome measures and methodological approaches, while highlighting inconsistencies, conceptual ambiguities and underexplored dimensions in the current evidence base. By integrating findings across disciplines, the review will contribute to greater theoretical coherence in research at the intersection of design, housing and mental well-being.

Comparison with prior work

Previous research has extensively examined the relationships between the built environment and human health, including mental health, well-being and quality of life across residential settings.33 34 36 A growing body of literature has also addressed health-oriented and salutogenic principles in architecture, particularly within healthcare facilities, workplaces and urban areas.3 17 28 However, existing reviews and empirical studies have focused largely on conventional housing typologies or broader urban-scale interventions, with limited attention given to high-density residential settings such as microhousing. Prior reviews addressing microhousing have emphasised economic affordability, spatial efficiency, regulatory frameworks and urban density outcomes.25 While some studies have acknowledged the potential psychological and experiential implications of small living spaces, the evidence remains fragmented and often lacks a coherent theoretical framework linking design interventions to health-related outcomes. In particular, the application of salutogenic theory, centred on resources that enhance comprehensibility, manageability and meaningfulness, has not been systematically synthesised within the context of microhousing. Moreover, existing reviews tend to examine isolated design features, such as IEQ or spatial flexibility, without integrating experiential and emotion-focused design perspectives derived from industrial and human-centred design research. This systematic review will address these gaps by proposing a theory-informed, interdisciplinary synthesis of salutogenic and experience-driven design strategies in microhousing. In contrast to prior reviews, the planned review will explicitly integrate architectural design, industrial design interventions and environmental experience within a unified conceptual framework. By grounding the synthesis in salutogenic theory and focusing on health-related outcomes, this review will generate a comprehensive and structured understanding of how design strategies may mitigate the psychological challenges associated with compact dwelling settings.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this review protocol include a transparent and rigorous methodology aligned with PRISMA-P guidelines, a PECO-based search framework and comprehensive coverage of PubMed/Medline, Scopus and WoS. The use of database-specific search strategies, controlled vocabulary, independent double screening by human reviewers and Gold Set validation will enhance reproducibility and minimise selection bias. However, several limitations are anticipated. Limiting the search to English-language studies may introduce language bias. Conceptual and methodological heterogeneity across studies may restrict direct comparability and preclude quantitative synthesis. Additionally, the focus on conceptual and noninterventional studies will require a qualitative assessment of confidence in the evidence rather than formal evidence grading. Geographic or socioeconomic clustering of studies may also limit generalisability, necessitating cautious interpretation of findings.

Future directions

Future research should focus on improving conceptual consistency in defining salutogenic design and well-being outcomes, generating longitudinal and context-sensitive evidence and examining culturally diverse populations. Such research will support the development of evidence-based design guidelines and practical interventions for microhousing and other compact living settings. The anticipated outcomes of this review will provide actionable insights for architects, industrial designers, urban planners and policymakers, contributing to the creation of healthier, psychologically supportive small-scale residential spaces.

Ethics and dissemination

This systematic review will synthesise evidence from previously published studies and will not involve the collection of primary data from human participants; therefore, ethical approval is not required for this study. The results of this review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentations at relevant scientific conferences and academic events. Any amendments to the protocol made during the review process will be documented and reported in the final publication to ensure transparency.
