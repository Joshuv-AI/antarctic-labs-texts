MFN2 and BAG6 Synergistically Protect Against Cerebral Reperfusion Injury by Regulating ROS Levels and Autophagic Flux
Authors: Dongting Lu, Yukun Yang, Guodong Huang, Qinlian Ye, Xingyun Quan, Britta Kaltwasser, Yanfen Liu, Dirk M. Hermann, Ulf Brockmeier, Ya-chao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:MFN2 (mitofusin-2), a transmembrane dynamin-like protein located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, plays a key role in regulating mitochondrial fusion and autophagy. In vitro studies suggested that MFN2 may exert neuroprotective effects postischemia. In gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments, we investigated MFN2’·s roles in regulating neuronal ischemia/reperfusion injury in vivo and in vitro.METHODS:MFN2 was knocked down by neuron-specific conditional knockout or siRNA-mediated knockdown and overexpressed by adeno-associated viral vectors or plasmid vectors in C57BL/6 mice of both sexes (10–12 weeks) exposed to middle cerebral artery occlusion and SY5Y cells exposed to oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation. Neurological deficits were examined using a 48-point score and rotarod tests. Infarct volume was assessed by 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining. A RealTime Apoptosis and Necrosis Assay was used to measure apoptotic and necrotic cell death. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation and autophagic flux were analyzed by functional assays. Protein expression and interaction were evaluated using Western blots, immunoaffinity chromatography, mass spectrometry, and immunoprecipitation analysis. To assess the role of MFN2’s interaction partner BAG6, BAG6 was overexpressed in middle cerebral artery occlusion mice and overexpressed or knocked down in SY5Y cells.RESULTS:Neuron-specific MFN2 deletion exacerbated cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury, while MFN2 overexpression reduced it. MFN2 deficiency elevated mitochondrial ROS levels and inhibited autophagy, whereas MFN2 overexpression decreased ROS levels. In immunoprecipitation studies, we found a direct interaction between MFN2 and BAG6. Of note, BAG6 overexpression mimicked the effect of MFN2 overexpression on cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury. Combined MFN2 and BAG6 overexpression synergistically reduced ischemia/reperfusion injury by drastically decreasing cerebral ROS levels, stabilizing mitochondrial function, and modulating autophagy.CONCLUSIONS:Our study suggests that MFN2 enhances stroke outcome through 2 pathways: by decreasing ROS levels and modulating autophagy via interaction with BAG6. BAG6 potentiates the ROS-lowering, cytoprotective MFN2 actions. The MFN2-BAG6 axis represents a promising target for stroke therapy.

Due to its high incidence and long-term disability, ischemic stroke is a major health burden.1 Intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy have meanwhile been established as efficacious reperfusion therapies.2,3 Due to restricted time windows, by far not all stroke patients benefit from reperfusion therapies,2,3 and treatment-associated complications, namely brain hemorrhage, may compromise clinical outcome.1 In case of successful reperfusion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation and cellular Ca2+ accumulation give rise to cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury.4,5 Autophagy plays a major role in the removal of denatured proteins under physiological and oxidative stress conditions.6 In ischemic stroke, the imbalance between autophagosome formation and autophagic flux can result in either insufficient or excessive autophagy, both of which compromise cell survival.7 Thus, a critical challenge in ischemic stroke treatment lies in effectively mitigating I/R injury by adjusting degraded protein removal.

The GTPase mitofusin-2 (MFN2) is located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, mediating mitochondrial fusion.8 Absence of MFN2 results in embryonic lethality.9 Conditional knockout studies revealed significant MFN2 functions in neurons.10,11 Thus, reduced MFN2 levels in the cortex and hippocampus were associated with elevated oxidative stress and neuronal death.12 In cortical neurons, MFN2 deficiency resulted in excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, ultimately leading to neuronal death.13 MFN2 knockout was found to inhibit autophagy in different cells and tissues, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria.14 With these concepts in mind, previous studies have suggested a role of MFN2 in controlling I/R injury in the liver.15,16 Indeed, MFN2 overexpression has been reported to reduce ROS production in cancer models,17 while MFN2 knockdown (KD) increased ROS levels.18 Furthermore, MFN2 overexpression was found to induce autophagy through the adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase and mammalian target of rapamycin pathways in cancer.17 In primary neurons exposed to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD)/reoxygenation (OGD/R), MFN2 downregulation was found to exacerbate I/R injury by inhibiting autophagosome-lysosome fusion, while MFN2 overexpression reversed autophagosome accumulation.19

BAG6 (BCL2-associated athanogene 6), also known as HLA-B associated transcripts (BAT3) or Scythe, is an integral component of numerous cellular processes, including apoptosis, autophagy, and protein quality control.20 As a co-chaperone for HSP/HSC70 proteins, BAG6 negatively modulates the protein folding capacity of HSP70, allowing the recognition of misfolded proteins and their proteasomal degradation.21 The C-terminal region of BAG6 has previously been shown to interact with multiple apoptotic regulators.22,23 Moreover, BAG6 has been implicated in the regulation of autophagy. BAG6 was shown to promote autophagy by transporting EP300/p300 to the nucleus for TP53 acetylation, while truncated BAG6 inhibits autophagy by retaining EP300 in the cytoplasm, leading to ATG7 hyperacetylation.24 These studies suggest that BAG6 acts as a pivotal factor balancing apoptosis and autophagy. Indeed, under endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress conditions, cleaved BAG6 interacts directly with the autophagy marker LC3 via its LIR motif, inhibiting autophagy and inducing apoptosis.25 Previous studies have already observed an interaction between MFN2 and BAG6.26 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that MFN2 might exert its effects through BAG6 in cerebral I/R injury.

In this study, we established MFN2 gain-of-function and loss-of-function models in vivo and in vitro. In a mouse transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model, we show that neuronal MFN2 KD aggravated cerebral I/R injury and MFN2 overexpression reduced it, thus improving stroke outcome. Furthermore, MFN2 deficiency elevated mitochondrial ROS levels, whereas MFN2 overexpression effectively reduced ROS levels. Of note, we identified a critical interaction between MFN2 and BAG6 in the cytoplasm, which involved the transmembrane domain 1 (TM1) of MFN2. As a consequence of this interaction, MFN2 KD downregulated BAG6 expression, thus inhibiting autophagy and exacerbating ischemia-reperfusion injury. Interestingly, BAG6 overexpression mimicked the effect of MFN2 overexpression on I/R injury, whereas combined MFN2 and BAG6 overexpression synergistically reduced I/R injury by drastically decreasing cerebral ROS levels and stabilizing mitochondrial function. Our data establish MFN2 as a survival-promoting protein that crucially regulates cell survival through BAG6 interaction.

Methods

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its Supplemental Material.

Animals

Conditional MFN2 knockout (MFN2f/f, C57BL/6 background) mice were provided by Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). To generate experimental mice with deletion of MFN2 in forebrain neurons, we crossed MFN2f/f mice with Camk2a-cre mice under the control of the neuron-specific Camk2a promoter, and obtained MFN2f/f Camk2a-cre (MFN2-cKO) mice and MFN2f/f mice as controls.

siRNA and Plasmid Transfection

For siRNA transfection, cells were transfected with scrambled siRNA, MFN2-targeting siRNA, or BAG6-targeting siRNA using the siTran 2.0 transfection reagent. Plasmid transfections were performed with pRluc-LC3-wildtype, pRluc-LC3-G120A, and MFN2.pcDNA3.1, HA-MFN2, or FLAG-BAG6 plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods and Table S1.

Adeno-Associated Virus Transfection and Stereotactic Virus Injection in Mice

The adeno-associated virus AAV9 was provided by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). The titer of recombinant AAV9 expressing MFN2 (AAV9-MFN2) was 1.16×1013 vector genomes/milliliter (vg/mL), and the control virus (AAV9-GFP) was 2.81×1013 vg/mL. The titer of recombinant AAV9-BAG6 was 7.41×1012 vg/mL, and the control virus (AAV9-CON) was 3.77×1013 vg/mL. Stereotactic AAV9 injections in mice were performed as described previously.27

Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion

Transient MCAO was performed on wildtype C57BL/6 mice, MFN2-cKO mice, and MFN2f/f controls (aged 10 to 12 weeks).27 C57BL/6 mice received striatal AAV9-MFN2 and AAV9-BAG6 or control vector (AAV9-GFP, AAV9-CON) injections 3 weeks before. For MCAO, a monofilament (Yushun Biotech, Guangzhou, China) was inserted into the internal carotid artery of anesthetized mice (1.0%–1.5% isoflurane) via an incision in the common carotid artery and advanced to the middle cerebral artery offspring. After 30 minutes, reperfusion was induced by monofilament removal. Regional laser Doppler flow was recorded above the middle cerebral artery territory. Neurological deficits were evaluated using a 48-point score and rotarod tests 24 hours after reperfusion.28 Mice were euthanized, and infarct volume was determined in coronal brain sections using 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining. Experiments were reported based on Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.29 Further details are provided in the Supplemental Methods and Table S2.

Oxygen-Glucose Deprivation

SH-SY5Y (hereafter SY5Y) cells were cultured in DMEM with High Glucose (2888791; Invitrogen, Darmstadt, Germany) supplemented with 12% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a 21% O2/5% CO2 atmosphere. OGD was induced by glucose-free DMEM (2715486; Invitrogen) in a hypoxia chamber (Toepffer Lab Systems, Göppingen, Germany; 1% O2, 5% CO2). For reoxygenation, cells were removed from the hypoxia chamber, and the medium was replaced with DMEM with High Glucose.

Analysis of Apoptosis and Necrosis

Apoptotic and necrotic events in SY5Y cells were assessed using the RealTime-Glo Annexin-V Apoptosis and Necrosis Assay (#A1011; Promega, Madison, WI). Apoptotic death was assessed by measuring luminescence generated by NanoLuc Binary Technology (NanoBiT) luciferase binding to phosphatidylserine exposed on the outer cell membrane. Necrotic cell death was detected via fluorescence measurement emitted by a non-membrane–permeable dye that binds to cytosolic double-stranded DNA. Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Analysis of ROS Formation In Vivo and In Vitro

ROS formation in ischemic brain tissue was evaluated by dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate staining, as previously reported.30 Fresh brain tissue was dissected into the ischemic and contralateral hemisphere and homogenized. Mitochondrial ROS formation in SY5Y cells was evaluated using the mitochondrial superoxide indicator MitoSOX Green (#M36005; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell ROS in SY5Y cells was evaluated using CellROX Green Reagent (#C10444; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Analysis

To evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP; Ϫѱ), 5 nmol/L tetramethylrhodamine-methyl ester perchlorate was added to treated cell cultures for 30 minutes. Subsequently, live cell confocal imaging was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 fully automated epifluorescence confocal microscope (Zeiss, Wetzlar, Germany).

Transcriptomics Analysis

Using transcriptomics, differentially regulated genes were studied in the brains of MFN2f/f control and MFN2-cKO mice 6 hours after MCAO. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed to define signal pathways regulated by MFN2 knockout. Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Analysis of Autophagosome Formation and Autophagic Flux

Autophagosome formation in the cerebral cortex was assessed by transmission electron microscopy in mice exposed to 20 minutes of global cerebral ischemia induced by bilateral common carotid artery occlusion. Autophagic flux in SY5Y cells was measured using a luciferase reporter assay. Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Western Blots

Protein extracts obtained from brain tissue samples or cells were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Protein detection was performed using antibodies described in the Supplemental Methods.

Protein Interaction Studies

For studying MFN2 interactions, HEK293T cells were used as an expression system. Transfected proteins were extracted. Soluble supernatant fractions were harvested and used for immunoaffinity chromatography/mass spectrometry and immunoprecipitation analysis. Cellular protein colocalization was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining. Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, CA). All experiments were independently repeated 3×. n represents individual animals in in vivo studies. For statistical comparisons between 2 groups, Student t-tests were applied. For comparisons between ≥3 groups, 2-way ANOVA was used. Unless otherwise specified, values were expressed as mean±SD. P≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistics results are shown in Table S3.

Results

Neuronal MFN2 Deficiency Aggravates Cerebral I/R Injury

To investigate whether MFN2 exerts neuroprotective effects in acute ischemic stroke, we established a neuron-specific MFN2 conditional knockout mouse model. By crossing Camk2a-Cre mice with MFN2f/f (control) mice, we generated MFN2-cKO mice exhibiting MFN2 deletion in the majority of excitatory forebrain neurons (Figure 1A). The deletion of MFN2 exons was confirmed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of mouse DNA samples (Figure 1A). We revealed the reduction of MFN2 protein level in the cerebral cortex of MFN2-cKO mice through Western blot analysis (Figure 1B). To explore the impact of MFN2 deficiency on stroke outcome, we assessed neurological deficits and infarct volume in male MCAO mice. Compared with control mice, MFN2-cKO mice exhibited significantly poorer performance in the neurological score and rotarod test, as well as increased infarct volume (Figure 1C).

Next, we tested if MFN2 depletion influenced the death of SY5Y neuroblastoma cells exposed to OGD/R, an in vitro ischemic stroke model. First, we induced MFN2 KD by 3 different siRNAs and chose the most effective siRNA (siMFN2-3) for subsequent experiments (Figure 1D). For continuous live-cell measurements, we used a RealTime-Glo Annexin-V Apoptosis and Necrosis Assay. Abrogation of MFN2 increased the early apoptotic peak after 30 min reoxygenation compared with control, whereas necrosis was not affected (Figure 1E). Together, these results indicate that MFN2 knockout or KD exacerbates I/R injury in vivo and in vitro.

MFN2 Overexpression Attenuates Cerebral I/R Injury

To further determine the neuroprotective effects of MFN2, an adeno-associated virus overexpressing MFN2 (AAV9-MFN2) or a corresponding control adeno-associated virus (AAV9-GFP) was stereotactically injected into the striatum of male wild-type mice (Figure S1). Compared with control mice injected with AAV9-GFP, mice injected with AAV9-MFN2 had significantly reduced neurological deficits post-MCAO and spent considerably longer time on the rotarod, indicating marked improvement in motor-coordination function (Figure 2A). 2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining revealed that AAV9-MFN2 overexpression significantly reduced infarct volume post-MCAO compared with AAV9-GFP controls (Figure 2A). The above results were also confirmed in female mice (Figure S2).

Likewise, we induced MFN2 overexpression in our in vitro stroke model in SY5Y cells by appropriate plasmids (Figure 2B) and conducted continuous apoptosis and necrosis measurements (Figure 2C). Although there was no detectable difference in the OGD/R-induced apoptotic peak following MFN2 overexpression, we noticed an increased necrosis in the late reoxygenation phase (24 hours of reoxygenation), indicating a potential detrimental effect on neuronal cells under these conditions.

MFN2 Reduces Mitochondrial ROS Formation Under Cerebral I/R Conditions

To investigate the impact of MFN2 on mitochondrial oxidative stress, we assessed ROS levels in the brain tissue of control and MFN2-cKO MCAO mice by dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate staining (Figure 3A). It is well established that ROS formation mediates cerebral I/R injury.31 ROS levels in the reperfused ischemic brain tissue were significantly increased in MFN2-cKO mice compared with control mice (Figure 3B). In contrast, MFN2 overexpression significantly reduced ROS levels in the reperfused ischemic brain tissue (Figure 3C).

Next, we performed gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments for MFN2 in SY5Y cells to test its impact on mitochondrial ROS under stroke conditions using the mitochondria-specific ROS detection agent MitoSOX. In line with our in vivo results, we observed that ROS levels depend reciprocally on the presence of MFN2 protein, indicating a cell-protective effect in the presence of MFN2 (Figure 3D and 3E). These results suggest that MFN2 attenuates I/R injury by reducing mitochondrial ROS levels.

MFN2 Is a Critical Factor for Autophagic Flux Following Cerebral I/R Injury

To further determine the underlying mechanism of the neuroprotective effect of MFN2 in acute ischemic stroke, we investigated the impact of MFN2 knockout on the gene expression profile of ischemic brain tissue via RNA sequencing. Tissues from the ipsilateral cortex post-MCAO were collected after 6 hours of reperfusion. We identified 1468 upregulated and 284 downregulated genes in the MFN2-cKO group compared with controls (|log2(fold change)|≥0.5; P<0.05; Figure S3). The result of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of regulatory pathways, including the lysosome signaling pathway, in the MFN2-cKO group compared with the control group (Figure 4A).

Hence, we determined the effect of MFN2 on autophagy and performed transmission electron microscopy to explore autophagosomes in cortical neurons. The results showed that after 24 hours of reperfusion, there were fewer autophagosomes in the MFN2-cKO compared with the control group (Figure 4B). The LC3II/LC3I ratio and P62 (SQSTM1) are widely regarded as indicators of autophagy initiation.7 Western blot analysis of LC3 and P62 revealed that neuron-specific MFN2 KD significantly decreased the LC3II/LC3I ratio and increased P62 expression after 24 hours of reperfusion (Figure 4C). In addition, we performed MFN2 KD experiments in SY5Y cells and tested its impact on LC3 and P62 during the early reoxygenation phase (Figure 4D). We noticed a strong depletion of autophagic flux in the absence of MFN2, which is in line with our in vivo results. Of note, this effect was I/R-independent and indicates a general dependency of autophagy on MFN2. Changes in the LC3II/LC3I ratio and P62 level only indicate the initiation of autophagy. To obtain information about the actual autophagic flux with the final formation of autophagolysosomes, a luciferase-based assay for autophagic flux was used in SY5Y cells. MFN2 gain and loss-of-function experiments confirmed the relevance of MFN2 for a functional autophagic process under stroke conditions (Figure 4E). These findings suggest that MFN2 KD inhibits autophagosome formation, thereby impairing autophagy under I/R conditions.

We also tested whether neuronal MFN2-cKO alters the cortical mitochondrial fission process measured by phosphorylation of its central regulator protein DRP1 (dynamin-related protein 1) at Ser616. Although there was a detectable increase in p-DRP1, it was not statistically significant (Figure 4F). We repeated this analysis in vitro after MFN2 KD in SY5Y cells and observed a pronounced upregulation of p-DRP1 (Figure 4F) independent of cellular oxygen and glucose levels, indicating that MFN2 depletion actively shifts the fusion-fission balance further toward fission.

MFN2 Interacts With BAG6

We hypothesized that there is an upstream or downstream protein that interacts with MFN2 and mediates MFN2’s effects on autophagy. We used a proteomic approach to identify proteins physically associated with MFN2 in HEK293T cells. Human MFN2, tagged with the HA epitope, was expressed and purified along with its associated proteins from cell lysates through immunoaffinity chromatography using anti-HA antibody. Among others, we found BAG6, which plays an important role in autophagy regulation,24 as an MFN2-associated protein (Figure 5A). To confirm the interaction between MFN2 and BAG6, we performed immunoprecipitation experiments. The results showed that FLAG-tagged BAG6 was detected in immunoprecipitates of cells expressing HA-MFN2 (Figure 5B), thereby confirming the MFN2 and BAG6 interaction. Furthermore, immunofluorescence assays revealed that both proteins were partly colocalized (Figure 5C). Additionally, MFN2 KD resulted in a significant decrease in BAG6 expression post-MCAO (Figure 4C). To pinpoint the specific interaction region of MFN2 with BAG6, we constructed various MFN2 mutants (Figure 5D). Our findings suggest that the TM1 region of MFN2 was indispensable for the interaction of both proteins (Figure 5E).

BAG6 Overexpression Attenuates Cerebral I/R Injury

Intrigued by the regulatory mechanism underlying the MFN2-BAG6 axis, we determined whether BAG6 exerts neuroprotective effects in an acute ischemic stroke model. We stereotactically administered an adeno-associated virus overexpressing BAG6 (AAV9-BAG6) or a corresponding control adeno-associated virus (AAV9-CON) into the striatum of wild-type mice (Figure S4). Remarkably, AAV9-BAG6 mice revealed significantly reduced neurological deficits post-MCAO compared with control mice, which went in line with motor-coordination improvements in the rotarod test (Figure 6A). Moreover, infarct volume was significantly reduced in AAV9-BAG6 mice compared with control mice (Figure 6A). These findings underscore the neuroprotective role of BAG6 for stroke outcome. In contrast to MFN2 overexpression, BAG6 overexpression did not affect cerebral ROS levels (Figure 6A), indicating that BAG6’s neuroprotective effects did not involve antioxidant actions. Notably, no significant effect of BAG6 overexpression on apoptotic or necrotic cell death was detected in our in vitro stroke model, although we noticed a tendency for a reduced Reox-triggered apoptotic peak and an increased necrosis post-Reox (Figure 6B). The plasmid-encoded FLAG-BAG6 overexpression was confirmed in SY5Y cells by Western blot (Figure 6C). We further performed luciferase-based autophagic flux assays in SY5Y cells overexpressing FLAG-tagged BAG6 and found a downregulation of autophagy compared with the control cells expressing an empty pcDNA3.1 plasmid (Figure 6C). To investigate the effects of BAG6 gain-of-function on cell stress and metabolism under stroke conditions, we conducted tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester assays in SY5Y cells to measure the MMP (ΔΨm) and CellROX assays to assess oxidative stress, but observed no differences compared with control cells (Figure 6D).

BAG6 Potentiates Cytoprotective Effects of MFN2 Post-I/R by Amplifying Its Antioxidative Action

To investigate in vivo implications of the MFN2-BAG6 axis, we established the mouse model co-overexpressing MFN2 and BAG6 by stereotactic AAV9-MFN2 and AAV9-BAG6 injection. While neurological deficits and motor-coordination were unaffected in mice co-overexpressing MFN2 and BAG6 compared with mice overexpressing MFN2 alone, they exhibited smaller infarct volumes (Figure 6E). Notably, co-overexpression of MFN2 and BAG6 strongly reduced ROS levels in ischemic brain tissue, compared with overexpression of BAG6 alone (Figure 6E), which had no significant effect on ROS levels poststroke (Figure 6A). Our data indicate a synergistic mode of action, BAG6 exerting its antioxidant effects through MFN2.

Neuronal BAG6 Functions as an Autophagic Suppressor Post-I/R, Supports Mitochondrial Integrity Via Antioxidant Effects, and Compensates for MFN2-Mediated Protection Against Apoptotic Injury

To more comprehensively understand the role of BAG6 in neuronal injury, complementary BAG6 loss-of-function experiments were conducted in vitro to support the gain-of-function findings. First, we depleted BAG6 expression using siRNA-mediated KD and measured continuously apoptotic and necrotic death events with our RealTime-Glo Annexin-V Apoptosis and Necrosis Assay. Although we detected no significant differences in the Reox-induced apoptotic peak, the lack of BAG6 slightly reduced necrotic cell death in the late Reox phase (Figure 7A). The efficient KD of BAG6 was confirmed by Western blot (Figure 7B). Next, we tested the BAG6 KD cells in our autophagic flux assay and found enhanced autophagy compared with control cells, which was in line with our BAG6 gain-of-function experiments and confirms the role of BAG6 as a negative regulator of autophagy in neurons (Figure 7B). We further performed tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester assays to assess mitochondrial function and noticed a reduced MMP under BAG6 KD (Figure 7C). Consistent with this finding was the drastic upregulation of cellular ROS in cells depleted of BAG6, suggesting a predominantly protective role of BAG6 poststroke (Figure 7C). To gain deeper insight into the dynamic interplay between MFN2 and BAG6, we tested whether BAG6 gain-of-function could rescue cells from increased apoptotic death after MFN2 KD (Figure 7D). Indeed, the MFN2-induced apoptotic peak after OGD/R was significantly reduced by BAG6 overexpression and showed a tendency to be even lower than in control cells. Of note, besides this beneficial effect on cell survival, we also detected a mild but significant increase in necrotic death in the early Reox phase (Figure 7D). The upregulation of BAG6 and downregulation of MFN2 in the rescue experiment were confirmed by Western blot analysis (Figure 7E). Moreover, we tested in the rescue experiment whether the autophagic modulator BAG6 influenced the MFN2-KD-mediated downregulation of autophagic flux. However, that was not the case in the early Reox phase, implying no synergistic effect on autophagy of co-expressed BAG6 and MFN2 (Figure 7E).

Discussion

Using gain-of-function and loss-of-function models, this study has yielded several significant findings in clinically relevant ischemic stroke models in vivo and in vitro, demonstrating that MFN2 protects against cerebral I/R injury by decreasing neuronal ROS levels and modulating autophagic flux by interaction with BAG6. Immunoaffinity chromatography combined with mass spectrometry and immunoprecipitation studies revealed that MFN2 directly interacts with BAG6 in a way that may depend on MFN2’s TM1 domain. Neuronal MFN2 knockout was found to reduce BAG6 levels and inhibit autophagy. It is noteworthy that BAG6 expression successfully ameliorated the neurological impairments associated with MFN2 KD. Furthermore, the combination of MFN2 and BAG6 expression resulted in a more substantial neuroprotective effect than that observed with MFN2 expression alone.

In response to ischemia and hypoxia, mitochondria produce excessive ROS, leading to the disruption of membrane integrity and permeability, which subsequently triggers cell death.32 ROS bursts induce the downregulation of mitochondrial fusion proteins and the upregulation of fission proteins, which further exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production. This creates a vicious cycle, ultimately leading to autophagy or apoptosis.33 In a cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury model, downregulation of MFN2 expression induced mitochondrial ROS formation, whereas overexpression of MFN2 alleviated mitochondrial oxidative stress, increased MMP, and protected renal tubular cells from apoptosis.34 The precise mechanisms, via which MFN2 influences ROS formation, remain to be elucidated. Some studies indicated that disruption of the integrity of mitochondria-associated ER membranes can promote mitochondrial fragmentation, impair mitochondrial respiration, and ultimately increase mitochondrial ROS production.35 Indeed, in vivo and in vitro studies using renal tubular cells have shown an increase in ROS formation with compromised mitochondria-associated ER membrane integrity, while restoration of mitochondria-associated ER membrane integrity significantly reduced ROS and partially recovered MMP.34 We do not know if the antioxidant effects of MFN2 overexpression involve ER membrane interactions. Yet, as an upstream regulator of the ER stress protein PERK (protein kinase R - like endoplasmic reticulum kinase), MFN2 can mitigate ROS production by reducing PERK phosphorylation, enhancing mitochondrial respiration, normalizing mitochondrial Ca2+ loading, and rescuing mitochondrial morphology and function.36 Future studies will have to examine the contribution of ER stress responses in the anti-oxidant responses induced by MFN2.

Autophagy is a crucial quality control mechanism intertwined with mitochondrial dynamics, essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis.37,38 As an intracellular decomposition process, autophagy involves the lysosomal degradation of cytoplasmic contents, including denatured proteins and damaged organelles, particularly under conditions of hypoxia-ischemia, starvation, and stress.39 Pharmacological inhibition of autophagy by 3-methyladenine reduced infarct volume in permanent focal cerebral ischemia models, but administered at the same dosage, increased ischemic injury in transient focal cerebral ischemia models via mechanisms involving decreased expression of autophagy-related proteins Beclin1 and ATG7.40 These studies suggest a survival-promoting effect of autophagy under cerebral I/R conditions. Overexpression of MFN2 in primary neurons has previously been shown to augment autophagy, prevent the accumulation of autophagolysosomes under conditions of OGD/R, whereas MFN2 downregulation exacerbated I/R injury by inhibiting autophagosome formation and their fusion with lysosomes in a variety of cellular systems.19 Consistent with the idea that MFN2 ameliorates I/R injury by autophagy promotion, our results revealed that compared with the control group, MFN2 KD significantly decreased the formation of autophagosomes, reduced the lipidation levels of the autophagy marker LC3, and increased P62 expression in cerebral I/R models in vivo and in vitro, whereas MFN2 overexpression increased autophagic flux.

In contrast to our in vivo experiments, in which adenovirus-induced MFN2 overexpression reduced infarct volume, the neuroblastoma cell line SY5Y showed increased necrosis in the late reoxygenation phase after exogenous MFN2 overexpression. At first glance, this finding was difficult to reconcile since mitochondrial fusion is generally regarded as beneficial, as it allows protein transfer between mitochondria for optimal functioning, especially under metabolic stress.41 However, polarized cells like neurons are highly sensitive to local energy shortages: Their energy demand is high at synaptic terminals and requires efficient microtubule-based axonal transport of smaller mitochondria. It was shown before that disturbance of the fusion-fission balance by DRP1 depletion, a critical factor for the fission of the outer mitochondrial membrane, resulted in the absence of synaptic mitochondria.42 Mitochondrial fusion cannot only be achieved by DRP1 depletion, but also by MFN2 overexpression, as demonstrated in pancreatic cancer cells, in which mitochondrial fusion triggered mitophagy and decreased ATP production, which was exploited for inducing cancer cell death.43 As shown in Figure 4F, the phosphorylation level of DRP1 after MFN2 KD showed an increasing trend. The same mechanism may have contributed to the necrotic death of MFN2-overexpressing SY5Y cells exposed to I/R conditions. This injury-promoting effect of MFN2 may have played a subordinate role in vivo in cerebral I/R injury, in which glia and endothelial cells might have compensated mitochondrial fusion-associated energy shortages.

MFN2 is a dynamin-like protein located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, characterized by a complex molecular structure comprising a coiled-coil heptad repeat domain (HR1), 2 transmembrane domains (TM1 and TM2), and a C-terminal heptad repeat domain (HR2). There exists a proline-rich domain interposed between HR1 and TM1, which likely facilitates interactions between MFN2 and other proteins.44 Through immunoaffinity chromatography combined with mass spectrometry analysis, we identified BAG6 as a protein interacting with MFN2, which we confirmed by immunoprecipitation analysis. Unexpectedly, our findings identified the TM1 region of MFN2 as crucial for MFN2 interactions with BAG6. Considering the protein’s structure, the absence of the TM1 region should significantly alter the insertion topology of MFN2 in the outer mitochondrial membrane. From this perspective, the loss of the MFN2–BAG6 interaction might reflect disturbed membrane integration of MFN2. So far, few studies have reported on MFN2’s TM1 region. A case report recently described a child with Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease 2A (CMT2A), who carried a homozygous deletion in the MFN2 gene, encompassing both the proline-rich and TM1 regions.45 Besides endocytic trafficking, mitochondrial function and autophagy are disturbed in CMT2A.46 If disturbed MFN2–BAG6 interactions are responsible for CMT2A neuropathology, remains to be examined in the future.

BAG6, a member of the BAG family, plays a pivotal role in various cellular quality control mechanisms, including autophagy and apoptosis, and is additionally localized to mitochondria despite a missing mitochondrial targeting sequence.25,47 Regarding the BAG6-MFN2 protein interaction, it is plausible to suggest that the outer mitochondrial membrane protein MFN2 acts as a shuttle for BAG6 or promotes recruitment and stabilization of BAG6. Moreover, the C-terminal region of BAG6 was shown to be cleaved by caspase-3, leading to its accumulation in the cytoplasm. This cleaved BAG6 binds to pro-LC3 or LC3I via the LIR motif, thereby inhibiting autophagy and promoting apoptosis.25 Our in vitro findings challenge this conclusion, at least under stroke conditions, as BAG6 overexpression, while reducing autophagy, did not increase apoptotic death, but instead slightly increased necrosis. Regarding BAG6’s dual function in ER-associated degradation of proteins via its ubiquitin-like domain through the Ubiquitin-Proteasome System48 and in autophagic protein degradation via its LIR domain, we prefer to argue that BAG6 is a key regulator between the Ubiquitin-Proteasome System and autophagy. We suggest that the reduced autophagic flux under BAG6 overexpression is due to the decreased availability of ubiquitinated cargo proteins for autophagosomal incorporation, as these are preferentially degraded by the Ubiquitin-Proteasome System. The BAG6-mediated rise of necrosis might be the consequence of a dysregulated ER-associated degradation leading to ER-related cell stress, since the mitochondrial integrity was not affected in our BAG6 gain-of-function experiments. Future studies need to address these questions in more detail. Our previous studies have shown that overexpression of BAG3, another BAG family member, exerts neuroprotective effects after transient MCAO in mice by activating autophagy and inhibiting apoptosis.27 Complementing these earlier findings, we herein demonstrated a neuroprotective role of BAG6 overexpression in the same mouse transient MCAO model.

In contrast to our findings of a beneficial role of cerebral MFN2 in stroke, a former report stated that hearts deficient in MFN1 and MFN2 were protected against acute myocardial infarction.49 These opposing effects of MFN2 can be explained by the morphological differences between neurons and cardiomyocytes: MFN2 plays a critical role in axonal mitochondrial transport through anchoring mitochondria to the Miro/Milton motor-adaptor complex,50 which guarantees synaptic energy supply. As a result, under ischemic stress, loss of MFN2 in neurons impairs mitochondrial dynamics, transport, and quality control, making neurons highly vulnerable, where depletion of MFN2 in cardiomyocytes prevents the spread of ROS and calcium-induced damage in mitochondria and promotes clearance of damaged mitochondria via mitophagy.50

By demonstrating that MFN2 protects against cerebral I/R injury in vivo and in vitro by inhibiting ROS formation and modulating autophagy via interaction with BAG6, our study provides important evidence on how oxidative stress responses and protein degradation are interconnected to regulate neuronal survival and death. Aiming at the removal of proteins and cell organelles exhibiting oxidative damage,39 autophagy plays a decisive role in restoring neuronal integrity post-I/R injury. Considering its dual role in regulating mitochondrial stress responses and denatured protein removal, the here-identified MFN2-BAG6 network offers a promising target for therapeutic interventions aiming at antagonizing the detrimental consequences of oxidative damage after stroke.
