The family wants everything done: a reasonable justification for futile surgery? Illustrative case
Authors: Jacqueline Boyle, Nicholas Comardelle, Alexis Carter, Jeffrey Klopfenstein, Todd McCall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThere is a consensus that withholding aggressive medical care in medically futile situations is ethically sound, even if the patient, surrogate, or family wants everything done. The authors aim to evaluate and discuss the situations in which this request is used as justification for intervention, specifically lifesaving surgery, when a situation is defined by a futile outcome.OBSERVATIONSThe authors utilize the illustrative case of an older female who presented with a traumatic brain injury and Duret hemorrhage, an unfortunately common scenario faced by neurosurgeons, to discuss the complex ethical and practical implications of these situations. There is a consensus that withholding aggressive medical care in medically futile situations is ethically sound even when the patient, surrogate, or family claim to want everything done.1,2 Not typically discussed are cases in which this request is used as justification for intervention, specifically lifesaving surgery, when a situation is defined by a futile outcome. This rather common situation raises the following questions that warrant consideration: What are the potential motivators for surgeons who perform surgery in situations that they recognize as futile? Is it ethical to operate in a medically futile situation in response to family or surrogate demands? How should a surgeon respond to these demands, and what is the most appropriate management of these cases?

An illustrative case is presented to provide context for further discussion of these important questions. While we have no specific training in medical ethics, we have attempted to engage in this subject from a realistic and pragmatic perspective, based on personal experience, that may prompt further discussions.

Illustrative Case

A functionally independent 77-year-old female, with a past medical history of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, was found unresponsive in her driveway at home. She was intubated at the scene and brought by ambulance to the emergency department. On initial examination, her pupils were nonreactive with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3T, indicating she was in a deep coma. A CT scan of her head demonstrated a large acute right subdural hematoma resulting in > 2 cm of midline shift, transtentorial herniation, and a Duret hemorrhage (Fig. 1). During discussions regarding treatment options, the patient’s family, including her daughter and son, requested to have everything done. The patient was taken urgently to the operating room and underwent a decompressive hemicraniectomy and evacuation of the subdural hematoma. Immediately postoperatively her GCS score remained 3T. On postoperative day 1, brain death testing was performed, and the patient was pronounced dead. This case was presented at our department’s peer-reviewed morbidity and mortality conference by the resident physician who performed the surgery. As this patient’s death was considered expected, given the imaging findings and poor examination, the case was classified as standard of care.

On further reflection over the following days, some of the aforementioned questions and concerns about the ethics of this situation arose. Overall, we can safely assume that neurosurgery residents, advanced practice providers, and neurosurgeons (both those recently in practice and those with extensive experience) could benefit from further understanding and training on the ethical management of these complex patient care dilemmas.

Informed Consent

This study describes an emergency intervention with no required consent, and the patient died shortly after the intervention.

Discussion

Defining Futility

The concept of futility has received extensive attention in the literature. Schneiderman and colleagues provide a practical definition of futility that can be applied in this case.3 The authors state “… when physicians conclude (either through personal experience, experiences shared by colleagues, or consideration of published empiric data) that in the last 100 cases a medical treatment has been useless, they should regard the treatment as futile. If a treatment merely preserves permanent unconsciousness or cannot end dependence on intensive medical care, the treatment should be considered futile.”3

In our case, the patient’s advanced age, poor GCS score at presentation, transtentorial herniation with midline shift > 2 cm, and a Duret hemorrhage in the setting of traumatic brain injury (TBI) synergistically created a futile situation. Although most physicians recognize that older patients who undergo decompressive craniotomy for TBI routinely experience poor outcomes compared with younger patients, robust data are lacking for this cohort.4 Data regarding older patients are limited given that most informative randomized controlled trials only enroll patients with an upper age limit of 60 to 65 years.5,6

The presence of a Duret hemorrhage warrants specific consideration. These small, often linear, hemorrhages occur in the midbrain or pons and can be categorized as traumatic or nontraumatic. Intracranial hypotension is a reported example of a nontraumatic Duret hemorrhage with good neurological outcome.7 Radiographic detection of a Duret hemorrhage in the setting of a TBI is a harbinger of a grim neurological prognosis or death.8 There are very few cases in the medical literature of good neurological recovery in patients presenting with a Duret hemorrhage and TBI. Of note, a majority of these patients described in the case reports were younger and ranged from 5 to 37 years of age.9–12 A systematic review and meta-analysis of patients with a Duret hemorrhage from all etiologies was consistent with clinical observations that older patients are more likely to have a poor neurological outcome.8 In only 1 case report do the authors describe a 65-year-old patient with an acute left subdural hematoma with 1.3 cm of midline shift taken for an emergency craniotomy. Postoperative CT and MR imaging was consistent with a new Duret hemorrhage. He was discharged 10 weeks posttrauma with only mild hemiparesis. While this is a rare, favorable postoperative neurological outcome in an older TBI patient, the absence of a preoperative Duret hemorrhage suggests that the timing of earlier operative intervention may have played a role in the outcome.13

Understanding the Motivation to Operate

Any large group of people will have a distribution of inherent moral compasses formed by both personal experience and underlying belief systems; physicians are certainly no exception. Unfortunately, medical decisions made by physicians may be guided by personal gain, whether intentional or subconscious. There will always be some moral outliers who make intentionally immoral decisions for selfish gain, for example, performing futile surgery because they will be paid to do so. Apart from acknowledging and condemning this problem, one hopes that this is a rare exception.

Addressing the notion of reflexive or subconscious medical decisions necessitates a mention of heuristics and cognitive biases. Heuristics, derived from the Greek word heurēka, meaning “I have found,” are mental shortcuts or “rules of thumb” that our brains utilize to find answers to questions.14 The use of heuristics allows for speed and efficiency at the cost of accuracy. Cognitive bias can develop when a heuristic process leads to a consistent, systematic error in decision-making.

Intervention bias in the medical setting is a bias toward action, which can range from simply placing an intravenous line to performing a life-prolonging surgery. This bias toward “doing something” persists even in cases in which intervention will have no clear benefit and may even be to a patient’s detriment compared with no intervention at all.15 There are several described theories to explain the existence of intervention bias. Foy and Filippone suggest that intervention bias is a manifestation of another well-recognized phenomenon, self-interest bias,15 while Ayanian and Berwick suggest that “clinical satisfaction may be greater for doctors when they recommend an intervention.”16 The concept of clinical satisfaction appears less plausible in the setting of futile surgery given that intervention will likely lead to failure. On some level, performing futile surgery may be less uncomfortable for a surgeon than telling a family that there is no intervention that will change the outcome. From an emotional standpoint, a decompressive craniectomy may feel like the path of least resistance to a surgeon who is trying to circumvent feelings of guilt over what may otherwise be perceived as doing nothing.

When discussing intervention bias, one must consider the role that malpractice litigation may play. Foy and Filiponne discuss how the medical liability environment in the United States has created a culture of defensive medicine.15 Fear of malpractice litigation influences physicians to practice defensive medicine and, at times, be swayed by intervention bias to do something, even if it may involve unnecessary testing or aggressive patient management when conservative management is medically appropriate.15

Autonomy in medical decision-making and education is a critical element of resident physician training. Both clinical decisions and surgical execution are completely different experiences when performed alone, without the safety net of an attending physician. Balancing autonomy in resident surgical training with patient beneficence and nonmaleficence has always been a challenge. The general trend has been to favor patient factors over resident training and autonomy, with the hope that utilizing futile medical situations as opportunities for residents to practice performing procedures does not occur.

Observations

When evaluating our case presentation from an ethics perspective, there are two schools of thought we chose for the evaluation: the four-quadrants approach originally described by Jonsen et al.17 and the four principles of medical ethics. The four-quadrants approach quickly proved problematic for our case given that our comatose patient had no advance directive, nullifying the quadrants of patient preferences and quality of life. Instead, we chose to apply the four principles of medical ethics: beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, and justice.18

From the perspective of the patient, operating in a clearly futile situation provides no reasonable benefit. Futile surgery can certainly cause harm, such as pain and suffering, contradicting the principle of nonmaleficence. Conversely, from the family’s perspective, withholding surgery, even if futile, can create a sense of guilt for not doing everything possible. One reasonable, morally acceptable option for surgeons in an emergency futile surgical situation is to not offer surgery. This path provides beneficence by reducing the mental anguish of decision-making at the cost of education and, potentially, autonomy. However, it could also be argued that autonomy is not compromised because surgery is not an effective treatment option. Regarding the concept of justice, resources, such as intensive care unit beds, operating rooms, and financial assistance, are limited. Therefore, utilizing medical resources for a futile case fails the test of justice.

Education Gap

Medical ethics does not take center stage at any level of medical education. Thus, even an experienced surgeon could easily believe that he or she has a moral obligation to operate in a medically futile situation if the family demands intervention. Every medical specialty has unique ethical circumstances that should be highlighted and thoroughly discussed throughout residency training. Routine conferences and didactic lectures provide superb opportunities for ethics education.

Society has shifted toward favoring patient autonomy over the historically favored paternalism. Now more than ever, it is the surgeon’s responsibility to educate families and bridge the gap from the medical professional to the layperson. Appropriate communication between the surgeon and family can greatly reduce the chance that a family will continue to demand futile interventions. However, emergency situations in which decisions must be made within minutes provide the added challenge of time constraints, which can limit effective communication.

Cognitive bias also plays an important role in patient education. Framing bias can develop when information is interpreted based on the context in which it is presented. For example, in our case, the surgeon might tell the family that the patient will die unless she undergoes surgery. This type of statement would likely influence a family to feel that they are obligated to choose surgical intervention. On the other hand, if the surgeon told the family that the best outcome for the patient, even with surgery, is permanent disability and dependence on intensive medical care, the family would likely perceive the situation in a way that opens the door for discussions.

Mental Anguish

The need to make decisions on the care of a loved one in a life-and-death emergency is an extraordinarily emotionally taxing scenario. In a study of 357 intensive care unit patients and 544 family members, Azoulay et al. found that 73% of family members had symptoms of anxiety, 35% developed symptoms of depression, and 50% sought help from a psychologist.19 Although there is a wide variance in the expectations of family involvement in medical decision-making across cultures, such situations are always a profound source of distress.19 Family members become patients when thrust into stressful positions, which is an important consideration in ethical decision-making.

Lessons

Medical care at the end of life is a challenge for providers, patients, and families, and can become even more difficult when a family insists on aggressive intervention in what is deemed a futile situation. The goal of medical practice is to provide ethical, competent, and compassionate care. Our current strategies for meeting these goals are always being challenged, and we must constantly seek new avenues to improve.

As previously discussed, there are several reasons a surgeon may be inclined to perform surgery in a medically futile scenario, ranging from personal bias to a desire to appease the family. Education and communication are the most common underlying challenges of managing medically futile situations, but these themes also present educational opportunities for improvement. Clear explanations and proper framing are critical to avoid unrealistic expectations. At teaching institutions, it is common for a surgical resident to be the first medical provider to deliver updates to the family. Residents, especially junior residents, can be unaware or unsure of the gravity of a situation and instill false hope in a family. To avoid this dilemma, surgical residency education should include opportunities, such as lectures and mentorship programs, that focus on clear communication during these difficult conversations.

Family members may struggle to accept that a loved one has no chance of a meaningful recovery, especially if the illness or accident was unexpected. An important concept is that a family member is not deciding for themselves; they are deciding for their loved one. They need to focus on what the patient would want for his or her quality of life, not just prolonging a life for the sake of survival. Most people have no interest in living in a vegetative state with permanent breathing and feeding tubes. This can be a difficult perspective for families to consider, especially during a stressful, futile situation. It is the job of the provider to be an advocate for the patient and help family members answer these important questions about quality of life.

Furthermore, our case emphasizes the need for deliberate and comprehensive ethics education at all levels of medical training, which is currently lacking. Resident education should not shy away from ethically complex situations and, instead, must provide resident physicians with a framework to utilize in these situations.

While the importance of education and communication should not be undermined, societal and religious factors can play a significant role in how a family will interpret a futile situation. Some families simply do not trust modern medicine, and it is not uncommon to hear, “We will pray for a miracle.” In some religions, the preservation of life should prevail despite all costs. In these circumstances, providers may want to consider additional input by asking for a second opinion or an ethics consultation. Unfortunately, an expedient ethics consultation for an emergency situation in the middle of the night is likely to prove equally futile.

As stated by Giambattista Vico, “In Latin, it was the verb humare, to bury, which gave the primary and proper meaning to the noun, humanitas, human civilization.”20 Death is one of the defining characteristics of the human experience, yet much of modern medicine is focused on avoiding the inevitable. Surgeons must remember that just because something can be done does not mean that it should be done in all circumstances.
