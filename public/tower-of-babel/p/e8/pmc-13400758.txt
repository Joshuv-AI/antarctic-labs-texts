Suspected early Aspergillus-related allergic airway disease with bacterial co-detection in a child with refractory asthma
Authors: Tung Viet Cao, Thuy Minh Ha, Ly Ha Pham, Khiem Van Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) is under-recognized in children with refractory asthma and may be obscured by concurrent respiratory infections. We describe a 10-year-old girl with poorly controlled asthma admitted with fever, productive cough, and wheezing. Investigations revealed marked peripheral eosinophilia (1.48 × 10⁹/L), markedly elevated total immunoglobulin E (1742 IU/mL), positive bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) galactomannan, and positive BAL Aspergillus antigen. Multiplex respiratory polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detected Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, whereas concurrent bacterial cultures, blood culture, fungal culture, and viral testing were negative. High-resolution chest computed tomography (HRCT) demonstrated diffuse ground-glass opacities and bronchial wall thickening without central bronchiectasis or mucus plugging. A. fumigatus–specific IgE was not available, precluding formal classification under contemporary diagnostic criteria. A. fumigatus IgM and IgG were positive on follow-up serology. After empirical antibacterial therapy with limited durable response, oral itraconazole was initiated with sustained clinical improvement, declining total IgE and eosinophils, and stable hepatic function. This presentation is best characterized as suspected early Aspergillus-related allergic airway disease rather than confirmed ABPA. The case highlights three lessons for infectious diseases clinicians: bacterial co-detection by multiplex PCR may anchor reasoning toward infection and delay recognition of allergic fungal airway disease; lower airway Aspergillus biomarkers are supportive but not diagnostic; and the absence of central bronchiectasis does not exclude early disease. Integrated clinical, immunologic, microbiologic, and radiologic reasoning is essential in pediatric refractory asthma.Keywords: Aspergillus, ABPA, Bronchoalveolar lavage, Galactomannan, Co-detection, Eosinophilia

Introduction

Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) is an immune-mediated airway disorder caused by hypersensitivity to Aspergillus fumigatus, occurring most commonly in patients with asthma or cystic fibrosis [1]. In children, ABPA remains under-recognized and represents an important, potentially reversible cause of poorly controlled asthma and persistent respiratory morbidity [2], [3]. Delayed recognition may lead to repeated exacerbations, recurrent systemic corticosteroid exposure, and irreversible airway remodeling [4], [5].

Diagnosis is particularly challenging in children. There are no fully established pediatric-specific diagnostic criteria, and early disease may occur without classic radiologic features such as central bronchiectasis or mucus plugging [6]. Clinical presentations are frequently nonspecific and overlap with severe asthma exacerbations or lower respiratory tract infections, particularly when fever, productive cough, raised inflammatory markers, or positive microbiologic results are present [7], [8]. From an infectious diseases perspective, the detection of bacterial respiratory pathogens by molecular methods may anchor early diagnostic reasoning toward infection and away from underlying allergic fungal airway disease, creating a diagnostic conundrum that is increasingly common as multiplex PCR is used in pediatric respiratory care [7], [9].

Current diagnostic frameworks emphasize the integration of clinical, immunologic, microbiologic, and radiologic information rather than reliance on any single test [1]. The absence of central bronchiectasis does not exclude early disease [1], [10], and nonstandard lower airway biomarkers such as bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) galactomannan or Aspergillus antigen should be interpreted cautiously and only as supportive evidence within a compatible clinical picture [11], [12], [13].

We describe a school-age girl with refractory asthma, marked eosinophilia, markedly elevated total immunoglobulin E, and positive lower airway Aspergillus biomarkers. Multiplex PCR also detected bacterial respiratory pathogens, while A. fumigatus–specific IgE was unavailable and HRCT showed no central bronchiectasis. This case is therefore best characterized as suspected early Aspergillus-related allergic airway disease rather than confirmed ABPA, highlighting the diagnostic complexity created when bacterial co-detection overlaps with features suggestive of allergic fungal airway disease in pediatric refractory asthma.

Case presentation

Initial presentation

A 10-year-old girl with bronchial asthma diagnosed in 2022 and persistent allergic rhinitis was admitted in June 2025 with a 2-week history of productive cough, wheezing, chest tightness, and fever up to 38.5 °C. Despite regular controller therapy with salmeterol/ fluticasone 25/125 µg twice daily via a spacer and montelukast 5 mg daily, she had experienced recurrent exacerbations over the preceding years, including repeated short courses of systemic corticosteroids and several hospital visits for poorly controlled asthma. Her course was consistent with persistent disease activity despite escalation of inhaled therapy.

On admission, oxygen saturation was 96% on room air. Respiratory examination revealed bilateral diffuse rhonchi and coarse crackles.

Laboratory investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation on 24 June 2025 showed a white blood cell count of 9.90 × 10⁹/L with marked eosinophilia (14.9%; absolute eosinophil count 1.48 × 10⁹/L). An extended diagnostic work-up was performed to exclude alternative causes of eosinophilia. Autoimmune screening was unremarkable, including negative antinuclear antibody, anti–double-stranded DNA antibody, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, and anti–glomerular basement membrane antibody. Interferon-gamma release assay for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative. Human immunodeficiency virus serology and Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibodies were negative. Serologic testing for parasitic infections, including Ancylostoma, Paragonimus, Filaria, Strongyloides stercoralis, Toxocara, cysticercosis, and Trichinella spiralis, was negative.

Microbiological evaluation

Microbiological testing initially complicated the clinical picture. Multiplex respiratory PCR on a sputum specimen (24 June 2025) detected Streptococcus pneumoniae (cycle threshold [Ct] 28.46), Haemophilus influenzae (Ct 28.49), and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; Ct 32.36). A subsequent multiplex respiratory bacterial PCR from a nasopharyngeal specimen (5 July 2025) was again positive for S. pneumoniae. However, nasopharyngeal bacterial culture, blood culture, mycobacterial culture, fungal culture, and respiratory viral testing were negative. The discrepancy between PCR detection and negative culture raises the possibility of asymptomatic colonization or molecular co-detection rather than active infection in the lower airway.

Allergological and functional assessment

Skin prick testing for respiratory allergens was positive for house dust mites and dog dander; Aspergillus-specific skin prick testing was not performed. Spirometry revealed mild restrictive ventilatory impairment with a positive bronchodilator response.

Imaging

HRCT of the chest demonstrated diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities and diffuse bronchial wall thickening without central bronchiectasis or mucus plugging (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). These findings were nonspecific but compatible with active airway and parenchymal inflammation.

Lower airway and serologic Aspergillus evaluation

Given persistent symptoms, eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, and inconclusive distinction between infection and allergic airway disease, bronchoscopy with BAL was performed. BAL galactomannan was positive on 24 June 2025 (index 1.632; reference < 0.5), and BAL Aspergillus antigen was positive. Follow-up serology for A. fumigatus showed positive IgM and IgG (309.8 U/mL and 93.8 U/mL on 24 November 2025; 178.6 U/mL and 185.2 U/mL on 22 December 2025), with decline to indeterminate levels by 18 January 2026 (68.7 U/mL and 72.7 U/mL respectively). A. fumigatus–specific IgE was not available at our institution and could not be obtained during follow-up. Aspergillus biomarker trends are summarized in Table 2.

Diagnostic synthesis

The combination of refractory asthma, recurrent exacerbations, marked peripheral eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, and positive lower airway Aspergillus biomarkers raised suspicion for early Aspergillus-related allergic airway disease. This suspicion was further supported by positive follow-up A. fumigatus IgM/IgG serology. The absence of central bronchiectasis on HRCT did not exclude early disease. However, the absence of documented A. fumigatus–specific IgE and the lack of Aspergillus-specific skin prick testing precluded formal classification under contemporary ABPA diagnostic frameworks [1]. Concurrent multiplex PCR detection of bacterial respiratory pathogens in the setting of negative cultures suggested airway colonization or molecular co-detection rather than definitive active lower airway infection [7], [9].

Treatment and follow-up

During the acute admission in early July 2025, the patient received cefotaxime, oral prednisolone, and inhaled bronchodilator therapy for presumed bronchopneumonia complicating an asthma exacerbation. She continued to have recurrent respiratory symptoms over the following months, including productive cough, nasal obstruction, and intermittent dyspnea. Oral itraconazole 100 mg twice daily was initiated in October 2025, and maintenance inhaled therapy was reassessed and adjusted. By the November 2025 follow-up she had no cough or rhinorrhea, and by 18 January 2026 she remained clinically stable without respiratory symptoms. Total serum IgE decreased to 1480 IU/mL, peripheral eosinophils to 10.5%, and the absolute eosinophil count to 0.81 × 10⁹/L. Hepatic transaminases remained within reference limits (AST 28.5 U/L, ALT 11.5 U/L). Itraconazole was subsequently discontinued, and the patient remains under outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis is increasingly recognized in children yet remains uncommon and is probably under-diagnosed. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis estimated pooled prevalences of Aspergillus sensitization of 16.1% and of ABPA of 9.9% among asthmatic children, although the wide confidence intervals, marked heterogeneity, and predominance of tertiary-center data indicate that the true burden is uncertain and likely underestimated [6]. Reported pediatric cases cluster in school-age children and adolescents, the age group to which our 10-year-old patient belongs [3], [5], [6]. Compared with ABPA in adults, childhood ABPA more often lacks the classic radiologic hallmarks of central bronchiectasis and mucus plugging early in its course, and its clinical picture overlaps closely with severe asthma exacerbations and lower respiratory tract infection, so that fever, productive cough, and positive microbiologic results may steer clinicians away from an allergic fungal process [1], [6], [7]. Because delayed recognition permits progression to fixed airflow obstruction and irreversible airway damage, early identification is critical to preserving long-term lung function [2], [4], [5].

This case illustrates a diagnostic conundrum at the interface of pediatric infectious diseases, respiratory medicine, and clinical immunology. The central question is how to interpret refractory asthma with eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, and positive lower airway Aspergillus biomarkers when the acute presentation is dominated by features suggestive of bacterial infection. We argue that the most defensible diagnostic label in this patient is suspected early Aspergillus-related allergic airway disease rather than confirmed ABPA, because A. fumigatus–specific IgE was not available at our institution, Aspergillus-specific skin prick testing was not performed, and HRCT showed no central bronchiectasis or mucus plugging [1]. This case is presented not to claim a confirmed diagnosis but to expose the reasoning required when established criteria cannot be fully satisfied at the bedside.

The role of bacterial co-detection in obscuring allergic fungal airway disease

Multiplex respiratory PCR detected S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and MRSA in sputum, with subsequent nasopharyngeal PCR again positive for S. pneumoniae. These results may have contributed to the initial interpretation of an acute febrile bronchopneumonic presentation and to the inflammatory response. However, they do not adequately explain the broader phenotype of persistent refractory asthma, sustained eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, or the subsequent positive Aspergillus biomarkers [1], [7]. Negative blood culture, negative bacterial culture from nasopharyngeal specimens, and negative fungal culture illustrate a well-recognized limitation of molecular methods: PCR positivity reflects nucleic acid presence and does not, in itself, distinguish active lower airway infection from upper airway colonization or transient carriage [8], [9]. In the context of pediatric asthma, anchoring on bacterial PCR positivity risks delaying recognition of an underlying allergic fungal airway process, particularly when antibiotic-treated bronchopneumonia fails to produce durable remission of respiratory symptoms.

Imaging: absence of bronchiectasis does not exclude early disease

Central bronchiectasis and mucus plugging are characteristic but late features of ABPA [1], [10]. In pediatric practice, early disease may exist before classic structural airway abnormalities develop, and reliance on advanced radiologic features alone may delay recognition until irreversible damage has occurred [2], [6]. In this patient HRCT showed diffuse ground-glass opacities and bronchial wall thickening without central bronchiectasis. These findings were nonspecific but compatible with active eosinophilic airway and parenchymal inflammation. Their absence should not be used to rule out early Aspergillus-related disease, but neither do these finding confirm it; they remain supportive contextual evidence only.

Lower airway biomarkers: supportive, not diagnostic

Positive BAL galactomannan and positive BAL Aspergillus antigen increased suspicion of lower airway Aspergillus involvement at a time when the clinical picture was confounded by possible bacterial infection and HRCT lacked classic advanced features [8], [12], [13]. These biomarkers were originally developed and validated for invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in immunocompromised patients; their performance characteristics in immunocompetent children with allergic airway disease are less well defined [12], [13]. Accordingly, they should be interpreted as supportive evidence of Aspergillus presence in the lower airway compartment, integrated with eosinophilic, immunologic, and radiologic data, but should not be used in isolation to establish or refute a diagnosis of ABPA [1], [11]. In this patient the BAL findings contributed to a more coherent diagnostic framework but did not transform a suspected diagnosis into a confirmed one.

Serologic findings and the missing A. fumigatus-specific IgE

Total IgE was markedly elevated, and A. fumigatus IgM and IgG were positive during follow-up before declining to indeterminate levels by January 2026. These trends are supportive of Aspergillus-related immune activity but do not substitute for A. fumigatus–specific IgE, which is a core component of contemporary ABPA criteria [1], [2]. The unavailability of A. fumigatus–specific IgE, combined with the absence of Aspergillus-specific skin prick testing, is the principal reason this case cannot be classified as confirmed ABPA. We emphasize this limitation prominently to model the diagnostic restraint that infectious diseases physicians should exercise when criteria are incomplete, however suggestive the surrounding evidence.

Treatment response: informative but not confirmatory

Despite empirical antibacterial therapy and asthma management during the acute admission, the patient continued to experience recurrent respiratory symptoms over the following months. After itraconazole was initiated in October 2025, she demonstrated sustained clinical improvement by November 2025 and remained well at follow-up in January 2026, This was accompanied by declining total IgE and eosinophil counts with stable hepatic function. The temporal association suggests that Aspergillus-related airway involvement may have contributed to ongoing disease activity [7], [14]. However, response to antifungal therapy alone does not confirm ABPA; corticosteroid co-administration, natural disease fluctuation, and azole anti-inflammatory effects are plausible contributors and must be acknowledged.

Limitations

We acknowledged several limitations. First, the absence of documented A. fumigatus–specific IgE and Aspergillus-specific skin prick testing prevents definitive classification under contemporary diagnostic frameworks [1], [2]. Second, the microbiologic data were derived primarily from molecular assays, which do not reliably distinguish active infection from colonization or co-detection [8], [9]. Third, as a single case, this report cannot support broad recommendations regarding the routine use of bronchoscopy or BAL biomarker testing in pediatric refractory asthma. Fourth, clinical improvement after itraconazole cannot be attributed solely to antifungal effect because corticosteroid therapy and adjustment of maintenance inhaled therapy occurred concurrently. These limitations do not diminish the educational value of the case but require explicit acknowledgement in the diagnostic interpretation.

Conclusion

In school-age children with refractory asthma, eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, and persistent or recurrent respiratory symptoms, suspected early Aspergillus-related allergic airway disease should be considered even when HRCT lacks central bronchiectasis or mucus plugging. Bacterial co-detection by multiplex PCR may complicate diagnostic interpretation and should not be treated as a stand-alone explanation. Lower airway Aspergillus biomarkers and positive A. fumigatus IgM/IgG are supportive findings, but without A. fumigatus–specific IgE, such cases should be described as suspected rather than confirmed ABPA. This case highlights the importance of diagnostic restraint and integrated reasoning at the interface of pediatric infectious diseases and allergic airway disease.
