Development and validation of a risk identification model for frailty in stroke survivors: new evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Jiaxian Wang, Rick Yiu Cho Kwan, Lorna Kwai Ping Suen, Simon Ching Lam, Ning Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Stroke survivors with frailty exhibit elevated rates of complications, mortality, disability, and hospital readmission. As frailty represents an early, reversible, and preventable stage of disability, developing a reliable risk identification model is essential. This study aimed to develop and validate a risk model for frailty among stroke survivors using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).MethodsData were extracted from the CHARLS database. Stroke survivors were identified and assessed across 30 indicators, including socio-demographic, physical, psychological, cognitive, and social variables. The data were divided by year, with 2013 and 2015 as the development set and 2018 and 2020 as the validation set. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was employed for variable selection. Logistic regression models were then developed based on univariate and LASSO-selected predictors. A nomogram was constructed to facilitate risk visualization. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis were used to evaluate model calibration and clinical utility.FindingsA total of 2,188 stroke survivors from the 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 follow-ups were included. Approximately 68% exhibited symptoms of frailty. Significant group differences were found by age, marital status, living alone, hypertension, and self-reported health status (all p < 0.05). Age, poor sleep quality, impaired balance, nervousness/anxiety, and living alone emerged as independent risk factors for frailty. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the development and validation sets was 0.833 and 0.838, respectively. Interpretation: The model derived from CHARLS data identified 5 readily assessable predictors (age, sleep quality, balance, anxiety, and living alone), allowing for early screening of frailty without specialized instruments. It demonstrated superior discriminatory performance compared to models from smaller-sample studies, supporting targeted interventions and providing valuable insights for identifying high-risk stroke survivors.InterpretationThe model derived from CHARLS data identified 5 readily assessable predictors (age, sleep quality, balance, anxiety, and living alone), allowing for early screening of frailty without specialized instruments. Introduction

Frailty is prevalent in older stroke survivors [1]; it is a non-specific state that refers to a decrease in physiological reserve function or various functional abnormalities in older people, leading to increased vulnerability and reduced stress resistance, which can lead to falls, disability, readmission, other adverse outcomes, and possibly death [2]. In addition, a meta-analysis showed [3] that the overall prevalence of frailty among stroke survivors is about 40%, and the percentage of survivors in the pre-frailty stage is as high as 45%. However, frailty is a dynamic developmental process that serves as a pre-existing state of clinically dangerous events that can reflect the health status of stroke survivors and identify poor prognosis [1]. Given that the development of frailty is insidious and stroke survivors are not readily aware of its symptoms, early identification of people at high risk of frailty and intervention would be of great significance in reversing the state of frailty, reducing the incidence of adverse outcomes, and improving the functional status of stroke survivors and their quality of life. Therefore, how to identify stroke survivors with a high risk of frailty at an early stage and how to reverse or delay the decline of stroke survivors have become urgent problems.

At this stage, research on frailty in older stroke survivors is currently limited to frailty assessment tools mainly frailty phenotype (FP), frailty index (FI), and corresponding derivatives [4]. Considering the professional and structural deficiencies of the health care system, the difficulty of obtaining complete frailty-related data after patient consultation, and the busy clinical workload facing health care workers [5], there is a need to construct a simple and reliable frailty risk identification model under the current health care system [6]. However, previously constructed models for predicting frailty in stroke survivors have been useful in clinical applications, but they still have some limitations and problems. In Zhang et al. [7], for example, the model they constructed was mainly based on post-stroke survivor data in hospitals, and the data source was relatively homogeneous, which led to the lack of generalizability of the model when applying it to a wide range of stroke survivors in the community. In addition, their model is deficient in variable selection and does not adequately consider important social, psychological, and behavioral factors. In terms of validation, the model was validated only in a small sample. Meanwhile, existing frailty screening tools require direct patient assessment. However, many stroke patients live in the community after discharge from the hospital, and they may not be aware of the onset of frailty. We need a model that utilizes community data to identify at-risk individuals prior to frailty screening, but such a model is currently lacking. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to establish an accurate frailty prediction model using data from the CHARLS database, followed by rigorous validation of the model using another independent dataset from the same database. Through this research, we aim to investigate the identification of frailty risk among older stroke survivors based on the CHARLS database, providing solid scientific evidence and decision support for early detection and prevention of frailty among this vulnerable population.

Methods

The study was reported following the Transparent Reporting of Individual Prognostic or Diagnostic Multivariate Predictive Models (TRIPOD) statement [8]. Figure 1 shows the flow chart of this study.

Flowchart of this study

Source of data

The data analyzed in our development and validation sets originated from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey led by the National School of Development at Peking University [9]. The project employed a four-stage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) sampling design: (1) Counties/districts were stratified by region, urban-rural status, and GDP per capita; (2) Villages/communities within selected counties were sampled via PPS; (3) Households were systematically mapped using GIS technology [10]; (4) Eligible individuals aged ≥ 45 years and their spouses were randomly selected per household. The baseline survey (2011) covered 17,708 respondents from 10,257 households across 450 villages in 150 counties/districts of 28 Chinese provinces, with follow-ups conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 [11].

Participants

In this study, older adults who participated in all four surveys simultaneously in 2013 (T1), 2015 (T2), 2018 (T3), and 2020 (T4) were considered. However, respondents with missing data exceeding 30% of the total variables were excluded from the analysis [12]. Additionally, only those who were ≥ 60 years old at T1 and had a history of stroke during the period from T1 to T4 were included in the final sample.

Outcome: frailty

Following an in-depth review of the relevant literature [13] is study adopted a conceptual framework for frailty grounded in the Frailty Index (FI) [14], and aligned with components from the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) [15], Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS) [16], Functional Independence Measure for Stroke (FIM-Stroke) [17], and Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI) [5, 18]. This multidimensional model comprises four recognized domains: physical, psychological, cognitive, and social frailty. Frailty in this study is defined by the presence of impairments in one or more of these domains.

Somatic frailty is characterized as the loss or weakening of bodily functions, including health status, difficulty walking, balance, and fatigue [19]. Psychological frailty is defined as a decline or weakening of an individual’s mental state and psychological functioning, including depression, anxiety, and coping skills [20]. Cognitive frailty involves an individual’s ability to think and remember. This includes the ability to understand, learn, remember, and solve problems [21]. Social frailty refers to vulnerability in a person’s social relationships and social support. This can manifest as social isolation, lack of close relationships, social loneliness, or inadequate social skills [22]. Social frailty can lead to emotional problems, mental health distress, and physical health challenges, including marital status, living alone, social activities, work situation and financial support [23].

The FI was calculated by dividing the number of diseases or deficits present in an individual by the total number of diseases or deficits considered within the assessment. Therefore, we set thresholds for the definition of a frailty state: FI ≥ 0.25 indicates that the older adult is in a state of frailty; FI < 0.12 indicates that the older adult shows no signs of frailty; and FI value between 0.12 and − 0.25 is considered a pre-frailty state [4].

Predictors

In developing the frailty risk prediction model for stroke survivors, we based variable selection on the Frailty Index (FI) and referenced items from established scales, including the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), Functional Independence Measure-Stroke (FIM-Stroke), and Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS). Correlation analyses were used to identify variables significantly associated with frailty, resulting in the selection of 30 key predictors from the CHARLS dataset—such as age, gender, and marital status—to ensure a comprehensive and accurate assessment. For consistency and validation, the same set of variables was retained in the model’s validation phase. The predictors were organized into four dimensions:

CHARLS socio-demographic aspects

Socio-demographics refer to the information about the social status, demographic characteristics, and behavior of individuals or groups. It includes age, sex, education level, and permanent residence address.

Aspects of the physical condition of the CHARLS database

Variables in this domain—such as unintentional weight loss, fatigue, visual acuity, hearing, walking difficulty, and balance—were self-reported and categorized using standard three-level scales (e.g., good/fair/poor) [20]. These indicators reflect the participants’ perceived health and functional status [24, 25].

Aspects of the psychological and cognitive impact of the CHARLS database

Cognitive function was assessed via immediate and delayed word recall tests, with scores based on the number of correctly recalled words. Depression and anxiety were measured using self-reported frequency of symptoms, categorized as “frequent” or “infrequent.” Coping ability was assessed by participants’ perceived capacity to manage stress, with responses indicating varying levels of difficulty and support needed.

Aspects of the social impact of the CHARLS database

This dimension included marital status, living arrangement (alone or with others), frequency of social interaction, employment status, financial support, and insurance coverage. All were dichotomized (yes/no or present/absent) to reflect the presence or absence of social and financial resources.

Sample size and missing data

The only dataset used in this study was from the CHARLS surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The sample size was calculated for this study using the sample size estimation method proposed by Richard D Riley and his team [26] for developing clinical prediction models. The sample was screened to include only individuals with a history of stroke who were aged 60 years or older at T1 and to exclude those who lacked key information (e.g., body mass index, family status, and physical dysfunction) by retaining duplicate entries to replace the most recent data, resulting in a final valid sample of 2,188 individuals. The specific process is described in Fig. 2.

Subject selection flow chart of this study

To address the problem of missingness in the included data, we used the multiple imputation method to effectively deal with it. This approach involved predicting the missing values and generating multiple complete datasets via Monte Carlo simulation. This approach allowed us to capture the uncertainty in the data while maintaining the original relationships among variables. Each of these datasets underwent rigorous statistical analysis, and the optimal results were selected based on a scoring function. This approach minimized information loss and bias, ensuring the reliability and accuracy of our findings.

Statistical methods

The study divided the data by year into a development set (2013, 2015; n = 2188) and a validation set (2018, 2020; n = 2188).

Data were divided by year into a development set (2013 and 2015; n = 2,188) and a validation set (2018 and 2020; n = 2,188). Logistic regression was employed to construct the frailty risk identification model, as it is widely used for assessing disease-related risk factors. To enhance interpretability and clinical applicability, the model was visualized using a nomogram.

In addition to univariate analysis, we performed correlation analysis to examine associations among variables. To assess multicollinearity, we calculated Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs), using a threshold of VIF < 5 to indicate acceptable collinearity levels. This ensured that the predictors included in subsequent modeling were sufficiently independent, thereby enhancing the robustness of the model. Following the reviewers’ methodological recommendation, we adopted a more inclusive threshold of p < 0.1 in the univariate screening stage to avoid prematurely excluding potentially relevant predictors. This threshold was applied to improve the sensitivity of variable selection during the initial phase. Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis [27, 28] with 10-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ) corresponding to the minimum mean squared error (MSE). LASSO was employed to minimize collinearity and overfitting among the predictors [29], ultimately identifying a set of variables with strong predictive utility. These selected variables were then entered into the multivariable logistic regression model for final risk estimation.

Development of risk identification model

After a combination of the LASSO algorithm and cross-validation, we identified the five most important features that were recognized as valid predictors of frailty status in stroke patients. Subsequently, these selected predictors were introduced into multifactorial logistic regression analysis to assess the likelihood of an observed subject belonging to a specific category by calculating the probability and exploring the deeper links between these predictors and the frailty status of stroke patients.

Among the results of multifactorial logistic regression analysis, we focused on the predictors with p < 0.05, which showed statistically significant differences and were regarded as key determinants of the frailty status of stroke patients. To highlight the significance of these key predictors, we used a nomogram model for visual presentation, helping researchers and clinicians clearly understand the deep connection between predictors and outcome variables.

Validation of risk identification model

In this study, we verified the validity and clinical utility of the risk identification model by analyzing the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), area under the curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA). First, we assessed the discriminative ability of the model using the ROC and AUC. The ROC curve describes the relationship between the true-positive and false-positive rates of the model at different thresholds, whereas the AUC quantifies the model’s classification performance. By calculating the AUC values, we were able to assess the ability of the prediction model in discriminating between the frailty states of stroke survivors.

Additionally, to fully assess the performance of the model, we prepared calibration curves and DCA. By comparing the proximity of the calibration curve with the ideal curve, we determined whether the model was overfitted or underfitted. DCA is a method of assessing the clinical utility of a model, which reflects the actual effectiveness of the model in clinical decision-making by plotting the net benefit curves under different threshold probabilities to assess the performance of the model under various decision strategies. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Figure 1 shows the flowchart of this study.

To maximize the power and generalizability of the results, we used relevant stroke survivors from the CHARLS database from 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2022 for our modeling and validation sets. All analyses in this study were performed using R software (version 4.1.3).

Ethical consideration

This paper is a retrospective study based on the CHARLS database. Participant information was hidden prior to the study. No participant informed consent was required, and there was no ethical conflict. The original version of CHARLS was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants signed an informed consent form at the time of participation. The study followed the guiding principles of the Declaration of Helsinki

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

In this study, 2,188 data identified as stroke survivors were included, of whom 1,124 (51.37%) were males. Of these participants, 1492 individuals (68.19%) in the development set exhibited frailty, while 1517 individuals (69.33%) in the validation set displayed frailty. We found no statistically significant difference in the detection rate of frailty in stroke survivors between the two sets.

These dimensions included age, marital status, residential status (living alone or not), hypertension, level of social activity, nighttime sleep duration, and self-perceived health, as detailed in Table 1. Visual inspection of the heatmap (Fig. 3) revealed stronger correlations between frailty and several key variables, including pain, balance impairment, living alone, life satisfaction, nervousness and anxiety, and physical fatigue. Conversely, weaker associations were observed with other variables [32] These findings suggest that frailty in stroke survivors is more strongly influenced by a specific subset of psychosocial and physical factors.

Validation of the risk identification model

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated using the development and validation sets to assess the ability of the risk identification model to discriminate between stroke survivors with onset frailty. The results showed that the AUC for the development set was 0.833 (95% CI = 0.810–0.855), with a specificity and sensitivity of 79.4% and 72.8%, respectively; the AUC for the validation set was 0.8376 (95% CI = 0.802–0.874), with a specificity and sensitivity of 80.0% and 75.3%, respectively. These values indicated that the model had a good predictive effect in distinguishing between frail and non-frail stroke survivors (Fig. 7A and B) and good discriminative power and predictive value in correctly identifying frail and non-frail stroke survivors.

Discussion

Summary of findings and model performance

This study aimed to identify key predictors of frailty among older stroke survivors and to develop a clinically applicable risk prediction model. Utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which encompasses over 17,000 participants across 28 provinces, we analyzed a subset of 2,188 older adults with a history of stroke. Frailty was found to be highly prevalent in this population, affecting 68.19% of the development cohort and 69.33% of the validation cohort. Through univariate screening, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, and multivariable logistic modeling, five independent predictors of frailty were identified: older age, impaired balance, nervousness and anxiety, living alone, and poor sleep quality. The final model demonstrated excellent discriminative performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.833 and 0.838 in the development and validation sets, respectively. Calibration curves closely aligned with the ideal reference line, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good model fit (p > 0.05). Decision curve analysis further confirmed the model’s clinical utility, showing positive net benefit across a range of thresholds. A web-based dynamic nomogram was developed to facilitate individualized frailty risk assessment by clinicians. These findings underscore that frailty in older stroke survivors is shaped by both physical and psychosocial factors. Moreover, a concise model based on easily obtainable variables offers practical value for early screening in both clinical practice and community health settings.

Practicality and scientific validity of the risk identification model

The strength of our model lies in both its empirical robustness and translational potential. By drawing from a large, nationally representative longitudinal dataset, the model achieves strong external validity and generalizability. High AUC values and well-aligned calibration curves underscore its excellent predictive performance and discriminative ability across diverse subgroups. A key feature of the model is its integration of conceptual frameworks from the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), Functional Independence Measure for Stroke (FIM-Stroke), and Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS). Frailty was operationalized across four well-recognized domains—physical, psychological, social, and cognitive—to enhance screening accuracy and construct validity.

In addition to univariate analysis, correlation analysis was conducted to examine interrelationships among candidate variables. Collinearity diagnostics were performed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), confirming the absence of multicollinearity. LASSO regression was employed to select the most relevant predictors while minimizing model overfitting. These selected predictors were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate odds ratios and p-values. To promote individualized frailty risk estimation and facilitate clinical application, a dynamic web-based nomogram was developed based on the final model.

Notably, physical frailty emerged as the predominant subtype, consistent with prior evidence indicating that stroke survivors commonly experience impairments in motor function, self-care, and overall physical resilience. Importantly, the predictors included in our model are easily accessible in community and primary care settings, requiring no advanced imaging, biomarkers, or specialized equipment [34]. This greatly enhances the model’s feasibility for broad implementation, particularly in resource-limited environments. Furthermore, the user-friendly web-based nomogram enables healthcare providers and public health workers to input patient-specific data and obtain immediate frailty risk estimates, supporting proactive and targeted interventions.

Pervasiveness and convenience of the frailty risk identification model

The simplicity of our model is one of its primary strengths. It incorporates only five predictors—age, balance, nervousness and anxiety, sleep quality, and living status—all of which are routinely collected during standard community health screenings. This minimal clinical input requirement enhances accessibility and ease of application in primary care settings and in resource-limited areas where advanced diagnostic technologies may not be available [35]. Because the model relies on self-reported and observational data, it can be consistently implemented even by non-specialist healthcare providers. In addition, the model’s structure accommodates the supplementation of missing data, ensuring continuity in clinical decision-making even when patient records are incomplete. This feature minimizes the need for additional patient visits or invasive diagnostic procedures [36, 37]. The model thus facilitates timely identification of at-risk individuals, enabling early intervention strategies to slow the progression of frailty and improve the quality of life for older stroke survivors.

Analysis of risk factors associated with frailty

Correlation analysis of age, sleep quality, and frailty in older stroke survivors

Age was confirmed as a significant predictor of frailty among older stroke survivors. The prevalence of frailty increased progressively with age, particularly among individuals aged 80 years and above. Between 2013 and 2015, 49.03% of stroke survivors in this age group were classified as frail; by 2018–2022, this rate had risen to 51.04%. This age-related trend highlights the biological vulnerability and cumulative health burden experienced by the oldest-old population [37].

Sleep quality also emerged as a critical determinant of frailty. Our analysis indicated that older stroke survivors with poor sleep quality had approximately six times the risk of developing frailty compared to those with good sleep quality. This finding aligns with existing literature linking fragmented sleep, short sleep duration, and poor subjective sleep quality to increased frailty risk. Sleep disruptions—such as circadian rhythm reversal and excessive daytime napping, both common among stroke survivors—can hinder physiological recovery and cognitive function, thereby exacerbating frailty. These results underscore the importance of routine sleep assessments and targeted interventions, including cognitive behavioral therapy and sleep hygiene education, in this high-risk population [38].

Frailty due to the interaction between nervousness, anxiety, and living alone

Psychological stressors and social isolation were significant predictors of frailty in this cohort. An increasing number of older adults in China now live alone—a trend reflected in our dataset, with the proportion rising from 53.88% in 2013–2015 to 62.25% in 2018–2022. Living alone (OR = 1.564) and experiencing nervousness and anxiety (OR = 2.200) were both significantly associated with elevated frailty risk.

Chronic psychological stress is known to impair immune function, dysregulate metabolic processes, and increase cardiovascular risk, all of which accelerate physical decline. The absence of social support may delay medical intervention, reduce adherence to treatment and rehabilitation, and extend recovery periods [39]. The synergistic effect of psychological distress and social isolation creates a compounding risk profile, reinforcing the need for integrated interventions [40]. These may include community engagement programs, peer support groups, and improved access to mental health services—all of which can play a critical role in mitigating frailty risk.

Role of balance in influencing frailty among stroke survivors

Post-stroke impairments in balance and coordination were also major contributors to frailty. Our analysis identified poor balance as a strong predictor of frailty (p < 0.001). Stroke survivors commonly experience gait disturbances, muscle weakness, and proprioceptive deficits, all of which increase the likelihood of falls and injury [41]. These physical limitations not only restrict mobility but also erode independence, creating a feedback loop that exacerbates frailty progression. The loss of balance often limits engagement in activities of daily living, which in turn intensifies both physical dependence and psychological distress [42]. Interventions such as targeted physical therapy, vestibular rehabilitation, and progressive strength training have shown efficacy in improving functional mobility and reducing fall risk. Nutritional support—including adequate protein intake and vitamin D supplementation—may also enhance musculoskeletal resilience [43]. Therefore, addressing balance impairment should be considered a core element of comprehensive frailty prevention strategies in older stroke survivors.

High incidence of frailty in older stroke survivors

The longitudinal design of the CHARLS dataset al.lowed us to examine frailty trends in stroke survivors over nearly a decade, revealing a persistently high—and slightly increasing—prevalence of frailty, rising from 68.19 to 69.33%. This elevated rate is consistent with findings from other large-scale studies, including Taylor et al. [29], who reported a 71% frailty prevalence among stroke survivors. Frailty-related symptoms—such as fatigue, muscle weakness, diminished endurance, and impaired self-care—substantially reduce independence and quality of life [44]. These findings highlight the urgent need for standardized frailty screening and risk identification tools within stroke care pathways. Early identification enables timely interventions, including physical rehabilitation, nutritional optimization, and psychosocial support, which can significantly enhance patient outcomes [45]. Future research should aim to externally validate this model in other populations and clinical contexts, and to integrate additional predictors such as inflammatory biomarkers, cognitive function assessments, and genomic indicators [46, 47]. The development of comprehensive, individualized care plans tailored to frailty risk profiles may represent the next frontier in improving long-term outcomes for older stroke survivors [48].

Limitations and recommendation

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although the CHARLS dataset provides comprehensive coverage of 150 cities across China, it is subject to attrition bias inherent to longitudinal studies, largely due to participant migration and mortality. While migration is assumed to occur randomly, individuals with longer life expectancy are more likely to complete follow-up assessments, potentially skewing model predictions in their favor. In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic caused a notable disruption in data collection, with surveys suspended from 2020 until December 2022 and only resuming in 2023. Furthermore, modifications to the CHARLS questionnaire limited the availability of frailty assessment variables to the 2013 and 2015 datasets.

To address this, we integrated components from the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), and a frailty index comprising 30 variables. While this approach enhanced the model’s comprehensiveness, it also introduced complexity and some degree of uncertainty. Another significant limitation was the absence of data on stroke severity and therapeutic interventions. The CHARLS database does not include clinical measures such as NIH Stroke Scale scores, thrombolysis history, or rehabilitation intensity, which restricted our ability to evaluate their confounding or mediating effects on frailty. Given the clinical relevance of stroke severity to frailty progression, this omission should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, due to demographic, ethnic, and lifestyle differences across populations, the model’s generalizability remains untested outside of the Chinese context. The absence of external validation in non-Chinese cohorts represents a critical gap, especially as racial and ethnic variations in frailty phenotypes and disparities in healthcare access may influence model performance. Future research should prioritize external validation across diverse populations and incorporate clinical stroke metrics to strengthen the model’s clinical utility and contextual relevance.

Conclusion

We developed and validated a risk identification model for frailty in stroke survivors using the CHARLS database, representing population-level data from mainland China. The logistic regression model demonstrated strong discrimination and calibration performance in both internal and external validation, indicating its practical utility and potential generalizability to broader populations. Notably, the model identified five easily accessible, non-invasive predictors—age, sleep quality, balance, nervousness and anxiety, and living alone—as independent risk factors for frailty in older stroke survivors. These findings suggest that community healthcare providers can feasibly implement early frailty screening without the need for advanced diagnostics, thereby enabling timely interventions to mitigate frailty progression and improve health outcomes in this vulnerable population.
