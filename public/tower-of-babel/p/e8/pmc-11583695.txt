Entrectinib-Induced Myocarditis and Acute Heart Failure Responding to Steroid Treatment: A Case Report
Authors: Mehraab N Majeed, Subramanian Venkatesan, Dionysis Papadatos-Pastos, Tanya Ahmad, Martin Forster, Polyvios Demetriades, Daniel Johnathan Hughes, Sarah Benafif, Siow Ming Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 72-year-old man presented to his general practitioner with worsening dyspnea and was diagnosed with having recurrent ROS1-positive stage IIIB NSCLC 8 years after initial diagnosis and radical treatment for early stage disease. He was subsequently started on entrectinib but required hospital admissions for recurrent acute kidney injuries on a background of chronic kidney disease. His entrectinib was withheld on day 20 since his first dose of treatment while he was being investigated. Nevertheless, he continued to experience worsening dyspnea and bilateral pedal edema and later developed acute pulmonary edema 31 days after his first dose of entrectinib, despite the drug being withheld for the past 11 days. Results of biochemical tests and cardiac imaging confirmed acute myocarditis. Initially, he was treated with standard heart failure medications without clinical improvement or decline in N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide levels. Nevertheless, he noticed significant improvement after starting a short course of prednisolone, which led to complete resolution of symptoms, improved N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide levels, and recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction. His treatment was subsequently changed to crizotinib, which was well tolerated. This is the third reported case of entrectinib-induced myocarditis and the first reported case which has been successfully managed with steroid therapy. This case was also associated with concurrent acute heart failure after entrectinib treatment which responded promptly to prednisolone (40 mg). Entrectinib-induced cardiotoxicity is an important adverse event to be aware of, particularly as patients may be asymptomatic for an initial period before significant deterioration.Keywords: Tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Entrectinib, Myocarditis, Acute heart failure, ROS-1, Case report

Introduction

ROS1 gene rearrangements are present in 1% to 2% of all NSCLC. Currently, there are only two approved ROS1 inhibitors to treat ROS1-postive advanced NSCLC: entrectinib and crizotinib. Here, we describe the case of a patient who was started on entrectinib for recurrent stage IIIB NSCLC. He developed acute myocarditis and heart failure within 1 month of therapy, which responded to a short course of prednisolone treatment. This grade 4 adverse event (AE) required close cardiac monitoring and initiation of supportive medications within a specialist cardio-oncology service.

Case Presentation

We present the case of a 72-year-old man with a background of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease. He was diagnosed with having T1aN0M0 right lung moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma at age 63 years and underwent a right lower lobectomy followed by four cycles of adjuvant platinum-vinorelbine chemotherapy.

After 4 years from completion of adjuvant chemotherapy, he presented with progressive dyspnea and new-onset weight loss. Investigations confirmed recurrence of a T3N3M0 right lung carcinoma, with a positron emission tomography (PET)-avid right hilar mass and ipsilateral mediastinal and supraclavicular lymph nodes. Histopathologic results confirmed an adenocarcinoma with programmed death-ligand 1 tumor proportion score of 90% and positive ROS1 immunohistochemical staining. Next-generation sequencing confirmed the ROS1 rearrangement and a TP53 mutation, with no other actionable genetic aberrations detected. He was started on entrectinib, 600 mg daily, a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

On day 9 since first dose of entrectinib, he was admitted with a stage 3 acute kidney injury (Fig. 1C). Entrectinib was withheld on admission. On clinical and biochemical improvement, entrectinib was restarted and he was discharged (d 14).

He represented on day 20 with decreased urine output, bilateral leg edema, dyspnea, and diarrhea. Entrectinib was withheld on his re-admission. Owing to minimal clinical improvement, he remained an inpatient while investigations conducted. On day 31, he experienced acute desaturation and dyspnea at rest. N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels increased above 10,000 ng/mL (Fig. 1A), and electrocardiograph changes revealed new T wave inversions in V5 to V6 and a partial left bundle branch block (Fig. 2). Echocardiogram results revealed a marked decompensation, with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) dropping from 55%–60% to 25% in 4 days (Fig. 1B). These events occurred 11 days after entrectinib was withheld.

ECG done at time of acute desaturation, revealing T-wave inversion in anterolateral leads V4 to V6 and partial left bundle branch block. ECG, electrocardiograph.

He was reviewed by the cardiology team, and heart failure treatment was initiated: furosemide 40 mg twice daily, bisoprolol 1.25 mg, and dapagliflozin 10 mg. On day 34, he was changed from furosemide to eplerenone 12.5 mg and ramipril 2.5 mg was added. The working diagnosis was entrectinib-related cardiotoxicity resulting in acute heart failure. The patient continued to experience dyspnea and bilateral leg edema despite being on multiple cardiac medications.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) done on day 36 revealed widespread and marked increases in T1 and T2 images, globally elevated T2 mapping values suggestive of edema, and a globally reduced LVEF fraction (Fig. 3A–D). The findings were overall indicative of myocarditis. Subsequently, on day 37, he was started on a 5-day course of 40 mg prednisolone, which resulted in immediate clinical improvement within 24 hours. On day 43, our patient had improving NT-proBNP levels (Fig. 1A), return of cardiac function to baseline LVEF of 55% to 60% (Fig. 1B), and a normal electrocardiograph result. He was subsequently discharged and entrectinib was not restarted.

Owing to two hospital admissions within a short time frame with identified grade 3 and 4 AEs, we decided to not rechallenge with entrectinib and instead switch his treatment to crizotinib, an alternative ROS-1–targeted therapy, which he went on to tolerate well. He currently continues on dose-reduced crizotinib of 200 mg twice daily due to a 1.5× rise of creatinine level from chronic kidney disease. A PET-computed tomography carried out 3 months after starting crizotinib revealed a complete metabolic response compared with the pre-entrectinib PET-computed tomography.

Discussion

Entrectinib is a multikinase inhibitor which targets ROS1, ALK, and pan-TRK. At present, four clinical studies (STARTRK-2, ALKA-372–001, STARTRK-1, and STARTRK-NG) have successfully revealed the efficacy of entrectinib in patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC.1 Entrectinib has been reported to be well tolerated, with the most common AE being of grades 1 to 2: dysgeusia (42.9%), vertigo (34.3%), constipation (31.4%), and fatigue (29.5%).2 Seven patients (3.3%) experienced grade 4 AE, including one case of myocarditis.2

There have only been two case-reports of entrectinib-induced myocarditis, to the best of our knowledge. The first case was what we identified and the second was reported in 2021 in a 51-year-old woman with stage IV NSCLC.3 This patient did not require corticosteroid therapy at presentation and was successfully restarted on entrectinib at a reduced dose of 400 mg daily.

Although the exact mechanism for entrectinib myocarditis is not yet known, it is understood that the pathways involved in cancer cell survival and proliferation may overlap with those involved in cardiomyocyte survival. As a result, targeting cancer cell pathways may also inhibit cardiac pro-survival kinases.4

Cardiac MRI is now a reliable noninvasive tool for identifying myocarditis, with diagnosis based on the Lake Louise 2018 criteria for myocarditis.5 Cardiac MRI (Fig. 3A–D) in our patient met these criteria, and a clinical decision to start steroids was made as his condition continued to decline despite discontinuation of entrectinib 2 weeks prior and having been on heart failure treatment for 5 days. The time frame of events in this case highlights that clinical improvement was a result of steroid initiation rather than discontinuation of entrectinib since day 20: our patient continued to experience pedal edema and dyspnea until acute desaturation on day 31, LVEF declined on day 31 as compared with his baseline on day 27, and NT-proBNP levels continued to rise to a peak of 18,580 ng/mL on day 36. All these parameters only began to recover after prednisolone was initiated on day 37.

Conclusion

Entrectinib-induced myocarditis is a rare but potentially life-threatening grade 4 AE. Prompt initiation of short-course prednisolone resulted in marked improvement in symptoms and recovery of heart function and cardiac biomarkers, highlighting the effectiveness of steroid therapy in treating acute entrectinib-induced myocarditis. There can be overlap between myocarditis (grade 4 AE) and heart failure (grade 3 AE) in patients receiving entrectinib, and managing these conditions requires close collaboration between oncology and cardiology.
