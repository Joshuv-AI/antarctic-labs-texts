Associations between dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes in middle aged and older Chinese
Authors: Jiayi Hao, Chunbian Tang, Qingqing Yang, Youyou Gu, Su Wang, Baoqi Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have shown that diabetes is linked to both low muscle strength and abdominal obesity. However, the combined relationship and synergistic effect on the risk of diabetes in middle-aged and elderly people are still unclear. Using nationally representative data collected between 2011 and 2020 by the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Dynapenic abdominal obesity was a measure that combined hand grip strength and waist circumference. 7934 participants aged ≥ 45 years were divided into four groups (neither dynapenia nor abdominal obesity, only dynapenia, only abdominal obesity, and dynapenic abdominal obesity). Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate the association of dynapenic abdominal obesity on diabetes. The reference group comprised participants with neither dynapenia nor abdominal obesity. Dynapenic abdominal obesity was associated with an increased risk of diabetes (HR = 1.52; 95%CI = 1.12–2.05). Only abdominal obesity group also had a higher risk of diabetes (HR = 1.54, 95%CI = 1.32–1.80), and only dynapenia was not statistically associated with the risk of diabetes (HR = 1.05, 95%CI = 0.80–1.39) and sensitivity analysis showed that the results were robust. Introduction

As the population ages more quickly, diabetes, a chronic metabolic disease marked by hyperglycemia, has emerged as one of the most prevalent and rapidly expanding diseases globally in recent years1. According to the International Diabetes Federation, around 10.5% of people worldwide had diabetes in 2021, and by 2045, that number is expected to increase to 12.2%2. Numerous consequences, including vascular diseases and neuropathy, are frequently present in individuals with diabetes, which not only interfere the quality of life, but also lead to death in severe cases3.

Dynapenic abdominal obesity refers to a condition characterized by decreased muscle strength and an increase in abdominal fat4, which is significantly linked to various chronic diseases and functional impairments. Unlike sarcopenia, which is defined as the age-related reduction in skeletal muscle mass, dynapenia specifically pertains to the age-related decline in muscular strength, resulting from mechanisms associated with damage to the nervous and skeletal systems5,6. It has been posited that dynapenia may serve as an early indicator of sarcopenia7, and it has been suggested that low muscle strength constitutes a primary criterion for diagnosing sarcopenia, with decreased muscle strength being indicative of “possible sarcopenia“8,9. Abdominal obesity, also known as visceral fatty obesity or central obesity, is characterized by an excessive accumulation of visceral adipose tissue (VAT). Studies have revealed that abdominal obesity predicts insulin resistance (IR) more strongly than general obesity, and the accumulation of VAT is positively correlated with diabetes10.

While the respective relationships between dynapenia and abdominal obesity with diabetes have been thoroughly examined, some studies found a statistical association between dynapenic abdominal obesity and hyperglycemia11–13, but it is unclear whether there is a synergistic increase in diabetes risk beyond the sum of individual effects. Moreover, some of these studies were cross-sectional studies11,13, so the time sequence of dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes can’t be determined. In addition, the association of gender, age, and body mass index (BMI) with dynapenia and abdominal obesity was mentioned in some studies13,14. A study showed that women are more likely to develop dynapenia and abdominal obesity, the BMI of individuals with dynapenia is relatively lower and the incidence of dynapenia increases with age13. However, the association of these variables on diabetes in the individuals with dynapenic abdominal obesity was not investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the joint association of motility disorders and abdominal obesity with incident diabetes in a cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese and analyze the potential influence of different age, gender and BMI on this relationship.

Methods

Participants

Data from the CHARLS spanning the years 2011 to 2020 were used in this study. CHARLS is a prospective cohort study using a multistage stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling design, which interviewed 17,708 people from 450 villages and houses of 150 counties and districts in 28 provinces of China during 2011–201215. Since the 2011–2012 baseline survey, follow-up visits to the individuals involved were conducted every 2 years, and blood samples were collected every 2 follow-up periods16. All participants were administered a standardized questionnaire to collect information on demographic background and health status, among other things. All participants provided written informed consent, and the CHARLS protocol was approved by from the Ethics Review Board of Peking University. Further details can be retrieved from the official CHARLS website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/).

A total of 17,705 individuals participated at baseline, of which 9,771 were excluded for various reasons: 368 individuals were under 45 years old, 3,835 lacked waist circumference data, and 255 were excluded due to missing information related to dynapenia. Additionally, 4,503 subjects were excluded for missing covariates. For the longitudinal analyses, 562 subjects with diabetes from CHARLS 2011, 152 subjects without follow-up data, and 96 subjects lacking diabetes data from CHARLS 2013–2020 were also excluded. Consequently, the final analysis included 7,934 individuals (Fig. 1).

Flow of the study.

Determination of dynapenia abdominal obesity status

The current investigation evaluated the prevalence of dynapenia, characterized by decreased muscle strength, and abdominal obesity, indicated by waist circumference measurements. Dynapenia was identified following the diagnostic criteria established by the Asian Working Group on Sarcopenia (AWGS), using a standard hand-held handgrip dynamometer twice for each hand, taking the maximum reading of the dominant hand; a grip strength of less than 28 kg in males or less than 18 kg in females was defined as dynapenia9. Waist circumference is a commonly used indicator of abdominal obesity. The subject took a standing position, circled around the umbilical-cord level with a tape measure, and held his breath for reading at the end of a calm expiration. Based on the characteristics of the Chinese adult population and health risk assessment, the threshold of abdominal obesity was 90 cm in men and 85 cm in women17. Both hand grip strength and waist circumference were collected at baseline. Depending on the presence or absence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity, subjects were stratified into four mutually exclusive groups: neither dynapenia nor abdominal obesity(both handgrip strength and waist circumference were normal), only dynapenia(handgrip strength < 28 kg for men or < 18 kg for women with normal waist circumference), only abdominal obesity(waist circumference > = 90 cm for men or > = 85 cm for women with normal handgrip strength), and dynapenic abdominal obesity(handgrip strength < 28 kg and waist circumference > = 90 cm for men or grip strength < 18 kg and waist circumference > = 85 cm for women).

Diagnostic criteria for diabetes

According to the classification criteria proposed by the World Health Organization, glucose metabolism status is divided into four categories: Normal glucose, impaired fasting glucose (IFG, fasting venous plasma glucose 6.1-7.0mmol/L, and 2 h after glucose load < 7.8mmol/L), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT, fasting venous plasma glucose < 7.0mmol/L, and 2 h after glucose load 7.8-11.1mmol/L) and diabetes18. In our study, diabetes was defined as a glycated hemoglobin level of more than 6.5%19, self-reported diabetes or high blood sugar (both IGT and IFG), physician-diagnosed diabetes or high blood sugar, or the use of traditional Chinese medicine, western medicine, insulin, or others to treat diabetes or high blood sugar, or knowledge of the time or age at diagnosis of diabetes or high blood sugar. At the same time, we also based “Our records from your last interview in R’s LAST IW MONTH, YEAR show that you have had/not had diabetes or high blood sugar, is this right?” this question to correct the accuracy of self-reported diabetes/hyperglycemia in 2011 and 2013. The diabetes outcome was assessed using summary data from four follow-up visits conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020.

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics and health status were assessed utilizing a structured questionnaire to mitigate the influence of potential confounding variables. The sociodemographic covariates included age, gender, residence, education level, and marital status. Health status was evaluated through metrics such as BMI, smoking habits, and alcohol consumption. Additionally, the presence of chronic conditions, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and depression, along with the use of antihypertensive medications was also recorded. BMI was computed by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters, with classifications of overweight or obese assigned based on thresholds of greater than 24–28 kg/m², respectively17. Hypertension was defined as: (1) measured blood pressure on three occasions with ≥ 140/90 mmHg on average, (2) use of antihypertensive medications, or (3) hypertension as reported by the individual or diagnosed by a physician. A person with dyslipidemia was diagnosed when the subject’s indexes met any of the following criteria: (1) serum total cholesterol ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, (2) triglycerides ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, or (3) low density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 4.1 mmol/L20. CVD included self-reported heart problems and stroke21. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, a validated screening tool for geriatric depression with established reliability in elderly populations22. All covariates were collected at baseline.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distributions were stated as mean ± standard deviations (SD), as medians [interquartile ranges (IQR)] for non-normally distributed variables were expressed, and categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages [%]). Group differences were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis test and Pearson’s chi-square test. Follow-up time was selected as the timescale which started from the first time of visit in 2011 and ended at the time of diagnosis of diabetes, death, loss to follow-up, or the most recent follow-up in 2020, which occurred first. Data from participants who were lost to follow-up or died were censored at the time of last contact with the investigator or death. All censored data were retained in the analyses and cox proportional hazards model was employed to quantify the association between dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes, after adjusting for confounding factors. Model 1 calculate crude hazard ratio (HR) 95% confidence interval (95%CI). In model 2, adjustments were made for age and gender. And model 3 adjusted for all covariates, including age, gender, education, marital, residence, BMI, smoking, drinking, depression, CVD, hypertension and dyslipidemia. In addition, further subgroup analyses were performed to detect associations in different populations. Additive interaction effects were quantified through three complementary metrics: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI = RR11 - RR10 - RR01 + 1), attributable proportion of interaction (AP = RERI/RR11), and synergy index (SI = [RR11-1]/[(RR10-1)+(RR01-1)])23. RR11 denotes the relative risk for individuals exposed to both factors, RR10 reflects the risk for those exposed to the first factor only, and RR01 represents the risk for exposure to the second factor alone. The null values of RERI = 0, AP = 0, and SI = 1 indicated absence of biological interaction. To make our findings more robust, we performed sensitivity analyses by changing the covariates of age and BMI as continuous variables. R 4.4.2 statistical software was used to analyze the data. A two-tailed P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 8182 subjects eligible for the study, 7934 completed 9 years of follow-up, of whom 1328 were diagnosed with diabetes during follow-up. The mean follow-up time was 8.15 (2.02) years, with 64,680 person-years for diabetes. Among the participants, 4235 had neither dynapenia nor abdominal obesity (53.4%), 2987 had abdominal obesity only (37.6%), 481 had dynapenia only (6.1%), and 231 had both dynapenia and abdominal obesity (2.9%). Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The study population included 3757 men (47.4%), and the median age of the overall population at baseline was 58.0 years. Compared with individuals without dynapenia or abdominal obesity, participants with dynapenic abdominal obesity were older (69.0 [60.5, 74.0] vs. 57.0 [51.0, 64.0]), had a higher proportion of women (69.7% vs. 43.5%), were less likely to drink (20.3% vs. 38.9%), and had a higher prevalence of depression (55.8% vs. 36.0%), CVD (22.5% vs. 10.0%), and hypertension (63.2% vs.29.6%). Participants with only dynapenia were less likely to have an education level of high school or above, more unmarried, more living in rural areas, and more smokers; and participants with only abdominal obesity had higher BMI and prevalence of dyslipidemia.

Association of dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes

Our study examined the associations between dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes using three models. In crude model, both the dynapenic abdominal group (HR = 2.37, 95%CI = 1.80–3.14, p < 0.001) and the group with only abdominal obesity (HR = 2.36, 95%CI = 2.10–2.64, p < 0.001) exhibited significantly higher hazard ratios compared to the reference group. In contrast, the group with only dynapenia showed no significant association (HR = 1.15, 95%CI = 0.88–1.50, p = 0.320). After adjusting for age and gender in Model 2, the results remained consistent, with dynapenic abdominal obesity group (HR = 2.35, 95%CI = 1.77–3.14, p < 0.001) and the only abdominal obesity group (HR = 2.33, 95%CI = 2.07–2.62, p < 0.001) continuing to show elevated risks, while the group of only dynapenia remained non-significant (HR = 1.14, 95%CI = 0.87–1.50, p = 0.335). Further adjustments in Model 3 for additional covariates attenuated the associations but maintained statistical significance for the dynapenic abdominal obesity group (HR = 1.52, 95% CI = 1.12–2.05, p = 0.007) and the only abdominal obesity group (HR = 1.54, 95%CI = 1.32–1.80, p < 0.001). Only dynapenia group remained non-significant (HR = 1.05, 95% CI = 0.80–1.39, p = 0.704). (Table 2, Figs. S1–S3).

To verify the robustness of the results, sensitivity analyses were performed. We converted the covariates of age and BMI into continuous variable. In multivariable adjusted models, individuals with dynapenic abdominal obesity and only abdominal obesity had a 53% and 65% higher risk of diabetes, compared with references (HR = 1.53, 95%CI = 1.14–2.06; HR = 1.65, 95%CI = 1.44–1.90) (Table S1).

We performed subgroup analysis (Fig. 2). The forest plot showed that dynapenic abdominal obesity was associated with an increased risk of diabetes in most subgroups, while only abdominal obesity showed an association with the risk of diabetes in all subgroups. Only dynapenia may not be an independent risk factor for the incidence of diabetes. In people younger than 60 years of age, all three models observed a significantly higher incidence of diabetes in the dynapenic abdominal obesity group than in the reference group. Only dynapenia was not significantly associated with diabetes, which is the same as the previous results. For those aged 60 years and older, dynapenic abdominal obesity demonstrated associations with incident diabetes in crude models and in models adjusted for age and sex, but was not significant after adjustment for all covariates. Moreover, the only abdominal obesity group was more likely to develop diabetes (Table S2). Both dynapenic abdominal obesity and only abdominal obesity were associated with diabetes in men, whereas only abdominal obesity was associated with a higher risk of diabetes in women(Table S3). We also performed analyses stratified according to BMI at baseline, in the fully adjusted model, individuals with BMI < 24 kg/m2, diabetes was more likely to develop in the group of dynapenic abdominal obesity (HR = 1.86, 95%CI = 1.15–2.99), which was not observed in the subgroups of overweight and obesity (Table S4).

Interaction of dynapenia and abdominal obesity on diabetes

To explore how dynapenia and abdominal obesity influence the risk of diabetes, the measures of RERI, AP, and SI were computed (Table 3). There was no additive interaction between them, after accounting for possible confounding factors in the general population (RERI =-0.08, 95%CI = -0.59-0.43; AP =-0.05, 95%CI = -0.4-0.29; SI = 0.87, 95%CI = 0.34–2.19). However, in the obesity subgroup, AP (0.46, 95%CI = 0.04–0.88) indicated a potential synergistic effect, while SI (4.77, 95%CI = 0.04–558.38) and RERI (1.1, 95%CI = -0.07–2.28) remained non-significant. No significant interactions were found in other subgroups.

Discussion

In this study, we found that dynapenic abdominal obesity was correlated with an increased incidence of diabetes, and this association remained significant after adjustment for multiple confounders. In contrast, dynapenia alone did not show a significant association with diabetes risk.

Prior researchers have demonstrated a correlation between abdominal obesity and an elevated risk of developing diabetes24,25, which is consistent with our findings. However, the results of studies on the relationship between dynapenia and diabetes are controversia26–28. Research conducted within a Korean population has identified a negative correlation between hand muscle strength and the incidence of type 2 diabetes26. Similarly, a study of Japanese men showed that dynapenia is associated with an increased prevalence of type 2 diabetes27. In contrast, a study from the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology reported no significant association between grip strength and the incidence of diabetes28. The correlation may differed depending on the age of the participants, the method used to measure handgrip strength (mean vs. maximum), and whether the chronological order of exposure versus outcome is considered.

Several studies have also evaluated the association of dynapenic abdominal obesity on diabetes11–13. A cross-sectional analysis derived from the Health, Well-being and Aging Study showed that the dynapenic abdominal obesity group had a high incidence of hyperglycemia (odds ratio[OR] = 2.05, 95%CI = 1.14–3.69), this was followed by individuals who have abdominal obesity (OR = 2.01, 95%CI = 1.25 to 3.22), while no statistically significant difference was found in the dynapenic alone group (OR = 1.39, 95%CI = 0.76 to 2.52)11, which is consistent with our study. Ramirez et al. also reported that dynapenic abdominal obesity and abdominal obesity were risk factors for the occurrence of hyperglycemia (IRR = 1.78, 95%CI = 1.02–3.10; IRR = 1.41, 95%CI = 1.18–1.69), while no association was observed between dynapenia only and hyperglycemia (IRR = 1.07, 95%CI = 0.59–1.92) in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing12. However, Kaur et al. conducted a similar study using the Longitudinal Aging Study in India, which found that individuals exhibiting sarcopenic abdominal obesity, characterized by the presence of potential sarcopenia and increased waist circumference, faced a greater risk of developing diabetes compared to those without sarcopenic abdominal obesity (OR = 1.88, 95%CI = 1.77-2.0), and possible sarcopenia is also associated with the risk of diabetes (OR = 1.18, 95%CI = 1.11–1.25). This is in contrast to the results of our study, may because the definition of possible sarcopenia includes low physical performance in addition to dynapenia13.

The underlying mechanisms linking dynapenic abdominal obesity to diabetes remain unclear, which may involve multiple levels of pathophysiological interactions. First, the excessive accumulation of VAT directly impairs insulin sensitivity by releasing inflammatory factors (such as tumor necrosis factors alpha and interleukin 6) and adipokines (such as leptin, adiponectin), while the lipotoxic environment damages mitochondrial function and further exacerbates IR29,30. Second, dynapenia may lead to a decrease in the muscle attenuation coefficient31, reduced oxidative enzyme activity and maximum aerobic capacity32, and decreased Glucose transporter type 4 expression33, thereby significantly reducing glucose uptake capacity. At the same time, if accompanied by a loss of muscle mass, insulin sensitivity further decreases, which may lead to a decrease in the basal metabolic rate and further aggravate the disorder of energy metabolism34. This may have led to the observation in our subgroup analysis that individuals with dynapenic abdominal obesity with BMI < 24 kg/m2 have a higher risk of diabetes, a result similar to previous studies35. Moreover, our subgroup analysis revealed that men with dynapenic abdominal obesity have a significantly higher risk of diabetes, which may be due to the fact that in the elderly population, especially in men, decreased sex hormone levels may accelerate muscle loss and fat accumulation, forming a vicious cycle of metabolic disorders36–38.

When abdominal obesity coexists with dynapenia, it may synergistically amplify the risk of diabetes through fat-muscle interactions39,40. However, in our study, there was no additive interaction between dynapenia and abdominal obesity, after accounting for possible confounding factors in the general population, perhaps it’s because dynapenia and abdominal obesity may affect diabetes through the same mechanisms (e.g., IR30,34, leading to a ceiling effect. There are no other studies on the interaction of grip strength and waist circumference on diabetes risk, probably because they independently affect diabetes risk through different physiological mechanisms, and may also be affected by other confounding factors, study design, and measurement errors. Future studies could further explore whether there is an interaction in specific populations or at the finer level of physiological mechanisms.

Our study has several strengths. First, this is the first cohort study based on a large Chinese population to elucidate the association between dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes in middle-aged and elderly people. Second, this study used a longitudinal design and participants were followed for 9 years to record the development of diabetes. Third, the subgroup analysis used in this study to evaluate the incidence of diabetes across various populations may help to target measures for prevention. However, our study has some limitations. First, all the populations were from China, and our results may not be generalizable to other populations, and there may be insufficient statistical power due to the small sample size of only dynapenia group. Therefore, further studies in other ethnic groups or in more cases are needed. Second, the diagnosis of diabetes was not based solely on diagnostic criteria, which may be subject to a certain degree of measurement bias, and diabetes was not accurately classified. Third, residual confounding from other covariates, like diet, exercise, and family history of diabetes, cannot be completely excluded, and we were unable to evaluate changes in grip strength and waist circumference over time. Fourth, we evaluated dynapenia only by grip strength, and did not consider lower limb muscle strength. Meanwhile, some participants had only one hand grip strength measured or hand grip strength measured only once due to injury, surgery, or other reasons, and may underestimate the maximum grip strength.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study longitudinally investigated the association between dynapenic abdominal obesity and diabetes in a large sample of middle-aged and elderly Chinese. Dynapenic abdominal obesity and only abdominal obesity might be associated with 52% and 54% higher risk of diabetes, while only dynapenia was not statistically associated with the risk of diabetes. Future studies on the causality of the observed associations are needed.
