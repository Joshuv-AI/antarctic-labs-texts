Assessing the prognostic value of SIL1 in pan-cancer cohorts and its practical application as a biomarker in glioma practice
Authors: Peiru Liu, Yao Peng, Ying Sun, Shenghui Hu, Dongyang Lv, Ying Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGliomas are aggressive brain tumors associated with a poor prognosis. Although SIL1, an endoplasmic reticulum chaperone factor, is known to maintain protein homeostasis, its specific role in glioma pathogenesis remains poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the clinical significance and biological functions of SIL1 in glioma.MethodsWe performed a pan-cancer multi-omics analysis using TCGA and GTEx datasets to evaluate SIL1 expression, its prognostic value, and its associations with genomic instability, tumor stemness, and immune infiltration. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was utilized to identify potentially involved signaling pathways. For in vitro functional validation, human glioma cell lines (U251 and A172) were subjected to siRNA-mediated knockdown and lentiviral rescue assays to assess cellular proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) dynamics.ResultsSIL1 was significantly upregulated in gliomas and correlated with poor patient survival. High SIL1 expression was associated with increased tumor heterogeneity (based on MATH scores), DNA methylation-derived stemness indices, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by the enrichment of M2 macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and immune checkpoint molecules. In vitro, SIL1 knockdown suppressed glioma cell proliferation and invasion, promoted mitochondrial apoptosis, and mitigated the EMT phenotype, potentially by impairing SNAIL nuclear translocation. Notably, these phenotypic changes were effectively rescued following lentiviral overexpression of SIL1.ConclusionOur findings suggest that SIL1 is a potential prognostic biomarker in glioma. Its elevated expression correlates with increased malignancy, stemness features, EMT, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, indicating that SIL1 may serve as a promising therapeutic target for glioma intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16241-6.

Introduction

Malignant neoplasms remain a leading global health threat, imposing a substantial burden due to continually rising morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. While therapeutic paradigms have evolved from conventional chemoradiotherapy to precision molecular targeting and breakthrough immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), durable clinical benefits remain elusive for the majority of patients with solid tumors [3, 4]. Treatment failure is primarily driven by tumor adaptability and heterogeneity, which precipitate frequent recurrence and acquired resistance [5]. Emerging consensus posits that malignant progression is governed not solely by intrinsic genetic aberrations but significantly by the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [6, 7]. Tumor cells exploit complex signaling networks to remodel the TIME and induce immune evasion phenotypes; decoding the molecular mechanisms driving this remodeling is therefore critical for mitigating therapeutic bottlenecks and improving patient outcomes.

Gliomas represent the most prevalent primary intracranial malignancies in adults, accounting for the majority of malignant brain tumors [8]. Despite standard-of-care regimens combining surgical resection with chemoradiotherapy (e.g., temozolomide), most patients—particularly those with glioblastoma (GBM)—eventually succumb to disease progression and therapeutic resistance, resulting in dismal long-term survival [9–11]. A major barrier to durable therapeutic response lies in the extensive heterogeneity of gliomas, which extends beyond genetic and metabolic profiles to deeply entrenched differences within their unique immune microenvironments [12, 13]. To decode this multidimensional complexity, recent advancements highlight the immense potential of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning. By integrating multimodal data—encompassing tumor microenvironment characteristics and specific molecular biomarkers—these emerging AI platforms show considerable promise in predicting glioma survival and guiding personalized interventions [14].

Attention has shifted toward the pivotal role of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis and the unfolded protein response (UPR) in tumor biology [15, 16]. As a key nucleotide exchange factor for the major ER chaperone BiP (HSPA5), SIL1 is indispensable for protein folding, secretion, and the maintenance of ER integrity [17]. Evidence suggests that the activation of ER stress signaling facilitates tumor survival and adaptation within hostile microenvironments [18]. Notably, aberrant SIL1 expression has been implicated in the initiation and progression of various malignancies, potentially influencing aggressiveness and prognosis via the modulation of protein quality control mechanisms [19–21]. However, comprehensive investigations into SIL1 in gliomas remain scarce, and its precise molecular mechanisms and clinical relevance require further elucidation.

In this study, we provide a comprehensive analysis of SIL1 expression profiles across multiple cancer types and evaluate its potential prognostic significance in glioma. Furthermore, we dissect the involvement of SIL1 in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and validate its biological effects in glioma cells through in vitro assays. Collectively, this work aims to provide deeper insights into the functional role of SIL1 in glioma tumorigenesis and progression, offering a theoretical basis for the identification of novel biomarkers and potential therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Cohort data acquisition and preprocessing

Gene expression profiles and corresponding clinical data were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://cancergenome.nih.gov/), the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (http://commonfund.nih.gov/GTEx/), and the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET; https://ocg.cancer.gov/programs/target) databases. Clinical data for diverse tumor types were retrieved from TCGA and TARGET, and tumor samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on median cutoff values.

Differential mRNA expression analysis

Differential mRNA expression between groups was assessed using Student’s t-test. The thresholds for statistical significance were set at an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) ≥ 1 and a p-value < 0.05.

Stemness analysis

Tumor stemness, which reflects the degree of oncogenic dedifferentiation, was evaluated using six distinct stemness indices as previously defined [22]. These indices included: (1) RNAss, an mRNA expression-based stemness score derived from global gene expression; (2) EREG-EXPss, an epigenetically regulated RNA expression-based score derived from 103 genes; (3) DNAss, a DNA methylation-based score computed using a signature of 219 stem cell-associated probes; (4) EREG-METHss, an epigenetically regulated DNA methylation-based score derived from 87 probes; (5) DMPss, a differentially methylated probe-based score derived from 62 probes; and (6) ENHss, an enhancer element-based DNA methylation score derived from 82 probes. Correlation analyses were subsequently performed to evaluate the association between SIL1 expression and these stemness indices.

Genomics heterogeneity analysis

We evaluated several key genomic biomarkers associated with tumorigenesis, genomic instability, and potential immunotherapy responses. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated as the total number of somatic mutations per megabase of the sequenced genome; elevated TMB is often correlated with an improved response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH) scores were computed to quantify intratumor heterogeneity, with higher values indicating a wider distribution of mutant allele fractions. Microsatellite instability (MSI) status was determined by evaluating shifts in microsatellite repeat lengths indicative of DNA mismatch repair deficiency. Furthermore, we assessed tumor neoantigen burden (representing immunogenic peptides derived from tumor-specific nonsynonymous mutations), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status (evaluated via specific genomic scar patterns, which may indicate sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors), and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) events across genomic regions containing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).

Immune cell infiltration

To quantify the abundance of immune and stromal cell infiltration within the glioma tumor microenvironment (TME), we utilized the ESTIMATE (Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data) algorithm. Specifically, the ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore for each glioma sample were calculated based on gene expression profiles using the estimate package (version 1.0.13) in R software. A higher ImmuneScore indicates a greater degree of immune cell infiltration within the tumor tissue. Furthermore, single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA), retrieved via the TISIDB database, was employed to infer immune cell type enrichment from gene expression data, thereby facilitating the deconvolution of cellular compositions.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To explore the biological pathways and underlying molecular mechanisms associated with SIL1 expression, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed utilizing the Biomarker Exploration of Solid Tumors (BEST) web server (https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/). Tumor samples were stratified into high- and low-expression cohorts based on median SIL1 expression levels. The Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) were employed as the reference signature (the full list of gene sets is provided in Additional file 1). For each pathway, a normalized enrichment score (NES) was computed. Statistical significance was defined by a nominal p-value < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Cell culture

The human glioblastoma cell lines U251, LN229, and A172, along with the human microglial cell line HMC3, were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were routinely maintained in a sterile, humidified incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The culture medium was refreshed every two to three days depending on cell density, and cells were passaged using trypsin-EDTA upon reaching approximately 80% confluence. All cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination prior to the functional experiments.

siRNA-mediated SIL1 knockdown

Cells were transfected with 50 nM SIL1-targeting siRNA duplexes using the jetPRIME® transfection reagent. Transfection complexes were prepared by mixing the siRNA with jetPRIME® buffer and reagent, followed by a 15-minute incubation at room temperature prior to addition to the cell cultures. The knockdown efficiency of SIL1 was assessed 48 h post-transfection via RT-qPCR and Western blotting.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion capacities were evaluated using 24-well Transwell chambers (8-µm pore size; Corning, USA). For the invasion assay, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) to mimic the extracellular matrix, whereas the migration assay was performed without Matrigel. Cell suspensions (5 × 10^5 cells/mL) in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, and medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 72 h at 37 °C, cells remaining on the upper surface were gently removed using cotton swabs. The migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope in randomly selected fields.

Lentiviral transduction and phenotypic rescue

To assess the on-target specificity of SIL1 knockdown, lentiviral vectors encoding the full-length human SIL1 sequence (Si + Lv) and negative control vectors were synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Glioblastoma cells (U251 and A172) were transfected with the optimal siRNA and transduced with the SIL1-overexpressing lentivirus according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stable expression and rescue efficiency were subsequently evaluated by RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses prior to downstream functional assays.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Firsher Scientific, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SIL1 (Abcam; 1:1000) and Β-ACTIN (Proteintech; 1:5000). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, the protein bands were visualized using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) kit and analyzed via an imaging system. Β-ACTIN served as the endogenous loading control.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 24-well plates and subjected to the designated treatments. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. The coverslips were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies against SIL1 and SNAIL (Abcam; 1:200). Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 and 594, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope to evaluate the subcellular localization and potential nuclear translocation of SNAIL.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the TRIzol reagent, and its concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer to ensure an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit under the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. The synthesized cDNA was stored at -20 °C. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using a SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS Kit on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. The 20 µL reaction mixture consisted of 10 µL of premix, 0.4 µM of each forward and reverse primer, 2 µL of cDNA template, and nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. All gene-specific primer sequences used for qPCR are detailed in Additional file 2. Primers were designed using the Primer-BLAST tool to span exon-exon junctions and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Primer specificity was evaluated by melt curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. Β-ACTIN was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2–∆∆Ct method. All experiments were independently repeated three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Clonogenic assay

Single-cell suspensions (500 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and subsequently counted.

CCK-8 proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2000 cells/well) and incubated with the CCK-8 reagent for 4 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1, and data visualization was conducted using RStudio and GraphPad Prism 10. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of variables. Differences between two groups with normally distributed variables were evaluated using the Student’s t-test (two-tailed), while the Wilcoxon test was used for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons among multiple groups, parametric ANOVA or non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied depending on data distribution, followed by Dunnett’s test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s method for normally distributed data or Spearman’s rank method for non-normally distributed data. Survival probabilities were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves, and statistical significance between high- and low-expression groups was determined via the log-rank test. To evaluate independent prognostic value, multivariate models were adjusted for key clinical confounders, including patient age, gender, and tumor stage; for glioma cohorts specifically, WHO grade, IDH mutation status, and molecular subtype were incorporated. In pan-cancer multi-omics analyses (e.g., correlations with immune signatures, stemness indices, and genomic instability markers), the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied for False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction, with significance defined as FDR < 0.05. Given the highly infiltrative nature of gliomas and the sparsity of Disease-Free Interval (DFI) data in TCGA glioma cohorts, we prioritized Overall Survival (OS), Disease-Specific Survival (DSS), and Progression-Free Interval (PFI) as the primary clinical endpoints. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Differential expression analysis of SIL1 in different tumor tissues

Comparative analysis of SIL1 expression between tumor tissues and their corresponding normal counterparts revealed significant differential expression across multiple cancer types, exhibiting distinct tissue-specific variations. Overall, SIL1 expression was significantly upregulated in a broad spectrum of cancers (Fig. 1A). Notably, lower-grade glioma (LGG) and the combined glioma cohort (GBMLGG), as well as glioblastoma (GBM), exhibited highly significant differential upregulation (P < 1.0e-80). Furthermore, SIL1 expression was significantly elevated compared to normal controls in several other malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD/COADREAD), kidney cancer subtypes (KIRC, KIRP, KIPAN), and skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), with all differences showing strong statistical significance (P < 0.01 to P < 1.0e-60).

Conversely, SIL1 expression was significantly downregulated in a distinct subset of cancers, including testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and kidney chromophobe (KICH), where its levels were markedly lower than in normal tissues (Fig. 1A).

In several other cancer types, SIL1 expression showed a general trend of upregulation. This included bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), gastric cancer (STAD/STES), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC)/uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), and cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL). Although significantly overexpressed compared to normal tissues, the specific fold changes and significance levels varied across these cancers, potentially reflecting underlying tumor heterogeneity (Fig. 1A). Notably, no significant differential expression associated with SIL1 was observed in Wilms’ tumor (WT) and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ).

In summary, the pan-cancer analysis reveals a complex dysregulation pattern for SIL1, providing a rationale for further functional investigations into its role in specific cancer contexts. Its frequent upregulation in major cancers, such as gliomas, BRCA, and LIHC, suggests that it may act as a potential oncogenic factor warranting further in-depth study.

Prognostic value of SIL1 across different tumor types

Using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for key clinical confounders (including patient age, tumor grade, IDH mutation status, and molecular subtype where applicable), we systematically evaluated the association between SIL1 expression and patient prognosis across various cancers, encompassing endpoints such as OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI. Significance thresholds for the pan-cancer analyses were stringently defined using FDR correction. This analysis utilized pan-cancer datasets from public databases, including TCGA and TARGET.

In the overall survival (OS) analysis (Fig. 1B), high SIL1 expression was significantly associated with poorer outcomes in several cancer types. Notably, in the TCGA-GBMLGG dataset (N = 619), elevated SIL1 expression strongly correlated with reduced OS (HR = 2.59, 95% CI: 2.18–3.08; P = 4.0e-26). A similar trend, where increased SIL1 expression indicated shorter OS, was observed in TCGA-LGG (N = 474, P = 2.3e-6, HR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.48–2.54), TCGA-GBM (N = 144, P = 3.0e-4, HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.37–2.87), TCGA-UVM (N = 74, P = 3.0e-4, HR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.55–4.75), TCGA-LAML (N = 209, P = 0.02, HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.03–1.39), TARGET-LAML (N = 142, P = 0.03, HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.03–1.67), TCGA-KIPAN (N = 855, P = 0.04, HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.02–1.56), TARGET-WT (N = 80, P = 0.04, HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.01–1.83), and TCGA-KICH (N = 64, P = 0.04, HR = 4.67, 95% CI: 1.10-19.77).

In the DSS analysis (Fig. 1C), the results were concordant with the OS findings, indicating that elevated SIL1 expression was significantly associated with unfavorable disease-specific survival. Strong statistical associations were observed in TCGA-GBMLGG (N = 598, P = 2.7e-24; HR = 2.61, 95% CI: 2.18–3.13) and TCGA-LGG (N = 466, P = 5.1e-6; HR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.47–2.59). Other notable datasets showing significant correlations included TCGA-UVM (N = 74, P = 3.7e-4; HR = 2.80, 95% CI: 1.56–5.03), TCGA-GBM (N = 131, P = 4.6e-4; HR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.37–3.06), TCGA-KIPAN (N = 840, P = 1.3e-3; HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.18–1.99), TCGA-THYM (N = 117, P = 0.02; HR = 3.47, 95% CI: 1.13–10.64), TCGA-KICH (N = 64, P = 0.04; HR = 5.81, 95% CI: 1.15–29.44), and TCGA-LUSC (N = 418, P = 0.05; HR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.00–2.51).

Regarding the DFI analysis (Fig. 1D), the link between SIL1 expression and disease-free interval was restricted to specific cohorts, namely TCGA-KIRP (N = 177, P = 0.02; HR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.13–4.20), TCGA-COAD (N = 103, P = 0.02; HR = 3.47, 95% CI: 1.27–9.45), and TCGA-KIPAN (N = 319, P = 0.04; HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.02–2.33).

In the PFI analysis (Fig. 1E), high SIL1 expression was broadly linked to a shortened progression-free interval. This association was most evident in TCGA-GBMLGG (N = 616, P = 5.2e-19; HR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.79–2.48) and TCGA-KIPAN (N = 845, P = 9.7e-5; HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.23–1.87). Significant trends were also identified in TCGA-LGG, TCGA-UVM, TCGA-GBM, TCGA-KIRP, and TCGA-LUSC (all P < 0.05). Overall, SIL1 expression demonstrated a consistent correlation with poor prognosis across multiple cancer types, particularly in gliomas (GBMLGG and LGG) and genitourinary tumors (KIPAN and KICH), suggesting that SIL1 may potentially serve as a robust pan-cancer prognostic biomarker.

Further validation using Kaplan-Meier survival curves focused on GBM, GBMLGG, LGG, and KIPAN (Fig. 2A-C). For OS, significant survival disparities were confirmed in GBM (P = 0.0089), GBMLGG (P < 0.0001), and LGG (P = 0.00013), with higher SIL1 expression corresponding to inferior clinical outcomes; however, no significant difference was reached in the KIPAN cohort (P = 0.12). For DSS, all four tumor types—GBM (P = 0.023), GBMLGG (P < 0.0001), LGG (P = 0.00018), and KIPAN (P = 0.0019)—displayed significant differences. Similarly, significant survival variations in PFI were observed across GBMLGG (P < 0.0001), LGG (P = 0.0039), KIPAN (P = 0.011), and GBM (P = 0.032).

Association between SIL1 expression and genomic heterogeneity

To explore the potential role of SIL1 in tumorigenesis, we systematically evaluated the correlation between its expression and six key metrics of genomic instability and heterogeneity across a pan-cancer landscape: tumor mutation burden (TMB), mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH), microsatellite instability (MSI), neoantigen load (NEO), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) (Fig. 3A–F). Visualized via Spearman correlation analyses, the data indicated that while SIL1 significantly intersects with genomic instability, the nature of these associations is highly context-dependent. Broadly, we observed that correlations with mutational load markers (TMB and NEO) were relatively restricted, reaching significance in only three tumor types (Fig. 3A, D); conversely, associations with structural and heterogeneity markers were more pervasive, with MATH, HRD, and LOH showing significant correlations in 12, 15, and 14 cohorts, respectively (Fig. 3B, E, F). Notably, while the global trend for MATH and HRD skewed toward negative correlations (Fig. 3B, E), specific oncogenic contexts revealed distinct positive associations. For instance, in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and colorectal cancers (COAD/READ), SIL1 expression paralleled TMB, MSI, and NEO (Fig. 3A, C, D), suggesting a link to mutational accumulation and neoantigen generation. Meanwhile, a positive correlation with HRD in ovarian (OV) carcinomas implicated SIL1 in DNA repair deficiencies; conversely, a negative correlation was observed in breast (BRCA) carcinomas.

Importantly, the analysis uncovered a distinct pattern in glioma (GBMLGG): unlike other malignancies where SIL1 tracked with mutational burden, in glioma, SIL1 was uncoupled from TMB and NEO (Fig. 3A, D) but exhibited a strong negative correlation with MATH (Fig. 3B). This divergence suggests that within the glioma ecosystem, SIL1 may not act as a driver of sheer mutational quantity. Instead, its inverse relationship with MATH indicates an association with reduced subclonal diversity. This implies that SIL1 might constrain, rather than expand, subclonal diversity, pointing toward a specific mechanism for glioma evolution and therapeutic resistance that diverges from the genomic instability patterns observed in other solid tumors.

Association between SIL1 expression and tumor stemness

By interrogating the correlation between six distinct stemness indices (DNAss, EREG-METHss, DMPss, ENHss, RNAss, and EREG.EXPss) and a pan-cancer cohort, we observed a landscape characterized by both broad presence and significant heterogeneity (Fig. 4A–F), supporting the notion that stemness is a pervasive biological feature across malignancies. While the majority of indices demonstrated significant associations across a broad spectrum of cancer types, the directionality varied. For instance, DNA methylation-based indices (DNAss, EREG-METHss, and DMPss) showed significant correlations in 17 to 18 tumor types, with a predominant trend toward negative correlation in approximately two-thirds of these cases (Fig. 4A–C). A similar, though less extensive, pattern was observed for ENHss and the RNA-based indices (RNAss and EREG.EXPss), which also skewed toward negative associations (Fig. 4D–F). However, the varying magnitudes of these associations reveal that the dominant molecular mechanisms maintaining stemness are highly context-dependent, contingent upon cellular origin and histological background.

The most salient finding emerged in gliomas (GBMLGG), which displayed a strong association with methylation-based stemness features. This distinct pattern suggests that SIL1 expression is more closely linked to aberrant DNA methylation landscapes rather than a static, highly expressed transcriptional program. Beyond gliomas, the analysis highlighted other biologically revealing trends; testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT) displayed uniform, high-magnitude negative correlations across all six indices (Fig. 4A–F), indicating a specific regulatory mechanism of stemness in these tumors. Conversely, cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) and hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) frequently exhibited significant negative correlations with multiple DNA methylation indices (Fig. 4A–C), suggesting that stemness in these hepatic malignancies is driven by distinct epigenetic regulatory mechanisms that deviate from the canonical patterns observed in other solid tumors.

To further evaluate the unique relationship between SIL1 expression and intratumoral heterogeneity in glioma, we systematically compared the SIL1-MATH correlation coefficients across pan-cancer cohorts using Fisher’s z-transformation (Additional file 3–4). The strong negative correlation observed in the combined glioma cohort (GBMLGG, r = -0.331) significantly differed from 27 other cancer types (P < 0.05). Furthermore, to provide additional insight into the link between SIL1 and clonal architecture, we analyzed subclonal reconstruction data (Subclonal Genome Fraction/ITH Score) [23]. Subtype stratification (Additional file 5) revealed that SIL1 expression was significantly negatively correlated with subclonal diversity specifically in Lower-Grade Glioma (LGG: R = -0.102, P = 0.022), mirroring the MATH results, while this correlation was absent in Glioblastoma (GBM: R = 0.002, P = 0.98).

Heterogeneous associations of SIL1 in the pan-cancer tumor immune microenvironment and its potential mechanisms in glioma immune escape

Pan-cancer analysis revealed a distinct tissue-specific dichotomy in how SIL1 expression correlates with the tumor immune microenvironment (TME). To establish a global landscape, we first evaluated the correlative patterns between SIL1 and representative immune-related pathways (Fig. 5A). SIL1 exhibited a strong positive association with immune signaling—including complement cascades and leukocyte transendothelial migration—notably in gliomas (GBM, LGG), testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), and stomach and esophageal carcinomas (STES). Conversely, a prominent inverse correlation with immune pathways was observed in thyroid carcinoma (THCA)—primarily reflecting suppressed T-cell receptor signaling—as well as in prostate (PRAD) and colon (COAD) adenocarcinomas.

To systematically dissect these contrasting immune signatures, we evaluated the bioinformatic activity scores of the seven critical steps of the cancer-immunity cycle (Fig. 5B). In SIL1-high tumors, particularly gliomas, inferred activity scores were generally elevated in early-stage processes (from inferred antigen release to immune cell recruitment), yet remained constrained in the terminal functional steps (T cell recognition and killing of cancer cells). This distinct pattern of high early-stage computational scores but constrained late-stage scores implies a transcriptomic profile characteristic of a functionally exhausted immune response.

To explore the transcriptional basis of the elevated “early-stage” computational scores, we examined the antigen presentation and cellular recruitment machineries. SIL1 demonstrated broad co-expression with major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I/II molecules (e.g., HLA-A, HLA-DRA) and the transporter TAPBP (Fig. 5C). This robust antigen-presenting capacity is strongly conserved in STES and gliomas, whereas THCA uniquely exhibited a potent inverse correlation with MHC molecules like HLA-DOA. This initial immunological visibility is further supported by SIL1’s extensive association with chemokine networks (e.g., CCL2, CCL5, CXCL9/10), which are classical transcriptomic markers for the active recruitment of both T-cell and myeloid lineages (Fig. 5D). Parallel trends across a broad spectrum of immunomodulatory receptors (Fig. 5E) highlight SIL1’s extensive involvement in the co-expression networks of TME receptor-ligand interactions, facilitating widespread immune cell infiltration.

However, addressing the apparent “late-stage incompetence” paradox requires examining the immunomodulatory balance. While SIL1 positively correlated with various immune stimulators (e.g., TNFRSF4, TNFSF13) in gliomas, STES, and TGCT (Fig. 5F), this stimulatory signal appeared to be substantially counterbalanced by robust co-expression with key immune inhibitory molecules, such as TGF-β1, IL10RB, and LGALS9 (Fig. 5G). This concurrent upregulation indicates the potential formation of an immunosuppressive transcriptomic niche. Importantly, SIL1 strongly co-expressed with key immune checkpoints, including PD-L1 (CD274), PD-1, CTLA4, TIM-3, and LAG3 (Fig. 5H). Notably, while THCA demonstrated a stark negative correlation with PD-L1 (consistent with its immune-excluded profile), the profound checkpoint co-expression in gliomas and TGCT suggests that SIL1-related inflammation is transcriptionally linked to adaptive immune resistance markers. Ultimately, SIL1 emerges as a potential biomarker of a dysregulated immune microenvironment, showing strong transcriptional links to both early inflammatory recruitment and terminal immunosuppressive exhaustion, partially explaining the poorer clinical outcomes observed in SIL1-high patients.

Analysis of the correlation between SIL1 expression and immune infiltrating cells in human cancers

To explore the potential mechanistic underpinnings linking SIL1 overexpression to adverse clinical outcomes, we conducted a comprehensive landscape analysis of the tumor microenvironment (TME) utilizing a suite of deconvolution algorithms, including CIBERSORT, TIMER, MCP-counter, ESTIMATE, and xCell (Fig. 6). On a pan-cancer scale, SIL1 expression exhibits a broad and significant correlation with specific TME components; consensus analysis across multiple algorithms indicated that SIL1 is positively associated with both ImmuneScore and StromalScore in the majority of cancer types. However, this increased cellularity does not appear to reflect an anti-tumor effector state. Rather, the pervasive “red hotspots” in the correlation landscape are preferentially enriched in myeloid and stromal lineages—specifically macrophages (notably the M2 phenotype), neutrophils, and fibroblasts—while associations with cytotoxic T cells are conspicuously weak or negative. This dissociation suggests that SIL1 participates in shaping a stroma-rich yet immunologically dysfunctional microenvironment. This trend is particularly pronounced within the glioma microenvironment (GBM and LGG), where heatmaps reveal dense clusters of strong positive correlations between SIL1 and macrophage subsets (M0, M1, M2), monocytes, and the overall Microenvironment Score. Notably, the robust association with M2 macrophages, corroborated by xCell and CIBERSORT, underscores a potential role for SIL1 in fostering an immunosuppressive niche. This distinct immunophenotype provides a plausible mechanistic rationale for the poor prognosis observed in SIL1-high glioma patients: rather than recruiting cytotoxic T cells, SIL1 appears to orchestrate a “pro-tumor” microenvironment by promoting the infiltration of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages and neutrophils while simultaneously increasing stromal density. High StromalScores imply the deposition of a dense extracellular matrix that not only serves as a physical scaffold for invasion and metastasis but may also act as a barrier impeding chemotherapeutic penetration and T-cell infiltration. Consequently, by potentially remodeling the TME into a state characterized by myeloid expansion and matrix stiffening, SIL1 overexpression may facilitate immune evasion and support malignant progression, ultimately contributing to shortened patient survival.

SIL1 expression patterns and functional enrichment analysis in glioma patients

Through a comprehensive bioinformatic interrogation, this study systematically characterizes the expression landscape and functional implications of SIL1 within the glioma microenvironment. Initial differential expression profiling revealed a marked upregulation of SIL1 in glioblastoma (GBM) and lower-grade gliomas (LGG) relative to normal tissues, as evidenced by distinctive distributions in box plot analyses (Fig. 7A) and the global variance landscape visualized via volcano plots (Fig. 7B). To evaluate the broad generalizability of SIL1 across the glioma spectrum, subsequent enrichment analyses of the combined CGGA301 glioma cohort uncovered a coherent molecular framework suggesting that SIL1 participates in a triad of neuro-immune signaling, extracellular matrix (ECM) structural dynamics, and neuronal-like electrophysiological adaptation. The convergence of Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways indicates that SIL1 expression may remodel glioma biology along three tightly coupled axes (Fig. 7C-D). First, the alignment of immune-related ontologies—such as leukocyte migration and humoral responses—with KEGG modules involving complement activation and cytokine-receptor interactions highlights a paradoxical immunophenotype; this state is characterized by chronic inflammation coexisting with immunosuppression, suggesting the potential co-option of host defense programs to facilitate tumor persistence and immune evasion. Second, ECM-centric signatures, including collagen organization and focal adhesion, map directly to AGE-RAGE signaling pathways, suggesting that dynamic matrix remodeling and the integration of oxidative stress responses could be key mechanisms underlying SIL1-associated invasion. Finally, the enrichment of neuronal and synaptic pathways—encompassing GABAergic synapses, neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, and ion channel regulation—implies that glioma cells might not merely respond passively to neuronal cues but could potentially integrate within neural networks. This may reflect the acquisition of a reinforcement-like, cyclic dependency phenotype analogous to reward-driven neural plasticity, rather than simple physiological adaptation.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further elucidates the functional repertoire associated with SIL1 overexpression, revealing a transcriptional landscape where heightened SIL1 levels are closely associated with an upregulated state of specific biological programs (Fig. 7E). The robustness of these findings is supported by rigorous statistical metrics, with Normalized Enrichment Scores (NES) ranging from 2.578 to 2.937 and a False Discovery Rate (FDR) as low as 3.1e-10, demonstrating the statistical reliability of the enrichment profile. Biologically, this high-SIL1 state is characterized by the concurrent activation of critical immune and inflammatory cascades, including TNF-α/NF-κB signaling, interferon-gamma and -alpha responses, and generalized inflammatory response pathways; these signatures position SIL1 as a potential orchestrator of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. Parallel to this immune modulation, the prominent enrichment of the EMT pathway suggests that SIL1 upregulation may concurrently confer enhanced cellular plasticity, thereby facilitating the migratory and invasive phenotypes essential for malignant progression.

We employed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate the prognostic accuracy of SIL1 in glioma for OS, DSS, and PFI. The analysis revealed that SIL1 exhibited favorable predictive performance for OS, with AUC values of 0.787 at 1 year, 0.797 at 2 years, and 0.804 at 3 years. For DSS, the AUCs were 0.785, 0.798, and 0.802 at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively. Similarly, for PFI, the AUC values reached 0.748 at 1 year, 0.758 at 2 years, and 0.724 at 3 years (Fig. 7F).

SIL1 expression profiles across the Classical, Mesenchymal, and Proneural TCGA glioma subtypes are shown in Fig. 7G. Levels were notably elevated in the Mesenchymal subtype compared to the other two subgroups (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 2.2e − 16). The ROC curve for a classifier based on SIL1 expression levels suggests potential diagnostic utility in differentiating among the various TCGA subtypes, particularly in distinguishing the Mesenchymal subtype from the others (Fig. 7G), with an AUC of 0.833 indicating favorable sensitivity and specificity.

SIL1 promotes glioblastoma malignancy potentially by coordinating EMT and mitigating mitochondrial apoptosis

To functionally validate the oncogenic role of SIL1 suggested by our multi-omics analysis, we first assessed its basal expression in in vitro models. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses indicated that SIL1 is significantly upregulated in human glioblastoma cell lines (U251, LN229, and A172) compared to the normal human microglial cell line (HMC3) (Fig. 9A; Additional file 6). To rigorously evaluate its biological function and mitigate potential RNA interference off-target effects, we established a loss-of-function and lentiviral rescue system in U251 and A172 cells. Transient transfection with independent siRNAs effectively suppressed SIL1 expression, while co-transfection with a full-length SIL1 lentiviral vector (Si + Lv) restored SIL1 to near-endogenous levels at both the mRNA (Fig. 9B, C) and protein levels (Additional file 6).

Phenotypic evaluations indicated that SIL1 plays a crucial role in glioma cell growth and motility. SIL1 depletion markedly inhibited cell proliferation in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 9D, E) and significantly impaired long-term clonogenic survival (Fig. 9F). Furthermore, Transwell assays demonstrated a substantial reduction in both migratory and invasive capacities in SIL1-knockdown cells (Fig. 9G). Importantly, the targeted reintroduction of SIL1 (Si + Lv) largely reversed these malignant phenotypes, suggesting that the observed functional impairments are mediated by SIL1 loss.

Mechanistically, integrating our in vitro data with the prior GSEA predictions, we investigated the molecular underpinnings of the SIL1-associated invasive and survival phenotypes. RT-qPCR profiling revealed that SIL1 depletion induced a shift in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) program, characterized by the upregulation of E-cadherin and the concomitant downregulation of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin, and MMP-2) (Fig. 9H, I). Additionally, SIL1 knockdown appeared to trigger a mitochondrial apoptotic response, as evidenced by the transcriptional activation of Bax, Caspase-9, and Caspase-3, alongside the suppression of the anti-apoptotic factor Bcl-2. To explore the upstream transcriptional regulation of the EMT alterations, we monitored the subcellular dynamics of SNAIL, a core E-cadherin repressor. Immunofluorescence imaging indicated that SIL1 knockdown reduced the nuclear translocation of SNAIL, an effect that was reversed by lentiviral SIL1 restoration (Fig. 9J). Collectively, these data implicate SIL1 as a potential driver of glioblastoma aggressiveness, likely facilitating EMT-mediated invasion via SNAIL nuclear import and buffering against intrinsic apoptosis (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Despite significant advances in surgical techniques and radiochemotherapy regimens in recent years, glioma (particularly glioblastoma, GBM) remains one of the most prognostically unfavorable malignant solid tumors due to its high invasiveness, heterogeneity, and propensity for recurrence [9, 24]. The mechanisms by which tumor cells adapt to harsh survival conditions by remodeling intracellular homeostasis and the extracellular microenvironment represent a central focus of current translational medical research. This study, through the integration of pan-cancer multi-omics analysis and glioma-specific experimental validation, systematically provides novel insights into the multifaceted roles of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) chaperone SIL1. Our findings implicate SIL1 as a potential driver of malignant glioma progression, highlighting its putative role in prognosis assessment, the remodeling of the immune microenvironment, and the modulation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).

A notable finding of this study is the potential of SIL1 to serve as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for glioma. As an ER-resident protein, SIL1 possesses an N-terminal ER-targeting sequence, two putative N-glycosylation sites, and a C-terminal ER retention signal [25]. Its primary physiological function is to act as a nucleotide exchange factor for the unfolded protein response (UPR) sensor, 78-kDa glucose-regulated protein (GRP78, also known as BiP), thereby regulating its ATPase cycle. The SIL1 protein consists of 461 amino acids and contains several important functional domains involved in protein folding, assembly, and quality control. In cellular physiology, SIL1 plays a critical role in maintaining ER homeostasis by modulating the ATPase activity of GRP78, a member of the heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) family essential for protein processing and secretory pathways. Mutations in SIL1 may reduce the hydrolysis rate of GRP78, impairing protein translocation and folding, which leads to the accumulation of unfolded proteins in the ER and subsequent ER stress. If this stress remains unresolved, it can trigger apoptosis [26]. For instance, Marinesco-Sjögren syndrome (MSS) is an autosomal recessive disorder directly linked to SIL1 mutations, which often result in a loss of protein function due to premature translation termination or aberrant splicing. These mutations disrupt the SIL1-BiP interaction, leading to abnormal GRP78 ATPase cycling, protein aggregation, and consequent cellular dysfunction and neurodegeneration [27]. Therefore, SIL1 dysfunction is considered a critical factor in central neuronal degeneration. In the context of oncology, altered SIL1 expression levels in various tumor cell lines have been associated with malignant behaviors, potentially by influencing cell cycle regulators and apoptosis-related signaling pathways, such as cyclin D1, p21, Bcl-2, and the Caspase family [19]. Furthermore, SIL1 may exert its effects by modulating the composition and function of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [28]. Dysregulation of the ECM can lead to altered mechanotransduction, potentially driving malignant transformation and progression. ECM components, such as collagen I and III, provide physical scaffolds for tumor cell migration, facilitating rapid proliferation and invasion. While ECM degradation and remodeling can be leveraged in cancer therapy, these processes may also inadvertently promote tumor cell invasion into surrounding tissues. Consequently, SIL1 likely plays a significant role in orchestrating ECM dynamics and influencing tumor cell behavior, thereby offering a potential target for the development of novel anticancer strategies.

Our pan-cancer analysis revealed that SIL1 is significantly overexpressed in various malignancies, including glioma (GBM/LGG), liver cancer (LIHC), and breast cancer (BRCA). This elevated expression is closely associated with shortened overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI). Given its role as a key nucleotide exchange factor for BiP [29], we hypothesize that SIL1 overexpression may represent an adaptive survival strategy for tumor cells under conditions of high metabolic demand and hypoxia, enhancing the processing capacity of the UPR to prevent apoptosis caused by proteotoxic stress. Notably, in glioma, SIL1 not only exhibits robust expression levels but also demonstrates favorable diagnostic potential in distinguishing molecular subtypes. Its expression is significantly elevated in the Mesenchymal subtype—which typically harbors the poorest prognosis—compared to the Proneural and Classical subtypes, suggesting a potential link between SIL1, cellular dedifferentiation, and the aggressive glioma phenotype.

Tumor heterogeneity is a fundamental driver of treatment resistance and recurrence. Another important observation in this study is the distinct genomic association pattern of SIL1 in glioma. Unlike in lung or colorectal cancers, where SIL1 expression increases synchronously with tumor mutational burden (TMB) or neoantigen load (NEO), SIL1 expression in glioma primarily shows a positive correlation with mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH), demonstrating no significant association with TMB. This suggests that SIL1 expression in glioma might not be substantially driven by a high mutation rate, but could instead facilitate the evolution of subclonal diversity, thereby enhancing the tumor’s adaptive capacity under therapeutic pressure. Furthermore, stemness analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between SIL1 and the DNA methylation-based stemness index (DNAss) in glioma, whereas its association with the RNA-based stemness index was weaker. This dissociation hints at a potential link between SIL1 and epigenetic stemness features rather than simple transcriptional activation. We acknowledge that these findings are based on statistical correlations; whether SIL1 mechanistically drives epigenetic reprogramming to maintain glioma stem cell properties remains to be elucidated through further functional genomic studies. This methylation-associated stemness may contribute to the highly malignant biological features and poor prognosis observed in SIL1-high gliomas, though its specific involvement in clinical drug resistance mechanisms warrants further pharmacological investigation.

The status of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is a critical determinant of immunotherapy efficacy. Our study suggests that SIL1 may foster an “immune paradox” phenotype in glioma: although high SIL1 expression is accompanied by substantial infiltration of immune cells (such as macrophages and neutrophils) and the release of chemokines (CCL2, CXCL9/10)—features seemingly characteristic of a “hot” tumor—this infiltration is predominantly composed of immunosuppressive subsets. Specifically, SIL1 exhibits a significant positive correlation with M2 macrophages, Tregs, and the expression of key immune checkpoints (PD-L1, CTLA-4, TIM-3). Guided by the GSEA enrichment of inflammatory signaling pathways (TNF-α/NF-κB), we propose that SIL1 may, via sustained inflammatory signaling, recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells while simultaneously associating with a dysfunctional T-cell phenotype, thereby potentially establishing a vicious cycle of inflammation and immunosuppression. However, we appropriately acknowledge that without direct quantification of T-cell exhaustion markers via flow cytometry or functional immune co-culture assays, our conclusions regarding terminal immune exhaustion remain inferential. Future investigations utilizing in vivo models and direct functional immunological assays are necessary to validate these multi-omics associations.

Furthermore, the robust correlation between SIL1 expression and both the stromal score and extracellular matrix (ECM)-related pathways suggests that it may promote the formation of a dense stromal barrier. This barrier could physically impede the deep infiltration of effector T-cells while also restricting drug delivery. This mechanism of immune exclusion and stromal desmoplasia, potentially modulated by SIL1, might partially explain the low response rates to immune checkpoint blockade therapy observed in glioma patients.

To functionally validate the predictions derived from our bioinformatic analyses, we conducted loss-of-function experiments in GBM cell lines. Notably, a previous study by Xu et al. [30] reported the upregulation of SIL1 in glioma, highlighting its oncogenic role in promoting cell proliferation and invasion via the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in U251 cells. While their work laid an important foundation, our current study expands the understanding of SIL1 across several dimensions. In addition to providing a comprehensive pan-cancer multi-omics landscape and immune microenvironment profiling, our in vitro validation broadened the experimental scope. Alongside U251, we incorporated the A172 cell line (which exhibits higher basal SIL1 expression) and implemented phenotype rescue assays. Our results indicated that silencing SIL1 significantly suppressed the proliferation, clonogenicity, and migratory capabilities of glioma cells across these models, while also appearing to induce mitochondrial pathway-mediated apoptosis (evidenced by the transcriptional activation of Bax, Caspase-9, and Caspase-3, alongside the downregulation of Bcl-2).

Importantly, our findings support a potential role for SIL1 in regulating the EMT. Upon SIL1 knockdown, the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin increased, whereas the levels of a core transcription factor (Snail), mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin), and invasion-related enzymes (MMP2, MMP9) were significantly reduced. This observation is consistent with the significant enrichment of the EMT pathway identified in our GSEA analysis. Combined with the strong association between SIL1 and ECM regulation suggested by our GO and KEGG analyses, we propose that SIL1 may serve as a linking node between ECM remodeling and the EMT program. By potentially supporting the high energy and membrane biosynthesis demands of tumor cells during invasion and metastasis, SIL1 may contribute to the malignant progression of glioma.

Limitations

Although this study provides a comprehensive pan-cancer multi-omics landscape and multidimensional in vitro validation of SIL1 in glioma, several inherent limitations must be acknowledged to ensure an objective interpretation of these findings.

First, the bioinformatic analyses are inherently correlative rather than definitively causal. While our extensive transcriptomic profiling established strong statistical associations between SIL1 expression and critical oncogenic features—such as genomic heterogeneity (MATH) and DNA methylation-based stemness (DNAss)—these computational associations do not constitute direct mechanistic proof. For instance, while the dissociation between DNAss and RNA-based stemness indices suggests a potential link between SIL1 and epigenetic stemness features, determining whether SIL1 mechanistically drives epigenetic reprogramming requires further functional genomic validations, such as targeted methylation modification assays. Therefore, our multi-omics findings should be interpreted as robust transcriptomic signatures and associations rather than established causal pathways.

Second, conclusions regarding the tumor immune microenvironment and immune exhaustion remain bioinformatically inferential. Our analyses demonstrate a pattern suggestive of initial immune activation followed by subsequent exhaustion, supported by strong co-expression between SIL1 and key immune checkpoints (PD-L1, PD-1, CTLA4, TIM-3). However, we explicitly acknowledge that without direct quantification of T-cell exhaustion markers via flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), or functional immune co-culture assays, the precise role of SIL1 in terminal immune exhaustion remains a transcriptomic hypothesis. Future investigations utilizing immunocompetent orthotopic xenografts or genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), alongside direct functional immunological assays, are necessary to further delineate the potential role of SIL1 in mediating adaptive immune resistance in vivo.

Third, the constraints of in vitro models limit the physiological recapitulation of the tumor ecosystem. To maximize generalizability and mitigate cell-line-specific biases, we expanded our functional and molecular validations—including phenotypic rescue assays and SNAIL nuclear translocation monitoring—across representative glioma cell lines (U251 and A172). Nevertheless, traditional two-dimensional in vitro cultures inherently lack the complex physiological microenvironment of a human tumor, as they are isolated from host immune surveillance, stromal interactions, and dynamic extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture.

Finally, bridging the gap between molecular discovery and clinical application requires advanced integration. While the precise in vivo interaction networks of SIL1 await further experimental elucidation, the paradoxical immune state and high tumor heterogeneity associated with this biomarker represent valuable molecular variables for emerging clinical tools. As recently highlighted in the literature [1], the integration of multimodal data—encompassing specific microenvironment characteristics and molecular biomarkers like SIL1—into emerging artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning platforms holds immense potential. Future research should focus on embedding these SIL1-associated multi-omics signatures into AI-driven predictive models to potentially improve the forecasting of glioma survival and guide personalized immunotherapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study not only delineates the prognostic landscape of SIL1 across cancers but also provides an in-depth exploration of its oncogenic associations in glioma. Our findings suggest that SIL1 may contribute to the initiation and progression of glioma through multifaceted pathways: potentially maintaining genomic heterogeneity, modulating stemness via epigenetic mechanisms, remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment, and facilitating the EMT signaling pathway. Given its favorable performance in distinguishing malignant subtypes and predicting prognosis, SIL1 shows promise as a novel biomarker for the stratified diagnosis and treatment of glioma. More importantly, targeting SIL1 and its associated immunosuppressive network may offer new strategic avenues for reversing the immune-tolerant state of glioma and enhancing the efficacy of comprehensive therapies. Future research should further explore the therapeutic potential of small-molecule inhibitors targeting SIL1 and their combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors.
