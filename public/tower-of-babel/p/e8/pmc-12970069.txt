Impact of disease burden on VO2max, physical performance and frailty in ankylosing spondylitis: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Arsalan Tariq, Muhammad Ammar, Abdul Saad, Sayed Alireza Mousavi Zadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between spinal mobility, disease activity, frailty and cardiorespiratory fitness (maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max)) in patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS).MethodsA cross-sectional comparative study was conducted at the University of Lahore and seven government hospitals (March–August 2025) including 74 patients with AS (40–55 years, modified New York criteria) and 77 age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls. Assessments included disease activity (Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index), functional limitation (Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index), spinal mobility (Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI)), chest expansion, frailty (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, Loss of weight scale), pulmonary function and cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) via symptom-limited cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Functional performance was measured with the 6 min walk test (6MWT), and physical activity with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Data were analysed using t-tests, χ2 tests, correlations and regression models to examine associations between clinical measures and cardiorespiratory fitness.ResultsAmong 151 participants (74 AS, 77 controls), AS patients showed significantly reduced pulmonary function (forced expiratory volume in one second 83.5% vs 91.2%, forced vital capacity 85.1% vs 93.0%), lower VO2max (27.8 vs 33.4 mL/kg/min), impaired spinal mobility (BASMI 5.1 vs 1.2) and decreased chest expansion (2.8 vs 5.6 cm; all p<0.01). They also had shorter 6MWT distance, higher frailty and lower physical activity. VO2max was inversely correlated with disease activity, spinal stiffness, frailty and structural damage and positively correlated with chest expansion, 6MWT and activity levels. AS status, higher disease activity, greater spinal stiffness and reduced chest expansion independently predicted low physical activity.ConclusionAS is associated with impaired pulmonary function, reduced spinal mobility and lower cardiorespiratory fitness, with disease activity, stiffness and restricted chest expansion potentially influencing physical activity and overall functional capacity.

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder predominantly affecting the spine and sacroiliac joints, often leading to reduced spinal mobility, compromised chest expansion and impaired lung function. These musculoskeletal and respiratory changes may contribute to reductions in exercise capacity, physical performance and health-related quality of life in patients with AS.

AS is regarded as a relatively rare condition, but its prevalence varies widely by geographic region and population. Population-based studies suggest that in Europe, the prevalence is around 0.20–0.25%, while in Asia, it is approximately 0.16–0.30%, depending on ethnic and genetic factors.1 For instance, a large systematic review of 36 studies reported mean AS prevalence per 10 000 population as 23.8 in Europe and 16.7 in Asia.2 While precise data for Pakistan are sparse, these global estimates suggest that AS may affect around 0.1–0.4% of the adult population in populations with moderate prevalence of human leukocyte antigen B27 (HLA-B27).

Aerobic capacity, commonly measured as maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max or VO2peak), is an important indicator of cardiovascular and pulmonary health. Studies have shown that people with AS have significantly lower VO2max compared with healthy controls. In a study of 45 AS patients versus controls, VO2max, peak workload and exercise duration were all significantly lower in AS; VO2max in that study was also negatively correlated with age and with the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI), indicating that worse spinal mobility is associated with worse aerobic fitness.3

Chest expansion and restriction of the chest wall have been implicated in reduced exercise performance in AS. Classic comparative studies showed that AS patients with marked chest wall restriction had lower VO2max than healthy controls, consistent with impaired aerobic capacity in the presence of limited chest expansion. More recent research also found that aerobic capacity and forced vital capacity (FVC) are significantly lower in AS patients and correlate with measures of chest expansion, underscoring the contribution of chest wall mechanics to cardiorespiratory limitations in this condition.4

Pulmonary function tests in AS also often show reduced FVC, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and other measures, which further correlate with reduced exercise capacity. In the study by Pulmonary Function, Aerobic Capacity and Related Variables in AS,5 AS patients had lower FEV1, lower maximum work on cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) and significantly reduced VO2max versus controls. BASMI was strongly correlated with both VO2max and CPET duration.5

Disease activity indices such as Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and inflammatory markers (such as C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate(ESR)) also play a role. In studies exploring this, higher BASDAI is often associated with poorer functional status (Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI)), increased spinal stiffness, reduced spinal mobility (higher BASMI) and lower VO2max. However, some studies report that in patients with low disease activity, VO2max may not differ significantly from healthy people, suggesting that maintaining low disease activity and mobility could preserve aerobic fitness.6

Despite multiple studies demonstrating associations among spinal mobility, disease activity and aerobic capacity, gaps remain. Few studies have explored the influence of frailty, physical activity and medication adherence on aerobic capacity in these populations. Chronic systemic inflammation, pain-related inactivity and progressive musculoskeletal restriction in AS may be associated with frailty-like states, making frailty an emerging and clinically relevant construct in this population.

Many studies are relatively small and do not adjust for all potential confounders (age, sex, body mass index (BMI), medications) or examine how spinal mobility and disease activity independently contribute to VO2max. Additionally, there is limited data from certain geographic regions, including South Asia, where genetic, lifestyle and healthcare factors may differ.

Given these gaps, the present study was conducted to confirm the presence of VO2max impairment in a South Asian cohort of AS patients compared with healthy controls and to investigate associations of VO2max with spinal mobility, disease activity, pulmonary function, frailty and physical activity, while adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates. In this study, disease burden is conceptualised as a multidimensional construct reflecting the cumulative impact of AS on patients’ health and daily functioning. Rather than focusing on disease activity alone, disease burden encompasses inflammatory activity, functional limitation, spinal mobility restriction and physical performance impairment. This broader perspective allows assessment of potential functional consequences beyond inflammation alone. Understanding these relationships may help guide targeted interventions, such as exercise or physical therapy, to potentially preserve or improve cardiorespiratory fitness and functional outcomes in AS.

Methods

This cross-sectional comparative study was undertaken to examine the association between spinal mobility, disease activity and cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) in patients with AS. The study was conducted at the department of physiotherapy and rehabilitation, The University of Lahore, between March and August 2025. Consecutive patients attending the outpatient rheumatology clinics who met the modified New York criteria (mNY) for AS were screened for eligibility.7 Participants were recruited from public-sector government hospitals, which primarily serve individuals from middle–low socioeconomic backgrounds.

Radiographic assessment of structural damage was performed for all AS patients. Plain radiographs of the sacroiliac joints were obtained to confirm fulfilment of the mNY. In addition, X-rays of the cervical, thoracic and lumbar spine were obtained for all patients to document structural changes relevant to functional limitations, such as syndesmophytes and bamboo spine, which are not included in the mNY criteria. These radiographs were independently reviewed by a trained musculoskeletal radiologist. Sacroiliitis was graded from 0 (normal) to 4 (complete ankylosis) for each sacroiliac joint according to the mNY. Syndesmophytes and bamboo spine were documented descriptively based on conventional radiographic features, including bridging marginal osteophytes and contiguous vertebral ankylosis. These features are important for understanding limitations in chest expansion and their potential impact on pulmonary mechanics and aerobic capacity. The modified Stoke Ankylosing Spondylitis Spine Score (mSASSS) was not calculated, as the primary aim of this study was to assess functional and physiological impairment rather than radiographic progression. Additionally, mSASSS primarily captures cervical and lumbar vertebral changes and does not directly quantify thoracic rigidity or chest wall restriction,8 which are most relevant to pulmonary function and aerobic capacity in this population.9 10

For comparison, age-matched and sex-matched healthy control participants were recruited from hospital staff and community volunteers. For participants unable to sign, the study was explained verbally in the local language and consent was documented by thumbprint in the presence of an independent witness.

Eligible patients were men and women aged 40–55 years with a confirmed diagnosis of AS of at least 1 year’s duration. The 40–55 year age range was selected because middle age represents a period in which AS-related structural changes of the spine and chest wall are commonly established and may be associated with functional impairment, while minimising confounding from age-related sarcopenia, cardiopulmonary decline and multimorbidity that become more prevalent after age 55.11

Patients were required to be clinically stable, defined as no change in disease-modifying therapy or systemic corticosteroid dose within the preceding 3 months. Exclusion criteria included known cardiovascular disease (eg, recent myocardial infarction, unstable angina or heart failure), chronic respiratory disease unrelated to AS (such as COPD or uncontrolled asthma), neuromuscular or severe orthopaedic conditions that would preclude safe CPET, current smoking, pregnancy or any other condition that, in the investigator’s judgement, could compromise participant safety or data interpretability.

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected using a standardised case report form and included age, sex, height, weight (for BMI calculation), disease duration, current medications (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), biologics, systemic corticosteroids), comorbidities and self-reported physical activity level (International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)short form). All questionnaires and clinical assessments were performed in the participant’s preferred language by trained study staff; staff underwent a training and calibration session to ensure consistency in administration and measurement techniques.

Disease activity was measured using the BASDAI, a validated self-administered instrument consisting of six 0–10 visual analogue items assessing fatigue, spinal pain, peripheral joint pain, enthesitis and morning stiffness; the final BASDAI Score is the mean of the items, with higher scores indicating greater disease activity.12 Functional limitation was assessed with the BASFI to capture patient-reported limitations in activities of daily living.13 Spinal mobility was quantified using the BASMI modified linear version, which comprises standardised measures including tragus-to-wall distance, lateral lumbar flexion, modified Schober’s test (lumbar flexion), intermalleolar distance and cervical rotation; each component was measured according to published protocols and combined into the BASMI Score, where higher values indicate greater mobility restriction.14 15 Chest expansion was measured at the fourth intercostal space using a tape measure as the difference in circumference between maximal inspiration and maximal expiration, recorded in centimetres; occiput-to-wall distance was also recorded to capture postural kyphosis. All physical measures were taken in triplicate, and the best/average value was used for analysis, according to instrument guidance. Disease burden was assessed using a combination of validated tools capturing complementary dimensions of AS. Disease activity was evaluated using BASDAI, functional limitation using BASFI and spinal mobility restriction using BASMI. Objective physical performance and aerobic capacity were assessed using the 6 min walk test (6MWT) and VO2max. Together, these measures were selected to represent the overall clinical burden of AS beyond inflammatory activity alone. Frailty was measured using the Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, Loss of weight (FRAIL) Scale, which classifies participants as robust (score=0), pre-frail (scores 1–2) or frail (≥3).16 Although frailty instruments were originally developed for older populations, frailty is increasingly recognised in younger individuals with chronic inflammatory diseases, including AS, where persistent inflammation, pain and functional limitation may lead to reduced physiological reserve and vulnerability.17

Pulmonary function testing was conducted by certified technicians using a calibrated spirometer and following the American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society(ERS) standardisation procedures.18 FVC, FEV1 and the FEV1/FVC ratio were recorded and expressed as a percentage of predicted values adjusted for age, sex, height and ethnicity. Spirometry manoeuvres were repeated until reproducible curves were obtained, and the highest acceptable values were used.

Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed by measurement of VO2max using symptom-limited CPET on an electronically braked cycle ergometer with breath-by-breath gas analysis (metabolic cart). Prior to testing, participants completed a standardised pre-test screening including resting 12-lead ECG, blood pressure and a clinical assessment for contraindications to maximal exercise testing. The CPET protocol consisted of a 2 min unloaded warm-up followed by a ramp incremental workload increase (work rate increments individualised, typically 15–25 W every 2 min) designed to achieve volitional exhaustion within 8–12 min; ECG and blood pressure were monitored continuously during testing and oxygen saturation was observed. Breath-by-breath respiratory gas exchange data were collected and VO2 was averaged over 10 s intervals. VO2max was defined as the highest 30 s averaged VO2 value attained at peak exercise. Where the formal VO2 plateau criterion was not met, VO2peak was reported and maximisation criteria (respiratory exchange ratio ≥1.10 and attainment of ≥90% age-predicted maximal heart rate) were used to judge effort adequacy.19

Tests were terminated on volitional exhaustion or when predefined stopping criteria were met, including inability to maintain the required workload despite verbal encouragement, achievement of a respiratory exchange ratio ≥1.10, attainment of ≥85% of age-predicted maximal heart rate, onset of limiting symptoms (eg, severe dyspnoea, chest pain, dizziness or leg fatigue), abnormal blood pressure responses, significant ECG changes or oxygen desaturation below 85%, in accordance with American Thoracic Society and American College of Chest Physicians recommendations.20 The assessor supervising and recording CPET outcomes was blinded to group allocation (AS vs control) and to clinical disease activity scores. Standardised verbal encouragement was provided to all participants to ensure reproducibility and consistency across tests.

Trained medical staff and resuscitation equipment were present during each test to manage potential adverse events; a physician was immediately available and standard emergency procedures were in place. Functional performance was measured with the 6MWT, conducted according to ATS protocols on a 30 m indoor course.21 Participants were encouraged with standardised phrases, and the total distance covered in metres was recorded.

Fatigue was assessed during exercise using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (6–20 scale). Participants were encouraged to remain within the ‘green zone’ (scores≤13, corresponding to moderate exertion) during the initial stages of testing, with progression towards higher effort only as tolerated. Testing was terminated in cases of volitional exhaustion, severe fatigue (Borg score≥17),22 development of limiting symptoms (chest pain, marked dyspnoea, dizziness or leg discomfort), abnormal blood pressure responses, significant ECG changes or when the supervising physician judged it unsafe to continue.

In addition to disease-related measures, detailed information on comorbidities, socioeconomic status and medication adherence was collected. Comorbidities were documented using a standardised checklist based on participant medical records and patient interviews. Socioeconomic status was assessed through education level, employment status and household income categories. Medication adherence was evaluated using a brief self-reported adherence questionnaire and cross-checked against prescription refill records when available. These variables were included to reduce potential confounding and ensure a more comprehensive characterisation of the study population.

All data collection instruments were standardised and documented in a study manual of operations. Equipment (spirometer, metabolic cart, ergometer) was calibrated daily and logs were maintained. To minimise inter-observer variability, the same two physiotherapists performed the majority of CPETs and mobility measures; intra- and inter-rater reliability checks were conducted on a subset of participants prior to data collection.

The required sample size was determined a priori using G*Power V.3.1. The test family was set to t-tests, with the statistical test defined as means: difference between two independent means (two groups). Parameters were entered as follows: two-tailed test, medium effect size (Cohen’s d=0.5), α error probability=0.05, power (1–β error probability)=0.90 and allocation ratio N2/N1=1. Based on these assumptions, the minimum required sample size was 146 participants (73 per group). To account for potential dropouts and missing data, we increased the target sample to 160 participants.

Data were entered into a secure electronic database with range checks and logic constraints to reduce entry errors; data quality was verified by double entry of a randomly selected 10% sample.

All analyses were performed using SPSS V.24.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using histograms, Q–Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean±SD, while non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with IQRs. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between VO2max, BASDAI, BASMI, 6MWT, frailty scores and IPAQ activity levels. Multivariable regression models were used to examine predictors of VO2max and low physical activity, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, BASMI, BASDAI and chest expansion. Model assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals and multicollinearity, were checked, and 95% CIs and effect sizes were calculated. Multiple linear regression models were constructed to identify predictors of VO2max, adjusting for age, BMI, BASDAI, BASMI and group status (AS vs control). Model fit was assessed using R². Binary logistic regression was applied to determine predictors of low physical activity (IPAQ low vs moderate/high), with ORs and 95% CIs reported. Model calibration was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and explanatory power with Nagelkerke R². Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for mean group differences and ORs for categorical outcomes. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion, excluding participants with incomplete variables from the relevant analyses. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p<0.05. Missing data were minimal. VO2max data were missing for four participants, spirometry variables for three participants, and questionnaire-based measures (BASDAI, BASMI, IPAQ, FRAIL) for 2–3 participants due to incomplete assessments. Patterns of missingness were examined and showed no significant association with age, sex, disease duration or disease activity, suggesting that data were missing at random. Therefore, listwise deletion was considered appropriate for the primary analyses.

Of these, nine participants were excluded, six from the AS group due to inability to complete assessments and three from the control group owing to incomplete data. The final study population comprised 151 participants, with 74 in the AS group and 77 in the control group. Among AS patients, the mean disease duration was 9.6±5.0 years. Treatment patterns reflected disease-specific management, with 35.1% of patients receiving biologic therapy, 78.4% using NSAIDs and a mean daily steroid dose of 7.5±2.1 mg (prednisone equivalent). Medication adherence in the AS group was reported as high in 40.5% of patients, medium in 44.6% and low in 14.9%. Notably, none of the participants in either group were current smokers.

Occupational distribution was similar between groups, with participants engaged in office/clerical work, manual labour, agricultural work and the service sector in comparable proportions. Comorbidities were present in 28.4% of AS patients and 24.7% of controls, most commonly hypertension and type 2 diabetes. All participants were from a middle–low socioeconomic status background, indicating similar socioeconomic profiles.

Table 2 summarises differences in respiratory function, spinal mobility and physical performance between the AS and control groups. Patients with AS showed significantly lower pulmonary function, including FEV1 (83.5±11.4% vs 91.2±8.9%, p=0.005, d=0.76), FVC (85.1±10.6% vs 93.0±9.2%, p=0.004, d=0.79) and FEV1/FVC ratio (0.72±0.06 vs 0.78±0.05, p=0.006, d=1.05), as well as lower aerobic capacity (VO2max: 27.8±5.3 vs 33.4±6.1 mL/kg/min, p=0.003, d=0.99). Frailty and physical activity distributions are presented in table 3. A significantly higher proportion of AS patients were classified as frail (32.4%, 9.1%; χ²=11.32, p=0.001) or pre-frail (45.9%, 28.6%; χ²=4.65, p=0.03), whereas robust status was more frequent in controls (21.6%, 62.3%; χ²=9.12, p=0.003). The odds of being frail were over four times higher in AS patients than in controls (OR=4.85, 95% CI 2.01 to 11.73). Low physical activity was also more prevalent in the AS group (41.9%, 15.6%; χ²=9.89, p=0.002; OR=3.94, 95% CI 1.73 to 8.96), while high activity levels were more common among controls (24.3%, 53.2%; χ²=8.07, p=0.005). These findings indicate a strong association between AS, increased frailty and reduced physical activity.

Table 4 details the association between medication adherence, frailty and physical activity among patients with AS. Non-adherent patients (low adherence) were significantly more likely to be classified as frail compared with adherent patients (54.5% vs 28.6%; χ²=4.21, p=0.04), with nearly threefold higher odds of frailty (OR=2.85, 95% CI 1.01 to 8.12). Low physical activity was also substantially more common in the non-adherent group (72.7% vs 34.9%; χ²=5.78, p=0.02), with almost fourfold increased odds (OR=3.92, 95% CI 1.16 to 12.2). No significant differences were observed between adherence groups for pre-frail status (36.4% vs 47.6%; χ²=0.47, p=0.49) or robust classification (9.1% vs 23.8%; χ²=1.17, p=0.28). These findings suggest that poor medication adherence in AS patients was associated with higher frailty scores and lower physical activity levels.

Online supplemental table 1 shows strong interrelationships among clinical, functional and structural measures in AS patients. VO2max was negatively correlated with disease activity (BASDAI; r = –0.58, p<0.01), spinal stiffness (BASMI; r = –0.62, p<0.01), frailty score (r = –0.55, p<0.01), sacroiliitis grade (r = –0.48, p<0.01), syndesmophytes (r = –0.44, p<0.01) and bamboo spine (r = –0.32, p<0.05) and positively correlated with 6MWT distance (r=0.71, p<0.01), physical activity (IPAQ; r=0.60, p<0.01) and chest expansion (r=0.65, p<0.01). Higher BASDAI and BASMI scores, as well as frailty, were linked to lower VO2max, reduced 6MWT distance, decreased physical activity and smaller chest expansion. Structural damage indicators were positively correlated with each other (sacroiliitis vs syndesmophytes r=0.62, p<0.01; syndesmophytes vs bamboo spine r=0.55, p<0.01) and negatively correlated with fitness and function. These results highlight that greater disease burden, spinal rigidity, frailty and structural damage are associated with markedly reduced aerobic capacity, physical activity and functional performance in AS patients.

Within the AS group, higher frailty scores were associated with greater disease severity. Frailty was positively correlated with radiographic measures, including sacroiliitis grade (r=0.39, p<0.01), presence of syndesmophytes (r=0.36, p<0.01) and bamboo spine (r=0.27, p<0.05). It was also linked to lower aerobic capacity and physical performance, with higher frailty scores associated with lower VO2max (r = –0.55, p<0.01), shorter 6MWT distance (r = –0.59, p<0.01) and reduced chest expansion (r = –0.50, p<0.01). Frailty was lower in patients receiving biologic therapy (mean FRAIL Score 1.6±0.9) compared with those receiving non-biologic therapy (mean FRAIL Score 2.3±1.1), although frailty remained prevalent across all treatment groups.

Multiple linear regression analysis (table 5) identified key predictors of VO2max in the study population. After adjusting for age, sex, BMI, BASMI, BASDAI, chest expansion, frailty and group status, older age (B = –0.28, 95% CI –0.50 to –0.06, p=0.012), female sex (B = –1.85, 95% CI –3.50 to –0.20, p=0.028), higher BMI (B = –0.35, 95% CI –0.63 to –0.07, p=0.017), greater spinal stiffness (BASMI; B = –1.42, 95% CI –2.30 to –0.54, p=0.003) and higher disease activity (BASDAI; B = –0.89, 95% CI –1.39 to –0.39, p=0.001) were negatively associated with VO2max. Conversely, greater chest expansion was positively associated with VO2max (B=1.78, 95% CI 0.90 to 2.66, p=0.002). Higher frailty was independently associated with lower VO2max (B = –1.12, 95% CI –1.98 to –0.26, p=0.012). Group status also had a significant effect, with AS patients showing lower VO2max compared with controls (B = –4.12, 95% CI –6.30 to –1.94, p=0.005). The model accounted for 61% of the variance in VO2max (adjusted R²=0.61, F=19.2, p<0.001), and variance inflation factors were all below 2, indicating minimal collinearity between functional indices and frailty. These results highlight that age, sex, BMI, disease activity, spinal mobility, chest expansion, frailty and AS status are all important determinants of aerobic capacity.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that patients with AS exhibited reduced aerobic capacity, impaired pulmonary function, restricted spinal mobility and higher frailty compared with age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls. Greater disease burden, reflected by higher disease activity and spinal mobility limitation, was associated with lower VO2max and reduced physical activity. These findings highlight the cumulative impact of musculoskeletal and functional impairments on physical performance and overall vulnerability in individuals with AS.

Our findings of reduced lung function (FEV1, FVC and FEV1/FVC) in AS align with previous studies reporting restrictive ventilatory defects in this population. Pulmonary function decline in AS is largely attributable to chest wall restriction and ankylosis of the costovertebral joints,23 and impaired lung capacity has been reported compared with healthy controls, even after adjustment for age and smoking.24 These impairments persist in middle-aged patients, highlighting the need for routine respiratory assessment.

The decrease in aerobic capacity (VO2max) observed in AS patients aligns with prior research showing that reduced exercise tolerance is associated with disease activity and musculoskeletal limitations.25 AS patients also show reduced VO2peak compared with healthy controls, and structured exercise programmes can enhance aerobic capacity while reducing cardiovascular risk.26 In line with these observations, our results show that higher BASDAI and BASMI scores are independently associated with reduced VO2max. Greater spinal mobility restriction (BASMI) was strongly correlated with VO2max and 6MWT performance, highlighting the impact of reduced spinal flexibility and chest expansion on cardiorespiratory function.27 These findings suggest that cumulative musculoskeletal restriction and reduced respiratory capacity may contribute to vulnerability in AS.

It is important to consider the socioeconomic context when interpreting these findings. All participants were recruited from government hospitals and predominantly belonged to middle–low socioeconomic backgrounds, which may influence access to therapies, rehabilitation, nutrition and opportunities for physical activity. Consequently, the results may not be fully generalisable to patients from higher-income or private healthcare settings, and future studies should include more socioeconomically diverse populations.

The prevalence of frailty in AS patients (32.4%) was markedly higher than in controls, consistent with Motta et al, who reported that chronic inflammatory rheumatic diseases are associated with increased frailty and pre-frailty compared with the general population.28 Frailty in AS may result from systemic inflammation, reduced mobility and physical deconditioning. In our study, frailty scores were strongly correlated with BASDAI and BASMI, which suggests that there may be a link between disease severity and increased vulnerability. Higher frailty scores were also associated with greater structural severity, reduced chest expansion and impaired lung function, highlighting the role of musculoskeletal and respiratory limitations in vulnerability. Similar associations have been reported in other inflammatory rheumatic diseases.17 29 Patients receiving non-biologic therapy had higher frailty scores, possibly due to poorer disease control, whereas biologic therapies may improve physical function and reduce frailty-related risk in AS.30 Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL Scale, which has not been specifically validated in AS, so overlap with disease-related functional limitations (eg, BASFI, BASMI) cannot be excluded.31 32

Levels of physical activity assessed by the IPAQ were reduced in individuals with AS, consistent with earlier studies that reported lower habitual activity among AS patients compared with healthy counterparts.33 Our logistic regression analysis demonstrated that disease burden, rather than demographic characteristics such as age or BMI, was the main predictor of low activity. These results echo the findings of Halvorsen et al, who reported that disease activity is a stronger determinant of inactivity than socioeconomic or lifestyle factors.34

Given the independent associations between higher BASMI, higher BASDAI, frailty and reduced VO2max, rehabilitation strategies in AS should be structured, progressive and individualised according to disease burden and functional status. Aerobic exercise is recommended 3–5 times per week at moderate intensity (approximately 50–70% of VO2max or a Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion of 12–14) for 30–45 min per session, using low-impact modalities such as walking, cycling or swimming. Resistance training targeting major muscle groups should be incorporated 2–3 times per week to counteract muscle weakness and frailty-related decline.3537 Rehabilitation should include thoracic mobility exercises, postural correction and respiratory muscle training, such as diaphragmatic breathing and chest expansion exercises. Patients with higher frailty or functional limitations may benefit from supervised, gradually progressive programmes. Exercise intensity and progression should be guided by regular monitoring of BASMI, chest expansion and VO2max to tailor interventions according to disease severity, functional limitations and frailty, optimising safety and long-term physical outcomes.

Taken together, our study supports and extends previous evidence that AS is associated with multisystem impairments, including reduced lung function, lower aerobic capacity, higher frailty burden and decreased physical activity. The consistency of our findings across populations highlights the robustness of these associations. Importantly, the use of objective cardiopulmonary testing (VO2max, spirometry) and validated functional measures (BASMI, FRAIL, 6MWT) strengthens the evidence base.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Comorbidities and medication adherence were assessed mainly through self-report and available medical records, which may have introduced recall or reporting bias. Participants were recruited exclusively from government hospitals in Lahore and predominantly belonged to middle–low socioeconomic backgrounds, which may limit generalisability to higher-income populations or private healthcare settings. The study focused on middle-aged adults (40–55 years), which limits generalisability to younger patients with more active disease or older patients with more advanced structural changes. Patients with major cardiovascular or pulmonary comorbidities were excluded to ensure CPET safety, which may underestimate the true burden of impaired aerobic capacity in the broader AS population. Although CPET procedures followed standardised protocols, VO2max may have been underestimated in some participants due to pain, stiffness or fatigue limiting maximal effort. Selection bias is possible because participants had to be clinically stable and able to complete CPET, which may have led to under-representation of those with severe disability or advanced disease. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reverse causation is possible, whereby frailty or reduced physical capacity may contribute to lower physical activity levels rather than the reverse. In addition, frailty was assessed using the FRAIL scale, which has not been specifically validated in AS. As a result, some participants may have been classified as frail due to AS-related functional limitations rather than true frailty, reflecting potential overlap with disease-specific disability (eg, BASFI, BASMI), which should be considered when interpreting associations with VO2max and physical activity. Finally, mSASSS scoring was not performed, which limits detailed quantification of spinal structural damage beyond the descriptive documentation of syndesmophytes and bamboo spine.

Conclusion

Patients with AS showed reduced cardiorespiratory fitness, lung function, spinal mobility and physical performance compared with healthy controls. Frailty and low physical activity were more common and were linked to poor medication adherence. Disease activity and mobility restriction were the strongest predictors of reduced VO2max and inactivity. These findings highlight the need for targeted exercise, adherence support and early management to preserve fitness and quality of life in AS.
