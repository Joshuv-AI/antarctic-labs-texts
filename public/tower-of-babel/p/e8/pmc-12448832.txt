Investigating Bacterial Vaginosis Pathogenesis Using Peptide Nucleic Acid-Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization With a Focus on the Roles of Gardnerella Species, Prevotella bivia, and Fannyhessea vaginae
Authors: Sheridan D George, Megan H Amerson-Brown, Lúcia G V Sousa, Tyler M Carter, Alexa H Rinehart, Ashleigh N Riegler, Sixto M Leal, Kristal J Aaron, Jiaying Hao, Ashutosh Tamhane, Chaoling Dong, John W Lammons, Jacob H Elnaggar, Keonte J Graves, Paweł Łaniewski, Melissa M Herbst-Kralovetz, Christopher M Taylor, Nuno Cerca, Christina A Muzny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial vaginosis (BV) is a vaginal dysbiosis characterized by polymicrobial communities of BV-associated bacteria (BVAB) adhered to the vaginal epithelium. Despite decades of research, its etiology remains unknown. We aimed to investigate BV biofilm formation over time among women who developed incident BV (iBV) using peptide nucleic acid-fluorescence in situ hybridization (PNA-FISH), focusing on 3 key BVAB (Gardnerella species, Prevotella bivia, and Fannyhessea vaginae).MethodsHeterosexual, nonpregnant women ages 18–45 with optimal vaginal microbiota were enrolled to self-collect twice-daily vaginal specimens for 60 days. iBV was defined as a Nugent score of 7–10 on ≥4 consecutive specimens. For women who developed iBV (cases), Gardnerella spp., P. bivia, and F. vaginae were visualized and quantified by PNA-FISH for up to 14 days prior to iBV, the day of iBV, and 3 days post-iBV. Cases were matched to women maintaining optimal vaginal microbiota (controls) based on age, race, and contraceptive method. Control specimens were matched to case specimens by day of menses.ResultsAmong 135 women enrolled, 18 developed iBV and were matched to 18 controls. Pooled median Gardnerella spp. counts significantly increased starting 5 days before iBV, while pooled median F. vaginae counts significantly increased on the day of iBV diagnosis. In contrast, pooled median P. bivia counts were not significantly different between groups.ConclusionsThese data suggest that Gardnerella spp. are early colonizers of the BV biofilm while F. vaginae is a secondary colonizer. P. bivia was not found to be significantly different between iBV case and control specimens.

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is a vaginal dysbiosis that affects up to 30% of reproductive-age women [1]. It is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth, acquisition of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), pelvic inflammatory disease, postoperative pelvic infections, and infertility [2–5]. Despite decades of research, BV etiology remains elusive [1]. However, epidemiological data and a recent male partner treatment trial strongly suggest that BV-associated bacteria (BVAB) are sexually transmitted [6–9]. Few studies have longitudinally tracked species-level changes preceding incident BV (iBV) to accurately depict the induction of BV. Consequently, the incubation period of BV remains controversial [8], although a prior study suggests that it may be around 4 days after sexual activity, similar to that of other bacterial STIs such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae [8].

Clinically, BV is associated with an elevated vaginal pH (>4.5), positive whiff test, and the desquamation of vaginal epithelial cells (VECs) covered with BVAB, which form characteristic “clue cells” visualized on wet mount microscopy [10, 11]. Mechanistically, BV is characterized by loss of protective, lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide-producing Lactobacillus spp. (eg, Lactobacillus crispatus, L. jensenii, and L. gasseri) and an overgrowth of facultative (Gardnerella spp.) and strict anaerobic bacteria such as Prevotella spp. and Fannyhessea vaginae [11–13]. These anaerobic bacteria adhere to VECs to form a polymicrobial biofilm [14]. While a recent study suggests that untreated sexual partners may contribute to BV reinfection [9], the persistence of bacterial biofilm on VECs is also likely a contributor to the high BV recurrence rates after antimicrobial therapy [15–17].

In our recent iBV pathogenesis study in African American women who have sex with women (WSW), we demonstrated that the mean relative abundance of Prevotella bivia and Gardnerella vaginalis increased significantly in women with iBV (n = 14) compared to healthy controls (n = 8) 4 days and 3 days prior to iBV, respectively [18]. In contrast, the mean relative abundance of F. vaginae significantly increased in iBV cases compared to controls on the day of iBV. P. bivia and Gardnerella spp. have been found to have a synergistic relationship in vitro that may aid in the development of the BV biofilm [19]. Furthermore, the growth of F. vaginae is known to be enhanced by Gardnerella spp. [20, 21].

Based on this study [18] and an extensive literature review [19, 22], we developed an updated hypothetical model for iBV pathogenesis [11], suggesting that G. vaginalis and P. bivia may establish early colonization of the BV biofilm, whereas F. vaginae may be a secondary colonizer. Although our study found that P. bivia may be the initial colonizer of the BV biofilm, a large body of evidence supports Gardnerella spp. as the primary pathogen [14, 18, 23, 24]; thus, the roles of these bacteria in BV biofilm development require further investigation among larger cohorts of women, including women who have sex with men (WSM).

In this observational, prospective follow-up study, we aimed to further investigate iBV biofilm formation over time among WSM who developed iBV, focusing on Gardnerella spp., P. bivia, and F. vaginae using peptide nucleic acid fluorescence in situ hybridization (PNA-FISH). We hypothesized that, among women who developed iBV compared to healthy controls, Gardnerella spp. would be the primary colonizer prior to iBV diagnosis, with subsequent colonization by P. bivia, followed by F. vaginae.

METHODS

Participants

This study was performed at the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Sexual Health Research Clinic; study methods have been published [25]. Briefly, potential participants were recruited through flyers, social media campaigns, local events, word-of-mouth, and radio advertisements. Women expressing interest scheduled a screening visit. Screening inclusion criteria included assigned female sex at birth, 18–45 years of age, history of sex with men, and a current male sexual partner. Screening exclusion criteria included antibiotic usage (oral or intravaginal) within the past 14 days, self-reported HIV infection, current pregnancy, current menses, and a history of sex with women. At the screening visit, women completed a written informed consent form and screening questionnaire, provided urine for a pregnancy test, and self-collected a vaginal swab to test for BV by the Amsel criteria and Nugent score [26, 27]. Nonpregnant women with no Amsel criteria and a Nugent score of 0–3 with no Gardnerella spp. morphotypes (read by a research clinician in the clinic and confirmed by a secondary reader in our laboratory) were enrolled into the study. Enrolled women completed a second written informed consent form and additional questionnaire on sociodemographic information, recent antibiotic use, sexual history, STI history, douching history, contraception method(s), and recent use of alcohol, tobacco, or illicit drug(s). Next, a pelvic exam was performed to evaluate for vaginal discharge or any signs of active infection and obtain a vaginal specimen for nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) for Chlamydia trachomatis, Trichomonas vaginalis, N. gonorrhoeae, and Mycoplasma genitalium using the Roche cobas 6800 CT/GC and TV/MG assays [28, 29]. Enrolled women who tested negative for STIs, BV, and symptomatic vulvovaginal candidiasis were taught how to self-collect vaginal specimens for the study, in addition to receiving written specimen collection instructions and materials and hard copies of daily diaries.

Specimen Collection

Enrolled participants self-collected 3 vaginal swab specimens twice daily for 60 days, or until iBV was diagnosed. Specimens were collected with nylon-tipped flocked swabs (Copan Diagnostics) and placed in separate tubes of 1 mL of a 1:10 dilution of AssayAssure Sample Preservative (Sierra Molecular Corporation) and 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution. One of these vaginal swabs was used to smear a glass slide for Nugent score determination prior to placing it into a collection tube. The slides for Nugent scoring were stored at room temperature in plastic slide holders at participant homes to prevent breakage. All vaginal specimens were refrigerated at the participant's home until weekly drop-off at the clinic, at which time compliance with the study procedures was monitored. One of the 3 specimens collected twice daily was used for this study. After the vaginal specimens were received in our laboratory, they were stored in a −80°C freezer. Daily diaries included questions on sexual activities, vaginal symptoms, douching, menses, and medication use [25]. All data were entered into a REDCap database [30]. This study was approved by the UAB Institutional Review Board (Protocol #IRB-300004547).

Selection of Cases and Controls

Twice daily smears of vaginal specimens were Gram-stained and read for Nugent score in our laboratory [27]. To determine the Nugent score, 6 fields were visualized per specimen. iBV was defined by a Nugent score of 7–10 on ≥4 consecutive specimens. Participants who developed iBV during the 60-day course of the study were deemed an iBV case. Healthy controls were selected if they maintained optimal vaginal microbiota for the majority of the study (eg, a Nugent score of 0–3 for at least 85% of study days) and were matched to each iBV case by age (±5 years), race, and contraceptive method. Control specimens were matched to case specimens by day of menses. For example, if a case developed iBV (denoted iBV day 0) on day 18 of her menstrual cycle, day 18 of her healthy control's menstrual cycle was designated as day 0. Pairing of cases and controls was based on data provided in the enrollment questionnaire and daily diaries.

PNA-FISH Fixation and Hybridization

Select vaginal specimens from cases and controls were subsequently thawed and gently vortexed prior to fixation to resuspend VECs and bacteria for PNA-FISH. This approach was based on previously validated protocols using PNA-FISH to study in situ microbial biofilms on VECs, which have been shown to retain in vivo-like bacterial adherence patterns [31–33]. Twenty microliters of each vaginal specimen from cases spanning 14 days prior to iBV, the day of iBV (day 0), and 3 days post-iBV were fixed onto epoxy-coated glass slides at 37°C using methanol, paraformaldehyde, and ethanol [25]. Specimens for controls were fixed for 18 consecutive days, matching their respective iBV case participant by day of menses. Subsequently, 10 µL of the hybridization solution [34] containing 200 nM of the custom-designed PNA probes with fluorophores targeting Gardnerella spp. 16S rRNA (Alexa Fluor 594-OO-CAGCATTACCACCCG), P. bivia 23S rRNA (Alexa Fluor 488-OO-ATTAAACGTCCATGTCC), and F. vaginae 23S rRNA (Alexa Fluor 633-OO-CGATGTGCGACTAAA) (PNA Bio) were hybridized to the target bacteria in the vaginal specimens [33–35] (Figure 1; Supplementary Table 1). Slides were incubated at 60°C for 90 min and subsequently washed at 60°C for 30 min. Then, counterstaining with 1:1000 4′,6′-diamindino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) diluted in PBS was used to detect the presence of bacterial DNA and the nuclei of VECs. For positive controls, American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) reference strains of G. vaginalis (14018), P. bivia (29303), and F. vaginae (BAA-55) were individually cultured on Columbia Agar plates with 5% Sheep Blood (Thermo Scientific) under anaerobic conditions using anaerobic gas-generating packs (AnaeroGen, Oxoid) and inoculated into 1× PBS at 1 × 106 colony forming units/mL determined by spectrophotometry [34, 36]. For each PNA probe, a 10 µL aliquot of a control vaginal specimen was spiked with 10 µL of the corresponding bacterium of interest, then fixed and hybridized. The spiked specimens demonstrated successful detection of the target organism by its respective probe (Supplementary Figure 1). For negative controls, a slide containing only 1× PBS was fixed and hybridized to control for contamination and background fluorescence or autofluorescence of the slide or reagents.

PNA-FISH Method Workflow. Vaginal specimens were self-collected twice daily from enrolled participants and screened for iBV by Nugent score. Specimens from iBV cases and controls were fixed to microscope slides and hybridized using PNA-FISH probes for Gardnerella spp. (Alexa Fluor 594), P. bivia (Alexa Fluor 488), and F. vaginae (Alexa Fluor 633). DAPI counterstaining was used to detect vaginal epithelial cells and bacterial DNA. Slides were imaged and bacterial colonies were quantified.

Fluorescent Imaging

Gardnerella spp. and P. bivia are thought to be early colonizers of the BV biofilm [11]; therefore counts of each were recorded for the twice daily vaginal specimens spanning 14 days prior to iBV diagnosis through 3 days after [25]. Based on our prior study suggesting that F. vaginae is a secondary colonizer [18], F. vaginae counts were analyzed in twice daily vaginal specimens 6 days prior to iBV diagnosis through 3 days after [11]. Twice daily vaginal specimens from controls were imaged across the same timeframe as their respective iBV cases, matched by days of menses. For a less biased comparison with the 6 fields viewed in the Nugent scoring methods, 6 fluorescent images were captured per specimen for Gardnerella spp. and P. bivia under a 60× objective using the TRITC and FITC filters on the EVOS M5000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. Due to the inability to view all 3 species simultaneously on the EVOS M5000 Imaging System, the NanoZoomer S60 Slide Scanner (Hamamatsu Corporation) system was used to image F. vaginae with the CY5 filter using the same imaging protocol as for Gardnerella spp. and P. bivia. Following image capture, counts of ≤10 bacterial species per field were recorded manually, while counts of >10 bacterial species per field were counted using an imaging software (EVOS M5000 or Fiji ImageJ 1.8.0). The imaging software quantified bacteria using semiautomated image analysis, in which author SDG optimized the contrast thresholds to distinguish bacterial signals from background signals. The software then automatically quantified the bacterial cells. For each case and control, a median bacterial count for each bacterial spp. of interest was calculated from the 6 fields imaged per specimen for each time point. The median bacterial counts for each bacterial spp. of interest per specimen at each time point were then pooled into respective iBV case and control groups. To assess if the utilization of 2 microscopes significantly affected the outcome of the experiments, Gardnerella spp. quantification was compared between the 2 microscopes using 35 specimens from an iBV case (Supplementary Figure 2); there were no statistical differences between the counts of bacteria detected across the timeframe selected.

Statistical Analysis

Conditional logistic regression with pair stratification was used to compare case and control characteristics, and P-values were derived from the Type 3 Analysis of Effects to assess overall differences between groups. Because the bacterial count data were non-normally distributed and paired, the nonparametric Wilcoxon matched pairs signed rank test was used to determine significant differences between cases and controls. All days of interest, including half days, totaled 35 different comparisons for Gardnerella spp. and P. bivia. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, adjusting the significance threshold to α of 0.05/35 tests = 0.0014. The number of total comparisons for F. vaginae was 19. The Bonferroni correction was applied again, adjusting the significance threshold to α of 0.05/19 tests = 0.0026. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 and GraphPad Prism 10.0.2. Interquartile range (IQR) was reported for all count data. The nonparametric Wilcoxon matched pairs signed rank test was also used to compare the counts between the EVOS M5000 and the NanoZoomer S60 microscopes.

RESULTS

This study enrolled from November 2020 to August 2024. In total, 412 women were screened in-person, with 160 women screened more than once if they failed their initial screening visit, totaling 629 screening visits for this study. Of those screened, 135 (32.8%) met enrollment inclusion criteria. Of these 135 participants, 95 (70.4%) completed the entire 60-day study protocol or met the primary endpoint of iBV diagnosis. Based on our iBV definition (Nugent score of 7–10 on ≥4 consecutive specimens), 18 (13.3%) participants developed iBV (cases) and were matched to 18 controls. Table 1 presents the characteristics of iBV cases and controls. No significant differences were observed between groups. The median time to iBV development after enrollment among cases was 17 days (IQR 5–29). A total of 980 twice-daily vaginal specimens (490 iBV case specimens and 490 control specimens) from 18 iBV cases and 18 controls were subsequently analyzed using PNA-FISH.

Prevalence of Gardnerella Species

The pooled median counts of Gardnerella spp. in iBV cases 14 days before iBV diagnosis and on the day of iBV diagnosis were 140 (IQR 20–296.5) bacteria and 325.5 (IQR 40.1–459.9) bacteria, respectively (Figure 2A). In contrast, control specimens had much lower pooled median Gardnerella spp. counts at these 2 time points: 1.5 (IQR 0.75–4.3) bacteria 14 days before the matched day of iBV diagnosis and 4 (IQR 1.6–6.3) bacteria on matched day of iBV diagnosis in cases. Across the majority of days analyzed, pooled median Gardnerella spp. counts in cases were over 10-fold higher than pooled median Gardnerella spp. counts in controls (Figure 2A). Pooled median Gardnerella spp. counts in iBV cases became significantly greater than pooled median Gardnerella spp. counts in controls starting 5 days before iBV diagnosis through 3 days post-iBV diagnosis (P < .001) (Figures 2A, 3, and 4).

Prevalence of P. bivia

In contrast to Gardnerella spp. counts, the amount of P. bivia was consistently low across all days imaged (<10), with few or no organisms observed in both case and control specimens (Figures 2B, 3, and 4). In case and control specimens, pooled median P. bivia counts across all days imaged and on the day of iBV was 0 (IQR 0–0) bacteria.

Prevalence of F. vaginae

Similar to Gardnerella spp., F. vaginae was detected in both iBV case and control specimens, although at lower counts. The pooled median counts of F. vaginae in cases throughout all days imaged was 18.25 (IQR 5–88.8) bacteria and 19.75 (IQR 9.1–152.4) bacteria on the day of iBV. The pooled median count of F. vaginae in controls was 5 (IQR 3–10.5) bacteria across all days and 4.5 (IQR 3–8) bacteria on the day matched to iBV diagnosis in iBV cases. Pooled median F. vaginae counts became significantly higher in cases compared to controls on the day of iBV diagnosis (P ≤ .0006) and remained significantly higher across the remainder of days imaged (Figures 2C, 3, and 4).

DISCUSSION

To gain a better understanding of bacterial colonization across time among WSM who developed iBV, Gardnerella spp., P. bivia, and F. vaginae were fluorescently visualized and quantified in iBV case and control vaginal specimens using PNA-FISH. Consistent with our hypothesis [18, 25], the comparison of pooled median bacterial counts in iBV cases and controls revealed a significant increase in the quantity of Gardnerella spp. and F. vaginae starting 5 days before iBV diagnosis and on the day of iBV diagnosis, respectively. Taken together, these data confirm an important role of Gardnerella spp. in early colonization of the BV biofilm and a secondary role of F. vaginae at the time of iBV diagnosis [11]. These findings are consistent with those of our previous study in African American WSW [18], although in a different and larger population of women (ie, WSM of all races/ethnicities) using different methods of detection of our key bacterial spp. of interest. This demonstrates that our previous findings are generalizable to a larger and more diverse group of women.

Contrary to other BV pathogenesis studies [18, 22, 37, 38] and to our hypothesis, we observed low counts of P. bivia in iBV case and control specimens. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantitate P. bivia using PNA-FISH in clinical vaginal specimens [34]. Previous studies emphasizing P. bivia's importance in iBV pathogenesis have primarily used in vitro or in silico methods with nonclinical isolates, shotgun metagenomic sequencing, or clinical culture, rather than directly visualizing bacterial-VEC interactions by fluorescence microscopy [18, 22, 37–39]. These different forms of examination may contribute to the discrepancy between our findings and those of others [40]. Furthermore, microbiome studies based on 16S rRNA sequencing cannot discriminate between closely related species within the Prevotella genus, making it possible that sequences previously attributed to P. bivia were derived from other Prevotella spp. By contrast, our PNA probe was designed to target a unique region for P. bivia, ensuring high species-level specificity and minimizing cross reactivity [34]. Although our prior study found a possible role for P. bivia in iBV pathogenesis, it included a different population than that included in this study (ie, WSW presenting to an STI clinic) [18]. Additionally, we used the PECAN database [41] for taxonomic assignments, which was in its initial testing stages at the time and may not have accurately distinguished between different Prevotella spp., especially given that there are now more robust, preferred tools that are in use [42]. Thus, these variables likely contributed to variations in vaginal microbiota composition between our iBV pathogenesis studies [18].

The importance of differentiating between Prevotella spp. has been further emphasized in a recent in vitro study utilizing PNA-FISH, which found that the ability of P. timonensis, but not P. bivia, to adhere to VECs and endocervical epithelial cells was similar to G. vaginalis [43]. Likewise, P. timonensis significantly removed VEC glycans more effectively than P. bivia and G. vaginalis, suggesting that P. timonensis may play an important role in iBV pathogenesis [39, 43]. Examining other members of the Prevotella genus including P. timonensis, P. amnii, and P. disiens may provide a more detailed understanding of Prevotella spp. colonization prior to iBV [39, 43, 44].

The pooled median bacterial counts of both Gardnerella spp. and F. vaginae were higher in iBV cases versus controls throughout all days analyzed, supporting the hypothesis that women colonized with both Gardnerella spp. and F. vaginae prior to iBV were more likely to develop iBV [45]. Further research is needed to elucidate factors that may promote the elevated bacterial loads observed prior to iBV and the trigger(s) that drive specific bacterial populations to expand in advance of iBV.

Given the recent taxonomic reclassification of the Gardnerella genus into 13 genomospecies [46, 47] and that Gardnerella spp. have been detected in women without a history of sexual activity [48] and women without BV [49], our finding of increased Gardnerella spp. abundance associated with iBV supports the possibility of specific virulence mechanisms utilized by select Gardnerella spp. Because the Gardnerella spp. PNA probe used in this study was not able to distinguish among different Gardnerella spp. [33], some may be more virulent than others, as has been shown in vitro [50]. In a prior study focusing on the individual roles of Gardnerella spp., qPCR results from BV and non-BV clinical specimens suggested that cooccurrence of multiple Gardnerella spp. was associated with BV [51]. Further identification of which species, or several species, contribute to the increase in overall Gardnerella spp. abundance prior to the onset of iBV is needed, including studies using species-specific Gardnerella PNA probes.

Our study provides additional evidence of a significant increase in Gardnerella spp. abundance starting 5 days prior to iBV diagnosis and a significant increase in F. vaginae counts on the day of iBV diagnosis. These results support the implementation of BV diagnostic tests targeting Gardnerella spp. and F. vaginae that detect increases in these bacteria in iBV cases [45]. Similarly, the lower abundance of these BVAB observed in control specimens suggests a potential threshold to distinguish between iBV positive and negative specimens. This would be most helpful in the setting of recurrent BV where women could potentially be screened at regular intervals and started on antimicrobial therapy early, particularly if the number of Gardnerella spp. rise above the threshold. However, as illustrated by our data, this threshold may be difficult to determine, as controls had a larger variability of Gardnerella spp. counts than cases, presumably from its fluctuating abundance within the vaginal microbiota [49]. Likewise, F. vaginae may also be a colonizer of the vaginal microbiota whose abundance only significantly increases after the initiation of BV biofilm formation [11 ]. Future research should further investigate specific Gardnerella spp. detection thresholds between non-BV and BV cases.

Limitations of this study included the self-collection and self-storing of participants’ vaginal specimens, which could have influenced specimen preservation and bacterial counts. However, this study design has been successfully used in other longitudinal vaginal microbiome studies [18, 52] and our research team maintained frequent, weekly contact with participants to troubleshoot any issues with specimen acquisition or storage. Additionally, although our cohort was relatively small, we enrolled a diverse population of women across different ages, races, education levels, and other characteristics. Notably, 2 Asian women developed iBV in this study and we did not have any additional Asian women who qualified to be healthy controls (Table 1). The vaginal microbiota of Asian and White women has been found to be more likely dominated by Lactobacillus spp. and less likely dominated by a diverse group of anaerobic bacteria [53]; thus, our 2 Asian iBV cases were matched to 2 White women who were healthy controls. Our Gardnerella spp. and P. bivia study results were not significantly impacted when these 4 participants were removed from the analysis; however, the median pooled count of F. vaginae was significantly higher in iBV cases only on the day of iBV diagnosis (data not shown). Another limitation is that our bacterial count data did not control for the number of VECs present in each image [54]. BV is known to cause epithelial exfoliation and BV specimens would likely have more epithelial cells, which could drive up bacterial counts. Additionally, there are dye-dependent signal differences inherent to FISH assays; however, the probes used in this study were previously validated under consistent imaging conditions [33–35]. Finally, we only visualized Gardnerella spp., P. bivia, and F. vaginae from vaginal specimens based on the current ability of PNA probes. BV is known to be a polymicrobial infection and the role of other key BVAB (ie, Sneathia spp.) should be further investigated.

CONCLUSIONS

Our results demonstrate the importance of Gardnerella spp. in iBV pathogenesis as an early colonizer of the BV biofilm and F. vaginae as a secondary colonizer. P. bivia was not found to have a significant role in this study. The role of other Prevotella spp. in iBV pathogenesis should be further investigated. These results provide additional evidence of the importance of Gardnerella spp. and F. vaginae in iBV pathogenesis.
