Clinical and diagnostic complexities of adipsic arginine vasopressin deficiency
Authors: Nadia Chaudhury, Abdur-Raoof Sheikh, Ranganatha Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipsic arginine vasopressin deficiency (AAVP-D) is a rare hypothalamic disorder, with inadequate vasopressin production and impaired thirst, resulting in sodium and water dysregulation. It poses multiple challenges, including increased morbidity and mortality. We present a 24-year-old female who developed confusion after neurosurgical debulking of a central neurocytoma. Investigations revealed hypernatremia alongside raised plasma osmolality, low urine osmolality, polyuria, and adipsia. Transient AAVP-D was suspected; it was successfully treated with intravenous desmopressin, which resolved predischarge. One week later, she re-presented to the hospital with confusion. Recurrence of hypernatremia, plasma hyperosmolality, and polyuria occurred and permanent AAVP-D was suspected. She was commenced on oral desmopressin 50 mcg/day, titrated to 150 mcg/day, with strict oral fluid regime of 2 L/day. She remains adipsic 24 months postoperatively. Her case highlights the multiple challenges of AAVP-D. Management includes calculating a daily fluid intake goal and titration with desmopressin to ensure consistent urine output, patient weight, and plasma sodium levels with careful outpatient monitoring.Keywords: adipsia, arginine vasopressin deficiency, vasopressin, thirst

Introduction

Arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVP-D) is typically characterized by polyuria (>2 mL/kg/h, low urine osmolality (<300 mOsm/kg), and serum hyperosmolality (>300 mOsm/kg) [1]. Adipsic arginine vasopressin deficiency (AAVP-D) is a rare hypothalamic disorder, in which the features of AVP-D are present with additional impaired thirst. Fewer than 200 cases are reported in the literature to date [2]. Multiple causes of AAVP-D exist, including anterior communicating artery aneurysm rupture ± clipping, craniopharyngioma, hypothalamic tumors, and congenital abnormalities of the corpus callosum [2, 3].

Whereas patients with AVP-D have increased thirst to counteract plasma hyperosmolality, patients with AAVP-D have lost these compensatory mechanisms. This results in an increased risk of morbidity and mortality [4].

We present a rare case of AAVP-D and highlight the complexities of diagnosis and management.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old female presented to her general practitioner in November 2020 with a 2-month history of recurrent headaches, photophobia, blurred vision, and dizziness. She had no medical history, no significant family history, and did not take any regular medications. Neuroimaging showed a 41-mm tumor within the lateral ventricle with midline shift and moderate hydrocephalus (Fig. 1A-1C). In December 2020, an exploratory craniotomy with debulking of the tumor was conducted (first neurosurgical intervention). Postoperative imaging showed large residual intraventricular tumor; thus, a repeat right frontal craniotomy and near-total tumor resection was conducted 1 week later (second neurosurgical intervention) (Fig. 1D-1E). Histology suggested atypical neurocytoma with increased mitotic activity (4 mitoses per 10 high-power fields) and Ki-67 proliferative index approximately 15%, World Health Organization 2016 grade II. She recovered well postoperatively, with no evidence of abnormal thirst or polyuria. Postoperative routine biochemical investigations returned to normal (Table 1).

Interval scans were conducted 3 months postoperatively and then annually, revealing slow tumor growth (Fig. 1F-1G). Redo frontal supratentorial craniotomy for debulking of the tumor was conducted in February 2024, as advised by the neurosurgical multidisciplinary team (third neurosurgical intervention) (full timeline is summarized in Fig. 2). Histology again confirmed the diagnosis of atypical central neurocytoma, with similar ki67 index as previously (∼10%-15%) and up to 4 mitoses/10 high-power fields seen.

Patient timeline.

She was monitored in the intensive care unit postoperatively. She commenced oral levetiracetam 500 mg twice daily for seizure prophylaxis, 3 days of intravenous flucloxacillin 1 g four times per day for postoperative cover alongside analgesia and laxatives. Prophylactic enoxaparin 40 mg was started day 2 postoperatively.

In the first 24 hours postoperatively, average urine output was 90 mL/hour (reference range 30-100 mL/hour). Her serum sodium level was 149 mEq/L (International System of Units [SI] 149 mEq/L) (reference range 133-146 mEq/L [SI: 133-146 mmol/L]), and Glasgow Coma Scale score (GCS) was 15/15. Approximately 36 hours postoperatively, urine output increased to 120 to 300 mL/hour (Table 1), alongside hypernatremia (163 mEq/L) (SI: 163 mmol/L) and increased plasma osmolality (330 mOsm/kg [SI: 330 mmol/kg]) (reference range 275-295 mOsm/kg [SI: 275-295 mmol/kg]).

She was given 1 mcg desmopressin intravenously, as well as intravenous fluid replacement. Surprisingly, she reported no thirst. GCS reduced to 14/15 due to new-onset confusion. She had ongoing hypernatremia and polyuria days 2 and 3 postoperatively and thus was administered intravenous desmopressin 1 mcg daily. (See Tables 1- 2 and Figs. 2 and 3 for full investigation list and timeline.) With regular endocrine input, she was stepped down to neurosurgical high dependency unit day 4 postoperatively.

She unfortunately re-presented to hospital 1 week postdischarge with 3 days’ history of worsening confusion, agitation, and polyuria. Return of AAVP-D was suspected. Initial observations measured blood pressure 103/67 mm Hg, heart rate 69 beats/minute, respiratory rate 18 breaths/minute, oxygen saturation 96% on room air, and temperature 36.8 °C [SI: 309.95 Kelvin]. Apart from confusion, her family denied any changes in appetite, weight, or sleep. Examination revealed signs of dehydration with dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, and central capillary refill time of 3 seconds. Abbreviated mental test score was 6/10 (10/10 on previous discharge), and GCS 14/15.

Diagnostic assessment

Readmission bloods showed hypernatremia (sodium levels 170 mEq/L [SI: 170 mmol/L]) alongside acute kidney injury (Table 2). She again reported lack of thirst. Copeptin level was <1.2 pmol/L with concomitant plasma sodium 170 mEq/L (SI: 170 mmol/L). Cranial MRI showed satisfactory postoperative appearance with no features suggestive of osmotic brain injury (Fig. 1H-1J). Anterior pituitary hormone profile conducted 6 weeks postoperatively was normal (Table 4).

Treatment

She received subcutaneous desmopressin 1 mcg, which resolved her polyuria (average urine output 87.5 mL/hour). She underwent hourly urine-output monitoring and 6-hourly electrolyte monitoring. Within the first 24 hours of care, her sodium levels improved to 160 mEq/L (SI: 160 mmol/L) alongside encouraged oral fluid intake 1650 mL and urine output 1849 mL. Abbreviated mental test score improved to 7/10. She was transferred to the endocrinology ward where a desmopressin tablet 50 mcg once per night was commenced. Two liters/day fluid intake was strongly encouraged with regular prompts and 2 × 1L water bottles placed on her bedside table. Subtle confusion was often difficult to recognize by health care professionals, with the assumption she was able to take her medication independently. However, on several occasions, she admittedly forgot to take her desmopressin tablets, with the medication being left at her bedside. Once recognized, supervised medication administration was adopted to ensure strict compliance. Her plasma sodium and urinary output subsequently stabilized (151-155 mEq/L [SI: 151-155 mmol/L] and 40-100 mL/hour, respectively). She was discharged 1 week later (sodium 151 mEq/L) (SI: 151 mmol/L) with oral desmopressin 25 mcg in the morning and 50 mcg in the evening.

Outcome and follow-up

She has received regular endocrinology follow-up since discharge, with serum sodium, osmolality, and weight monitoring. Oral desmopressin dose was titrated to 50 mcg in the morning and 100 mcg in the evening. She still lacks thirst 24 months postoperatively but has adopted a strict oral fluid intake regime of 2 L/day, with increased fluid consumption in hotter periods/during exercise. At her most recent appointment, her serum sodium is stable at 143 mEq/L (SI: 143 mmol/L). She reports a 3-kg weight gain despite regular exercise, which is suspected secondary to increased appetite. Nevertheless, she again denies symptoms of hypothalamic dysfunction such as changes in temperature, menstrual cycle, sleep pattern, or mood. Her short-term memory has greatly improved, and she is gradually increasing her work activities.

Discussion

Vasopressin is synthesized by magnocellular neurons of the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei located in the anterior hypothalamus. It is transported via the pituitary stalk to the posterior pituitary, where stored and secreted. Damage to this pathway results in AVP-D. In healthy individuals, vasopressin acts on renal collecting ducts via vasopressin-2 receptors to increase water reabsorption by enhancing insertion of aquaporin 2 channels [5]. More than 90% of the vasopressin-producing neurons need to be destroyed for polyuria to occur [6].

Plasma osmolality is maintained using a careful balance of 2 mechanisms: thirst and AVP release. Increase in plasma osmolality by 1% stimulates vasopressin release, whereas 2% to 3% increase induces thirst sensation [7, 8]. Osmoreceptors in the subfornical organ (SFO) and organum vasculosum lamina terminalis (OVLT) detect changes in plasma osmolality. Stimulatory signals are sent to the median preoptic nucleus, which further relays signals to the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei to synthesize vasopressin, and to the SFO and OVLT for conscious perception of thirst [9, 10]. Thus, injury of the osmoreceptors, median preoptic nucleus, SFO, or OVLT may result in AAVP-D (Fig. 4).

Pathophysiology of plasma osmolality maintenance.

AAVP-D can be difficult to diagnose. Historically, a water deprivation test was the first-line investigation [11]. More recently, plasma copeptin, a stable glycopeptide cleavage product of vasopressin synthesis, is used to aid diagnosis. Novel data suggest plasma copeptin <4.9 pmol/L after hypertonic saline infusion test has high diagnostic accuracy [12]. In our case, as clinical suspicion was highly suggestive of AAVP-D, and co-peptin was measured when the patient had confirmed hypernatremia (170 mmol/L), we decided further confirmation tests were unnecessary. Thirst can be measured using a 10-cm visual analog scale, alongside a water deprivation test or hypertonic saline test, to diagnose adipsia [2]. Absence of thirst in presence of hyperosmolality with no other plausible causes (for example, postoperative complications of pain, sedation, nausea), can confirm clinical diagnosis of AAVP-D.

Management of AAVP-D includes replacement of water deficit and vasopressin, alongside treatment of the underlying condition (protocol designed by Ball et al is highlighted in Table 5) [13]. If fluid balance is complicated by memory impairment, behavioral modification techniques may be used to encourage drinking [14]. Treatment with desmopressin (vasopressin analogue) is usually required. It is available in parenteral, oral, and nasal forms, with parenteral bioavailability 100× the oral formulation [6]. The dose of desmopressin is individually tailored to achieve normal plasma sodium levels alongside daily urine output of 1.5 to 2 L. Common desmopressin doses reported are: oral tablets 0.1 to 1.2 mg, sublingual melts 60 to 720 mcg, intranasal spray 10 to 40 mcg, or intravenous injection 1 to 4 mcg, split into 1 to 3 doses per day [15]. It is recommended to avoid food consumption within an hour of administration of oral desmopressin to maximize absorption [5].

There are several learning points from this case. First, the recurrence of disease makes this case particularly interesting and suggests the possibility of triphasic AVP-D. The initial phase of the triphasic response (∼5-7 days) results in vasopressin deficiency and polyuria because of “stunning” of the vasopressin neurons. The second phase (∼2-14 days) involves unregulated vasopressin release from stores in degenerating neurons leading to syndrome of inappropriate ADH with resultant oliguria and hyponatremia. This may have occurred in our patient after discharge, but it is difficult to ascertain without monitoring urine output and serum sodium. The final phase is the return of polyuria because of permanent vasopressin deficiency following depleted vasopressin stores in the damaged vasopressin neurons [16].

What further differentiates our case from others is that isolated AAVP-D developed with intact anterior pituitary functioning. Anterior pituitary hormone deficiency is common in patients with AAVP-D, with literature estimates ranging from 72% to 100% of individuals with AAVP-D affected [3, 4]. Therefore, our case is an uncommon presentation.

AAVP-D is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, with significantly higher risk of death as compared to nonadipsic patients (odds ratio 9.53 [P = .007]) [8]. Significant hypernatremia is an important complication seen in AAVP-D, seen rarely in those with normal thirst mechanisms [17]. Hypernatremic dehydration is associated with hypotension, renal failure, increased thrombosis risk, and hypovolemic shock [18]. Our case displayed acute kidney injury on readmission to hospital; a complication commonly seen in patients with AAVP-D [19]. This occurs secondary to polyuria and dehydration and is hypothesized to be secondary to renal vasoconstriction and tubular injury from myoglobin and heme proteins [19]. Additional complications seen include hypothalamic abnormalities such as obesity, sleep apnea, venous thrombosis, thermoregulatory dysfunction, and seizures [4, 20]. Hypothalamic dysfunction is common and is estimated to affect 33% to 76% of patients with AAVP-D [3, 4]. It is difficult to confirm whether our case is displaying initial signs of hypothalamic dysfunction; she notes 3-kg weight gain secondary to increased appetite; however, does not display evidence of hyperphagia (defined as insatiable hunger alongside abnormal food-seeking behaviors [21]). Close monitoring is required for early identification of hypothalamic dysfunction.

Learning points

High clinical suspicion of AAVP-D is necessary after neurosurgical intervention and regular electrolyte monitoring and fluid balance is required, even in cases where AAVP-D is suspected to have resolved.
