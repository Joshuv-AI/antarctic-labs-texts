First Mediterranean record and north‐eastern range extension of the African seahorse Hippocampus algiricus
Authors: Jorge Hernández‐Urcera, Sira Pereira, Antonio A. Ocaña, Miquel Planas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe African seahorse Hippocampus algiricus has been considered restricted to the eastern Atlantic coast of Africa, with occasional records in Macaronesian archipelagos. The species is currently classified as Vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN). Here, we report the first confirmed occurrence of this species in the Mediterranean Sea, based on photographic and video evidence. The specimen was identified using diagnostic morphological characters and morphometric analysis. This record represents a north‐eastern range extension and aligns with ongoing tropicalization processes in the western Mediterranean driven by rising sea temperatures.

The geographic distributions of marine species are changing rapidly in response to climate warming, with poleward range shifts increasingly reported across multiple taxa (Parmesan & Yohe, 2003; Poloczanska et al., 2013). In the Mediterranean Sea, one of the fastest warming marine regions worldwide, the changes are contributing to a progressive tropicalization of coastal systems (Albano et al., 2024; Azzurro et al., 2019; Marbà et al., 2015).

The Alboran Sea represents a key transition zone between the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean basin, characterized by the inflow of Atlantic surface waters and complex circulation patterns that enhance connectivity between regions (Sánchez‐Garrido & Nadal, 2022). These conditions, together with increasing sea surface temperatures (Pisano et al., 2020; Vargas‐Yáñez et al., 2010), may facilitate the arrival of warm‐affinity species from the Atlantic.

Seahorses (genus Hippocampus) are coastal fishes with limited mobility and strong habitat associations (Lourie et al., 2016). In the western Mediterranean, two native species are recognized: Hippocampus guttulatus and H. hippocampus (Curtis & Vincent, 2006; Planas et al., 2024). The African seahorse Hippocampus algiricus Kaup, 1856 is primarily distributed along the eastern Atlantic coast of Africa, from Western Sahara to Angola (Pollom, 2017). The species is currently classified as Vulnerable by the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN), reflecting concerns about its population status and limited ecological knowledge (Pollom, 2017).

Records outside continental Africa include the Canary Islands (Otero‐Ferrer et al., 2015) and Cabo Verde (Wirtz et al., 2013), where the species has been shown to occur in sympatry with H. hippocampus. These observations suggest that H. algiricus may be capable of dispersal beyond its core range, potentially following pathways mediated by oceanographic processes.

Detecting such early‐stage biogeographic events remains challenging, particularly for cryptic and low‐density species such as seahorses. In this context, citizen‐science initiatives have emerged as valuable tools for expanding observational coverage and enabling the detection of unexpected occurrences (García‐Soto et al., 2021; Kelly et al., 2020). Recent work has demonstrated that community science can substantially improve knowledge of seahorse distribution, habitat use and life‐history traits, including the detection of previously undocumented range extensions (Camins et al., 2024; Ruiz‐Jarabo et al., 2024).

Here, we report the first confirmed occurrence of Hippocampus algiricus in the Mediterranean Sea and discuss its biogeographic significance. The occurrence of a warm‐water Atlantic species such as H. algiricus is of particular interest in the context of ongoing climatic change.

A single individual of H. algiricus was recorded on 18 June 2025 in the Alboran Sea (Almuñécar, southern Spain) at a depth of 9 m (Figure 1). The record was obtained through a citizen science observation, including photographs and a short video (video S1), providing additional confirmation of the identification. All visual materials were captured using a GoPro 11 equipped with an Inon UCL‐G100 SD macro lens and two Bigblue CB 7200P lights. The photographs were uploaded to the citizen‐science platform Observadores del Mar, within the Project Familia Signátidos (www.observadoresdelmar.es/Projects/13). The location of the observation is reported at an approximate spatial resolution (36.7° N, 3.6° W) to avoid potential disturbance or collection of this Vulnerable species.

Known coastal distribution of Hippocampus algiricus (blue line) according to Pollom (2017), with the red dot indicating the location of the new record.

The individual was attached to a mooring line colonized by filamentous algae over sandy substrate. The site is known to host H. guttulatus and occasionally H. hippocampus. No previous records of H. algiricus were known from the area (A. Ocaña; pers. obs.).

The specimen was identified as a female (absence of brood pouch) and estimated to be approximately 10 cm in total length. The identification was based on morphological characters, including a slender elongated snout and a posteriorly oriented tuberculate crown (Figure 2a). Rounded dark spots along the flanks were also observed (Figure 2a,c), consistent with descriptions of the species (Lourie et al., 2004). In contrast, H. hippocampus (Figure 2b) displays a shorter, more robust snout and a simple triangular crown, characteristic of this species. The pigmentation pattern observed in the studied specimen is also consistent with H. algiricus (Figure 2c). To further support this identification, head length (HL) and snout length (SnL) were measured following the standardized protocol of Lourie et al. (2004). The HL/SnL ratio was 2.29, falling within the diagnostic range reported for H. algiricus (2.1–2.6) and well below that typically reported for H. hippocampus (2.8–3.4) (Lourie et al., 2004). The combination of morphological traits, morphometric data and video (Video S1) evidence supports the identification of the specimen as H. algiricus. No additional individuals were observed.

Intraspecific variation in morphological and morphometric traits is well documented in seahorses and may arise from phylogeographic structure, environmental conditions or differences in sample type (fresh specimens, dried material or images). Such variability has been reported for both Atlantic and Mediterranean populations of Hippocampus spp. (Lourie et al., 2016; Otero‐Ferrer et al., 2015). Considering this expected range of variation, the morphometric value obtained for the Alboran specimen remains fully consistent with H. algiricus and clearly distinct from values typically observed in H. hippocampus. This provides additional support for the identification of the individual as H. algiricus within a broader biogeographic context.

Even though the species has also been reported from the Cape Verde archipelago, confirmed records outside continental Africa were, until now, restricted to the Canary Islands, where morphometric and genetic analyses verified its presence (Otero‐Ferrer et al., 2015). Importantly, the Canary records provided evidence of successful reproduction, suggesting that H. algiricus may be capable of establishing temporary or persistent populations outside its core African range. Although no evidence of hybridization exists for the Iberian record, the possibility of future interactions with local seahorse populations cannot be dismissed. Hybridization events, even if rare, may have consequences for genetic integrity, reproductive dynamics and conservation planning. Moreover, the presence of a third seahorse species in the region could influence local habitat use, competition or predator–prey interactions, although such effects remain speculative at this stage.

The occurrence of H. algiricus in the Mediterranean Sea and, particularly in the Alboran Sea, represents a notable north‐eastern extension of the known range of the species and is consistent with ongoing climate‐driven changes in the mediterranean fish assemblages (Azzurro et al., 2019; Pisano et al., 2020). The region is strongly influenced by Atlantic inflow and characterized by high connectivity, which may facilitate the transport of organisms across the Strait of Gibraltar (Sánchez‐Garrido & Nadal, 2022).

The mechanisms underlying the presence of H. algiricus in the Mediterranean cannot be determined, but passive transport via surface currents is the most likely explanation. Individuals may be dispersed as pelagic juveniles or associated with floating substrates (rafting). Episodic environmental conditions, including marine heatwaves, may further enhance dispersal success.

The processes proposed here are consistent with those previously suggested for the occurrence of H. algiricus in the Canary Islands (Otero‐Ferrer et al., 2015), supporting the interpretation that the present record may be part of a broader expansion from the eastern Atlantic. In this context, oceanographically connected regions may facilitate progressive dispersal across the Atlantic‐Mediterranean boundary. Similar northward shifts have been documented for multiple Atlantic fishes entering the Mediterranean through the Strait of Gibraltar (Azzurro et al., 2019), reinforcing the idea that the region acts as a biogeographic corridor for warm‐affinity species.

Whether this observation represents an isolated vagrant or the early stage of range expansion remains unclear. The Alboran Sea provides suitable habitats for seahorses, although temperature constraints may still limit long‐term establishment. Continued monitoring will be required to determine whether additional individuals occur in the region. Although based on a single observation, similar first records have historically provided early evidence of range shifts later confirmed by additional observations (Otero‐Ferrer et al., 2015). From a conservation perspective, this record is relevant given that H. algiricus is currently classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN. New occurrence data may contribute to improving knowledge on its distribution and population status. Future monitoring efforts may benefit from incorporating non‐lethal meristic, morphometric and genetic sampling to assess phylogeographic structure and evaluate the potential for interspecific hybridization, as previously documented in Atlantic populations (Otero‐Ferrer et al., 2015). Such approaches would provide deeper insights into the evolutionary and conservation implications of the presence of H. algiricus in the Mediterranean.

This study also highlights the value of citizen‐science platforms for detecting rare or unexpected species. Recreational divers frequently encounter cryptic or low‐density taxa that may be overlooked by traditional monitoring programmes (García‐Soto et al., 2021; Kelly et al., 2020). Recent global syntheses have shown that community‐science data can significantly expand known geographic ranges and improve life‐history knowledge for seahorse species (Camins et al., 2024). Although such observations are subject to bias, they provide an important complement to traditional monitoring approaches. Observadores del Mar, in particular, has proven effective in documenting early signals of biogeographic change in Spanish waters. This record also aligns with recent findings from the national citizen‐science initiative aimed at mapping seahorse species along the Spanish coastline (Planas et al., 2024), which until now had only documented H. algiricus in Atlantic waters. Community‐science observations have proven essential for detecting early biogeographic signals, evolutionary processes and climate‐driven range shifts in cryptic or low‐density species, including first records of seahorses in new regions. Comparable patterns have been documented elsewhere. For example, citizen‐science initiatives in Taiwan revealed the first national records of the pygmy seahorses Hippocampus japapigu and H. denise, demonstrating how observations contributed by recreational divers can uncover previously unknown species occurrences and biogeographic extensions (Heard et al., 2019). These findings highlight the capacity of community‐based monitoring to detect early signals of distributional change in small, cryptic and low‐density taxa, a context directly relevant to the present record of H. algiricus in the Mediterranean. Continued collaboration between scientists, citizen observers and monitoring networks will be essential to track the future occurrence of H. algiricus and to better understand the ecological implications of tropicalization along the Iberian Mediterranean coast.
