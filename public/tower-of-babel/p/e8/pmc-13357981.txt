Primary Malignant Pericardial Epithelioid Mesothelioma Presenting as Cardiac Tamponade: A Case Report With Multimodality Imaging and Radiologic–Pathologic Correlation
Authors: Saubhagya Dhakal, Paulo Savoia, Nikahat Yasmine, Subhashis Mitra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma is an exceptionally rare and aggressive malignancy that frequently presents with nonspecific cardiopulmonary symptoms, recurrent pericardial effusion, constrictive physiology, or cardiac tamponade. Early diagnosis is challenging because an infiltrative pericardial tumor may remain occult on initial imaging studies performed for acute cardiopulmonary evaluation. A 67‐year‐old woman presented with chest discomfort and shortness of breath. CT pulmonary angiography and thoracic aortic computed tomography angiography (CTA) were performed to evaluate for pulmonary embolism and acute aortic pathology. The examinations were negative for pulmonary embolism and acute aortic abnormality; however, they revealed a large mildly hyperattenuating circumferential pericardial effusion measuring approximately 35 HU, concerning for complex mixed hemorrhagic and serous fluid with tamponade physiology. No discrete enhancing pericardial mass was confidently identified on the initial CTA examination. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a large pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. Pericardiocentesis removed approximately 600 cc of fluid, with cytologic analysis nondiagnostic for malignant cells. The patient returned approximately 2 weeks later with progressive dyspnea. Cardiac MRI demonstrated an extensive heterogeneously enhancing infiltrative anterior pericardial mass extending toward the great vessels and left atrial region. FDG PET/CT showed an intensely FDG‐avid infiltrative pericardial mass without distant metastatic disease or FDG‐avid lymphadenopathy. Thoracoscopy and pericardial biopsy confirmed epithelioid mesothelioma. Immunohistochemistry revealed positivity for CK7, calretinin, CK5/6, and WT1 and negativity for claudin‐4 and TTF‐1. The patient was treated with carboplatin and pemetrexed, with planned radiation therapy. This case highlights that primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma may initially present as complex hemorrhagic pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology, with infiltrative tumor occult on nondedicated acute CTA examinations. Persistent or recurrent symptoms following drainage should prompt further evaluation with cardiac MRI and PET/CT for tissue characterization, staging, and biopsy planning.

1. Introduction

Primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma is a rare malignancy arising from mesothelial cells of the pericardium. Pericardial mesothelioma represents a very small subset of mesothelioma cases, and metastatic disease involving the pericardium is substantially more common than primary pericardial malignancy [1, 2]. Among primary malignant pericardial tumors, mesothelioma remains one of the most frequently described entities despite its exceptional rarity [2].

Clinical presentation is often nonspecific and may include dyspnea, chest pain, recurrent pericardial effusion, constrictive physiology, arrhythmia, or cardiac tamponade, frequently resulting in delayed diagnosis [1–4]. Echocardiography is typically the initial imaging modality for evaluating pericardial effusion and hemodynamic compromise; however, infiltrative pericardial soft tissue may not be adequately characterized by echocardiography alone [2, 5].

Cross‐sectional imaging therefore plays a central role in detection, characterization, and staging. CT may demonstrate pericardial thickening, nodularity, calcification, mass effect, and complex pericardial effusion. MRI provides superior soft‐tissue characterization and assessment of tumor enhancement, myocardial invasion, and relationship to adjacent mediastinal cardiovascular structures. PET/CT adds metabolic characterization and staging information, including evaluation for nodal or distant metastatic disease [1, 2, 5–7].

We report a case of primary malignant pericardial epithelioid mesothelioma initially presenting as complex hemorrhagic pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. The infiltrative pericardial malignancy was occult on initial CTA examinations performed for acute pulmonary embolism and aortic evaluation and was later characterized with cardiac MRI and PET/CT, with final diagnosis established through histopathology and immunohistochemistry.

2. Case Presentation

A 67‐year‐old woman presented to the emergency department with chest discomfort and progressive shortness of breath. On presentation, she was mildly tachycardic with a heart rate of approximately 115 beats per minute and had borderline hypotension with a blood pressure of approximately 100/60 mmHg. The patient had no known history of prior malignancy. Available clinical history did not reveal known asbestos exposure, asbestosis, or other significant occupational or environmental exposure risk factors.

Given the acute cardiopulmonary presentation, CT pulmonary angiography and thoracic aortic CTA were performed to evaluate for pulmonary embolism and acute aortic pathology. The examinations were negative for pulmonary embolism and acute aortic abnormality. However, CTA demonstrated a large circumferential mildly hyperattenuating pericardial effusion measuring approximately 35 HU, compatible with complex mixed hemorrhagic and serous fluid. Imaging findings were concerning for tamponade physiology. No discrete enhancing pericardial mass was confidently identified on the initial CTA examination. This likely reflected the nondedicated nature of the acute CTA protocol combined with obscuration of infiltrative tumor by the large complex effusion (Figure 1A,B).

Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a large circumferential pericardial effusion with findings supportive of tamponade physiology (Figure 1C). The patient underwent pericardiocentesis with removal of approximately 600 cc of fluid, and a pericardial drain was left in place. Cytologic analysis of the drained pericardial fluid was nondiagnostic for malignant cells. The patient was subsequently discharged.

The patient returned approximately 2 weeks later with progressive dyspnea. Given persistent concern for underlying pericardial pathology following incomplete clinical resolution, cardiac MRI was performed. MRI showed an extensive heterogeneously enhancing infiltrative anterior pericardial mass extending from the mid‐heart toward the great vessels and left atrial region (Figure 2A–C).

Cardiac MRI findings. (A) Axial postcontrast fat‐suppressed T1‐weighted image demonstrates heterogeneous enhancement of the infiltrative anterior pericardial mass (white arrow). (B) Oblique sagittal cine true fast imaging with steady‐state precession (TrueFISP)/bright‐blood image demonstrates infiltrative pericardial soft tissue extending along the anterior pericardium (white arrow). (C) Oblique coronal cine TrueFISP/bright‐blood image demonstrates extensive infiltrative pericardial mass extending toward adjacent mediastinal cardiovascular structures (white arrow).

Subsequent FDG PET/CT demonstrated intensely FDG‐avid infiltrative pericardial soft tissue with associated left pleural effusion and without FDG‐avid lymphadenopathy or distant metastatic disease (Figure 3A,B).

FDG PET/CT findings. (A) Whole‐body maximum intensity projection (MIP) FDG PET image demonstrates intensely radiotracer‐avid infiltrative pericardial mass (orange arrow) without evidence of distant FDG‐avid metastatic disease. (B) Axial fused FDG PET/CT image demonstrates intense FDG uptake within the infiltrative pericardial mass (white arrow). No FDG‐avid lymphadenopathy is identified.

The patient underwent thoracoscopy and pericardial mass biopsy. Histopathologic examination revealed epithelioid mesothelioma. Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated polygonal atypical cells within fibrocollagenous stroma with prominent nucleoli and eosinophilic cytoplasm (Figure 4A,B). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated positivity for CK7, calretinin, CK5/6, and WT1 and negativity for claudin‐4 and TTF‐1, supporting epithelioid mesothelioma (Figure 5A–F).

MRI brain demonstrated no intracranial metastatic disease. The patient was treated with carboplatin and pemetrexed, with planned radiation therapy through a multidisciplinary oncology approach.

3. Discussion

Primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma remains diagnostically challenging because imaging findings are often nonspecific and may overlap with other malignant and inflammatory pericardial processes [1, 2, 5, 6, 8]. Acute CTA examinations successfully excluded pulmonary embolism and acute aortic pathology; however, the infiltrative pericardial tumor was not confidently identified on the initial examination. Acute CTA protocols optimized for pulmonary vascular or aortic assessment may have limited soft‐tissue characterization for infiltrative or plaque‐like pericardial malignancies compared with dedicated cardiac MRI protocols [2, 5, 6]. Similar challenges have been reported in the evaluation of other uncommon thoracic cardiovascular pathologies requiring multimodality imaging assessment [9].

The large hyperattenuating pericardial effusion likely further obscured infiltrative soft tissue. Hyperattenuating pericardial fluid may suggest hemorrhagic or proteinaceous content rather than simple transudative effusion [2, 5]. In this patient, the combination of complex effusion, tamponade physiology, recurrent symptoms, and incomplete clinical resolution following drainage appropriately raised concern for underlying malignancy.

Cardiac MRI served as the key problem‐solving imaging modality. Compared with CT, MRI provides superior soft‐tissue contrast and allows better characterization of infiltrative pericardial tumor, enhancement pattern, myocardial involvement, and relationship to adjacent mediastinal cardiovascular structures [2, 5, 6]. In this case, MRI demonstrated extensive heterogeneously enhancing infiltrative pericardial soft tissue extending toward the great vessels and left atrial region, favoring aggressive pericardial malignancy rather than inflammatory pericarditis or organized hematoma.

PET/CT provided important staging information. FDG PET/CT may identify metabolically active tumor, evaluate nodal disease, and help exclude distant metastatic disease or an extracardiac primary malignancy [1, 2, 4]. This distinction is clinically important because metastatic disease involving the pericardium is substantially more common than primary malignant pericardial neoplasm [2]. In the present case, PET/CT demonstrated intense FDG uptake confined to the infiltrative pericardial mass without FDG‐avid metastatic disease or lymphadenopathy, supporting a primary pericardial process.

Recent advances in cardiovascular imaging have substantially improved the detection and characterization of infiltrative pericardial malignancies. Contemporary cardiac MRI techniques provide superior tissue characterization, assessment of myocardial invasion, and evaluation of enhancement patterns, whereas hybrid FDG PET/CT combines metabolic and anatomic information for staging and treatment planning. These multimodality‐imaging advances facilitate earlier recognition of rare pericardial neoplasms and improve diagnostic confidence when initial imaging findings are inconclusive [2, 5–7].

The imaging differential diagnosis for infiltrative FDG‐avid pericardial soft tissue includes metastatic pericardial disease, lymphoma, angiosarcoma, inflammatory pericarditis, and primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma. Metastatic pericardial involvement remains the most common malignant process involving the pericardium and may arise from lung carcinoma, breast carcinoma, melanoma, lymphoma, leukemia, or other systemic malignancies [2, 11]. However, no extracardiac FDG‐avid malignancy was identified in this case.

Cardiac angiosarcoma may also present with hemorrhagic effusion and aggressive cardiovascular involvement, although angiosarcoma most commonly arises from the right atrium and frequently demonstrates necrotic or vascular enhancement patterns [6, 8]. In this case, the lesion was centered in the anterior pericardium rather than the right atrium and demonstrated infiltrative pericardial growth characteristics favoring mesothelioma.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry remain the gold standard for definitive diagnosis because epithelioid mesothelioma may mimic metastatic adenocarcinoma morphologically. Mesothelial differentiation is supported by positivity for calretinin, WT1, and CK5/6, whereas negative claudin‐4 and TTF‐1 staining help exclude metastatic adenocarcinoma [1, 10]. The immunophenotype in this case supports epithelioid mesothelioma.

Treatment of primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma remains difficult because diagnosis often occurs late in the disease course and tumors frequently involve critical cardiovascular structures. Management is individualized and may include surgery, platinum‐based chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and selected immunotherapy approaches [1, 4]. In this patient, treatment was initiated with carboplatin and pemetrexed, with planned radiation therapy.

This case emphasizes several important radiology teaching points. First, primary malignant pericardial mesothelioma may initially present as complex hemorrhagic pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. Second, infiltrative tumor may remain occult on acute nondedicated CTA examinations. Third, persistent symptoms or recurrent effusion following drainage should prompt further evaluation with cardiac MRI. Fourth, PET/CT plays an important role in staging and exclusion of metastatic disease. Finally, radiologic–pathologic correlation is essential for accurate diagnosis of infiltrative pericardial malignancy.

4. Conclusion

Primary malignant pericardial epithelioid mesothelioma is a rare but important diagnostic consideration in patients presenting with complex hemorrhagic pericardial effusion and tamponade physiology. Acute CTA examinations performed for pulmonary embolism or aortic evaluation may fail to confidently identify infiltrative pericardial malignancy, particularly when obscured by a large complex effusion. Persistent or recurrent symptoms following drainage should prompt dedicated cardiac MRI for tissue characterization, whereas FDG PET/CT assists with staging and exclusion of metastatic disease. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathologic examination with immunohistochemical confirmation. Advances in multimodality imaging and diagnostic techniques continue to improve disease detection, risk stratification, prognostic assessment, and treatment planning. This case highlights the importance of integrating multimodality imaging with radiologic–pathologic correlation in the evaluation of rare infiltrative pericardial malignancies.
