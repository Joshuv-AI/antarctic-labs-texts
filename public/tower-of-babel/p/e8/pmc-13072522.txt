Epicardial fat thickness is associated with reduced vertebral artery flow in healthy adults
Authors: Hakan İskender, M Bahadır Omar, İsmail Balaban, Abdulrahman Naser, Tolga Sinan Güvenç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpicardial fat tissue is increasingly recognized as a metabolically active adipose compartment linked to vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. While links between EFT and coronary or carotid disease have been widely reported, data on its relationship with cerebrovascular hemodynamics remain limited.ObjectiveThis study examined the association between epicardial fat thickness and vertebral artery blood flow in healthy individuals, with particular attention to potential side-specific differences.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 98 healthy adults. Epicardial fat thickness was measured using transthoracic echocardiography. Right and left vertebral artery flow volumes were assessed by color Doppler ultrasonography. Associations between EFT and vertebral artery flow were analyzed using Pearson correlation. Multivariable linear regression identified independent determinants of left vertebral artery flow volume (LVAF). ROC analysis was used to assess whether EFT could identify low LVAF.ResultsEpicardial fat thickness was not significantly associated with right vertebral artery flow volume (r = − 0.155, p = 0.127). In contrast, a significant inverse relationship was observed between EFT and LVAF (r = − 0.359, p < 0.001). In adjusted analyses accounting for age, sex, body size, smoking status, and vertebral artery diameter, EFT remained the only independent negative predictor of LVAF (β = −8.14, p = 0.001). Participants with higher EFT values (≥ 5.0 mm) exhibited significantly lower LVAF compared with those with lower EFT values. ROC analysis demonstrated modest discriminative performance for low LVAF (AUC = 0.65).ConclusionEpicardial fat thickness was independently associated with reduced left vertebral artery flow. These findings suggest a potential link with altered cerebrovascular hemodynamics, although larger prospective studies are needed to confirm this association.Keywords: Epicardial fat thickness, Vertebral artery flow, Carotid Doppler ultrasonography, Cardiovascular risk, Cerebrovascular circulation, Subclinical atherosclerosis

Introduction

Epicardial fat tissue is a visceral adipose tissue located on the outer surface of the heart, surrounding the coronary arteries. Rather than serving solely as an energy store, EFT actively participates in metabolic and inflammatory signaling. It secretes a variety of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), which can influence both systemic and local vascular beds. In this study, the abbreviation EFT is used exclusively to refer to epicardial fat thickness.

Previous studies have demonstrated a close association between EFT and several cardiovascular risk markers, such as coronary artery disease, carotid intima–media thickness, and arterial stiffness. However, the potential impact of EFT on the cerebrovascular circulation—particularly through the vertebral arteries—has not been adequately explored. The vertebral arteries supply the brainstem and posterior cerebral regions, and alterations in their flow may contribute to cerebral hypoperfusion and ischemic events. Vertebral artery hypoplasia and dominance are common anatomical variants that may significantly influence posterior circulation hemodynamics. Therefore, these factors should be considered when interpreting vertebral artery flow measurements (2,3).

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between epicardial fat thickness and vertebral artery blood flow. We therefore focused on potential asymmetry in the association between EFT and vertebral artery flow. In addition, we also aimed to explore whether a clinically meaningful EFT threshold could be identified for predicting reduced vertebral artery flow using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study included 98 healthy individuals aged ≥ 18 years who were referred for routine check-up examinations to the cardiology and radiology departments of a university hospital between 2022 and 2023.

Individuals with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, known cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, structural heart disease, significant valvular disease, carotid or vertebral artery pathology, or vertebral artery hypoplasia (defined as diameter < 2.0 mm) were excluded.

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the local ethics committee (Approval No: 0134/X/29) and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized data. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Epicardial fat thickness measurement

Epicardial fat thickness was assessed using standard transthoracic echocardiography (Philips G5 system and 2–4 MHz phased-array transducer). Measurements were obtained in the parasternal long-axis view at end-diastole, at the level where the epicardial fat layer over the right ventricular free wall was most clearly visualized. Although systolic measurement is commonly reported, diastolic assessment has also been shown to provide reproducible results and is widely accepted in clinical research. EFT was defined as the distance between the outer myocardial surface and the visceral pericardium. All measurements were performed by the same experienced cardiologist, and the mean of three consecutive measurements was used for analysis.

Vertebral artery flow assessment

Vertebral artery Doppler assessment was conducted using a 5–12 MHz linear vascular probe. Examinations were performed with participants in the supine position and the head gently rotated to the contralateral side. Right and left vertebral arteries were assessed separately. Peak systolic velocity (Vmax), end-diastolic velocity (Vmin), and mean flow volume (ml/min) were recorded for each artery. All Doppler measurements were conducted by an experienced radiologist who was blinded to the EFT measurements.

Doppler angle correction was maintained below 60 degrees in all measurements. Vertebral artery diameter was measured in B-mode imaging at an arterial segment free of plaque during end-diastole. Flow volume was calculated as time-averaged mean velocity multiplied by cross-sectional area.

Data collection

Demographic variables (age, sex, height, weight, smoking status) as well as EFT, right vertebral artery diameter, and left vertebral artery diameter were recorded. Body mass index was calculated using height and weight. Smoking was defined as active smoking for at least five years. Detailed information regarding daily cigarette consumption was not consistently available; therefore, cumulative smoking exposure could not be quantified.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python software (Statsmodels and Scikit-learn libraries). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since epicardial fat thickness followed an approximately normal distribution, Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the associations. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage).

The relationships between epicardial fat thickness (EFT) and vertebral artery flow volumes were evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Correlation strength was interpreted using conventional thresholds.

Variables showing a significant association with epicardial fat thickness in univariable analysis were selected for further evaluation. These variables were then entered into a multivariable linear regression model using left vertebral artery flow volume as the dependent variable. The model included epicardial fat thickness, age, sex, height, body weight, smoking status, and right and left vertebral artery diameters as covariates. Regression results are presented as unstandardized beta coefficients (β) with corresponding p values. Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²), and overall model significance was evaluated using the F-test.

Participants were categorized into two groups according to epicardial fat thickness (< 5.0 mm and ≥ 5.0 mm). Differences in LVAF between groups were compared using the independent samples t-test.

The discriminative ability of epicardial fat thickness for predicting low LVAF was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut-off value, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. Low LVAF was defined according to the median LVAF value of the study population.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 98 healthy individuals were included in the study. The mean age of the participants was 39.5 ± 7.0 years, and 74.5% were male. The mean height and body weight were 173.6 ± 9.4 cm and 82.1 ± 15.8 kg, respectively. Smoking was present in 41.8% of the study population. The mean epicardial fat thickness (EFT) was 4.29 ± 2.15 mm. Mean right and left vertebral artery flow volumes were 135.1 ± 49.8 ml/min and 139.5 ± 49.2 ml/min, respectively. Association between epicardial fat thickness and vertebral artery flow volumes

Correlation analysis revealed no statistically significant association between EFT and right vertebral artery flow volume (RVAF) (r = − 0.155, p = 0.127). In contrast, EFT demonstrated a moderate and statistically significant negative correlation with left vertebral artery flow volume (LVAF) (r = − 0.359, p < 0.001). Accordingly, higher EFT values were accompanied by lower LVAF, while no such pattern was observed for RVAF. Multivariable linear regression analysis

A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent determinants of LVAF. After adjustment for age, sex, height, weight, smoking status, and right and left vertebral artery diameters, EFT emerged as the only independent and statistically significant negative predictor of LVAF among the variables included in the model (β = −8.14, p = 0.001). None of the other covariates showed a significant association with LVAF. The regression model explained 14.7% of the variance in LVAF and demonstrated borderline overall significance (R² = 0.147, model p = 0.067), which is acceptable given the exploratory nature of the analysis. Participants were stratified into two groups based on epicardial fat thickness (< 5.0 mm and ≥ 5.0 mm). The mean LVAF was significantly higher in individuals with lower EFT values compared with those with higher EFT values (151.5 ml/min vs. 117.0 ml/min, p = 0.0007). Receiver operating characteristic analysis

The predictive performance of EFT for identifying low LVAF was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.65. An EFT cut-off value of 5.0 mm yielded a sensitivity of 47% and a specificity of 78% for the prediction of low LVAF. These findings indicate a modest discriminative ability of EFT for low LVAF. Discussion

Our findings show that increased epicardial fat thickness is independently associated with reduced left vertebral artery flow. This finding suggests that EFT may influence not only cardiac structures but also the cerebrovascular circulation [1].

Correlation analysis revealed a moderate negative association between EFT and LVAF (r = − 0.359, p < 0.001), whereas the relationship between EFT and right vertebral artery flow (RVAF) was weak and not statistically significant (r = − 0.155). This asymmetric pattern is consistent with previously described vertebral artery dominance, whereby the left vertebral artery is dominant in approximately 50–70% of individuals and carries a greater proportion of posterior cerebral blood flow [2, 3].

In multivariable regression analysis adjusting for age, body size, smoking status, sex, and vertebral artery diameters, EFT remained the only independent negative predictor of LVAF (β = −8.14, p = 0.001). This finding supports the concept that EFT may act as an independent determinant of vertebral circulation, beyond conventional anatomical or demographic factors [4].

ROC analysis identified an EFT cut-off value of > 5.0 mm for predicting reduced LVAF, with an AUC of 0.65, specificity of 78%, and sensitivity of 47%. Although the discriminative performance was modest, this threshold should be viewed as exploratory and not as a standalone clinical screening tool [5].

Paracrine and endocrine signaling appears to be the dominant pathway through which EFT affects vascular function. Epicardial fat secretes inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species that promote endothelial dysfunction, vasoconstriction, and arterial stiffening [6, 7]. The direct origin of the left vertebral artery from the aortic arch may expose it more directly to these inflammatory mediators, potentially explaining the stronger association between EFT and LVAF [8, 9].

In contrast, the right vertebral artery typically arises from the subclavian artery and may therefore be less influenced by EFT-related paracrine effects [10]. Notably, even after including right and left vertebral artery diameters in the regression model, vessel diameter did not significantly affect LVAF, whereas EFT remained significant. This observation suggests that inflammatory and functional mechanisms may be more important than purely anatomical factors [11].

Previous studies have shown that EFT is associated with carotid artery thickness, coronary artery disease, and impaired cerebral perfusion [12, 13]. EFT-related vascular dysfunction extends beyond luminal narrowing and includes increased arterial stiffness, reduced compliance, and intima–media thickening [14]. These mechanisms may disproportionately affect vessels closer to the aorta, such as the left vertebral artery [15].

The impact of perivascular adipose tissue on arterial tone has been demonstrated across multiple vascular territories, including cerebral circulation [16]. Similar studies have suggested that EFT may impair cerebral blood flow reserve, autoregulation, and microcirculation [17, 18]. Our findings support these mechanisms specifically within the vertebral arterial system.

In elderly and obese populations, increased EFT has been associated with impaired vertebral and carotid artery flow [19, 20]. Although our study included a relatively young and healthy cohort, the observed association between EFT and LVAF suggests that EFT may serve as an early marker of cerebrovascular risk.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the sample size was relatively small and consisted only of healthy individuals, limiting generalizability to populations with established cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease. Third, the predictive performance of epicardial fat thickness for low vertebral artery flow was modest (AUC = 0.65), and the identified cut-off demonstrated limited sensitivity. Fourth, inter-observer variability was not assessed, as all measurements were performed by a single experienced operator. Finally, the study was conducted at a single center using a single imaging system, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Information on cumulative smoking exposure, such as pack-years or Brinkman index, was not available, which may have limited adjustment for smoking-related vascular effects. Given the modest sample size and exploratory nature of the regression model, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that increased epicardial fat thickness is associated with reduced left vertebral artery flow, independent of anatomical vessel characteristics. These findings suggest that epicardial fat may influence cerebrovascular circulation through systemic metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms rather than purely structural effects. Epicardial fat thickness may offer preliminary insight into cerebrovascular hemodynamics, but larger prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
