Measuring “Cardiovascular Health” in Everyone Including Cancer Patients
Authors: Tochi M Okwuosa, Donald Lloyd-Jones
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: breast cancer, cardio-oncology, cardiovascular health

The National Cancer Institute estimates that nearly 40% of Americans will be diagnosed with cancer during their lifetime. Because of significant advances in cancer therapeutics, individuals with cancer are living longer, with the number of cancer survivors in the United States expected to rise from 18 million in 2022 to 26 million by 2040. Although this progress is promising, it is important to highlight that cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer have a coprevalence rate of ∼16.2% in the United States,1 and individuals with cancer face a 42% higher risk of developing CVD compared with those without cancer.2 The co-occurrence is attributed to both risk factors because cancer and CVD share a “common soil” of behavioral, physiologic, and environmental risk factors and/or CVD risks associated with cancer therapies.

In this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, Wadden et al3 examine associations between the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) score,4 a measure of cardiovascular health (CVH), and incident CVD among 7,165 women (median age = 70.1 years) with newly diagnosed breast cancer enrolled in the WHI (Women’s Health Initiative) study. This analysis built on prior studies in the general population that showed a higher risk of CVD events and CVD death among those with lower LE8 scores,5,6 prompting the authors to examine whether the same association is present in cancer, specifically breast cancer, patients.

The primary exposure was the LE8 score, which ranges from 0 to 100 with a higher score indicating better CVH, and is based on assessments of diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, average sleep duration, body mass index, blood pressure, blood lipids, and glycemia. The primary outcome was adjudicated incident CVD (including coronary heart disease, CVD death, and stroke). As expected, there were disparities in baseline characteristics indicating that higher income, higher education attainment, and White race were associated with higher overall CVH. As in the general population, most of the study participants had moderate CVH, with the smallest proportion in the high CVH category (LE8 score of 80-100).

During a follow-up period of 6 years after breast cancer diagnosis, there were 490 composite CVD events; rates were highest for those in the low CVH category (16.9%) compared with the intermediate (9.1%; 43% lower adjusted hazards) and high (1.5%; 66% lower adjusted hazards) CVH categories, respectively. A model including LE8 score and age was predictive of incident CVD (especially congenital heart disease [CHD]/myocardial infarction) after breast cancer diagnosis with a C-statistic of 0.74. The findings were robust to further adjustments for cancer stage or treatment and were similar across multiple subgroups. Overall, nicotine exposure, blood pressure, and glycemia were the CVH metrics having the strongest associations with outcomes. Some of the study limitations, including the nondiverse cohort made up of a majority of White participants and the lack of data on specific breast cancer treatments, such as anthracyclines, anti–human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–targeted therapy, and chest radiation therapy, which are known to influence cardiovascular outcomes, are worth noting. However, the significant study results despite the short duration of follow-up further the impact of this study. Furthermore, the authors are to be commended on the additional analysis that compared CVD outcomes in those who developed breast cancer vs matched controls without cancer, which demonstrated similar associations of LE8 scores with CVD incidence.

The American Heart Association has placed a growing emphasis on primordial and primary prevention to enhance CVH and lessen the impact of CVD and its associated risk factors. To this end, the CVH concept was formally defined in 2010 using a set of 7 metrics that could be used to measure and monitor current CVH status as a positive construct in individuals and populations, rather than defining health status by future potential risk. In 2022, the concept was updated and expanded to include sleep as an eighth metric. Both the original Life’s Simple 7 and updated LE8 scores have shown consistent and strong associations with greater overall longevity, compression of morbidity, higher quality of life, and markedly reduced incidence of all major chronic diseases of aging (CVD, cancer, dementia, and so on) in the general, asymptomatic population. However, to date, the implications of the CVH construct, specifically LE8, had been less clear among individuals with existing chronic diseases like cancer.

Unfortunately, overall LE8 scores are suboptimal in U.S. adults, with an overall mean score of 64.7, with <20% of the U.S. adult population achieving high CVH status (LE8 score ≥80).7 The study by Wadden et al3 suggests similarly low CVH scores in people with breast cancer and also indicates a similarly strong association with later CVD outcomes in this important group. The study also suggests the predictive ability for CVD of the LE8 score measured before cancer diagnosis and subsequent treatment. These findings underscore the advantages of maintaining a healthy lifestyle across the life course, with a downstream positive impact on CVH and cardiovascular outcomes even in the setting of systemic stressors such as cancer and cancer therapy.

Wadden et al3 observed that nicotine exposure, blood glucose, and blood pressure were significantly associated with CVD (CHD/myocardial infarction) risk, whereas diet, physical activity, and sleep were not. However, a previous study of 19,951 adults showed that of the 8 LE8 metrics, physical activity, blood pressure, and blood glucose accounted for a large portion of CVD-specific mortality,6 and, specifically among breast cancer patients, another study within the WHI cohort8 observed that baseline exercise before breast cancer diagnosis is associated with a graded lower risk for CHD deaths in long-term survivors of breast cancer. Differences in exposures of interest, precision of measurement, definition of outcomes, and length of follow-up likely all contribute to these seemingly divergent findings. Physical activity remains a crucial component of CVH, and optimizing it should be encouraged among all our patients, including those with cancer.

As patients with cancer live longer, we owe it to them to recognize and address modifiable health factors and to help formulate concrete preventive strategies to positively influence health. Public health strategies, such as tobacco control policies, initiatives to increase access to healthy foods and prevent obesity, physical activity promotion in neighborhoods and schools, workplace wellness programs, blood pressure and cholesterol screening programs, and health advice on the benefits of sleep and stress reduction, that positively influence CVH are all potentially successful strategies. Future cardiovascular research involving individuals with cancer should encompass a broader range of demographics to provide more comprehensive insights for a diverse patient population affected by cancer. Furthermore, to better inform cardio-oncology specialists managing cancer patients with cardiovascular issues, there is a need for additional data on the impact of optimal CVH on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with cancer currently undergoing cancer therapy.

Perhaps what we have learned most from the formally defined concept of “cardiovascular health” is that it is a useful, current metric of an individual’s or a population’s general health and is related strongly to upstream determinants as well as downstream health outcomes across the spectrum of chronic diseases in all subgroups and across the life course. CVH represents a new vital sign that we should be measuring and addressing as appropriate in all patients at every visit. Proof of concept is overwhelming; it is time for widespread implementation, including among persons with cancer.
