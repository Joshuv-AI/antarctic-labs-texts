Multiple Roles of Protamine Kinase SRPK1 and Phosphatase PP1γ in Sperm Development
Authors: Alberto de la Iglesia, Mélina Blanco, Meritxell Jodar, Olivier Feudjio, Marjorie Leduc, Judit Castillo, Julie Cocquet, Rafael Oliva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProtein phosphorylation regulates key events of male germ cell differentiation. In the testis, SRPK1 and PP1γ are involved in protamine phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, respectively, and their deregulation is linked to defective chromatin structure and male infertility. The aim of this study was to expand our knowledge on the molecular role of these phosphorylation‐modulating proteins through the identification of novel partners in the testis, potentially supporting additional roles in sperm development. Using immunoprecipitation followed by LC‐MS/MS, we identified yet undescribed interactions of SRPK1 and PP1γ with proteins involved in sperm formation and regulation of spermatogenic gene expression. We reported that SRPK1 interacts with the key splicing regulator ASF/SF2, as well as with other proteins involved in posttranscriptional regulation such as PABP1 or IGF2BP3. We showed that the testis‐enriched isoform 2 of PP1γ (PP1γ2) interacts with PIHI1D1, part of the R2TP chaperone complex, and predicted that ZFR, NDUFB10 and ILF2 interactions with PP1γ2 happen within the C‐terminal sequence which is specific of PP1γ2 proteoform. Both SRPK1 and PP1γ interactomes include ILF2 and TBLX1R1, with roles in gene expression regulation. Most of the identified proteins are known phosphoproteins in male germ cells, supporting a functional interaction. Overall, our results suggest additional roles for SRPK1 and PP1γ during spermatogenesis, shedding light into the orchestrated regulation of the complex mechanisms driving sperm formation. Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifiers PXD054959 and PXD054960.Significance of the StudyIn the present work, we report the first testicular protein interactome of two broad phosphorylation‐modulating proteins, the kinase SRPK1 and the phosphatase PP1γ, known to be essential regulators of chromatin remodeling during male germ cell development. Our novel protein‐protein interactions reveal additional roles for SRPK1 and PP1γ in male germ cells, in key processes including splicing, gene expression, protein homeostasis, or formation of sperm‐specific structures. Our data will therefore contribute to a better understanding of the complex and orchestrated molecular mechanisms controlling testicular activity and proper sperm formation.The lack of male germ cell‐specific protein–protein interaction datasets currently challenges the research on the still largely unknown molecular mechanisms driving male germ cell differentiation. This jeopardizes exploratory approaches and in silico validations in a testis‐specific manner, as most available datasets have been obtained in somatic cells. Thus, our protein‐protein interaction data will be a useful resource to those interested in identifying the origins of male reproductive health problems related to protein phosphorylation. Furthermore, our analyses suggesting that some of these interactions may also occur in other organs extend the relevance of our findings to the broader scientific community.

Introduction

The testis is the organ with the highest transcriptomic complexity and the largest number of protein species and tissue‐specific proteins [1, 2, 3]. A high number of these proteins regulate the process of spermatogenesis, which is divided in three well‐defined phases (mitosis, meiosis, and spermiogenesis), giving rise to highly specialized spermatozoa [4]. During spermiogenesis, haploid spermatids undergo extensive cytoplasmic and nuclear remodeling and new structures are generated, such as the acrosome and flagellum, and a unique chromatin organization [5]. Male germ cell differentiation requires an orchestrated regulation involving different layers of information, including protein post‐translational modifications (PTMs) [6, 7, 8, 9]. Among all PTMs, phosphorylation concerns ∼30% of proteins globally [10]. In male germ cells, it is involved in maintaining sperm differentiation by regulating key male germline‐specific events, such as the acquisition of proper flagellar activity, capacitation, or acrosome reaction [2, 8]. In line with this, different families of kinases and phosphatases are critical during all phases of sperm development by regulating protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation dynamics [8, 11]

At the nuclear level, protein phosphorylation is essential for the extensive chromatin remodeling process which takes place during spermiogenesis. This process is characterized by the replacement of most histones by highly basic protamines, which entails the characteristic high degree of sperm nuclear compaction [12, 13]. Our team and others have characterized that phosphorylation is the most abundant PTM in protamines [14, 15, 16], and its dynamics contributes to the adequate incorporation of protamines into the chromatin [17, 18]. Serine/arginine protein kinase 1 (SRPK1) is responsible for protamine 1 (PRM1) phosphorylation at residues Ser9 and Ser43, which is required for paternal pronucleus decompaction [19, 20, 21]. SRPK1 kinase activity towards protamine 2 (PRM2) has been reported in vitro in Ser8, Ser10, Ser56, and Ser69 [19]. Dysregulation of SRPK1 levels in humans [22] and SRPK1‐dependent PRM1 phosphorylation sites in mouse models [19] are associated with male infertility. While no PRM1‐specific phosphatases have been described yet, testis‐enriched isoform 2 of Serine/threonine‐protein phosphatase PP1‐gamma catalytic subunit (PP1γ2) is involved in removing phosphate groups from the residue Ser56 of PRM2, an event that has been demonstrated to be essential for male fertility [23]. In the mouse testis, PP1γ2 presents from 8‐ to 10‐fold higher expression than isoform 1 (PP1γ1) [24, 25]. PP1γ2 is highly expressed in differentiating meiotic and postmeiotic cells, while isoform 1 is restricted to mitotic and somatic cell types [24, 25]. PP1γ2 is the sole PP1γ isoform in sperm and participates in sperm motility and capacitation [26, 27].

Besides their roles in male germ cells, SRPK1 and PP1γ are well‐known modulators of phosphorylation‐dependent cellular functions, as broadly described. SRPK1 is a moonlight protein known to regulate mRNA splicing by promoting spliceosome assembly upon phosphorylation of SR splicing factors [28, 29]. It is involved in chromatin organization [30, 31] or cell cycle [28], and their deregulation is associated with tumor hallmarks such as cell growth, apoptosis, or oncogenic signaling [32, 33, 34, 35]. PP1γ is part of the superfamily of phosphoprotein phosphatases, which are responsible for most protein dephosphorylation events in a cell, regulating processes associated with glutamate receptors or neuronal cytoskeleton organization for brain function [36, 37], chromatin regulation during mitosis [38], or vesicular trafficking [39], as reviewed in [11, 40, 41].

According to available data from public repositories (Human Protein Atlas [42], https://www.proteinatlas.org/), Srpk1 and Ppp1cc are highly expressed in spermatogenic cell types (particularly, in spermatids) compared to all other cell types analyzed at the single‐cell level, highlighting their biological relevance in male germ cells. Considering that both proteins regulate key cellular functions via protein phosphorylation in other cellular contexts, and that they act in synergy to modulate protamine phosphorylation during spermiogenesis, the aim of the present study is to expand our understanding on their molecular roles in the mouse testis, more specifically in post‐meiotic cells. Thus, we explored the crosstalk between SRPK1 and PP1γ with other proteins to identify their testicular interactive protein networks. In this work, we report a complex subset of proteins susceptible to be regulated by (de)phosphorylation mediated by SRPK1 and PP1γ in the testis, with potential implications for proper sperm formation.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

To achieve the goal of identifying the testicular protein interactive network of SRPK1 and PP1γ, a total of 6 adult C57BL/6 male mice in reproductive age (2‐3 months old) were used. The whole testis protein extract from one mouse was sufficient to perform IP against the protein of interest in parallel with the corresponding IgG negative control. Three biological replicates were conducted for each IP. Half of the immunoprecipitated material was used to validate the correct IP efficiency by WB, and the other half was kept for proteomic identification of the interacting proteins under strict inclusion criteria (FDR 1%, ≥1 unique peptide). Proteins only identified in the SRPK1‐ or PP1γ‐IP and not in the IgG negative control, as well as those significantly enriched in the SRPK1‐ or PP1γ‐IP (p‐value< 0.05; FC>1.5), in comparison with the IgG negative control, were considered as interacting proteins. Bioinformatics and statistical methods for analysis are further described in the following sections. All procedures were subjected to local ethical review (Comité d'Ethique pour l'Experimentation Animale, Université Paris Descartes, registration number CEEA34.JC.114.12, APAFIS 14214‐2017072510448522v26).

Srpk1 and Ppp1cc Testicular Expression and Ppp1cc Isoform Analysis

Data from the ReproGenomics Viewer (https://rgv.genouest.org/) [43] extracted from Green et al., [44] was used to identify testicular gene expression of Srpk1 and Ppp1cc at the single‐cell level. To the best of our knowledge, no information on Ppp1cc1 and Ppp1cc2 isoform expression is available in published datasets; we therefore conducted isoform analyses on bulk transcriptomic data on spermatogenic cell types from [45, 46]. Paired‐end RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) data, consisting of 100‐bp reads, were obtained from 3 major wild‐type spermatogenic cell types: differentiating spermatogonia (Kit+), primary spermatocytes (SCI), and round spermatids (RS). In total, there were three replicates for Kit+ and five replicates for SCI and RS. Sequence adaptors were removed, and low‐quality reads were filtered out using fastp (version 0.23) with default parameters (–qualified_quality_phred 20). Alignment to the mouse reference genome mm10 (GRCm38) merged with a Spike‐in genome (for internal normalization and gene expression analysis) was conducted using STAR [47] (version 2.7.2d) along with Ensembl annotation GTF (http://www.ensembl.org/) [48]. The alignment process included the following arguments: –quantMode TranscriptomeSAM GeneCounts –twopassMode Basic. Differential isoform usage was performed using isoform‐level quantification data from RSEM (v1.3.3) [49] based on read alignments on the mm10 reference genome and the IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR package (v2.1.2) in R [50]. This tool identifies isoform switches and usage from RNA‐seq data, incorporating annotation sources like protein domains, signal peptides, Intrinsically Disordered Regions (IDR), and Non‐sense Mediated Decay (NMD) sensitivity to predict functional consequences. It highlights isoform switches based on factors like protein domain loss. Isoform fraction, defined as the relative abundance of each isoform compared to a gene's total expression, is used to assess an isoform contribution to gene expression within a sample. A gene isoform was considered differentially used if it had a change of proportion of at least 0.1 (dIF>0.1) with an adjusted p‐value of 5% (p‐value< 0.05). We performed pairwise comparisons between Kit+ vs. SCI and SCI vs. RS. According to Ensembl, mouse transcript Ppp1cc‐202 encodes the canonical PP1γ1 isoform (323 amino acids) and Ppp1cc‐201 encodes the germ cell specific PP1γ2 isoform (337 amino acids).

Testicular Soluble Proteins Extraction

Mouse testes were freshly dissected and washed in DMEM (Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA). Tunica albuginea was removed to minimize somatic contamination and the resulting testicular mass was subjected to mechanical disaggregation and homogenization in lysis solution (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris‐HCl pH 8.0, 0.5% NP40, 5 mM EDTA) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (#P8340, Sigma–Aldrich), 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM Sodium butyrate, and phosphatase inhibitors (10 mM sodium fluoride, 3 mM tetrasodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM trisodium vanadate). Lysis was carried out by incubating for 1 h at 4°C with rotation. Soluble proteins were recovered in the supernatant after centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The protein extract was immediately used for immunoprecipitation experiments.

Protein Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed in biological triplicates as described in [51], with minor modifications. Briefly, for each experiment, 5 µg of primary antibody against SRPK1 (#14073‐1‐AP, ProteinTech, Chicago, IL, USA), PP1γ (#sc‐515943, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), PIH1D1 (#19427‐1‐AP, ProteinTech), ILF2 (#67685‐1‐Ig, Proteintech) and corresponding IgG isotype controls (#02‐6102 and #02‐6502, Invitrogen) were cross‐linked with 50 µL of protein A/G‐coated magnetic beads (#4630, Ademtech, Pessac, France). IP against the corresponding IgG was performed in parallel as negative control. Magnetic beads were washed three times with 0.65% Tween‐20 in PBS prior primary antibody incubation 30 min at room temperature (RT) with rotation. Then, bead‐antibody complexes were crosslinked with dimethyl‐pimelimidate 200 mM at pH 9.0 at RT for 30 min. The reaction was stopped with 50 mM Tris‐HCl at pH 7.5. Crosslinked beads were incubated with the testis lysates overnight at 4°C. Elution of retained proteins was done with PAG Elution Buffer (#10701, Ademtech) incubating for 5 min at RT. The pH was neutralized with triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) pH 8.4. Eluted material was used for IP validation by western blot and for the identification of the candidate proteins by label‐free bottom‐up proteomics.

Western Blot

For IP validation, 50% of the total eluted material was denatured with 4X NuPage LDS sample buffer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% β‐mercaptoethanol, incubating for 5 min at 95°C. Denatured proteins were loaded on a 12% SDS‐polyacrylamide gel for electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and subsequently transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane at 80 V during 1.5 h. Blocking was performed incubating 1 h at RT with 5% skimmed milk in PBST. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution (1:750 anti‐SRPK1 #14073‐1‐AP from Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA; 1:2000 anti‐PP1γ #sc‐515943 from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) and membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C. Corresponding secondary antibodies coupled with HRP (goat antirabbit #31460, goat antimouse #31430; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) were diluted 1:3000 in blocking solution for incubation during 2 h at RT. Imaging was performed with Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP substrate (Millipore) on ImageQuant LAS 4000 imager (GE Healthcare, Chicaco, IL, USA). For co‐IP validation by WB, the same protocol was followed with minor modifications. Anti‐PP1γ2 (#MBS395821, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA) and anti‐SRPK1 (#14073‐1‐AP, ProteinTech) antibodies were used in 1:1000 dilution. Proteins were transferred using the Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), and image acquisition was performed in iBright FL1500 Imager (ThermoFischer, Waltham, MA, USA). Full WB membranes can be found in Figure S5.

Sample Preparation for Mass Spectrometry

Eluted proteins were denatured in 200 mM TEAB pH 8.5 and 2% SDS, while disulfide bonds were reduced using 10 mM TCEP (tris 2‐carboxyethyl phosphine) and subsequent free thiols groups were protected using 50 mM chloroacetamide for 5 min at 95°C. Proteins were trypsin digested overnight using the suspension trapping (S‐Trap) method to collect peptides, as previously described [52]. Eluted peptides were vacuum‐dried in a Speed Vac (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany).

C18 Liquid Nanochromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS)

NanoflowHPLC and MS analyses were performed at the Proteom'IC facility (University Paris Cité) using an U3000 RSLC system (from ThermoFisher) hyphenated to a timsTOF Pro MS (from Bruker). All mobile phases were made with milliQ‐grade H2O produced with a milliQ integral‐3 (from Merck‐Millipore). Peptides were solubilized in 10 µL of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and 10% acetonitrile (ACN). One µL was loaded, concentrated, and washed for 3 min on a C18 reverse‐phase precolumn (5 µm particle size, 100 Å pore size, 300 µm inner diameter, 0.5 cm length, from Thermo Fisher Scientific) with loading solvent containing 0.1% TFA and 2% ACN. Peptides were separated on an Aurora C18 reverse phase resin (1.6 µm particle size, 100 Å pore size, 75 µm inner diameter, 25 cm length mounted to the Captive nano spray Ionization module, from IonOpticks, Middle Camberwell Australia) with a 35 min binary gradient starting from 99% of solvent A containing 0.1% formic acid and ending in 40% of solvent B containing 80% ACN, 0.085% formic acid. The mass spectrometer acquired data throughout the elution process and operated in DDA PASEF mode with a total cycle time of 1.88s, with Timed Ion Mobility Spectrometry (TIMS) enabled. Ion accumulation and ramp time in the dual TIMS analyzer were set to 166 ms each and the ion mobility range was set from 1/K0 = 0.6 Vs cm−2 to 1.6 Vs cm−2. Precursor ions for MS/MS analysis were isolated in positive polarity with PASEF in the 100‐1.700 m/z range by synchronizing quadrupole switching events with the precursor elution profile from the TIMS device.

Protein Identification and Quantification from MS Data of IP Samples

Identifications (protein hits) and quantifications were performed by comparison of experimental peak lists with a database of theoretical sequences using MaxQuant version 2.0.1.0 [53]. The databases used were Mus musculus reviewed sequences from Uniprot Swissprot protein database (release December 2020) and a list of in‐house frequent contaminant sequences. The cleavage specificity was trypsin's with maximum 2 missed cleavages. The precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm. Carbamidomethylation of cysteines was set as constant modification, whereas acetylation of the protein N‐terminus and oxidation of methionines were set as variable modifications. Second peptide search was allowed and minimal length of peptides was set at 7 amino acids. The false discovery rate (FDR) was estimated using the target‐decoy method using an automatically generated reversed database and was kept below 1% on both peptides and proteins. Label‐Free protein Quantification (LFQ) was done using both unique and razor peptides according to [53].

Statistical Analysis of MS Data

For LFQ analysis, data were imported into the Perseus software version 1.6.15.0 [54]. Reverse, contaminant proteins, and proteins identified only by site were excluded from analysis. We defined as valid interacting proteins those only present in the SRPK1‐ or PP1γ‐ associated proteomes (present in 3 of the 3 replicates), and those significantly enriched in the SRPK1‐ or PP1γ‐ associated proteomes compared to the corresponding negative control. For comparison, one‐side Student t‐test (p‐value < 0.01) on proteins present in the three replicates of either the IP of interest or the respective negative control, with fold‐change (FC) >1.5, was applied.

Downstream Analyses of Protein Interactomes

The list of identified proteins was analyzed with the gene ontology (GO) tool (http://www.geneontology.org/) to check enriched biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components [55, 56]. PANTHER Overrepresentation Test was done on GO terms retrieved from the GO Ontology database [last release: PANTHER 18.0, 2024‐01‐17, using Mus musculus (all genes in database) as reference list]. Significance of the enrichment analyses was calculated by Fisher Exact Test (p‐value < 0.05 after multiple‐comparisons adjustment with Bonferroni correction). Enriched pathways were determined using Reactome version 87 (https://reactome.org/), with p‐value < 0.05 after hypergeometric distribution test corrected for false discovery rate using the Benjamani‐Hochberg method. The STRING 12.0 database (https://string‐db.org/) was used to identify protein networks and connections between the identified proteins [57]. Only those interactions with highest confidence interaction score (score ≥0.9) were considered.

AlphaBridge web server (https://alpha‐bridge.eu/) [58] was used to identify predicted protein‐protein interactions involving the C‐terminal end of PP1γ2, analyzing the top‐ranked predicted 3D protein complex model between PP1γ2 and its interactors retrieved from AlphaFold Server [59] using protein sequences from Uniprot Knowledgebase (UniProtKB, http://www.uniprot.org/) [60]. AlphaBridge generates the AlphaBridge score, indicating the global confidence of predicted interactions, and the individual predicted interaction Confidence Score (piCSi; which indicates the importance of each identified interface for complex formation). A standard threshold of confidence prediction (0.7) was set to identify the interaction interfaces.

We retrieved protein abundance in different spermatogenic cell types (spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, round spermatids, elongating spermatids) from Li et al. [61], to establish individual protein dynamics across male germ cell differentiation. We benefited from the protein clustering established by Li and colleagues according to normalized protein abundance in the different cell types to identify the most relevant candidates for our study. Biological functions shown in Tables S1 and S3 were obtained from UniProtKB [60].

Phosphorylation status of the interacting proteins was retrieved from available datasets [62, 63] on mouse whole testis and spermatid phosphoproteomes, respectively, and using PhosphositePlus (http://www.phosphosite.org/) [64]. Lists of phosphoproteins in human testis and sperm was retrieved from [8, 65], respectively. Non‐phosphorylated proteins were checked in the corresponding native proteomes, to confirm their presence on its non‐phosphorylated form at the protein level. Since Wang et al. only provides human sperm phosphoproteomic data, human sperm proteomic data from [66] was used. Venn diagrams were created using InteractiVenn [67].

Gene expression data in different mouse organs was obtained from the FANTOM5 Project (Functional Annotation of the Mammalian Genome) [68, 69]. Testis‐enriched proteins were defined as those presenting higher expression in the testis than in at least 90% of the other organs (FC>1.5).

Results

SRPK1 Interacts with Proteins Involved in Post‐Transcriptional Regulation in the Testis

To identify SRPK1 protein network in male germ cells, we performed immunoprecipitation (IP) against SRPK1 followed by MS in biological triplicates, in parallel with the corresponding negative control IP (isotype IgG, in triplicates). According to available single‐cell transcriptomic data in adult mouse testis [44] (Figure 1A, B), Srpk1 is mostly expressed from meiosis onwards and, at the protein level [61], SRPK1 reaches a peak during spermiogenesis (Figure 1C). Srpk1 expression is higher in germ cells than in somatic cells, i. e. 7‐fold higher in spermatocytes (SC), 5.5‐fold higher in round spermatids (RS), and 2‐fold higher elongating spermatids (ES) than in the somatic compartment (Figure 1B). Besides, somatic cells are scarce compared to germ cells in an adult testis [70]. Hence, most of SPRK1 and its interactome detected in this tissue come from germ cells. The efficiency and specificity of IP was validated by WB detection of SRPK1 (Figure 1D) before proceeding with LC‐MS/MS. MaxQuant analyses (FDR 1%, ≥1 unique peptide) revealed a protein interactive network of 40 proteins. The presence of SRPK1 as the second most abundant protein found in the immunoprecipitated material by MS confirmed the good quality of the IP (Figure 1D, Table S1). Among the interacting proteins, 17 were exclusively found in SRPK1‐IP, and 23 were significantly enriched in SRPK1‐IP compared to negative control (p< 0.01, fold‐change enrichment >1.5; Figure 1D and Table S1). We validated the interaction of SRPK1 and ILF2 by co‐IP followed by WB (Figure 1E). STRING analysis evidenced serine/arginine‐rich splicing factor 1 (ASF/SF2) –a known SRPK1 interactor in other cell types– which showed additional interactions with other proteins from SRPK1 network (Figure S1A).

GO enrichment analyses showed that SRPK1‐associated proteins are mainly involved in co‐ and post‐transcriptional regulation (such as ASF/SF2, NCBP2, RBM17, and SF3A1 in mRNA splicing; IGF2BP3, PABP1, hnRNP D0, or hnRNP U in mRNA stabilization; and hnRNP C1/C2, THRAP3, PRPF4 or SNRNP40 in mRNA processing), RNA transport and localization (THOC4, NCBP2, or hnRNP A2/B1), and assembly of ribonucleoprotein complexes (NGDN, RPLP0, MRTO4, PRPF3) (Figure 1F). As molecular functions, RNA and single‐stranded DNA binding, m6a‐RNA reader, and protein‐RNA adaptor activities were found. Cellular location was coherent with the functions, revealing the presence of SRPK1‐interacting proteins in exon‐exon junctions, spliceosome, small nuclear ribonucleoprotein complexes, ribonucleoprotein granule, and nuclear speck (Figure 1F). Reactome analysis confirmed GO findings, identifying mRNA splicing and mRNA processing as the two unique enriched pathways (p < 0.05 after FDR correction). Additionally, information about RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) identity in male germ cells from Li et al. [61] indicated that 60% (24/40) of SRPK1 interactors are RBPs (Table S2 and Figure S1A), reinforcing their roles described above.

We also integrated our IP data with the proteomic data from purified germ cells recently published by Li and colleagues [61]. To understand the relevance of the identified interactions across spermatogenesis, we used their classification system in clusters according to normalized intensities per cell type, allowing us to retrieve the spermatogenic dynamics of each interactor (Table S2). SRPK1 being at its highest level in elongating spermatids (ES), we focused on interacting proteins particularly abundant during late spermiogenesis (mainly corresponding to clusters 4, 8, and 9 according to the classification system of Li et al. [61] based on protein dynamics; Figure 1G, H and Table S2). Proteins such as PABP1 from cluster 8, and AP2B1, COX2, and ZPBP2 from cluster 9, present their highest levels after meiosis (in round spermatids ‐RS‐ and/or ES) (Figure 1G). Despite being classified as cluster 9 and their levels are high in postmeiotic cells, T30A2 and TSP50 maintain their high abundance from meiosis onwards. Finally, several proteins belonging to cluster 4 (RPL10A, RPLP0, and TBL1XR1) also have high abundance in ES, accompanied by high levels in spermatogonia (SPG), a previous germ cell type (Figure 1H). Even though the raise in SRPK1 levels RS to ES is more drastic, these three proteins also increase their abundance in late spermiogenesis. Thus, around a quarter of all identified interactions (9/39) are particularly present at the end of spermatid differentiation, where SRPK1 is more abundant. As expected, some of these proteins showcase more specialized roles in sperm differentiation and function (Table S1).

PP1γ Associates with Protein Complexes Involved in Sperm Differentiation

Similarly to Srpk1, Ppp1cc, the gene encoding both PP1γ isoforms (Figure S2A) is mostly expressed in primary spermatocytes (SC I) and RS [44] (Figure 2A, B), and PP1γ protein levels [61] increase along male germ cell differentiation (Figure 2C). Specifically, Ppp1cc expression is 8‐fold higher in SC, 6‐fold higher in RS, and 2‐fold higher in ES than in somatic cells (Figure 2B), evidencing a limited contribution of somatic cells to our data. Isoform analyses in adult male germ cells revealed that, from all Ppp1cc transcripts, the proportion of Ppp1cc2 increases along the mitotic‐to‐meiotic (adj p‐value < 0.001) and meiotic‐to‐post‐meiotic transitions (adj p‐value < 0.05) (Figure S2). Ppp1cc2 is the predominant isoform in SC I and RS, representing a 78.3% and 92.5% of total transcripts, respectively (Figures S2C, D). While Ppp1cc1 proportion is similar to Ppp1cc2 in differentiating spermatogonia, it drops to values close to zero (adj p‐value < 0.001) from SC I onwards (Figure S2C). This indicates that the PP1γ testicular interactome may mostly represent interactions involving the male‐germ cell specific PP1γ2 isoform.

We therefore investigated the PP1γ protein network using the same approach as for SRPK1, consisting in IP against PP1γ and its negative control in biological triplicates, followed by western blot validation (Figure 2D) before LC‐MS/MS detection. Following strict identification criteria (FDR 1%, ≥1 unique peptide), the PP1γ protein interactive network consisted in 55 PP1γ‐associated proteins (Figure 2D and Table S4). PP1γ was the most abundant protein found in the IP material, confirming the quality of the IP. Out of 55 interactors, 47 proteins were exclusively found in PP1γ‐IP, and 8 proteins were enriched compared to the negative control (p < 0.01, fold‐change enrichment >1.5; Figure 2D). STRING analyses revealed previously known interactions described for PP1γ (i. e. PNUTS, PPP1R7, PPP1R2, and PPP1R9B), and additional protein‐protein interactions among other proteins from the set (Figure S1B). Interestingly, ILF2 and TBLX1R1 were commonly identified in both SRPK1 and PP1γ interactomes. We validated the interaction of PIH1D1 with the testis‐enriched PP1γ2 isoform by reverse IP (Figure 2E), and identified predicted interactions of ZFR, NDUFB10 and ILF2 with the PP1γ2‐specific C‐terminal sequence (amino acids 315‐337) (Figure S3A). AlphaBridge scores, quantifying the confidence of detecting molecular interfaces within the specific protein complex, were 0.8 for PP1γ2‐ILF2, 0.78 for PP1γ2‐ZFR, and 0.77 for PP1γ2‐NDUFB10. All predicted interactions disappeared when the protein complexes were predicted with the ubiquitous isoform PP1γ1 (Figure S3B), supporting their germ‐cell specificity.

GO enrichment analyses showed that proteins associated with PP1γ mainly participate in assembly of small nucleolar ribonucleoprotein (snoRNP) complex, such as PIH1D1, RUVBL1, and RUVBL2; mRNA degradation, like PYM1, WDR61, MAGOH, and RBM8A; and protein dephosphorylation regulation by different PP1 regulatory subunits (PPP1R2, PPP1R7, PPP1R9B or PPP1R11) (Figure 2F). Enriched molecular function terms included protein phosphatase inhibitor activity (either general or in serine/threonine phosphatases) and binding to protein phosphatase 1. The GO enrichment analysis of cellular components allowed us to identify proteins belonging to specific complexes, such as R2TP complex, RPAP3/R2TP/pre‐folding‐like complex, and PTW/PP1 phosphatase complex (Figure 2F). No Reactome enriched pathways were found within PP1γ‐interacting proteins, suggesting that the high number of proteins identified participate in processes involving different molecular pathways. A 23.6% (13/55) of PP1γ interactors have been described as RBPs by Li et al. [61] (Table S4 and Figure S1B).

As for SRPK1, we determined the abundance dynamics of PP1γ interactors using normalized data from Li and colleagues [61] and observed that PRMT5, WDR77, DNAH17, IFT81, WDR34, and COPS5 belong to the same cluster as PP1γ (i.e. cluster 9, Figure 2G and Table S4), exhibiting higher levels in RS and ES. We also explored clusters where expression levels are high in ES (i. e. clusters 4 and 10, n = 10) or high in RS (i. e. clusters 7 and 8, n = 9) to identify relevant proteins during spermiogenesis (Figure 2H, I). Together with those belonging to cluster 9 (including PP1γ), 47.3% of interacting proteins (26/55) are enriched in postmeiotic cells, reinforcing the role of this interacting network for spermatid differentiation.

Phosphorylation Status of SRPK1 and PP1γ Interacting Proteins in Mouse and Human Male Germ Cells

Since the endpoint of the interaction between SRPK1 and PP1γ with other proteins is the modulation of their phosphorylation status, we checked whether the identified proteins have been reported to be phosphorylated in male germ cells. Thus, we cross‐compared the lists of SRPK1‐ and PP1γ‐ interacting proteins with whole mouse testis phosphoproteins reported by Qi et al. [63], and mouse spermatids phosphoproteins from Li et al., [62]. Proteins bearing phosphorylation sites (therefore susceptible to be phosphorylated by SRPK1 or dephosphorylated by PP1γ) are described in Figure 3A, B and Tables S5 and S6. In total, 26 SRPK1‐interacting proteins and 32 PP1γ‐interacting proteins were described as phosphoproteins in the testis. The majority of them are also phosphorylated in mouse spermatids (25 and 27 in SRPK1 and PP1γ interactomes, respectively; Figure 3A, B and Tables S5 and S6).

Knowing the essential role of protein phosphorylation for male germ cell development and sperm function, we checked whether the identified mouse testicular phosphoproteins were also phosphorylated in human testis and sperm, using ours and other's datasets (Figure 3C, Tables S5 and S6). Remarkably, human orthologs of 16 SRPK1‐interacting proteins and 17 PP1γ‐interacting proteins conserved their phosphorylation status, according to available testicular phosphoproteomes. From them, SRPK1‐interacting proteins RPLP0 and TRAP150, and PP1γ‐interacting proteins FN1, KPNA3, PPP1R2, and PPP1R7 were also phosphorylated in human sperm (Figure 3C, Tables S5 and S6). The maintenance of several phosphoproteins in the testes from mouse to human indicates conserved roles of protein phosphorylation in testicular function.

SRPK1 and PP1γ Testis‐Specific and Ubiquitous Interactions

Although SRPK1 and PP1γ are testis‐enriched proteins, they have multiple roles in phosphorylation‐dependent regulation of cellular processes in other cell types. Therefore, we explored whether both proteins and their interactors identified in mouse testis were also expressed in other organs.

Interestingly, we identified both testis‐specific and ubiquitous SRPK1‐ and PP1γ‐ interacting proteins, according to their gene expression pattern in different tissues (Figure S4). Among the ubiquitous, some were testis‐enriched, evidencing higher gene expression in the testis than in at least 90% of other organs. Among the SRPK1 interactome, testis‐enriched proteins included SRPK1 itself, as well as 14 interactors (AP2B1, H1F1, hnRNP A2/B1, hnRNP U, ILF3, LSM12, MCM7, MRTO4, PABP1, TSP50, RBM27, RPLP0, T30A2, and ZPBP2) representing a 37.5% of the SRPK1 interactome. Similarly, 11 PP1γ interactors (CETN4, COPS5, eIF3a, IFT80, LRRC34, LYPD4, PIH1D1, PNUTS, PPP1R11, UBE2M, and ZAP3) show testis‐enriched gene expression (20% of all interacting proteins). IGF2BP3 and TSKS, interactors of SRPK1 and PP1γ, respectively, showcase a testis specific pattern of expression (Figure S4). Among these testis‐enriched or testis‐specific proteins, SRPK1 interactors AP2B1, TSP50, PABP1, RPLP0, T30A2, and ZPBP2, and all PP1γ interactors except PNUTS and ZAP3, presented high levels in postmeiotic cells according to protein abundance dynamics. All these features highlight the specificity of part of the SRPK1 and PP1γ interactive network towards male germ cell processes, particularly on the highly specialized process of spermatid differentiation.

Additionally, as it can be pictured from Figure S4, many proteins from both interactomes are also expressed in other organs, albeit at a lower level than in the testis for some. These findings indicate that while the identified SRPK1‐ and PP1γ‐ protein‐protein interactions are relevant for a proper male germ cell development, they can happen in other tissues where SRPK1 or PP1γ are present.

Discussion

The kinase SRPK1 and the phosphatase PP1γ are widely known for regulating protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation events essential for proper cellular function, such as splicing, chromatin organization, cell cycle, or cytoskeletal organization, among others [11, 32, 35, 41]. SRPK1 and PP1γ are abundant proteins in the testis, whose levels increase across male germ cell differentiation, and have been described to be involved in protamine phosphorylation regulation [11, 19, 23, 40]. In the present study, we provide new evidence of testicular protein‐protein associations that shed light on additional roles of both proteins during spermatogenesis, with special attention to post‐meiotic cells, where both proteins show their highest abundance. Our findings are in line with the essential role of protein phosphorylation in maintaining proper testicular activity [8, 63].

The testicular SRPK1 interactome is enriched in RNA‐binding proteins (RBP) involved in different mechanisms of co‐ and post‐transcriptional regulation. RBP are essential for male germ cell differentiation, and their loss of function have been associated with male infertility [71]. Along with the transcriptional shutdown happening during spermiogenesis, transcription is decoupled from translation, and alternative splicing events and other post‐transcriptional processes mediated by ribonucleoproteins are essential regulatory mechanisms for the post‐meiotic gene expression program [72, 73, 74, 75]. ASF/SF2‐mediated alternative splicing has been recently identified as a crucial mechanism for spermatogenesis maintenance and male fertility [76]. We report that the SRPK1‐ASF/SF2 interaction, described in other cellular contexts to be one of SRPK1's most functionally relevant interactions [28, 77], also happens in the mouse testis. Among SRPK1 interactors, we found IGF2BP3 and PABP1, RPB described to localize in the chromatoid body—a large ribonucleoprotein granule found specifically in spermatids—and to participate in the post‐transcriptional regulation of mRNAs involved in the histone to protamine transition (such as Tnp1, Tnp2, Prm2, Dot1l, or Hdac11) [73, 75, 78]. PABP1‐mediated post‐transcriptional regulation is phosphorylation‐dependent [50], adding a new layer of regulation to the histone to protamine transition via RBP post‐translational modification. Interestingly, PABP1 has been found to interact with SRPK1a [79], a less abundant isoform of SRPK1 that retains a N‐terminal spliced‐out region in the canonical form. SRPK1a has similar localization and substrate specificity than SRPK1 and is predominantly expressed in the testis compared to other tissues [80]. Other proteins from the SRPK1 interactome involved in adenylation‐dependent mRNA decay or with N6‐methyladenosine binding activity showed different protein abundance dynamics than that of SRPK1. However, the different protein dynamics does not exclude a potential functional interaction. This is evidenced by hnRNP A2/B1, hnRNP C1/C2, or hnRNP U, previously described to interact with SRPK1a [79].

While the function of SRPK1 core interactome is overrepresented by co‐ and post‐transcriptional mRNA related processes, some interacting proteins enriched in elongated spermatids show roles in sperm formation or are associated with sperm‐specific structures. For example, loss of TSP50 leads to multiple morphological defects of sperm flagella and head [81, 82], and ZPBP2 is necessary for proper acrosomal compaction, apart from its known role in binding of sperm to zona‐pellucida [83]. Other proteins such as AP2B1 and T30A2 (IFT70A2) are localized in post‐meiotic specific structures, such as the acrosomal membrane or the manchette, respectively [84, 85]. This functional spectrum of the SRPK1 interactome throughout spermatogenesis is in line with its moonlight protein identity.

PP1γ interactome was larger and presented more diverse roles than that of SRPK1. We identified the four members of R2TP complex (regulatory heterodimer RPAP3‐PIH1D1 bound to the catalytic ATPases RUVBL1 and RUVBL2) and confirmed the interaction of PIH1D1 with the testis‐enriched PP1γ2. The R2TP complex acts as a versatile co‐chaperone with unusual function specialized in quaternary protein folding with substrates like spliceosomal small nucleolar RNAs and RNA polymerases [86]. Our structural protein complex predictions identified interactions of ZFR, NDUFB10, and ILF2 with the specific C‐terminal sequence of PP1γ2. ZFR is a zinc‐finger protein with DNA and RNA binding ability mostly expressed during meiosis [87], involved in splicing regulation and necessary for histone macroH2A1 expression [88]. NDUFB10 is a subunit of the NADH dehydrogenase (mitochondrial respiratory complex I), whose phosphorylation is necessary for the complex activity in cancer cells [89]. Lastly, ILF2 is a testis‐enriched protein that is regulated during meiosis and is associated with active transcription [90]. Interestingly, ILF2 was also part of SRPK1 interactome, suggesting an interplay between SRPK1 and PP1γ2 in the control of ILF2 phosphorylation status. Among the most abundant PP1γ interactors in post‐meiotic cells, we found an enrichment of proteins essential for spermiation, such as TSKS, necessary for cytoplasm removal during spermatid development and previously identified to interact with PP1γ2 [91, 92], or involved in flagella structure and maintenance or sperm motility, such as DNAH17, IFT81, WDR34, and AK1 [93, 94, 95, 96]. Both PP1γ2 and DNAH17 are decreased in sperm from infertile men presenting oligoasthenoteratozoospermia [97], supporting a functional relevance of the identified interaction. This points out a role for this phosphatase in multiple aspects of the vast cellular remodeling giving rise to morphologically‐shaped sperm in the testis. The dimer PRMT5/WDR77 is also among the postmeiotic‐enriched PP1γ interactors, involved in arginine methylation of Piwi proteins [98], necessary for a correct piRNA pathway, and of histones [99], even though the role of PRMT5‐mediated symmetric histone methylation during spermatogenesis remains underexplored.

Due to antibody availability, PP1γ‐IP was performed against PP1γ (recognizing both PP1γ isoforms 1 and 2), instead of the testis‐enriched PP1γ2. Nevertheless, our Ppp1cc isoform analysis, in line with the literature [25, 100, 101], supports that the identified interactions in whole testis mostly involve PP1γ2. The identification of other proteins described to interact with PP1γ2 (LMTK2, PPP1R2, PPP1R7, NIPP‐1/PPP1R8, PPP1R11, and TSKS) [92, 102, 103, 104] or its C‐terminal sequence (COPS5) reinforce this [104]. Additionally, our experimental validation of the interaction between PIH1D1 with PP1γ2 and our in silico predictions of interactions between ILF2, NDUFB10, and ZFR and the specific C‐terminal PP1γ2, support this interpretation. The only evident exceptions would be PHACTR1, absent in the male germ cells’ proteome from Li and colleagues [61], and PNUTS and ZAP3, described to interact exclusively with PP1γ1 by in vitro approaches [103, 104].

Several SRPK1‐ and PP1γ‐interacting proteins were detected as phosphoproteins in mouse spermatogenic cells. The conservation of the phosphorylation status of 16 SRPK1‐ and 17 PP1γ‐ interactors in the human testis highlights the physiological relevance of this modification to maintain testicular activity. Most phosphoproteins in the human testis do not present a phosphorylated status in human sperm, which may point out to important roles of protein phosphatases, including PP1γ, in modulating phosphorylation status towards the end of spermatogenesis or during post‐testicular sperm maturation [19, 23]. Multi‐organ gene expression comparison revealed some testis‐specific and testis‐enriched interactors among SRPK1 and PP1γ interactomes. We identified a higher proportion of testis‐specific and ‐enriched proteins in the SRPK1 interactome than in that of PP1γ, likely due to the own testis‐enriched identity of SRPK1. From them, 6 out of 14 SRPK1‐ and 9 out 11 PP1γ‐ related proteins were enriched in post‐meiotic cells, suggesting that a part of both interactomes do not only show essential roles in maintaining normal testicular activity but are particularly relevant for the differentiation of spermatids into highly specialized spermatozoa. Despite that, the majority of the interactors were also expressed in other tissues. Importantly, all proteins from both interactomes except PHACTR1 are present in spermatogonia, with 21 and 20 SRPK1‐ and PP1γ‐ associated proteins, respectively, showing even higher levels in spermatogonia than in other germ cell types. This is in line with the fact that many spermatogonia‐expressed genes are ubiquitously expressed [105]. Therefore, even though we show that the reported interactions are important to maintain testicular activity, they can potentially exist in other cell types, broadening the impact of our findings.

The identification of protein–protein interactions in whole testis challenges the specific assignment of the described interactions to specific testicular cell populations. However, based on the expression pattern of SRPK1 and PP1γ in the mouse testis, the contribution of interactions from the somatic testicular compartment is expected to be minimal. Indeed, from both interactomes, PHACTR1 (PP1γ‐interacting protein), is the only protein found from our whole testis data that is absent in the male germ cell proteome reported by Li and colleagues [61]. Considering that both SRPK1 and PP1γ are enriched in meiotic and post‐meiotic cells, the majority of the interactions are likely to happen in these cell types. Unfortunately, we cannot rule out a mixed contribution of the testicular cell populations in our data, which can be a potential reason behind the identification of target proteins with different protein dynamics to that of SRPK1 and PP1γ.

In short, our SRPK1 and PP1γ interactomes in the adult mouse testis expand the previously described roles of both proteins in protamine (de)phosphorylation in male germ cells. Novel functions are particularly relevant for spermiogenesis, but also to maintain germ cell differentiation throughout spermatogenesis at different stages. Newly identified SRPK1 and PP1γ interactions shed light into the complex protein post‐translational regulation during spermatogenesis. The limited knowledge about protein‐protein interactions in male germ cells challenges the current approaches to target these pathways in a testis‐specific manner, as source data mostly come from other somatic tissues. Hence, we believe that SRPK1 and PP1γ testicular protein interactomes will be a useful source to those interested in SRPK1 and PP1γ roles in male germ cells.
