Determinants affecting the medical students adoption of mobile learning: extended UTAUT
Authors: Mohammed A. E. Suliman, Wenlan Zhang, Kamal Abubker Abrahim Sleiman, Rehab Suliman, Zhao Xiaoni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis research investigates the fundamental factors which may stymie or simplify the adoption of m-learning by medical college educators in Sudan. We made use of the Extended Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use Technology (UTAUT) with COVID-19 fear (CF) and social isolation (SIS) as exogenous components.ObjectiveThis research aims to find out factors impacting medical students’ behavioural intention to use mobile learning.MethodsData was gathered from 499 undergraduate learners from different universities in Sudan. Partial Least Square (PLS) and structural equation modeling (SEM) were used to examine the data.The resultsShowed that expected performance, effort expectancy (EE), social influence (SI), facilitation condition (FC), COVID-19 fear, and social isolation (SIS) are predictors of behavioural intentions (BI). In addition, our findings show that COVID-19 fear and social isolation increase student adoption of mobile learning during the COVID-19 epidemics in developing countries.ConclusionsThe study unveiled that the suggested framework, derived from UTAUT, effectively discerns the factors impacting students’ intentions in mobile learning during pandemics. Sudanese medical students’ can strategize and execute effective classroom environments based on these findings.

Introduction

Technology has emerged as a pivotal facilitator in enhancing pedagogical practices within the realm of education [1]. Mobile learning, denoting the utilization of portable electronic devices in educational settings, has emerged as a contemporary paradigm in higher education worldwide, propelled by significant advancements in mobile technologies and the increased accessibility of the Internet [2, 3]. This shift towards e-learning technologies is revolutionizing instructional methodologies, transitioning from traditional instructor-centric approaches to learner-centered models, thereby enabling flexible learning modalities including remote and on-demand instruction [4, 5]. Underscoring the importance of understanding students’ perceptions and readiness to embrace m-learning technologies. Despite its vast potential, m-learning continues to evolve amidst the dynamic landscape of application platform technologies and network infrastructures, positioning itself as a promising trend in higher education [2]. The gradual integration of mobile learning into educational institutions is influenced by multifaceted factors that shape system design and utilization patterns [6]. In his study, [7] identified factors affecting medical student adoption of the rain classroom, extending UTAUT with anxiety, motivation, and self-efficacy, while [8] extended UTAUT with attitude and computer literacy. Importantly, existing research underscores the variability in technology adoption and usage among students across demographic, cultural, and national contexts [9]. In the contemporary globalized landscape, the proliferation of such technologies is particularly pronounced in developing nations, reflecting the increasing interconnectedness and technological diffusion shaping educational practices worldwide. While the majority of scholarly investigations in e-learning have predominantly focused on developed nations [10], a limited body of research has applied the UTAUT framework to examine e-learning adoption in underdeveloped contexts [11]. Thus, this research contributes to the scholarly discourse by exploring the adoption of e-learning within the African context, with a specific focus on Sudan. Notably, student motivation and engagement in mobile learning activities have been identified as critical determinants of success in m-learning initiatives [12]. This research answers the urgent call by [13] to study on mobile learning within the African context. Despite a growing body of literature on mobile learning, scant attention has been directed towards understanding the unique factors influencing educational institutions in developing countries [14]. Particularly lacking are investigations into the facilitators and barriers to effective adoption of mobile learning programs in these contexts, with limited insights available on the reception of such initiatives in African developing countries like Sudan. Research within the African context, particularly in Sudan, is essential due to unique challenges such as resource constraints, limited technology access, and unreliable internet connectivity. Cultural and social factors significantly influence technology adoption, making it imperative to study these dynamics specifically. The mandated shift to mobile learning in Sudan, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, highlights the need to understand factors influencing technology usage behavior. This focused investigation is crucial for developing strategies to effectively leverage mobile learning and improve educational outcomes in Sudan and similar developing countries. Moreover, the impact of factors such as the fear of COVID-19 and social isolation on mobile learning adoption remains unexplored within the context of African developing countries. Understanding the factors influencing mobile learning adoption in developing countries is paramount, given their reliance on conventional pedagogical methods due to resource constraints and a scarcity of skilled professionals [15]. Additionally, Sudan presents a distinct cultural and social backdrop compared to Western nations, where the bulk of research on technology acceptance and usage behavior has been conducted [15]. Importantly, in Sudan, mobile learning is mandated for both students and educators, leading to varying levels of exposure and attitudes towards e-learning within the classroom setting. Given the nascent stage of e-learning adoption in Sudan and the prevailing reliance on traditional pedagogy in higher education institutions, it becomes imperative to examine the factors shaping students’ technology usage behavior in developing country contexts [14, 15].

The pandemic prevention measures have led to the emergence of novel emotional states, prompting researchers to explore new psychological dimensions such as social isolation and fear of COVID-19 [16–18]. These variables warrant thorough investigation within the Sudanese context due to their potential influence on attitudes towards mobile learning adoption. Previous research indicates that the fear induced by the pandemic may hinder individuals’ willingness to embrace online learning [19, 20]. However, some studies suggest that social isolation and the perceived fear of COVID-19 can positively influence protective behaviors, thereby fostering societal acceptance of technology [17, 18]. Understanding the factors influencing mobile learning adoption in developing countries like Sudan is crucial due to their reliance on conventional teaching methods, resource constraints, and a shortage of skilled professionals [13]. Sudan’s unique cultural and social context differs significantly from Western nations, which are the focus of most research on technology acceptance [13]. Despite the mandate for mobile learning, there is varying exposure and attitudes towards e-learning among Sudanese students and educators. Given the nascent stage of e-learning in Sudan and the reliance on traditional pedagogy, it is vital to examine the factors shaping technology usage behavior [14, 15]. Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the potential for mobile learning as a protective measure [21], yet there is a lack of research on its use for pandemic protection, particularly incorporating concepts like fear of COVID-19 and social isolation. This study aims to fill this gap by integrating these concepts into the UTAUT framework to assess factors influencing mobile learning adoption among Sudanese medical students, offering theoretical contributions and practical recommendations for improving mobile learning uptake in Sudan and similar contexts [20–25].

Theoretical framework

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) was developed to understand factors influencing technology adoption in workplaces [23]. Mobile learning is the most studied among technology acceptance theories [24]. This theory integrates insights from eight theoretical frameworks, including TAM, TRA, MM, TPB, IDT, MPCU, and SCT. UTAUT identifies four key constructs: PE, EE, SI, and FC, PE relates to perceived job performance improvement, while EE concerns ease of system use, While the UTAUT model has gained widespread acceptance [25], it has been enhanced by the inclusion of additional components [26]. SI gauges peer endorsement importance, and FC assesses organizational and technical support. In their studies [27, 28] found SIS to have significant important role on BI, while [16, 29] found fear of COVID-19 to play significant important role to adopt mobile learning during pandemic. However, UTAUT has been criticized for its narrow focus on end-users, overlooking other stakeholders and interpersonal networks. This study applies the UTAUT framework to examine the determinants of mobile learning adoption among Sudanese students. The effectiveness of a model lies in its simplicity, parsimony, and robustness, qualities for which UTAUT is recognized and widely employed in recent research. This study incorporates social isolation and fear into the model, recognizing their significance in influencing technology adoption [27–29]. Sudan is selected as the research context due to its unique historical, academic, economic, and linguistic characteristics, offering a distinct environment for investigation that diverges fundamentally from other studied contexts [16, 30]. The purpose of this study is to bolster the notion that frameworks and theories from other relevant domains should inform work in medical context studies [31]. Fear of COVID-19 and social isolation have played a big role in pushing people towards adopting mobile learning. The fear of the virus makes people want to avoid in-person interactions, while social isolation creates a strong need to stay connected and keep up with education. These pressures make technology seem more valuable and necessary, driving higher adoption rates. Adding these factors to the UTAUT model helps us understand the human side of why people turn to technology during pandemics.To pinpoint the groundbreaking effect of fear from the disease in a selected educational environment. The study adopted the UTAUT model which has been proved to be widely-spread in research that focuses on technology adoptions and it was regarded as an influential and effective tool in previous studies [32]. Recent studies [7, 8, 33] have made it a well-established. The paper is intended to implement a variant of the UTAUT model and introduce an external factor to the model that will support the research hypotheses. The addition of the fear of COVID- 19, and social isolation sets our model apart from other previous studies and contributes to the novelty of the paper.

Performance expectancy (PE)

Performance expectancy refers to an individual’s perception of the extent to which utilizing a system will enhance their job performance [31, 32, 34]. This variable holds significance within the UTAUT framework, assessing the anticipated improvement in performance while engaging with the information system [35]. In the realm of academic research, performance expectancy can be conceptualized as a student’s optimism regarding the potential of mobile learning to enhance their academic accomplishments [33, 35]. In the context of mobile learning, a learner’s belief that mobile learning can augment their comprehension of course material and offer a more immersive learning experience contributes to their propensity to adopt and persist in using this technology [36]. Both UTAUT1 and UTAUT2 posited that performance expectancy directly influences behavioral intentions to adopt new technologies. Previous investigations have corroborated the association between performance expectancy and usage behavior across various technological platforms [35]. Additionally, research on medical students’ adoption of mobile learning revealed that performance expectancy significantly impacts students’ behavioral intentions [7]. Prior studies about mobile learning found PE to have significance influence on BI [37, 38]. The current investigation aims to examine the subsequent hypothesis:HP1: PE would have a positive influence on the proclivity to accept m-learning.

HP1: PE would have a positive influence on the proclivity to accept m-learning.

Effort expectancy (EE)

Performance expectancy assesses an individual’s perceived level of ease regarding the utilization of technology [32, 39]. This factor is directly influenced by user expectations [36, 37]. Broadly speaking, EE pertains to the user’s anticipation of ease and the availability of various amenities without significant exertion or minimal effort on their part to acquire them [6]. In the setting of his study, EE pertains to the extent to which a student perceives using mobile learning as requiring minimal effort. EE is wildly acknowledged as a key determinant of behavioral intention [40]. Previous research examining the intention to adopt new methods or technology has suggested that EE significantly influences users’ behavioral intentions [37, 41, 42]. The current investigation aims to examine the subsequent hypothesis:HP2: EE has a positive influence on students BI.

HP2: EE has a positive influence on students BI.

Social influence (SI)

Social influence (SI) pertains to a student’s opinion of the beliefs held by others regarding their utilization of mobile learning [35, 43, 44]. SI is constructed as the external social pressures surrounding individuals, potentially influencing their behavior during specific engagements [23]. Research conducted by [40, 45] indicated that SI within the UTAUT framework positively affects students’ intentions to utilize mobile learning. Numerous scholarly inquiries have demonstrated that SI significantly impacts the behavioral intentions to adopt mobile learning [27, 46, 47]. Consequently, the following hypothesis is posited in this inquiry:HP3: SI has a positive influence on learners’ BI.

HP3: SI has a positive influence on learners’ BI.

Facilitating conditions (FC)

In the context of this research, facilitating conditions pertain to the presence of technical resources and infrastructure essential for supporting the utilization of technology [32, 38]. Specifically, in this study, facilitating conditions are defined as the perceived availability of tools and resources by medical students to facilitate their engagement with mobile learning. This construct is considered a direct determinant of behavioral intention [48–51]. Building upon previous findings, it is hypothesized that an abundance of tools and resources would lead to an increase in medical students; adoption of mobile learning, thereby enhancing their confidence in utilizing these tools proficiently. The current investigation aims to examine the subsequent hypothesis:HP4: FC has a positive influence on BI.

HP4: FC has a positive influence on BI.

Social isolation (SIS)

Social isolation, defined as limited engagement in meaningful social connections [27], is anticipated to become a significant risk factor during quarantine situations, similar to social distance [52, 53]. The COVID-19 pandemic has necessitated lockdown measures and reduced social gatherings to curb the virus spread [27], leading to social isolation due to the closure of educational institutions and public venues, and the cancellation of events [69. This study posits that individuals experiencing social isolation may show increased interest in informal mobile learning. The conceptual underpinnings of mobile learning adoption in this study are based on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which includes performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. The pandemic-induced social isolation drives the need for mobile learning as a means to maintain education and social interaction despite physical restrictions. By integrating social isolation and mobile learning adoption within the UTAUT framework, this study aims to understand the factors influencing mobile learning among Sudanese medical students, providing insights for enhancing mobile learning in similar developing country contexts. The current investigation aims to examine the subsequent hypothesis:HP6: SIS has a positive impact on BI.

HP6: SIS has a positive impact on BI.

The effect of corona fear (CF)

According to [50], fear is an adaptive emotion that drives individuals to address potential threats. Unprecedented events like epidemics can evoke feelings of dread, representing a psychological variable during the COVID-19 pandemic [51]. Fear has disrupted educational institutions, impeding teaching and learning activities and significantly influencing perceptions of e-learning [17, 29]. It manifests in various forms, including fear of insecurity, fear of missing out, and fear of experimentation, such as trying new activities [48, 49]. During the COVID-19 crisis, fear may impact the acceptance of innovation, as many educational institutions turn to distance learning to mitigate risks. The widespread prevalence of COVID-19 has instilled heightened levels of terror, profoundly affecting the educational landscape [29, 50]. This underscores the importance of assessing fear’s influence on learners, particularly concerning the acceptance and utilization of mobile learning in academic settings, to sustain educational continuity. The conceptual underpinnings of mobile learning adoption in this study are grounded in the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). The fear of COVID-19 influences these constructs by driving the urgency to adopt mobile learning as a safer educational alternative. Understanding how fear impacts technology acceptance is crucial for developing strategies to enhance mobile learning adoption among Sudanese medical students and maintaining educational continuity during crises. Methodology

Study design

The primary aim of this study is to scrutinize the determinants influencing students’ inclination towards adopting mobile online learning in Sudan. Sudan was selected as the focal point of this investigation due to the absence of prior studies probing students’ intentions regarding mobile learning adoption in this locale. Additionally, no research employing the UTAUT framework has been conducted in the Sudanese context, indicating a research gap in this area, particularly within developing nations [51, 52]. This study endeavors to contribute to the scholarly discourse on mobile learning adoption across diverse settings.

Participant

To make sure they met the requirements of the study, participants were chosen based on four inclusion criteria and five exclusion criteria. Requirements for inclusion: willingness to engage, current enrolment in a medical colleges, and mobile learning users who are at least 18 years old. Students who are not enrolled in a medical course or program at a medical college in Sudan are excluded. Participants in the study were junior college, undergraduate from Al-Razi University, University of Technology, and Meroe University.

Sample of the study

The study cohort consisted of students enrolled in various disciplines at Sudanese universities during the COVID-19 quarantine period. The sample comprised 344 female students and 155 male students, all of whom possessed prior experience with online course delivery. Table 1 presents an overview of the sample demographics. In light of the pandemic restrictions, data collection was carried out online, with participation being entirely voluntary. Prior to survey dissemination, a pilot study involving 22 mobile learning users and six professionals in the mobile learning domain was conducted to ensure the content and face validity of the questionnaire [54]. Based on the findings of the pilot study and expert feedback, minor revisions were made to the survey instrument. It is imperative to note that all participants voluntarily consented to partake in the study after being briefed on its primary objectives. Ethical clearance was obtained, and participants were duly informed of their right to abstain from completing the survey or withdraw from the study at any stage of the data collection process. The online questionnaire was designed to take approximately 10–15 min to complete. Data analysis was performed using Partial Least Squares (PLS) and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) techniques. The tool for data collection

A preliminary study was conducted with 35 Sudanese medical university students who were in their 2nd to 4th year of study and three mobile learning specialists to attain face validity and content validity, The Cronbach’s alpha was used to measure the internal dependability of the constructions’ elements, a reliability coefficient of 0.70 or above are considered to be acceptable. The purpose of the study was to evaluate the level of clarity of the instrument being used. Following the pilot, certain modifications were implemented to the phrasing of the questionnaire items. We gathered the metadata from March 11, 2020, to January 5, 2021, by distributing self-administered online surveys. A total of 522 students received surveys. The analysis included 499 surveys as a result. [55] States that 354 is the bare minimum sample size for quantitative research. Consequently, the study’s sample size is adequate. The suggested items were changed based on research done by [23] on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). These changes were made to fit the needs of mobile learning adoption. We assessed PE, EE, BI, SIS, FC, SI, and CF using three items for each, and evaluated facilitating conditions using four items. We adapted these constructs from a variety of sources [54–59]. Table 2 provides the measurement instrument. Furthermore, we evaluated all components using a five-point Likert scale, which ranged from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5). Participant demographic variables, including age, gender, experience, and devices used for mobile learning, were also obtained. The SmartPLS tool was utilised for the structural equation modelling (SEM) data analysis. Data analysis

The structural model underwent examination employing the Partial Least Squares (PLS) technique because it provides an accurate result, and robustness in handling a sample size of 499, and capability to manage complex models. It is recognized as a potent statistical method within component-based structural equation modeling. As elucidated by [64], PLS yields parameter estimates that optimize the explained variance of dependent constructs and support objectives oriented towards prediction in the structural model. Moreover, it possesses the capability to accommodate small sample sizes, intricate models, and non-normal data distributions, as articulated by Leguina (2015). Within studies employing the UTAUT model, such as those conducted by [31, 63, 64], PLS emerges as a favored methodology for scrutinizing the relationships between constructs to validate research hypotheses. The analysis was conducted utilizing the SmartPLS 4 software.

Results

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) served as the analytical tool to scrutinize the adequacy of the model. The validation process encompassed assessments of convergent and discriminant validity, elucidated in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. We evaluated convergent validity, as defined by [65], by analyzing the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values for each construct. To assert convergent validity for the questionnaire, the AVE values of individual components ought to exceed 0.5. We commonly use composite reliability (CR) to assess internal consistency, assigning a value of 0.70 or higher to each latent variable. As indicated in Table 5, none of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values for the constructs fell below 0.5. Following the steps described in [65, 66], discriminant validity was checked by comparing the AVE values with the squared correlations between the constructs. An analysis is conducted on the loadings of individual items on their respective latent variables, focusing on standardized loadings of 0.50 or greater, ideally exceeding 0.70, and ensuring statistical significance for all items (see Table 6). All constructs met the stipulated criteria. Additionally, this study encountered no issues with multicollinearity, as evidenced by Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values well below the recommended threshold of 10 [63, 67]. Each construct’s VIF score fell within the acceptable range of 3.3 to 5, as suggested by [68]. Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to ascertain the factors influencing the inclination of Sudanese undergraduate students towards embracing mobile learning. Performance expectancy (PE) emerged as a pivotal determinant facilitating the adoption of mobile learning in Sudan, a finding supported by [17, 28, 63, 66, 67]. This result can be attributed to several factors, Firstly, PE reflects the perceived belief among students that utilizing mobile learning technology will enhance their academic performance. Given the unprecedented challenges posed by the pandemic, students view mobile learning as a practical solution to maintain educational continuity while adhering to social distancing measures. Additionally, the accessibility and flexibility afforded by mobile learning platforms during crises contribute to heightened expectations regarding their performance benefits. Moreover, the proliferation of digital resources tailored to medical education may further amplify students’ confidence in the effectiveness of mobile learning, reinforcing the association between PE and BI to adopt mobile learning among medical students in Sudan.

The findings indicate a positive association between EE and BI, corroborating [64, 66–69]. Unlike the findings of [70, 71], the significant relationship between EE and BI among Sudanese medical students during the COVID-19 pandemic is influenced by several key factors. In a developing country like Sudan, where access to traditional educational resources is limited to big cities and towns, the perceived ease of use of mobile learning platforms becomes crucial. EE reflects students’ perception of how easily they can use these technologies. During the pandemic, rapid adaptation to remote learning environments increased the importance of user-friendly mobile learning interfaces. Additionally, the convenience and accessibility of mobile learning further reinforce medical students’ intention to adopt these technologies for educational purposes. SI emerges as another influential factor in mobile learning adoption, consistent with the findings of [28, 72, 73], but it contradicts [74]. Therefore, SI reflects the impact of peers, instructors, and societal norms on individuals’ decisions to adopt new technologies. In the context of Sudan, where communal and social interactions play a significant role, the endorsement and encouragement from peers and instructors regarding mobile learning can greatly influence students’ perceptions and attitudes towards its adoption. Additionally, during the pandemic, when face-to-face interactions are limited, the reliance on social networks and peer recommendations for educational support and guidance may be heightened, further accentuating the influence of social factors on students’ behavioral intentions. However, the contradictory findings observed by [74] may stem from differences in sample demographics, cultural contexts, or methodological approaches, underscoring the complexity of SI dynamics in mobile learning adoption among medical students. Moreover, the construct of FC is deemed crucial in the adoption of mobile learning, aligning with the observations of [75, 76]. Hence, mobile learning providers must address technical support issues and offer training to facilitate students’ interaction with mobile learning platforms. Additionally, ensuring access to free and reliable wireless networks is imperative for institutions to support mobile learning initiatives effectively. Authorities can play a pivotal role in this regard by providing students with convenient and reliable internet access. The results indicate a favorable association between SIS and the acceptance of mobile learning, aligning with the findings of [28, 77]. Notably, SIS emerged as a significant predictor within this study, suggesting that students experiencing greater levels of isolation, coupled with robust independent learning capabilities, are more inclined to embrace mobile learning as a means to acquire knowledge and accomplish course objectives compared to their counterparts in less isolated social contexts [27, 59, 78]. This unique aspect underscores the study’s innovative contribution within the Sudanese educational landscape, particularly underscored by the backdrop of the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, CF emerges as a significant determinant of students’ intention to adopt mobile learning during the pandemic-induced quarantine, consistent with findings from [29, 56, 74]. The integration of mobile learning serves to alleviate fears surrounding pandemics, as evidenced by the predominant role of CF as a primary driver of students’ behavioral intention to adopt mobile learning during the pandemic period. Additionally, the results of the integrated path model analysis underscore the pivotal role of perceived COVID-19 fear as a primary determinant of students’ behavioral intention to utilize mobile learning during this challenging period. Theoretical Implications

The results of this study suggest that most of the key effects proposed by the UTAUT are applicable to mobile learning, which indicates the validity of employing UTAUT, the current research is one of the pioneer empirical studies that identified the social isolation (i.e., during COVID-19 or any confined situation) as a situational variable and fear of COVID-19 as a psychological variable which influence medical students’ behavioral intention toward mobile learning adoption. Moreover, the current literature on mobile learning mainly ignored the influence of the situational variable on students’ mobile-based learning usage intention in pandemic times. The utilization of m-learning by medical students was predicted by this study using all of the UTAUT components with strong support. Additionally, the results of this study add to the body of published literature by integrating two distinct factors (social isolation and COVID-19 fear) and enhancing our understanding of the use of m-learning among medical students. It’s interesting to note that the impact of SIS and CF on BI is a recent discovery adding to the body of knowledge on mobile learning among medical students.

Practical Implications

The research provided some practical implications for mobile learning practitioners. The practitioners may understand the main factors influencing and affecting the intention to adopt mobile learning and the acceptance during the pandemic period. The findings of this study have practical implications on medical students’ strategic plan for mobile learning. Firstly, the findings showed that there is a significant positive relationship between EE, CF, PE, IS, SIS and behavioral intention, so the higher education institutes should conduct needs assessments of students when designing mobile learning (e.g. a stable and mature online learning platform). Secondly, social influence plays an important role to affect behavioral intention in our learning context that was found, thus systematic studies of mobile learning in developing countries contexts are required to encourage teachers and students to actively try to use mobile learning to explore the mode of mobile learning suitable for their own disciplines with the aim to foster an online learning culture among students. Thirdly we found that facilitating conditions have a significant positive influence on medical students’ behavioral intention, therefor the system should provide a convenient channel for feedbacks on curriculum and technical issues, Teachers should provide online learning guidance and respond to students’ curriculum problems timely and effectively while the information department should establish an information technology service platform with professional staff dedicated to provide students with technical support which will help learners concentrate on online learning rather than on solving technical issues. Fourthly, the results showed that there is no significant relationship between behavioral intention and students’ use behavior which is different from most previous studies, so in mandatory blended learning environments where teachers need to play the leading role of guides to encourage students to display their subjective initiative and help them immerse themselves in online learning.

Conclusion

During the epidemic era, mobile learning adoption was seen as inevitable. Therefore, it is important to assess how well students accept mobile learning, specifically in Sudan. To achieve the study’s main goal, we used the UTAUT model of EE, PE, SI, as well as FC and BI, in addition to CF and SIS, as innovative constructs that have not been much investigated. Our study also examined how 499 students used mobile learning as part of the extended UTAUT. The findings show that PE, EE, SI, FC, SIS, and CF have a positive impact on students’ behaviour intention to adopt mobile learning. The sample results validate the conceptual and empirical value of the UTAUT framework in investigating the acceptance of m-learning. Overall, the findings indicate that most of the participants had mainly positive reactions to the constructs evaluated in the conceptual model. In our research, we found that learners are willing and ready to use mobile learning to help them with their education. The findings demonstrate that SI, PE, EE, FC, SIS, and CF, have all been demonstrated to have an impact on the adoption of mobile learning in Sudan.

Limitations

In acknowledging the limitations of our study, we recognize that the data collection occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced students’ perceptions and intentions regarding mobile learning. This unique context could introduce biases that affect the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, our study focused on a specific set of variables, and there is a need for further research to explore additional factors and moderator effects that could impact mobile learning adoption. We recommend that future studies incorporate a broader range of variables and employ mixed-methods approaches to provide a more comprehensive understanding of these dynamics. Furthermore, conducting a longitudinal study of mobile learning in a post-COVID-19 context would be beneficial to observe changes over time and assess the long-term impact of the pandemic on mobile learning adoption.
