Integrated hypoxia and metabolic gene signature to predict clinical outcomes and therapeutic vulnerabilities in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yuquan Ma, Mengmeng Yang, Zhitao Sun, Fei Liu, Boying Zheng, Yanwei Wang, Junfeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the therapeutic landscape of advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet ~50% of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients exhibit primary or acquired resistance, underscoring the urgent need for predictive biomarkers. Hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming (HMR) are intertwined oncogenic hallmarks that reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) and drive immune evasion, representing promising targets for signature development. Herein, we constructed an integrated HMR gene signature to predict clinical outcomes and therapeutic vulnerabilities in LUAD.MethodsTranscriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were analyzed. Hypoxia and energy metabolism pathways were scored via single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), and subtypes were stratified. A 9-gene prognostic model was constructed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and validated across cohorts. Single-cell RNA (scRNA) data and in-house experimental validation explored associations between gene expression, immune microenvironment, and treatment outcomes.ResultsWe developed a 9-gene HMR signature (RHOV, CYP4B1, KRT6A, TNS4, HMGA2, FIGF, SFTPB, TMPRSS11E, GRIA1) incorporating hypoxia and energy metabolism pathway activity. This signature robustly stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct overall survival [OS; hazard ratio (HR) =2.24, P<0.001]. High-risk patients exhibited enhanced immune evasion, characterized by elevated Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores (P<0.001) and a suppressed antitumor immune landscape, whereas distinct immune checkpoint expression patterns and subtype-specific drug sensitivities were observed between risk groups. scRNA sequencing revealed RHOV and TNS4 co-expressed with exhaustion markers (e.g., FOXP3, BTLA) and were enriched in non-major pathological response (non-MPR) patients. Validation in 39 ICI-treated NSCLC patients via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and immunofluorescence (IF) confirmed that RHOV+ regulatory T cells and TNS4+ exhausted T cells were more abundant in poor responders.ConclusionsOverall, our integrated HMR signature exhibits robust prognostic and predictive value, unravels TME-driven ICI resistance mechanisms, and identifies subtype-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities. This tool has potential for clinical application, as it links HMR to clinical outcomes and provides novel insights to guide precision treatment strategies for LUAD patients.Keywords: Hypoxia, energy metabolism, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), immunotherapy, prognostic signature

Highlight box

Key findings

• A novel 9-gene hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming (HMR) signature was established, which stratifies lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients into distinct risk groups with significantly different survival outcomes and immune profiles.

• High-risk patients exhibited enhanced immune evasion, elevated tumor immune dysfunction and rejection scores, and distinct immune checkpoint expression patterns.

• Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) validation in 39 immunotherapy-treated patients confirmed that RHOV and TNS4 co-expression with exhaustion markers was enriched in poor responders.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior studies have linked tumor hypoxia or metabolic reprogramming individually to immunosuppression and immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) resistance, yet these factors have rarely been integrated into a unified prognostic framework.

• This study constructs the first integrated HMR signature combining both pathways, demonstrating independent prognostic value and predictive utility for ICI response, with mechanistic validation through single-cell transcriptomics and an independent clinical cohort.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• LUAD patients with elevated HMR signature scores should be regarded as a high-risk group for immunotherapy resistance, and treatment planning could consider hypoxia-metabolic profiling. A promising direction emerging is the combination of hypoxia-metabolism modulation with ICIs for LUAD.

Introduction

Lung cancer results in millions of lost lives each year (1). In recent years, the therapeutic paradigm for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has undergone substantial transformation, largely driven by the broad implementation of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Their use has markedly improved patient outcomes, translating into a rise in the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate from 8.5% to 21.9% (2). However, a significant limitation is that around 50% of NSCLC patients derive no benefit from ICI therapy; this non-responsiveness leads to poor prognosis and fuels the critical demand for predictive biomarkers to guide patient selection (3,4). Moreover, the primary mechanisms underlying resistance to ICIs remain poorly understood, limiting the optimization of treatment strategies.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a dynamic assembly of multiple components, which comprises endothelial cells, immune cells, and stromal fibroblasts, along with a rich array of cytokines and extracellular matrix components (5,6). These elements collectively influence the antitumor functions of immune cells, particularly through interactions with stromal cells, the degree of hypoxia, and harmful metabolite accumulation (7-9). Hypoxia is recognized as a major driver of resistance to immunotherapy (10,11). Hypoxia triggers immune evasion and is often associated with the metabolic reprogramming of TME cells and the efflux of acidic metabolites, collectively impairing antitumor immune function (12-14).

Extensive studies have demonstrated that immune checkpoint pathway activity is significantly influenced by energy metabolism and hypoxia (10,15,16). Consequently, combining metabolic inhibitors or hypoxia-targeted therapies with ICIs represents a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy (17-20). However, although numerous studies have independently examined the roles of energy metabolism and hypoxia in ICI response, integrated analyses exploring their combined impact are limited. Based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with hypoxia and energy metabolism, we aimed to develop a prognostic signature for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). By validating this signature in training and test cohorts, we stratified patients into different risk groups and analyzed the enriched biological pathways distinguishing these groups. Then, we investigated the association between the risk score and immune cell infiltration in the TME and assessed its potential to predict immunotherapy outcomes. Furthermore, to facilitate clinical application, we constructed a clinical nomogram that integrates the risk score with other clinical features to predict survival probabilities for LUAD. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of LUAD biology and provides new insights into personalized clinical treatment strategies. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1370/rc).

Methods

Data acquisition

Transcriptomic data (568 LUAD samples, including 510 tumors) and clinical data (497 tumors) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and UCSC Xena platform, respectively. In addition, RNA microarray and survival data were drawn from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), including the GSE31210 (N=226), GSE72094 (N=398), and GSE135222 (N=28) datasets, to validate the findings of the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Moreover, single-cell RNA (scRNA) sequencing data (34 LUAD tumors, GSE176021) were downloaded to investigate the association between model features and immunotherapy efficacy. All eligible patients with complete data were included from the above database to maximize statistical power and generalizability. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis.

Hypoxia and energy metabolism-related (HEMR) subtype identification

Genes associated with energy metabolism and hypoxia pathways were retrieved from the reactome pathway and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) databases. The single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) scoring method was used to assess the enrichment scores of these two pathways.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were generated to evaluate differences in OS among groups categorized by the median ssGSEA score: low hypoxia-low energy metabolism (LL), high hypoxia-low energy metabolism (HL), low hypoxia-high energy metabolism (LH), and high hypoxia-high energy metabolism (HH). Differential expression analysis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort between the HL and LH groups, which exhibited the most significant differences in survival curves, was performed exclusively using the “limma” R package (version 3.54.2) (21). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted in the TCGA-LUAD cohort via the “clusterProfiler” R package (version 4.6.2) (22). Additionally, differential abundance (DA) and GSEA analyses were performed on the TCGA-LUAD cohort to assess the activity levels of the enriched pathways. Further evaluation of changes in key biological processes (BPs) in the two subgroups was conducted in the TCGA-LUAD cohort using ssGSEA scoring of cell death pathways (23) and metabolic and cancer-related pathways in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. As no cross-platform integration was performed, batch effect correction across the datasets was not required.

Prognostic risk score model generation

We selected the intersection of the hypoxia-related gene set (HALLMARK_HYPOXIA; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/cards/HALLMARK_HYPOXIA) and the energy metabolism-related gene set (Reactome: R-HSA-163685; https://reactome.org/content/detail/R-HSA-163685) as the candidate gene set. The hypoxia-related gene set and the energy metabolism-related gene set were shown in Table S1. Tumor-specific DEGs were identified by comparing HL and LH groups, and 9 signature genes were selected via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. A prognostic forest plot was generated. Patients were categorized into low- and high-risk subgroups based on the median risk score. OS data from TCGA-LUAD, GSE31210, and GSE72094 cohorts were used to assess the prognostic performance of the risk model. KM and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the “survival” (version 3.3.1) and “timeROC” (version 0.4) R packages (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Finally, univariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to assess the association between OS and clinical parameters. Variables showing significant association with survival in the univariate analysis (P<0.05) were subsequently subjected to multivariate Cox regression to determine independent prognostic factors.

Nomogram construction and survival analysis

A predictive nomogram for patient survival at 1, 3, and 5 years was developed with the “rms” (version 6.7.0) R package.

Estimation of tumor immune microenvironment infiltration and potential implications for immunotherapy

The Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm within the “IOBR” R package (version 0.99.9) was applied to quantify immune cell infiltration levels (24). Additionally, the ssGSEA algorithm was used to score immune-related pathways, based on previously published gene sets (GSEA). A comparative analysis of 65 immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) was performed between different risk groups. Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores were subsequently computed to assess the potential clinical relevance for immunotherapy. The performance of the risk model and its constituent genes was validated using an independent GSE135222 dataset.

Drug sensitivity prediction

Drug response was evaluated by estimating the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of commonly used chemotherapeutic and targeted agents in both risk groups using the “prorophetic” R package (version 0.5). Differences in drug sensitivity between the groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

scRNA sequencing data processing and analysis

Data processing was executed via the Seurat toolkit (version 5.1.0) (25). The quality control criteria were as follows: Cells with >200 detected genes were retained, whereas those with >10% mitochondrial genes, >50% ribosomal genes, >1% hemoglobin genes, <500 total reads, or genes per unique molecular identifier (UMI) <0.8 were excluded. The DoubletFinder package (version 2.0.4) was employed for doublet detection and removal (26). Data were normalized with LogNormalize using the NormalizeData function, centered, and scaled before principal component analysis (PCA).

Batch effects between samples were mitigated through the Harmony package (version 1.2.3), integrated via the RunHarmony function with parameters set as theta =1, lambda =0.5, max.iter =20, and early_stop =−Inf. A shared-nearest neighbor (SNN) graph was constructed using the FindNeighbors function, with parameters n.neighbors =40, min.dist =0.5, and dims =1:30. The RunUMAP function (dims =1:30, n.components =2, n.neighbors =40, and min.dist =0.3) was applied to perform uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction. Cell clustering was conducted using FindClusters, applying a resolution of 0.5 for all T cells and 0.6 for CD4+ T cells. After removing the contaminating myeloid and B cells, 184,250 cells were retained. Cell subpopulations were annotated using marker genes from the CellMarker database (version 2.0).

Tissue sample collection, RNA purification, and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Between 8 May 2016 and 25 July 2024, patients with LUAD who underwent surgical resection with lymph node dissection at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University were enrolled in this retrospective study. Tumor tissues were collected at the time of surgery and archived as formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples in the Department of Pathology. A total of 39 patients were included in the present analysis. Patients were eligible if archived FFPE tumor tissue samples and complete clinical and follow-up data were available. Among the included patients, some received immunotherapy as part of routine clinical management. Clinical and pathological data were obtained from the institutional medical record system. The median follow-up time was over 18 months. Progressive disease (PD) was defined as recurrence or distant metastasis confirmed by imaging or histology, assessed from surgery to the first event. Progression events were confirmed by multidisciplinary tumor board review, including at least two radiologists and one thoracic surgeon or oncologist, with final consensus required for all PD determinations. OS was defined as the time from surgical resection to death from any cause. Patient characteristics are shown in Table S2. Patients were stratified into good (non-PD, N=20) and poor (PD, N=19) response groups based on immunotherapy outcomes. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (No. 2023KS123). As the data were de-identified, individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Total RNA was isolated from tissues using an RNA extraction kit (# R1001, Sinogene, Beijing, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was then synthesized from the RNA via reverse transcription, employing the Thermo First cDNA Synthesis Kit (SinoGene, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. qRT-PCR was performed using 2× SG Green qPCR Mix (with ROX) (SinoGene, China) to quantify messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels. The primer sequences for the 9 genes analyzed (and one internal control gene, ACTB) are provided in Table S3.

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

For IF analysis, we deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections (5 μm). After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, we blocked nonspecific binding using 10% goat serum and 5% fetal bovine serum in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) at room temperature for 2 hours. Primary antibodies against TNS4 (1:5,000, BSM-60276R, Bioss, Woburn, MA, USA), CD3 (1:5,000, 60181-1-Ig, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), BTLA (1:2,000, BS-0624R, Bioss), FOXP3 (1:400, 22228-1-AP, Proteintech), or RHOV (1:500, sc-515072, Santa Cruz Biotech, Dallas, TX, USA) were incubated with the sections at 4 ℃ overnight. Secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor™ 488 or Cy3 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. A Nikon Eclipse C1 fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was used to capture fluorescence images after the samples were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Images were captured on a Pannoramic MIDI scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) and analyzed with the Pannoramic Viewer software.

Statistical analysis

We performed all statistical analyses using R software (version 4.2.0). Group comparisons were carried out with the Wilcoxon test for two groups, or with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)/Kruskal-Wallis tests for multiple groups, whereas Spearman correlation was used to assess associations. A two-sided P value threshold of 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Identification of HEMR subtypes

We first performed ssGSEA to assess the activity levels of HEMR pathways in 1,134 LUAD samples from three cohorts (TCGA, GSE31210, and GSE72094). KM survival analysis revealed that the interaction between HEMR pathways might have opposing effects on prognosis. Specifically, the HL group exhibited the worst prognosis, whereas the LH group exhibited the most favorable survival.

To delineate the molecular characteristics underlying the prognostic disparity between the HL and LH groups (P<0.001; Figure 1A), we identified transcriptomic features distinguishing the two groups (P<0.05; Figure 1B). Functional enrichment analyses, including GO, BP, and GSEA-reactome, were conducted to explore the potential biological functions of the DEGs (Figure 1C,1D). These analyses revealed that DEGs associated with hypoxia and energy metabolism were significantly enriched in pathways involved in immune regulation, metabolism, and apoptosis. Further ssGSEA scoring of KEGG and Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) pathways, including those associated with cell death, cancer, and metabolism, confirmed these findings, highlighting the differences between HL and LH subgroups (Figure S1).

Construction and validation of a risk model

Next, 2,244 DEGs were identified between normal and tumor samples. From these, 138 HEMR DEGs were selected using the same thresholds (Figure 2A). Then, Cox and LASSO regression analyses were performed on the TCGA-LUAD cohort to identify the optimal HEMR genes for constructing a prognostic model. This procedure yielded 9 key genes, including RHOV, CYP4B1, KRT6A, TNS4, HMGA2, FIGF, SFTPB, TMPRSS11E, and GRIA1 (Figure 2B). A prognostic forest plot was generated. From individual gene survival analyses, we identified that high expression of RHOV, KRT6A, TNS4, HMGA2, and TMPRSS11E, and low expression of CYP4B1, FIGF, SFTPB, and GRIA1 were significantly associated with poor prognosis (Figure 2C).

The prognostic risk score was calculated:Risk score = (0.134 × RHOV) + (−0.007 × FIGF) + (−0.018 × SFTPB) + (−0.064 × CYP4B1) + (0.124 × KRT6A) + (0.032 × TNS4) + (−0.037 × GRIA1) + (0.047 × HMGA2) + (0.018 × TMPRSS11E).

We stratified patients into different groups using the median risk score as the threshold (Figure 3A, left). In the TCGA-LUAD training cohort, the high-risk group contained a higher rate of deceased patients relative to the low-risk group (Figure 3B, left). The scatter plots depicted the expression profiles of the nine risk genes for each patient in the low- and high-risk groups (Figure 3C, left). K-M and ROC analyses were conducted to evaluate the prognostic performance and predictive accuracy of the risk model. The prognostic model effectively stratified patients into groups with distinct outcomes, confirmed by a significantly lower OS in the high-risk group (Figure 3D, left) and further evidenced by time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.71 (1-year), 0.69 (3-year), and 0.65 (5-year) (Figure 3E, left).

To further validate the prognostic model, the same analytical approach was applied to the independent validation cohorts GSE31210 and GSE72094, supporting the stability, reliability, and favorable predictive performance of the model (Figure 3A-3E, middle and right).

Clinical value of the prognostic signature

Based on the clinical-pathological features from the TCGA cohort, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Figure 4A,4B). The hazard ratio (HR) derived from the univariate and multivariate Cox models was 2.24 [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.79–2.81; P<0.001] and 1.89 (95% CI: 1.47–2.43; P<0.001), respectively. To facilitate clinical application, we constructed a clinical nomogram that integrates the risk score with other clinical features (for example, clinical stage) to predict survival probabilities for patients with LUAD (Figure 4C). The nomogram demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting short-term survival outcomes. The calibration plots indicated that the nomogram’s predictions displayed a close fit to the actual observed survival rates (Figure 4D). The nomogram yielded an AUC of 0.746 for OS prediction, with AUC values of 0.75, 0.73, and 0.72 for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival predictions, respectively (Figure S2), outperforming the risk model and individual clinical factors (Figure 4E).

Correlations of the TME, immunotherapy, and chemotherapeutic and targeted therapy responses with the risk score signature

Given the critical role of hypoxia and energy metabolism in regulating antitumor immunity, we assessed immune cell infiltration using CIBERSORT in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. The results revealed that mast cells, monocytes, resting dendritic cells, activated natural killer (NK) cells, and plasma cells were significantly enriched in the low-risk group (Figure 5A). We further computed the rank correlation coefficients between the HEMR gene matrix and immune cell matrices, revealing that nearly all prognostically relevant HEMR genes were negatively correlated with CD8+ T cell levels, although some correlations were not statistically significant (Figure 5B). Notably, TNS4, despite being negatively correlated with CD8+ T cell levels, showed a positive correlation with regulatory T cell levels.

Next, based on deconvolution results, we assessed immune-related pathways in GSEA, showing that pathway scores were generally elevated in the low-risk subgroup. The mast cell activation, involved in immune response pathways, revealed the most significant differences, suggesting that the risk score signature is linked to immune response (Figure 5C). In parallel, 29 immune gene sets were quantified using ssGSEA (Figure 5D). The high-risk subgroup displayed diminished immune functions, such as B cells, HLA, NK cells, paraininflammation, Treg, proinflammatory cells, and interferon (IFN) responses.

We also analyzed the expression levels of 65 ICGs between different risk groups (Figure S3A). Figure S3B showed the correlation between the selected HEMR genes and common checkpoints. High-risk patients exhibited increased sensitivity to several drugs, whereas they had higher IC50 values of erlotinib, phenformin, and rapamycin, suggesting that these drugs may possess greater efficacy in the low-risk group (Figure S3C). A heatmap of differentially expressed ICGs revealed that most immune cell-driven ICGs (IC-ICGs) were markedly upregulated in the low-risk group, whereas tumor cell-driven ICGs (TC-ICGs), including CD276 and PVR, were predominantly expressed in the high-risk group (Figure 6A). Additionally, we assessed immune responses, including TIDE. The results revealed significant differences between the two groups, with lower TIDE and exclusion scores in low-risk patients, and lower dysfunction scores in high-risk patients. Notably, risk scores were positively correlated with TIDE scores, suggesting that low-risk patients may exhibit a higher likelihood of favorable responses to immunotherapy (Figure 6B-6F).

Furthermore, the predictive efficacy of the risk score and its associated HEMR genes was further validated in an independent external cohort of 28 NSCLC patients treated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) therapy (GSE135222) (Figure 6G,6H and Figure S4). Consistent with our previous findings, high-risk patients had lower survival, with 79% of patients experiencing PD (Figure S4).

Correlation of risk score signature with single-cell characteristics

To better understand the connection between model features and immunotherapy efficacy, we used a single-cell dataset to characterize neoantigen-specific T cells with distinct transcriptional programs in patients with LUAD who received neoadjuvant immunotherapy. This analysis aimed to explore the potential relationship between HEMR genes and T cells under immunotherapy. Following the official Seurat tutorial, we performed normalization, batch effect removal, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, which identified 10 T cell subpopulations (Figure 7A). Next, we examined the expression of the model feature genes within each T cell subpopulation. RHOV was predominantly expressed in FOXP3+ CD4-Tregs, whereas TNS4 and SFTPB were co-expressed in ZNF683+ CD8 T cells (Figure 7B). Additionally, we assessed the expression of exhaustion-related genes across T cell subsets. RHOV exhibited co-expression with FOXP3, CTLA4, and TIGIT in FOXP3+ CD4-Tregs, whereas TNS4 co-expressed with CTLA4, PDCD1, TIGIT, and BTLA in BTLA+ CD4-exhausted T cells. UMAP plots illustrating the distribution of these marker genes are presented in Figure 7C-7E. Meanwhile, we also examined the expression of the model signature genes in the total cell population (Figure S6).

To explore the association between key gene expression and immunotherapy efficacy, patients with major pathological response (MPR) and those with non-MPR were compared for the expression levels of RHOV, TNS4, and SFTPB in T cell subpopulations. RHOV and TNS4 exhibited significantly higher expression in the non-MPR group (Figure 7F), suggesting that these genes may contribute to T cell exhaustion and poor response to immunotherapy.

Validation of the expression of HEMR genes under different immunotherapy responses in LUAD

To validate the association between the expression levels of the HEMR genes and immunotherapy efficacy, we retrospectively collected surgical tissue specimens from 39 patients with NSCLC (including 21 with LUAD) who had received immunotherapy. Patients were categorized into a good response group (non-PD, N=20) and a poor response group (PD, N=19) based on their immunotherapy outcomes. Before this clinical validation, we analyzed the expression of these HEMR genes in different model risk groups and LUAD tissues from the TCGA dataset. As expected, RHOV, KRT6A, TNS4, HMGA2, and TMPRSS11E were significantly overexpressed in the high-risk group and tumor tissues (P<0.001). Conversely, CYP4B1, FIGF, SFTPB, and GRIA1 were significantly under-expressed in the high-risk group and in the tumor tissues (P<0.001, Figure 8A,8B).

Next, we examined the differential expression of model feature genes within different immunotherapy responders. In the LUAD group, RHOV (P=0.17), FIGF (P=0.07), CYP4B1 (P=0.08), KRT6A (P=0.07), TNS4 (P=0.01), HMGA2 (P=0.08), and TMPRSS11E (P=0.02) exhibited elevated expression in the poor response group, while SFTPB (P=0.59) and GRIA1 (P=0.08) were more highly expressed in the good response group (Figure 8C-8K). Similarly, more significant findings were observed in the overall NSCLC cohort. The elevated expression of RHOV and TNS4 in the poor response group further supports our earlier conclusions.

We examined T cell infiltration in clinical tissue samples from LUAD patients with varying responses to anti-PD-1 therapy, aiming to elucidate the regulatory functions of RHOV and TNS4 within the tumor immune microenvironment. IF analysis revealed significantly increased RHOV+ regulatory T cell levels in tumors from poor responders compared to good responders. Similarly, TNS4+ exhausted T cells were more abundant in poor responders (Figure 8L,8M). Collectively, these results indicate that increased RHOV and TNS4 expression may drive infiltration of regulatory or exhausted T cells into tumor tissues, potentially impairing immunotherapy efficacy.

Discussion

Based on HEMR-related genes, we developed a model to predict outcomes and potential immunotherapy responses in patients with LUAD. Our findings highlight the critical role of TME characteristics, particularly hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming (HMR), in influencing immune responses and treatment efficacy. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into LUAD biology and candidate biomarkers and inform the development of personalized treatment strategies.

First, we identified two distinct LUAD subtypes characterized by differential activity of hypoxia and energy metabolism pathways. Based on their expression profiles of hypoxia and metabolism-related genes, LUAD patients were stratified into distinct risk groups. The 9-gene signature (RHOV, CYP4B1, KRT6A, TNS4, HMGA2, FIGF, SFTPB, TMPRSS11E, and GRIA1) demonstrated a robust prognostic performance across multiple cohorts. High expression of genes, including RHOV, KRT6A, and TNS4, was associated with poor clinical outcomes, whereas genes such as SFTPB and CYP4B1 were correlated with favorable survival. Notably, the prognostic relevance of these genes is supported by their known biological functions. For instance, RHOV is associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis (27), whereas HMGA2 drives oncogenic signaling and immune suppression (28-30).

Additionally, the genes in the HMR signature exhibit differential levels of association with hypoxia and glycolytic metabolism. HMGA2 is a direct HIF1α target that promotes glycolysis while suppressing oxidative phosphorylation (31). KRT6A enhances the pentose phosphate pathway via MYCdriven G6PD upregulation (32). TNS4 facilitates aerobic glycolysis through β-catenin/c‑Myc signaling (33). CYP4B1 correlates with HIF‑1α and has been incorporated into hypoxiarelated prognostic models (34,35). FIGF, a VEGF family member, is a downstream target of HIF‑1α and may support glycolysis through hypoxia‑driven angiogenesis. TMPRSS11E engages an EGFRSTAT3 feedback loop, both known regulators of metabolic reprogramming (36). RHOV encodes a Rho family GTPase involved in cytoskeletal reorganization and cell motility. Its role in hypoxia-induced cellular adaptation remains to be experimentally validated. SFTPB is a lung-specific surfactant protein synthesized by alveolar type II cells, essential for surfactant lipid packaging and surface tension reduction. Its dysregulation reflects alveolar epithelial dysfunction. GRIA1 encodes the GluA1 subunit of the AMPA receptor. While GRIA2/3 are direct HIF-1α targets, GRIA1 may be indirectly modulated by hypoxia-induced glutamate release in the TME. Although evidence varies among individual genes, the risk score derived from all nine genes robustly enriches hypoxia, glycolysis, and energy metabolism pathways in GSEA and KEGG analyses (Figure 2C,2D).

A comparative analysis between risk groups exhibited significant differences in their immune cell infiltration landscapes. The low-risk group exhibited higher infiltration of mast cells, monocytes, and activated NK cells, which are associated with enhanced antitumor immunity. Conversely, the high-risk group exhibited elevated levels of myeloid dendritic cell cytokine production and inflammation, which may be associated with pro-tumor inflammation and immunosuppression. The differential expression of ICGs further highlighted this distinction. Higher risk scores were associated with increased expression of tumor-intrinsic immune checkpoints such as CD276 and PVR (37-39), which could promote immune escape within the TME. Supported by our data and previous studies (40,41), hypoxia appears to fuel immune escape by reshaping tumor metabolism in a way that cripples immune cells. For instance, hypoxia can induce lactate and other acidic metabolite accumulation, creating a hostile environment for cytotoxic T and NK cells (42-44).

Furthermore, hypoxia-inducible factors can upregulate ICGs, thereby reinforcing immune resistance (45,46). Our study extends these insights by associating specific HEMR genes with immune cell infiltration patterns, although these findings represent correlative observations rather than direct, strong mechanistic evidence. Nevertheless, experimental validation is required to establish causality, and these findings define testable hypotheses for TME remodeling.

The heterogeneous response to ICIs in NSCLC creates a pressing need for predictive biomarkers. Our risk model was associated with multiple immunotherapy-related indicators in the retrospective datasets. Specifically, low-risk patients exhibited lower TIDE scores and higher immune activity, suggesting that they may benefit more from ICI therapy. Conversely, high-risk patients revealed elevated TIDE scores, indicative of immune dysfunction and exclusion, which are hallmarks of poor ICI response. These results were further validated in the independent GSE135222 cohort NSCLC receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, in which high-risk patients exhibited lower survival rates and a higher likelihood of disease progression. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis further elucidated the relationship between key signature genes and T cell exhaustion. RHOV and TNS4 were predominantly expressed in exhausted T cell subsets (FOXP3+ CD4-Tregs and BTLA+ CD4-exhausted T cells), particularly in patients with poor immunotherapy responses. These observations suggest that the risk signature may reflect an immune microenvironment associated with T cell dysfunction, rather than directly explaining immunotherapy resistance. However, these findings are derived from retrospective analyses and have not yet undergone prospective interventional validation. Therefore, the HEMR signature should be regarded as a potential predictive biomarker of ICI response, pending further validation.

To bridge our findings to clinical practice, we developed a prognostic nomogram. This model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to individual clinical parameters, reinforcing its potential value for risk stratification in NSCLC. Additionally, drug sensitivity analyses revealed that high-risk patients may be more responsive to certain chemotherapeutic agents, whereas targeted therapies, such as erlotinib and rapamycin, exhibited greater efficacy in the low-risk group. Importantly, external validation in an independent ICI‑treated cohort (GSE135222) showed that high‑risk patients still had significantly worse progression-free survival (PFS). Therefore, high‑risk patients may require more intensive or alternative regimens, such as ICI combined with anti‑angiogenic agents, metabolic modulators targeting hypoxiareprogrammed pathways, or drugs to which they show heightened sensitivity (as detailed in Figure S3C). Conversely, the lower IC50 values of erlotinib, phenformin, and rapamycin in low‑risk patients suggest that these agents, or their clinical analogs (such as metformin for phenformin, everolimus for rapamycin), may be more effective in this subgroup. Based on these findings, we propose a risk‑stratified treatment framework: for high‑risk patients, consider alternative combination strategies or high‑risk‑sensitive drugs rather than ICI‑therapy only; for low‑risk patients, ICI‑based regimens may be more beneficial, and targeted options such as erlotinib, metformin, or mTOR inhibitors could be further explored. Our results highlight the potential for personalized treatment strategies tailored to metabolic and hypoxic profiles. These hypotheses provide clinically actionable insights, though prospective validation is required.

This study has several limitations. First, due to incomplete clinical annotation in publicly available datasets, important prognostic variables, including driver mutation status (for example, EGFR alterations) and detailed treatment information, were unavailable, which may influence survival outcomes and confound the estimated independent prognostic value of the HEMR risk score. For example, patients with EGFR mutations often benefit from tyrosine kinase inhibitors and have better survival outcomes (47,48). Moreover, combining untreated patients with those who received immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or chemotherapy may introduce survival heterogeneity and potentially affect our results. Second, as mentioned above, the analyses associating the signature to immunotherapy outcomes are retrospective, and no prospective validation has been conducted. Additionally, our study lacks in vitro functional experiments to establish a causal role of the identified genes (such as RHOV, TNS4) in immune evasion, so the current evidence is correlative rather than causal. Therefore, further studies with comprehensive clinical data, functional experiments, and prospective cohorts are warranted to validate the HEMR signature’s prognostic and predictive utility and to clarify its mechanism in TME regulation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have established a novel gene signature reflective of hypoxia and energy metabolism that stratifies LUAD prognosis and predicts immunotherapy response, with implications for personalized patient management. This signature advances our understanding of the interplay among hypoxia, energy metabolism, and the immune landscape of LUAD and provides a potential tool for guiding personalized treatment strategies. A promising direction emerging from our findings is the combination of hypoxia-metabolism modulation with ICIs for LUAD.
