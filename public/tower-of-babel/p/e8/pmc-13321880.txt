Method qualification of an electrochemiluminescence assay to quantitate anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies: a case study of an antibody-based biomarker
Authors: Matthew J. Lawless, Nikki Chan, Kruti Patel, Sonika Mudiyala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic spontaneous urticaria is a common immunological disease that affects over 1% of the population. Recently, the anti-FcεR1α autoantibody has been discovered as a prominent biomarker related to the disease. Therefore, there is a need for robust, qualified assays to characterize and measure anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies in human serum. However, biomarker assay qualification is a topic of discussion and can sometimes be left open to interpretation. Currently, there is little guidance or best practices outlined for the method qualification of autoantibodies. These molecules are typically endogenous and such assays fall somewhere between quantitative and qualitative.Herein, we develop an electrochemiluminescence assay for the detection of anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies and provide an outline of method qualification. Two assay formats are investigated with one being fully developed and optimized. Other considerations stemming from immunogenicity qualifications are also assessed, such as isotyping, cut-point, and a confirmatory assay.

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is an immunological disease characterized by recurrent wheals and/or angioedema [1]. Patient reports show over 1% of the population being currently affected and continually increasing [2]. Across these reports, it is unclear if their primary symptom (angioedema) is caused by either differing disease pathophysiology or different phenotypes of CSU [3]. The volatility of the disease is also influenced by its symptoms’ ability to arise independently of external stimuli. This flare can last for prolonged periods of several years, which can greatly debilitate a patient’s lifestyle.

Two types of CSU are known to exist and are classified as type I and type II hypersensitivity. While both types involve mast cells as key effector cells, type I and type II hypersensitivity are defined by being triggered by either immunoglobulin E (IgE) or immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies, respectively [4]. In type II CSU, a major contributor toward mast cell activation is the autoantibody against the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεR1) [5]. It is also shown that IgG autoantibodies against the FcεR1α chain were detected in up to 77% of CSU patients [6]. Moreover, recent studies have reported that immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin (IgM) play a role in mast cell activation [7]. Therefore, it is not only advantageous to be able to quantitate but also to characterize FcεR1α present in serum samples.

Several in-house ELISA methods have been developed and are used in various studies to detect FcεR1α [6,8–10]. These works all employed different methodologies including various standard curves, characterization of IgA/IgG/IgM in multiple assays, and using confirmatory assays similar to antidrug antibody (ADA) assays commonly used to assess immunogenicity. These works have provided tremendous contributions to our understanding of the relationship between FcεR1α autoantibodies and CSU. However, there is still a need for a high-throughput and sensitive method that can quantitate the overall autoantibodies against FcεR1α. Across all CSU patients, increased levels of FcεR1α autoantibodies are not consistent, thus a method able to accurately quantitate even small changes compared to a baseline of healthy volunteers would be tremendously advantageous in both diagnosis and treatment monitoring of CSU. Herein, this manuscript presents a sensitive and robust method using an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) assay on the Meso Scale Diagnostic (MSD) platform that is able to report on the levels of autoantibodies against FcεR1α. ECL provides several advantages over ELISA methods and is a highly widespread technology available to most researchers [11]. Two assay formats were considered during this development. The first uses a sandwich format where the capture and detection molecules are both recombinant FcεR1α proteins. The second format uses a recombinant FcεR1α as a capture molecule paired with an antihuman antibody detection. During development, there was no success in using the first format, therefore conditions of the second format were optimized for increased sensitivity, and the assay was heavily characterized.

Furthermore, qualification of a biomarker assay for the context-of-use of a primary endpoint remains as a topic of debate. Current regulatory guidance from the FDA states that a ligand-binding biomarker assay should use a fit-for-purpose validation which adheres as close as possible to the ICH M10 document [12–14]. However, there is little to no guidance on the best practices for validation of an assay that detects autoantibodies [6,15,16]. Autoantibody assays have similar characteristics to both pharmacokinetic (PK) and immunogenicity experiments; therefore, there is no straightforward answer when authoring a qualification plan. Herein, a new method to quantitatively measure anti-FcεR1α via electrochemiluminescence is presented for the first time. This manuscript provides what the authors currently consider to be a fit-for-purpose qualification to show the assay’s reproducibility, stability and ability to recognize and quantitate the endogenous analyte in serum samples accurately.

Methods

Reagent preparation

Taking advantage of the free lysine residues found on FcεR1α, human recombinant FcεR1α was conjugated in-house with either biotin or SULFO-TAG via linkage to NHS esters. NHS-Biotin purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (cat #A39257), was reconstituted at a concentration of 10 mM. With a challenge ratio of 20:1, the 10 mM biotin solution was added to the protein and incubated for at least 30 min. Following incubation, excess unconjugated biotin was removed by centrifugation in a Zeba Spin Column. Protein concentration is determined by absorbance on a Nanodrop. NHS-SULFO-TAG purchased from MSD (cat #R91AO), was reconstituted at a concentration of 150 nmol. With a challenge ratio of 12:1, the 150-nmol SULFO-TAG solution was added to the protein and incubated for at least 2 h protected from light. In lieu of purification, the reaction was quenched with 50 mM of glycine. Quenching the reaction replaces any need for purification as any unconjugated SULFO-TAG will be removed during a washing step.

Capture solution is prepared by using our in-house biotinylated FcεR1α protein (BioLegend, Cat. #789106) diluted in 1× PBS for a final concentration of 1 µg/mL. Fifty microliters of the capture solution was added to the wells within an MSD Small Spot Streptavidin plate and incubated for an hour at room temperature with a shaking at 700 rpm. During coating incubation, the standard curve and quality controls are prepared fresh at the time of experiment, using Anti-FcεR1α recombinant antibody (Creative Labs, Cat. #TAB-033WM). Samples and eQC will also need to be prepared in this time by using a minimum required dilution (MRD) of 60-fold. The standards, quality controls, and samples are diluted using Assay Diluent containing 10% FBS in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST. After coating, the wells are completely aspirated and washed with 300 µL of Wash Buffer containing 1× PBS with 0.05% Tween three times. Following the washing step, 50 µL of all standards, quality controls, and samples are then added to the plate and incubated for an hour as described above. Before the end of sample incubation, a detection solution of SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgG Antibody (MSD, Cat. #D21ADF) is prepared at 1 µg/mL in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST. The plate containing the samples is washed as described above and 50 µL of detection solution is added to all the wells. The plate is then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. Following the final incubation, the plate is washed and 150 µL of MSD Gold Read Buffer A is added to all wells. The plate is then immediately read on an MSD SECTOR instrument. All data were collected using an MSD SECTOR S600 reader via the MSD Methodical Mind software. Data analysis and regression were performed using the SoftMax Pro Software, version 7.0.3.

Standards were prepared by spiking human anti-FcεR1α in 10% FBS in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST at the following concentrations: 20,000, 6667, 2222, 741, 247, 82.3, and 27.4 pg/mL. Standard curve regressed from SoftMax Pro Software is shown in Figure S1.

Quality controls (QCs) were made by spiking human anti-FcεR1α in 10% FBS in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST at the following concentrations: 10,000, 1000, and 50 pg/mL (QCH, QCM, QCL, respectively).

An endogenous quality control (eQC) was prepared by pooling six individual prescreened and confirmed samples that were obtained from BioIVT. During accuracy and precision, the eQC was established at 544 pg/mL. eQC was treated with a 60-fold MRD during the time of sample preparation.

The percent relative error (%RE) was calculated using the following formula:%RE=ActualConc.−ExpectedConc.ExpectedConc.x100

Confirmatory assessment

Like a traditional ADA, a confirmatory assay was tested to observe if there was any information in the data that can be used to help support clinical predictions. Sixty individual healthy human serum samples were assayed neat and treated with 100 μg/mL of recombinant FcεR1α. Both instances of samples were incubated on the same plate to ensure there was a direct and consistent comparison. Samples were then incubated as per the method for the sample incubation step and the calculated concentrations were analyzed by using a treated-to-untreated ratio.

Cut-point assessment

Cut-point factor was calculated using the JMP 18 software [17,18]. Data sets used, and resultant analysis values are shown in Table S1 and Figure S2, respectively. The dataset was first normalized to the average value and outliers were determined using two iterations of the Quantile Range Outliers analysis with a tail quantile of 0.25 and Q = 3, which are standard for cut-point calculation [18]. Three outliers were determined in the first iteration and removed from the cut point calculations. No additional outliers were calculated upon the second iteration. The outliers removed are shown in Table S1 and were removed for the cut point factor calculation. The distribution of the normalized dataset was not deemed normal (normal distribution is defined as p value ≥0.5 and skewness <1) [18]. Therefore, the log10 of the normalized values was then analyzed using the Distribution tool in the JMP 18 software and showed a normal distribution. Finally, the cut-point factor (CPF) was calculated using the following equation:CPF=10Mean+t∗STDev

where the mean is the average of all analyzed values not including the statistical outliers, t = 2.009 (which is the t-value based on our degrees of freedom, n = 56) [18] and STDev is the standard deviation of all analyzed values not including the statistical outliers.

Isotyping

For isotyping experiments, the assay was run as described above with the exception that the detection solution was changed. The following detection solutions were prepared:

IgA: SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgA Antibody (MSD, Cat. #D21ADE) is prepared at 1 µg/mL in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST.

IgG: SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgG Antibody (MSD, Cat. #D21ADF) is prepared at 1 µg/mL in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST.

IgM: SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgM Antibody (MSD, Cat. #D21ADD) is prepared at 1 µg/mL in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST.

Total: SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgA Antibody, SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgG Antibody, and SULFO-TAG Anti-Human IgM Antibody were all prepared at 1 µg/mL in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST.

Parallelism

To ensure the data being reported is accurate, parallelism of the sample with endogenous analyte will be compared against the synthetic standard curve. Four individual normal human serum samples were analyzed. Each sample was diluted with the assay diluent of 10% FBS in 1% BSA in 0.05% PBST and tested at four different dilutions: one-, two-, four- and eight-fold after the initial MRD. The parallelism data for all samples is shown in Figure 2(B). Experimental data show that there is linearity in calculated concentrations, particularly between one-, two-, and four-fold dilutions, across all samples. These data suggest good parallelism. Data are represented in Figure 2(B) by normalizing all calculated concentrations at each dilution by dividing the measured concentration at the four-fold dilution. The acceptable criteria in this assay has been set to ±30%. These data suggest that an MRD range of 60–240-fold is sufficient to produce reliable results from individual human serum samples.

Stability

Freeze–thaw stability was assessed for up to three freeze–thaw cycles on the eQC sample. Each freeze–thaw cycle consisted of the eQC thawing and being kept at ambient temperature for 30 min followed by storing at −80°C for at least of 12 h. eQC was measured at n = 3 in duplicate, and the average %RE for all replicates was 2.75%. These data suggest that human serum samples can be analyzed after up to three freeze–thaw cycles. Data are shown in Table S3.

Short-term stability was measured by thawing eQC samples and allowing them to remain at ambient temperatures. eQCs were measured at n = 3 in duplicate, and the average %RE for all replicates was 12.9%. These results suggest that human serum samples can be analyzed after remaining at ambient temperatures for up to 25.8 h. Data are shown in Table S4.

Long-term stability was measured to assess how long human serum samples can be stored at −80°C before losing integrity. At the time of this publication, long-term stability has been assessed for up to 42 days. eQCs were measured at n = 3 in duplicate, and the average %RE for all replicates was 5.02%. These results suggest that human-serum samples can be analyzed after storage at −80°C for up to 42 days. Data are shown in Table S5. Long-term stability assessments are still currently being performed.

The assay was also performed in a qualitative manner to assess if IgG, IgA, and IgM isotypes can be detected individually and globally. Data are shown in Figure 3(C). The assay was run as stated in the ECL protocol section but the detection step was switched between IgG, IgA, IgM, and a cocktail of all three isotypes, respectively. Since the assay was built and qualified to detect an IgG-based standard curve, the IgA, IgM, and isotype cocktail was analyzed using signal space. All signals are shown in Table S8. The data are shown in signal space of the individual isotypes compared to the total response of IgG, IgA, and IgM isotypes. Discussion

At the time of writing this paper, to the author’s knowledge, no commercially available solution for quantitating anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies in human serum exists. This manuscript attempts to provide a novel approach by building a qualitative assay with quantitative characteristics using commercially available reagents. Since CSU appears to manifest in various ways across its patient population, a highly sensitive method able to accurately differentiate subtle shifts from baseline is required. For this reason, we chose the MSD ECL platform as it has become a widespread technology throughout industry and academia presenting several advantages over traditional ELISA methods [11]. The ECL method’s primary benefit is that its detection signal is generated via an applied voltage across carbon electrodes. The electric potential causes the MSD ruthenium-based SULFO-TAG label to luminescence in the presence of read buffer. Since the source is orthogonal to the detected signal, this typically creates a much broader dynamic range with significantly higher sensitivity compared to traditional ELISA that use absorbance [19,20]. The authors also find ease of development and customization on the MSD platform. It also serves as an advantage of single analyte detection of soluble biomarkers compared to other platforms, such as the ELLA, SIMOA, and Luminex. However, the method described in this document might be applicable to alternative platforms. It is the author’s suggestion to start with the recombinant FcεR1α and anti-FcεR1α antibody combination described in the methods section as a starting point for method development.

While the MSD platform is quite a popular choice among research institutions, many laboratories may not have the capability to reliably perform protein conjugations such as the biotinylation used in this document. This assay is also able to be performed using standard bind plates coated overnight with FcεR1α with great success; however, the authors did not qualify the method using the non-conjugated capture reagent. If feasible, the authors recommend the format of biotinylated FcεR1α coated onto streptavidin plates. The biotin–streptavidin mechanism allows the assay to be performed same day, which not only increases the throughput but in the author’s experience, typically yields in lower day-to-day variability. The SULFO-TAG detection antibody is commercially available from the MSD catalog.

Our first attempt at developing a novel assay was to use the protein-sandwich format shown earlier in Figure 1(B). The concept behind this assay was to create a format that would report on the global autoantibody response instead of only quantitating IgG autoantibodies or isotyping IgG, IgA, IgM across multiple, successive assays. The assay initially was successful using multiple commercially available mouse antihuman FcεR1α antibodies. However, multiple-assay formats were tested including overnight capture coating with unconjugated FcεR1α, a master mix adding both capture and detection molecules to the sample at once, as well using acidification steps and affinity capture elution (ACE). In every instance, no signal was detected from the human serum samples or from recombinant human anti-FcεR1α. The data suggests that there is no binding between human anti-FcεR1α and either the biotinylated or SULFO-tagged FcεR1α recombinant. To further pinpoint the observed binding problem, human anti-FcεR1α antibodies were coated onto a plate overnight, and ratios of biotin/SULFO-TAG conjugated FcεR1α were added. If the antibody can only bind to one at a time, the signal response should be linear. The data is shown in Figure S3. The theoretical data overlays well onto the plot of % signal inhibition versus % of biotin-conjugated FcεR1α added. These data suggest a 1:1 binding relationship of human anti-FcεR1α and FcεR1α proteins meaning that both biotinylated and SULFO-tagged proteins cannot bind simultaneously to anti-FcεR1a. Possible solutions to this exact format fall out of the scope of this paper and would not be readily available and viable for a vast majority of labs looking to analyze anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies. Therefore, we elected to use the format of a biotinylated FcεR1α capture recombinant followed by a SULFO-TAG labeled antihuman IgG detection antibody.

To develop a method using the newly selected assay format, multiple recombinant FcεR1a proteins were used to assess as capture molecules. All capture molecules were screened against the recombinant human anti-FcεR1a at multiple dilutions, a mouse antihuman FcεR1a as control, a diluent blank, and multiple-serum samples at two dilutions. Unlike the previous format, this initial screening showed signal for both the recombinant human anti-FcεR1a and the serum samples. Capture molecules were selected based on the initial dilution linearity of the recombinant human anti-FcεR1a as well as signal-to-background ratios. Diluent optimization nor blocker optimizations were tested as the selected capture molecule performed exceedingly well and showed background signals that would not be able to be improved upon. Finally, parallelism was tested which showed less than ideal results. Sample diluent optimizations were tested including increased salt concentration, detergent addition, and inclusion of fetal bovine serum (FBS). Addition of 10% FBS showed the strongest parallelism data. At this point, the assay was considered optimized and ready for qualification experiments.

As anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies become increasingly used as an important biomarker for CSU, the eventual goal of this assay would be for use in clinical sample analysis. Therefore, to demonstrate the applicability of this assay pertaining to the context-of-use of clinical trial endpoints, the method was qualified using a fit-for-purpose plan. Best practice for qualification of a quantitative biomarker assay is to adopt to the standards set by the ICH M10 document when possible [13]. The anti-FcεR1α autoantibody method described here falls somewhere between a pharmacokinetic (PK) assay and an antidrug–antibody (ADA) assay. There is little direct documentation or guidance pertaining to best practices of method qualification for such an assay; therefore, this document describes the process the authors followed for qualification of such an assay.

Like a PK assay, this autoantibody assay has a reliable reference material in which to create a standard curve and QCs. However, unlike a PK assay, which uses the exact material for its standard curve as being detected in samples, the use of a recombinant calibrator does not always reliably provide an accurate, parallel assessment of the endogenous analyte. To account for this, an eQC using pooled individual human serum samples was prepared and included in the accuracy and precision assessment. ICH M10 standards states that the assay shows good accuracy and precision across all QCs and eQCs [14].

The parallelism experiment is an essential metric for defining the reliability of a biomarker assay that utilizes a recombinant calibrator to construct its standard curve. Good parallelism may suggest that the reference material is suitable for the quantitation of the endogenous analyte in question and establishes a true lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) [21,22]. The data obtained from parallelism can also help assess which surrogate matrix or assay buffer is appropriate, as well as determine the minimum required dilution (MRD) for the assay [21,22]. The anti-FcεR1α assay shows good parallelism and that an MRD of 60-fold is sufficient to mitigate matrix effect and maintain all samples within the dynamic range of the assay. Six samples were tested from patients with CSU as a proof of concept, and all samples fell within the assays dynamic range without the need for a different dilution.

For ADA assays, typically, a cut-point factor is established denoting a specific value at which point a sample is confirmed as positive [23,24]. Typically, this cut-point factor is then applied to the analyzed value of the negative control to establish a cut-point. This is performed because, aside from background noise or nonspecific binding, an ADA assay’s control sample should be effectively zero if the individual has not been exposed to the drug previously. An autoantibody is different in that the antibody is present in healthy individual serum; therefore, there are no true “negative” samples. For this autoantibody assay, the cut-point factor was instead used to estimate a level to define as normal and determine if samples with higher analyzed concentrations could be defined as elevated using this metric. Here, we applied the cut-point factor to the average of all normal human serum samples (not including statistical outliers) to determine an effective cut-point. These data show that from the 60 normal human serum samples measured, calculated concentrations above 40.1 ng/mL can be labeled as elevated. The assay presented in this manuscript is by no means a test that could be applied as an in-vitro diagnostic or used as something that could be used for a medical diagnosis.

A further step, called the confirmatory step, is performed to determine if the sample is a false-positive or not [23,24]. This confirmatory assay is done by preincubating the sample with the molecule in question, the study drug in most cases. If the positive sample does indeed have antidrug antibodies, the antibody would bind to the confirmatory molecule preventing it from binding to the capture/detection molecules in the assay which results in a decrease in signal. If the signal then falls below the established cut-point, the sample is then confirmed positive to contain ADAs. A pre-incubation of 100 μg/mL was selected thanks to the work from Altricher and coworkers [7]. In the case of the method presented here to detect anti-FcεR1α antibodies, the confirmatory assay provided minimal information. The ratio of FcεR1α treated and untreated samples is shown in Figure 3(B). The average ratio is 0.94, which indicates that the confirmatory assay is difficult to interpret. These data appear consistent with ratios seen in previous works [7,9,10]. Six disease state CSU samples were also assessed with a treated/untreated ratio as a proof of concept. While the average ratio of these six samples was lower than normal human serum, it would be ideal to measure the treated/untreated ratio in a larger cohort of CSU disease state serum samples to determine if this ratio increases or decreases comparatively. However, for this specific assay, the ability to procure an apt reference standard of the human antibody supersedes the necessity of a confirmatory step. For autoantibody assays that cannot obtain a reliable reference standard, comparing the treated-to-untreated ratio could potentially be used in cut-point calculations instead.

Several studies have also commented on the prevalence of multiple immunoglobulin isotypes as autoantibodies against FcεR1α [7–9]. These isotypes primarily include immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin A (IgA), and immunoglobulin M (IgM). Human serum samples were assessed with the assay developed above using commercially available SULFO-TAG labeled anti-IgG, anti-IgA, and anti-IgM individually to assess if the method was able to detect each isotype. While direct quantitation with this assays is not feasible due to a lack of suitable reference standard for IgA and IgM, data show that qualitative measurements can still be performed with this assay of each individual isotype. Furthermore, the assay was performed on the same set of samples using a cocktail detection solution containing all three anti-isotypes. The additive signals detected by each individual anti-isotype sums within 10% of the total signal detected by the cocktail detection. These data support that the assay can provide a global, qualitative measure of total anti-FcεR1α antibodies agnostic of immunoglobulin isotype or a qualitative measure of the individual isotypes as well. Data suggest that the levels of IgM are the highest and that the levels of IgG are the lowest for seven out of the eight normal human serum samples that were assessed. Only in one sample was the signal of IgG greater than the signal from IgA. IgM showed the highest signal in all eight samples as shown in Figure 3(B).

The method provided in this document is the first solution that can quantitatively assess anti-FcεR1α in human serum using a suitable reference material. No published method hitherto has used a human anti-FcεR1a molecule as a reference standard, which may lead to less variability in interpretation of results. Furthermore, thanks to the broad ECL dynamic range, this assay is the first to show the ability to assess global levels of immunoglobulin regardless of isotype from a single sample. Compared to other methods that can only report on one isotype at a time, this method presents an interesting option for researchers interested in total immune response.

Conclusion

Herein, we have presented an electrochemiluminescence assay for the detection and quantitation of anti-FcεR1α antibodies in human serum. The method described uses commercially available reagents and shows good accuracy and precision along with acceptable parallelism and stability. Furthermore, the manuscript is presented to serve as a guide for considerations when developing and quantifying a biomarker assay for the detection of autoantibodies. For anti-FcεR1α autoantibodies, this document considered the possibility of assessing multiple immunoglobulin isotypes both separately and together, establishing a cut-point, and investigating the usefulness of a confirmatory assay. For biomarker assays, direct regulatory guidance is minimal for including such experiments within a qualification plan. As the utility of autoantibody detection and quantitation increases, especially in the case of CSU, more guidelines will certainly be welcomed into the field. We hope that this document can act as a possible blueprint for those wishing to establish their own autoantibody assay in their study design.
