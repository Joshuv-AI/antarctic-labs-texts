Adverse childhood experiences, early menopause, cognition, and dementia in older adults in the United States
Authors: Miharu Nakanishi, Jordan DeVylder, Gemma Knowles, Masato Hasegawa, Marcus Richards, Atsushi Nishida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) may accelerate early menopause and influence neurodegenerative processes, contributing to sex-specific dementia risk. This study examined associations with cognitive decline and incident dementia in ACEs and early menopause.MethodsWe studied 6093 women and 5784 men aged ≥50 years from the Health and Retirement Study, a US cohort spanning between 2010 and 2022. Incident dementia was based on self-report of physician-diagnosed dementia. Cognition was measured using a composite score of immediate and delayed recall, serial sevens subtraction, and counting backwards (range, 0–27). ACEs were assessed using seven items and classified into none, 1, 2, and ≥3 ACEs. Age at menopause was classified into <47, 47 to 52, and ≥53 years. We included men and defined the four categories of sex and age at menopause, assessing varying levels of female hormones with age at menopause serving as the proxy indicator of those differences. Covariates included lifestyle risk factors for dementia. We used a longitudinal panel design with each observation containing baseline covariates and 2-year follow-up outcomes. We applied a random-effects model to survival analysis for incident dementia and to linear regression analysis for cognition.ResultsHere we show that experiencing 2 (hazard ratio: 1.32 [95% CI: 1.07–1.62]) or ≥3 ACEs (1.32 [1.03–1.68]) is associated with higher dementia risk compared with no ACEs. Dementia risk does not differ by sex and age at menopause in women. Women with early menopause (β = −0.22 [95%CI: −0.41 to −0.03]) and men (−0.92 [−1.06 to −0.78]) have worse cognition than women with late menopause. ACE level is not associated with cognition. No mediation by early menopause is observed between multiple ACEs, dementia, or cognition in women.ConclusionsMechanisms linking ACEs and early menopause to dementia risk may differ. Risk reduction strategies should consider preventing and addressing ACEs and early menopause, respectively.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience, Risk factors

Introduction

Dementia is a syndrome arising from neurodegenerative diseases that impair brain and cognitive functioning1. Although women generally have greater cognitive reserve, they experience faster cognitive decline2 and higher incidence rates of dementia than men3. Clarifying these sex differences is essential4,5. Sex-related variation in tau pathology—specifically the accumulation of abnormal tau protein—has been implicated, with studies reporting a higher burden among women than men6–8. The menopausal decline in estrogen may further accelerate tau accumulation, contributing to faster cognitive decline9,10 and increased dementia risk11–13, potentially explaining the sex disparity in dementia prevalence1,14,15.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)—encompassing early-life traumatic events and household challenges such as abuse, neglect, and violence—occur more frequently among women than men and may contribute to their increased dementia risk16–18. Exposure to ACEs has been linked to impaired neurocognitive development19 and persistent biological alterations associated with accelerated aging20 and neurodegeneration21. Experiencing ACEs thus can lead to disrupted stress regulatory systems and alter stress responses, resulting in increased amyloid burden in older adulthood. ACEs are more prevalent in women than men18 and associated with early menopause16. Therefore, early menopause can mediate the association between exposure to ACEs and the sex disparity in dementia prevalence. However, evidence on the association between ACEs and later-life cognitive outcomes remains mixed22. Prior studies have reported elevated dementia risk23, poorer cognitive performance24–26, or no association with cognitive decline27 and incident dementia25,28,29. Evidence is lacking on whether ACEs and early menopause are independently associated with dementia risk or whether early menopause mediates the influence of ACEs on cognitive outcomes. Elucidating these relationships may clarify mechanisms contributing to sex differences in dementia risk and inform targeted public health and clinical strategies.

This study examines the independent and joint associations of ACEs and early menopause with cognitive outcomes in mutually adjusted models and assesses whether early menopause mediates the relationship between ACEs and these outcomes. Our central research interest is to clarify whether (1) early menopause mediates the association between ACEs and dementia, suggesting that it falls along the causal pathway between ACEs and later cognitive health, or (2) early menopause and ACEs exert independent effects on dementia risk, suggesting that they serve as independent risk factors. Our research question also includes whether sex differences in dementia risk is attributable to an accelerated risk by early menopause in women, or to gender disproportion in exposure to ACEs.

Among 11,877 older adults in the United States, we find that exposure to multiple ACEs is associated with increased risk for incident dementia. The association is not mediated by early menopause. In contrast, experiencing early menopause is associated with faster cognitive decline, independent of the childhood adversity level. Our findings highlight the importance of preventing and addressing ACEs and early menopause, respectively, in risk reduction strategies for dementia.

Methods

Datasets

We used a longitudinal design and data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative US cohort on aging that began in 199230. Adults aged over 50 years and their spouses participate in biennial surveys, and refresher samples are enrolled approximately every 6 years. The initial 1992 HRS cohort included 12,652 persons born 1931 to 1941 and their spouses of any age. Assessment of age at menopause has been included since 2008, and presence of physician-diagnosed dementia has been included since 2010. Therefore, we retrieved participant data spanning 2010 to 2022. We constructed panel-format data in which each observation contained baseline covariates, outcomes, and 2-year follow-up outcomes per individual. This study complied with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were: (1) adults aged ≥50 years, (2) direct interviews rather than proxy responses, (3) no Alzheimer’s disease or other dementias at baseline, (4) complete primary exposure variables (ACEs for both sexes and age at menopause for women), and (5) complete outcome measures. Older adults with age at follow-up or death identified were eligible for the analysis of incident dementia (diagnosis cohort). Individuals in this cohort who also had complete baseline and follow-up cognition data were further included in the analysis of cognition (cognition cohort). The diagnosis cohort included 51,848 observations from 11,877 adults aged ≥50 years, comprising 6093 (51.3%) women and 5784 (48.7%) men. Of these observations, 17,216 were excluded from follow-up cognition analyses because of missing baseline cognitive measures (n = 9194), deaths before follow-up assessment (n = 1965), or missing follow-up cognitive measures (n = 6057). The resulting cognition cohort comprised 34,632 observations from 9767 participants (Fig. 1). Among 25,897 adults aged 50+ years without dementia at baseline, excluded 25,897 adults compared with included 11,877 adults in the diagnosis cohort had a younger mean age, more women, less non-Hispanic White adults, and more adults with low educational attainment (Supplementary Table 1).

Measurements

Our outcome measures included the onset of dementia diagnosis and cognitive functioning. Primary independent variables were the level of ACEs and early menopause. Covariates consisted of race and ethnicity, parity, modifiable risk factors for dementia, and other health measures associated with menopause. Variables used in this study are summarized in Supplementary Data 1.

Dementia diagnosis

We used self-reported dementia diagnosis, which shows moderate agreement with cognitive performance-based models of dementia31. We avoided cognitive performance-based diagnostic criteria because such data are limited by the extent and pattern of missingness32. Participants were asked during each survey whether they had ever received a physician-diagnosed Alzheimer’s disease or other dementia diagnosis. Age at follow-up or age at death was used to define survival time in models estimating risk for incident dementia.

Cognition

We calculated an overall cognitive functioning composite score derived from the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS)33,34, including immediate and delayed recall, serial sevens subtraction, and backward counting. The total score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating better functioning. For online respondents, the summary score is mode-adjusted by subtracting one point35. In this study, baseline mean score was significantly lower among participants with incident dementia than those without (t(34,630) = 13.38). The interclass correlation coefficient between baseline and 2-year follow-up was 0.55 (P < 0.001).

Adverse childhood experiences

Childhood financial and social adversity was classified according to the total number of ACEs, assessed using seven items: relocation due to financial difficulties, receipt of financial assistance, and father’s unemployment before age 16; repeating a school year, trouble with police, parental physical abuse, and parental alcohol or drug use before age 1820. Participants were grouped into four categories: none, 1, 2, or ≥3 ACEs.

Age at menopause

Female participants were first asked whether they had undergone hysterectomy—defined as surgical removal of the uterus, regardless of ovary removal—before the onset of natural menopause. If yes, they were asked to report the age at which the procedure occurred. If a hysterectomy had not been performed, or was performed after menopause, participants were asked to report the age at menopause. Age at surgical or natural menopause was used in this study. Using the first and third quartiles, women were categorized into three menopause timing groups: early (<47 years), middle (47–<53 years), or late (≥53 years). We defined the four categories of sex and age at menopause based on previous UK study10, assessing varying levels of female hormones with age at menopause serving as the proxy indicator of those differences.

Covariates

Race and ethnicity, education, and parity were included as time-invariant covariates because of previously reported associations with incident dementia36,37. Race and ethnicity—conceptualized as indicators of structural and social inequities—were self-reported according to categories consistent with the 2000 US Census. The HRS dataset for researchers recoded race into three categories (White/Caucasian, Black/African American, and others), and ethnicity was a binary variable (Hispanic and non-Hispanic). We combined the two variables into single 4-category variable: (1) non-Hispanic White, (2) non-Hispanic Black, (3) Hispanic, and (4) other.

We also included time-variant covariates identified as modifiable dementia risk factors: social isolation, depression, hypertension, diabetes, vision loss, hearing loss, obesity, heavy alcohol consumption, smoking status, and physical inactivity1. Stroke, whose risk may be increased by early menopause and contribute to vascular dementia13, and sleep disturbance, potentially related to early menopause and cognitive decline12, were also included.

Marital status and cohabitation were used as available indicators of social isolation. Depression was defined as a score ≥3 on the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D)38–40. Hypertension, diabetes, and stroke were identified via self-report of physician-diagnosed illness or condition. Sleep disturbance was assessed using items adapted from the Jenkins Sleep Scale41,42. Hearing or vision loss was defined as fair or poor self-rated or vision (with corrective devices, if used)43. Obesity was defined using World Health Organization (WHO) body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) cutoffs, based on self-reported height and weight. Alcohol consumption was categorized by average weekly intake44. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current. Physical inactivity was assessed using the Physical Activity Index Score45,46.

Covariates with missing data were categorized as missing, and participants with missing covariate values were retained in the analyses.

Statistical analysis

We summarized participants’ characteristics by sex and age at menopause for women. We compared the prevalence of each ACE between women and men using unadjusted binomial logistic regression. Among women, we also compared mean age at natural menopause across childhood adversity levels using one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction. We tested racial and ethnic difference in distribution of sex and age at menopause, and level of childhood adversity using unadjusted multinomial logistic regression.

We re-shaped the dataset into a panel format wherein each observation contains baseline covariates and 2-year follow-up outcomes. For incident dementia, outcome measure constituted age and dementia diagnosis at 2-year follow-up. For cognition, outcome measure was composite score at 2-year follow-up adjusting for baseline score47. A random-effect modeling was adopted to account for the between-person difference in number of assessments included in the analysis.

The diagnosis cohort

We analyzed the time from birth to incident dementia diagnosis and censored observations at the end of follow-up using a survival analysis. To visualize censoring, we plotted Kaplan–Meier curves of dementia-free survival by childhood adversity level and sex and age at menopause. We fit a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model treating death as a competing risk. The survival model used a random-effects specification to include multiple observations per individual and included childhood adversity level and sex and age at menopause as independent variables. Baseline age, time-invariant covariates, and time-variant covariates at baseline were also included as independent variables. We calculated population attributable fractions (PAF) of all-cause dementia in a population attributable to ACEs. We used the formula PAF = p(RR − 1)/[1+p(RR − 1)] where p is the prevalence of the exposure and RR is the hazard ratio of dementia associated with the exposure.

We performed a mediation analysis for the survival analysis using the Stata command med4way48 to examine whether early menopause mediated in the association between ACEs and incident dementia diagnosis in women. Multiple ACEs and early menopause were coded as binary variables (present = 1, absent = 0). For computational simplicity, we applied a single-level survival analysis using the last observation per individual and included baseline age, time-invariant covariates, and time-variant covariates at baseline.

The cognition cohort

We evaluated differences in cognition by childhood adversity level and sex and age at menopause. Cross-sectional associations were assessed using a random-effects linear regression model adjusting for age at cognitive assessment and its squared value. We then plotted age-related cognitive trajectories for each category of childhood adversity level and sex and age at menopause.

Longitudinal associations were assessed using a random-effects linear regression model with follow-up cognition as the outcome. Independent variables included childhood adversity level and sex and age at menopause. Baseline age and its squared term, baseline cognition, time-invariant covariates, and time-variant covariates at baseline were also included as independent variables.

We performed a mediation analysis for a multilevel linear regression analysis using the Stata command gsem49,50. The generalized structural equation model included baseline age and its squared term, baseline cognition, time-invariant covariates, and time-variant covariates at baseline.

All analyses and graphical outputs were generated using Stata 19.0 MP (StataCorp, Texas, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

First, to examine sex differences in the association between childhood adversities and dementia risk, we re-analyzed adjusted models for incident dementia diagnosis and follow-up cognition stratified by sex. In analyses restricted to women, we added type of menopause (natural or surgical) as a covariate. Second, we re-analyzed adjusted models stratified by race and ethnicity groups. Third, we reconstructed a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model for time-to-incident Alzheimer’s disease. Fourth, we reconstructed a linear regression model for cognition excluding participants who were diagnosed with dementia within two years of their baseline assessment. Finally, to evaluate selection bias related to missing cognitive data, we re-analyzed random-effects linear models for cognition including participants with missing cognitive measures. Full information maximum likelihood51 was implemented using Mplus 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, California, USA) to address missing data.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. The HRS study is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board (IRB numbers: HUM00061128, HUM00056464, HUM00002562, HUM00074501, HUM00079949, HUM00080925, HUM00085942, HUM00099822, HUM00103072, HUM00106904, HUM00122335, and REP00000046). All participants provided informed consent before enrollment.

Results

Sample characteristics

Baseline characteristics by sex and age at menopause for the diagnosis cohort are reported in Supplementary Data 2. Participants had a mean age of 67.2 years (standard deviation [SD] = 10.9) at baseline; 3773 (31.8%) were in their 50 s, and 3175 (26.7%) were in their 60s. Average follow-up was 8.6 years (SD = 3.7). Two-fifths (42.8%) of participants completed all seven surveys, and 13.1% of participants completed six surveys. Three-quarters (73.3%) of participants were non-Hispanic White, 13.1% were non-Hispanic Black, and 10.5% were Hispanic persons. At the final observation, 596 (5.0%) participants were in their 50 s, 3,244 (27.3%) were in their 60 s, and 3648 (30.7%) were in their 70 s.

Among the 6093 women in the diagnosis cohort, 1329 (21.8%) experienced menopause before 47 years, 2877 (47.2%) experienced menopause between 47 and <53 years, and 1887 (31.0%) experienced menopause at ≥53 years (Supplementary Data 2). Cumulative childhood adversities (≥3 ACEs) were reported in 9.7% of women and 13.2% of men. Prevalence of each ACE by sex is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Men were more likely than women to experience father’s unemployment, repeating a school year, or trouble with police, whereas women were more likely than men to experience parental physical abuse (Supplementary Table 2). Among women with natural menopause (n = 5181), those reporting ≥3 ACEs had a younger mean age at menopause (49.8 years) than those reporting no ACEs (50.5 years; F(3) = 2.81, P = 0.038; P = 0.084 with Bonferroni correction).

Compared with non-Hispanic White group, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic groups had higher prevalence of those experiencing early menopause. These groups also included more participants with ≥3 ACEs than non-Hispanic White group (Supplementary Table 3).

Dementia diagnosis

During the study period, 758 participants (6.4%) reported newly physician-diagnosed all-cause dementia, including 360 women (47.5%) and 398 men (52.5%). Among the 360 women, 89 (24.7%) experienced menopause before 47 years, 171 (47.5%) between 47 and <53 years, and 100 (27.7%) at ≥53 years. The 758 participants with dementia comprised 333 with no ACEs (43.9%), 211 with 1 ACE (27.8%), 125 with 2 ACEs (16.5%), and 89 with ≥3 ACEs (11.7%). Dementia-free survival curves are shown in Fig. 2. Onset of Alzheimer’s disease was reported in 227 participants (1.9%).

Sensitivity analysis

Sex-stratified models for incident all-cause dementia and follow-up cognition are presented in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5. In men, hazard ratios for dementia diagnosis did not differ by childhood adversity level. Among women, those with two ACEs had higher hazard ratios for dementia than those with no ACEs. Childhood adversity was not associated with follow-up cognition in either sex. The association between early menopause and worse follow-up cognition persisted in women. Including type of menopause (natural vs. surgical) as a covariate did not alter these results. Type of menopause was not associated with all-cause dementia (HR = 0.80 [95% CI: 0.59–1.08]) or follow-up cognition (β = 0.06 [95% CI = −0.13 to 0.25]).

Models stratified by race and ethnicity for incident all-cause dementia and follow-up cognition are presented in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7. In non-Hispanic Black group, those with two ACEs had higher hazard ratios for dementia than those with no ACEs. The association between childhood adversity and incident dementia was not found in non-Hispanic White or Hispanic group (Supplementary Table 6). Early menopause and childhood adversity were associated with worse follow-up cognition in Hispanic group, which was not observed in non-Hispanic White or Black groups. In Hispanic group, participants with two ACEs had better follow-up cognition than those with no ACEs (Supplementary Table 7).

Adjusted hazard ratios for incident Alzheimer’s disease are reported in Supplementary Table 8. Risk did not differ by childhood adversity, sex, or age at menopause. Adjusted coefficients for follow-up cognition, excluding participants who were diagnosed with dementia within two years of their baseline assessment are displayed in Supplementary Table 9. Associations between male sex, early menopause, and worse cognition remained statistically significant. Adjusted coefficients for follow-up cognition, including participants with missing cognitive measures, are shown in Supplementary Table 10. Again, associations between male sex, early menopause, and worse cognition remained statistically significant.

Discussion

Individuals experiencing multiple ACEs had an increased risk of all-cause dementia compared those reporting no ACEs; this association did not differ by sex or age at menopause for women. In contrast, men and women experiencing early menopause exhibited worse cognitive performance than women with late menopause, independent of the childhood adversity level. In women, early menopause did not mediate the association between multiple ACEs and cognitive outcomes.

These epidemiological findings align with biological evidence suggesting that ACEs influence neurodegeneration risk20,21. Our results further suggest that the mechanisms linking ACEs to incident dementia may differ from those underlying worse cognition adjusting baseline associated with early menopause, which is partly driven by estrogen decline52. Incorporating early menopause into the models provides further insights into the association between ACEs and incident dementia, which was not observed in previous studies not considering the role of early menopause in dementia risk25,28,29. Although ACEs have been associated with earlier menopause16, the elevated risk of dementia among individuals with multiple ACEs does not appear to be explained by sex or age at menopause. Exposure to ACEs may disrupt stress-regulatory systems and persistently alter stress responses, potentially increasing amyloid burden in later life53,54. Given the different contributions to increased risk, public health and clinical strategies for dementia risk reduction should endeavor to prevent and address ACEs and early menopause, respectively.

Our study also replicated prior observations linking early menopause to accelerated cognitive decline, consistent with findings in older British adults10. Additionally, men exhibited faster cognitive decline than women with late menopause, supporting a protective role of female sex hormones in cognitive aging15. However, ACEs were not associated with longitudinal cognitive changes. Our observation on cognitive change was limited to a 2-year observation, while incident dementia was based on chronological age, reflecting a long-term effect of ACEs on accumulating dementia risk. Nonetheless, cross-sectional analyses suggested inverse associations between ACEs and cognition, consistent with findings in Canadian24 and Chinese older adult populations26. Poor cognitive achievement in early adulthood associated with childhood adversities may restrict the range for subsequent cognitive decline in later life55.

Sex-stratified analyses replicated the association between childhood adversities and all-cause dementia primarily in women, suggesting that ACEs may contribute to long-term morbidity disproportionately in women29. Notably, the prevalence of cumulative ACEs (≥3) was higher in men (13.2%) than women (9.7%), which contrasts with previous findings18. This discrepancy may reflect the HRS focus on financial and social adversity while excluding sexual abuse and other forms of gender-based violence that are more common among women56,57. Further research is needed to clarify sex differences in neurodegenerative risks according to the type of childhood adversity, and to investigate resilience factors that may mitigate dementia risk27.

Notably, racial and ethnic differences were found in the associations between childhood adversity, early menopause, and cognitive outcomes. Non-Hispanic White and Hispanic groups did not replicate the associations between childhood adversity and incident dementia. Furthermore, the Hispanic group exhibited a reverse association, better cognition among participants with two ACEs than those without ACE. Sociocultural resilience factors against ACEs58,59 and post-traumatic growth60 might explain cognitive reserve following adversities in the Hispanic community. However, given the relatively small number of participants in non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic groups and the infrequent occurrence of outcome events, the low statistical power might lead to missing the associations between ACEs and incidence. Racial and ethnic bias in cognitive assessment61 and culturally specific ACEs such as racial discrimination62, which was unmeasured in this study, should also be considered with caution. Further investigations to identify racial and ethnic variation in cognitive trajectories and the sociocultural source of cognitive resilience will provide more insights into brain health strategies for the diverse population.

Another sensitivity analysis did not demonstrate a significant association between childhood adversities and Alzheimer’s disease, which may reflect the low incidence of this outcome (2%) and the relatively young age of the cohort at baseline (primarily individuals in their 50s or 60s). The average follow-up of 8.6 years may be insufficient to capture the full incidence of dementia. Incident dementia is typically preceded by several years of cognitive decline63–65, which may not be fully captured by baseline cognitive impairment measures66. Future studies, including older populations and longer follow-up periods, are necessary to quantify the contribution of ACEs to lifetime dementia risk.

The study has several strengths. First, the large sample size and prospective biennial assessments enabled robust estimation of associations. Second, the panel-format analyses allowed inclusion of older adults with variation in age and length of follow-up outcomes, improving the generalizability of findings. Third, inclusion of multiple time-variant covariates for 2-year follow-up outcomes strengthened the robustness of the results. While our specific focus in this study was on ACEs, menopause, and cognitive decline, our results also identified other pathways for future exploration, including further understanding the role of social isolation and heavy drinking in these relationships. Given that the association between parity and dementia risk is increasingly suggested37, further investigation can also provide insights into the role of reproductive factors in dementia risk.

However, several limitations should be considered. Age at menarche was not captured in HRS surveys, precluding calculation of the reproductive period, which more precisely reflects cumulative endogenous exposure to estrogens and progestogens than age at menopause alone67. Since the HRS surveys did not specify removal of the ovaries, age at surgical menopause might have been underestimated in some participants. Additionally, childhood cognitive attainment was not available, as participants were recruited from age 50 onwards. Early-life cognitive ability is associated with later menopause68, cognitive reserve, and reduced risk of dementia in later life69,70. Although educational attainment may serve as a partial proxy for social class and health literacy, it may not account for early-life cognitive differences, which could affect dementia diagnosis and cognitive outcomes71,72. This study also relied on self-reported physician diagnoses of dementia, which may underestimate the true incidence73. Similarly, other time-variant covariates, including comorbidities, were not clinically validated. Data from aging cohort studies that recruited older individuals relies on use of retrospective, self-reported measures for ACEs, which could introduce recall bias and bias due to personality or recent stressors74,75.

Despite the limitations, the current study provides a detailed examination of the role of childhood adversity and early menopause in dementia risk. Multiple ACEs were associated with an increased hazard of incident all-cause dementia, and early menopause was associated with greater cognitive decline. These findings underscore the need for further research to clarify mechanisms underlying sex differences in dementia risk. Risk reduction strategies for dementia should consider preventing and addressing ACEs and early menopause, respectively.
