Innovative strategies for high-throughput discovery and engineering of plant immune receptors
Authors: Wanying Zhao, Tingting Sun, Yuanyuan Zhang, Zheng Qing Fu, Youxiong Que
Published: 2025

Main text

Plants can be infected by diverse pathogens, which can cause severe diseases and reduce crop productivity. Receptors located on the plant cell surface allow plants to detect external cues. In the presence of pathogens, plants recognize distinctive molecular signatures known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), thereby activating PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI) (Boller and Felix, 2009). PRRs are broadly classified into two groups: receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and receptor-like proteins (RLPs), the latter lacking a kinase domain (Boutrot and Zipfel, 2017). Among these, RLKs and RLPs containing leucine-rich repeat ectodomains (LRR-RLKs and LRR-RLPs) constitute the two largest PRR families (Boutrot and Zipfel, 2017). LRR-RLKs are further divided into 20 subgroups, with subgroup XII specialized in recognizing PAMPs and promoting immune responses (Dufayard et al., 2017).

Although several subgroup XII RLKs (LRR-RLK-XIIs), such as the Arabidopsis thaliana flagellin receptor FLS2 and elongation factor receptor EFR, have been well characterized (Gómez-Gómez and Boller, 2000; Zipfel et al., 2006), the functions of most LRR-RLK-XIIs in non-model plants remain poorly understood.

A recent study (Ngou et al., 2025) addressed this gap by analyzing LRR-RLK-XIIs across 285 plant species and identifying a receptor named selective cold shock protein receptor (SCORE), which recognizes cold shock protein (CSP) peptides. Remarkably, the authors also engineered modified SCORE variants capable of detecting CSP peptides from multiple pathogens that were previously unrecognized.

Despite decades of research, fewer than ten LRR-RLK-XII receptor–ligand pairs have been identified, largely because of the challenges associated with isolating and characterizing these receptors in non-model plant species (Ngou et al., 2025). In their recent study, Ngou et al. identified 13 185 LRR-RLK-XII proteins across 350 plant genomes (Figure 1). Internal LRR residues play an essential role in recognizing PAMPs and determining ligand specificity. To capture this diversity, the authors applied repeat conservation mapping to align the ectodomains of the 13 185 LRR-RLK-XIIs. By focusing on the inner LRR motifs and removing consensus residues, they retained only the non-consensus residues for alignment. To identify candidate immune receptors among these LRR-RLK-XIIs, Ngou et al. performed transient expression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana using chimeric constructs composed of LRR-RLK-XII extracellular domains fused to the intracellular Xb kinase domain of the brassinosteroid-insensitive 1 (BRI1) receptor (Figure 1) (Ngou et al., 2025). They monitored dephosphorylation of the downstream signaling component BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1) as a readout following ligand treatment. By screening 210 LRR-RLK-XII–BRI1 chimeras with bacterial suspensions, they identified seven receptor candidates that triggered immune responses. Among these, Receptor 181 stood out because it responded to both live and boiled Agrobacterium tumefaciens suspensions. Further experiments revealed that Receptor 181 undergoes phosphorylation and physically associates with the SERK co-receptor BAK1 upon exposure to boiled A. tumefaciens, leading the authors to prioritize this receptor for detailed investigation.

To determine its ligand, Ngou et al. fractionated Agrobacterium tumefaciens suspensions and identified cold shock proteins (CSPs) as the most active components (Ngou et al., 2025). Through detailed biochemical and functional analyses, they showed that Receptor 181 specifically recognizes the CSP peptide 15 (csp15) (Figure 1). Interestingly, Receptor 181—renamed SCORE—differs mechanistically from the previously described cold shock protein receptor (CORE) in ligand recognition (Ngou et al., 2025).

Ngou et al. further explored how SCORE receptors detect different CSP variants by synthesizing 103 representative csp15 peptides derived from viruses, archaea, bacteria, fungi, and other sources (Figure 1). They then fused the ectodomains of 20 plant SCOREs to the intracellular domain of EFR and assessed the immune responses of these chimeras to the synthesized csp15s (Ngou et al., 2025). The results revealed that SCOREs from different plant species vary in their ability to recognize csp15, likely due to sequence differences at positions 6 and 7 of the peptide. Based on these positions, SCOREs can be categorized into three types: those preferring negatively charged residues (e.g., Punica granatum ortholog PG), those favoring peptides without negatively charged residues (e.g., pomelo SCORE CM, Citrus maxima), and those whose binding does not depend on charge, such as CORE (Figure 1).

Using AlphaFold2 structural modeling, the researchers examined how SCORE binds to csp15 and identified three primary interaction sites within the receptor ectodomain (Figure 1) (Ngou et al., 2025). Site-directed mutagenesis of these regions revealed three critical amino acid residues—positions 300, 302, and 304 within the 10th LRR motif—that are essential for ligand recognition. Substituting these residues altered the surface charge of the inner LRR region, which in turn affected ionic interactions with the 6th and 7th residues of csp15. These findings demonstrate that the residues at positions 300, 302, and 304 precisely determine the surface charges of the 8th to 11th LRR motifs, thereby controlling SCORE ligand specificity.

Ngou et al. generated 37 SCORE variants by altering key amino acid residues and assessed their ability to recognize specific csp15 peptides (Figure 1) (Ngou et al., 2025). Among these engineered receptors, several synthetic SCORE variants were able to recognize CSPs from diverse harmful pathogens, including Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (the causal agent of citrus greening disease), Erwinia sp., Ralstonia sp., and root-knot nematodes—pathogens that were not detected by the wild-type CM receptor. This achievement provides a promising strategy for developing synthetic immune receptors with broad-spectrum disease resistance, particularly in non-model and perennial crops that are difficult to improve through conventional breeding approaches.

LRR-RLPs, similar to LRR-RLKs, also play critical roles in pathogen recognition. For example, the LRR-RLP protein RLP23 in Arabidopsis detects nlp20, a peptide derived from necrosis- and ethylene-inducing peptide 1–like (NLP) proteins. In their study, Ngou et al. focused on LRR-RLKs that perceive pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from the bacterial pathogen A. tumefaciens (Ngou et al., 2025). Building on this work, future research should aim to identify LRR-RLKs and LRR-RLPs that recognize PAMPs associated with fungal, oomycete, viral, and nematode pathogens (Zipfel, 2008). In addition to PAMP recognition, many LRR-RLKs and LRR-RLPs are involved in detecting damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and herbivore-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs) (Reymond, 2021; Tanaka and Heil, 2021; Snoeck et al., 2022). Future studies could therefore expand efforts to discover additional receptors that sense DAMPs and/or HAMPs, broadening our understanding of plant innate immunity. According to the classical zig-zag model, PAMP-triggered immunity can be suppressed by pathogen effectors, resulting in effector-triggered susceptibility (Jones and Dangl, 2006). Consequently, a key direction for engineering LRR-RLK variants will be to design receptors that avoid or minimize interference by pathogen or herbivore effectors, thereby maintaining durable immune function in crops.
