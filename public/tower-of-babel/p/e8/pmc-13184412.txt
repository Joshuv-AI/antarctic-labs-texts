Microcirculatory perfusion is improved with plasma, but not with crystalloids, in a rat polytraumatic shock model
Authors: Tarık Gözden, Bulent Ergin, Can Ince, Nicole P. Juffermans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic shock is associated with microcirculatory dysfunction. Plasma resuscitation for traumatic shock outside the context of bleeding may exert benefit by augmenting intravascular volume and inhibiting endotheliopathy. However, its effects on microcirculatory perfusion and tissue oxygenation in trauma remain poorly understood.Study Design and MethodsFollowing polytraumatic injury and shock induction with controlled bleeding, rats (n = 11) received crystalloids or plasma until mean arterial pressure (MAP) was 65 mmHg for 2 h, after resuscitation was stopped. Total vessel density (TVD), functional capillary density (FCD), proportion of perfused vessel density (PPV), and tissue red blood cell perfusion (tRBCp) were evaluated. Microcirculatory oxygen availability (μHbO2sat) was measured by tissue spectroscopy. Plasma hyaluronan and sVCAM were assessed for endothelial integrity.ResultsAfter resuscitation, MAP was restored in both groups. However, TVD, FCD, and tRBCp were impaired in the crystalloid group as compared to the sham (p < 0.05), which did not occur in the plasma group. tRBCp was significantly improved by plasma resuscitation compared to crystalloid (p < 0.05). After resuscitation had stopped, TVD, FCD, and tRBCp remained significantly higher in the plasma group compared to the crystalloid group (p < 0.05). sVCAM decreased significantly by plasma compared to crystalloid (p < 0.05). There was no significant change in hyaluronan and μHbO2sat levels.ConclusionsPlasma, but not crystalloids, preserved microcirculatory diffusion and perfusion, which was associated with correction of endothelial functionality in rats requiring fluids following traumatic shock. Results suggest that plasma may be used in traumatic shock patients with persistent need of volume after bleeding has been contained.

INTRODUCTION

Following hemorrhage control, or even in the absence of bleeding, trauma patients may still require fluid volume replacement to improve intravascular volume and organ perfusion. In this setting, crystalloid fluids are used. However, the capacity of crystalloids to expand blood volume is limited, as only 25% remains in the intravascular space, 1 increasing the risk of tissue edema by reducing colloid osmotic pressure. 2 In addition, traumatic shock is associated with microcirculatory dysfunction. 3 , 4 Even after correction of mean arterial pressure (MAP) and cardiac output, microcirculatory abnormalities persist, as found in patients with traumatic hemorrhagic shock and in pigs subjected to hemorrhagic shock and resuscitation. 3 , 5 This dissociation between macro‐ and micro‐hemodynamics is associated with organ failure. 3 Therefore, investigating alternative fluids with an impact on restoring intravascular volume and microcirculatory perfusion is of interest.

In trauma, plasma resuscitation is used to treat coagulopathy, as it is thought to restore the deficit of coagulation factors and may decrease mortality in those with longer transit time to the hospital. 6 , 7 However, an impact of plasma on hemostasis in trauma patients has not consistently been shown. 8 Alternatively, prehospital plasma resuscitation may also be beneficial by decreasing endothelial activation and inflammation and improving glycocalyx integrity compared to patients who received standard of care. 9 However, there are limited data about the effects of plasma resuscitation on microcirculatory perfusion and tissue oxygenation.

The use of incident dark field illumination imaging (IDF) and tissue spectroscopy could provide more insight on the effects of resuscitation on the microcirculation. IDF video microscopy is used for quantifying sublingual microcirculatory perfusion characteristics in different clinical settings. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Since total vessel density (TVD) and functional capillary density (FCD) indicate the diffusion capacity of the microcirculation, tissue red blood cell perfusion (tRBCp) is calculated by algorithm‐based analysis as the oxygen delivering capacity of the microcirculation. 14

Thus, we hypothesized that plasma resuscitation is superior to crystalloids in resuscitating traumatic shock by providing intravascular volume support, thereby improving microvascular diffusion and perfusion, as well as endothelial integrity in a rat model of polytrauma and controlled hemorrhagic shock.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

The Animal Research Committee of Erasmus Medical Center, Rotterdam (SP2300065), and the National Committee of Animal Experimentation (CCD209765) approved experiments. During the experiments, the Dutch national law, the Experiments with Animals Act (Wod, 2014), and the European Parliament Directive (2010/63/EU) were adhered to. Thirty‐three male Wistar rats (Charles River Laboratories, The Netherlands, 350–450 g and around 12 weeks old) were kept in the animal housing facility on the premises with unlimited access to food and water, and their day‐night cycle was a regular 12‐h.

Plasma manufacturing method

Plasma was prepared using syngeneic donor rats in accordance with national blood bank guidelines. To put it briefly, rats anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of 90 mg/kg ketamine, 0.25 mg/kg dexmedetomidine, and 0.05 mg/kg atropine then a heparinized syringe was used to puncture the heart and extract whole blood, which was then promptly centrifuged (15 min, 2500G, 18°C) to separate its constituent parts. Two‐thirds of the plasma part of the first centrifugate was centrifuged a second time (15 min, 2500 × g, 4°C). Following the second centrifugation, two‐thirds of the plasma was removed and stored at −80°C. The plasma was thawed in water at 37°C on the day of the experiment.

Animal model

An intraperitoneal injection of a combination of 90 mg/kg ketamine, 0.25 mg/kg dexmedetomidine, and 0.05 mg/kg atropine was used to sedate the rats. The rate at which maintenance anesthesia was administered was 33 mg/kg/h of ketamine. Rats were connected to a mechanical ventilator following tracheostomy. FiO2 was set at 0.4, PEEP at 3 cmH2O, Pinsp at 13–20 cmH2O, and tidal volume at 8% of body weight. The respiratory rate was adjusted to target an end‐tidal PCO2 of 40–50 mmHg. Using a heating pad, body temperature was kept at 37 ± 0.5°C. Anesthesia was monitored by checking absence of reflexes, absence of movement, muscle relaxation, and heart rate every 15 min.

The right carotid artery was cannulated for measuring blood pressure and heart rate. The catheter inserted into the right jugular vein was used for fluid support and maintenance of anesthesia during the experiment. The left femoral artery and left femoral vein were cannulated with polyethylene catheters for blood sampling and resuscitation, respectively. After this, a small incision was made on the right biceps, and the skin and the fascia of the muscle were removed to allow microcirculatory measurements with Cytocam (Breadius Medical BV, The Netherlands) and the O2C device (LEA Medizintechnik GmbH, Germany). In order to induce polytrauma and shock which responds to resuscitative interventions, 15 the duodenum and left liver were clamped for 3 × 5 s and 2 × 5 s, respectively, using a metal clamp coated in silicone tubing. The left femur was fractured with handcrafted pliers. Then, animals were hemorrhaged until a MAP of 40 mmHg (approximately 35% of their circulation volume) for 1 h. Animals were randomly assigned (n = 11 per group) to undergo resuscitation with plasma or crystalloid solution (Baxter, Plasma Lyte) at a rate of 16 mL/h in order to attain the goal MAP > 65 mmHg. After reaching this goal for 5 min, infusion was stopped. The sham group underwent anesthesia and surgery. Measurements were taken at every time point.

Blood was drawn prior to trauma (T0), 1 h after shock (T1), and at every hour throughout the experiment (T2, T3, T4, T5, T6). Animals were exsanguinated at the end of the experiment (T6). Arterial blood gas and electrolytes were measured at all time points.

Microcirculatory measurements

To monitor the microcirculation, a handheld vital microscope with incidence dark field (IDF) imaging, CytoCam, was placed on the exposed biceps muscle after removal of the skin and the fascia. At T0, T3, and T6, 100‐frame clips (100 s) were captured. Microtools Automated Vascular Analysis software (Active Medical BV, Leiden, the Netherlands) was used to analyze the microcirculatory parameters. 16 TVD, FCD, and proportion of perfused vessel (PPV) were assessed as diffusion parameters of the microcirculation. tRBCp was calculated for the combination of convective and diffusive capacity of the microcirculation. 14

Microcirculatory oxygen availability (microvascular hemoglobin oxygen saturation‐mHbO2sat) and muscle capillary blood flow (mBF) were measured by use of spectrophotometry and laser Doppler (Oxygen to See System [O2C, LEA medical, Germany]). This technique illuminates tissue with white light and analyzes the spectrum of reflected white light to calculate microcirculatory oxygen availability (mHbO2sat). 17 , 18 The O2C probe, positioned adjacent to the phosphorimeter light guide, also houses a micro laser Doppler flowmeter, enabling the measurement of muscle capillary blood flow (mBF). At each time point, the O2C device was placed on exposed biceps tissue following the Cytocam measurement.

Statistical analysis

With time to achieve the predefined MAP target as the main outcome of resuscitation and a treatment effect of 15% with a standard deviation of 30, 9 rats per group have an 80% chance of detecting a statistically significant difference in MAP, according to earlier studies in trauma models testing interventions with various resuscitation fluids. 15 With a mortality of 20%, two more rats were added, making 11 rats per experimental group.

The software GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2) was used to analyze data and create graphs. First, a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine whether all parameters were normal. A mean and a standard deviation or standard error was used to display the data according to their distribution. The Mann–Whitney U test, Student's t‐test, or chi‐squared test was used to check for differences in the parameters. Friedman analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for paired (T0 vs. T6) nonparametric differences within groups. p‐values below 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. A linear regression model with a 95% confidence interval was used for determining the correlation between MAP and microcirculatory parameters measured at T0, T1, T3 and T6.

RESULTS

Two animals died prematurely because of shock and bleeding complications of instrumentation. In the remaining animals, hemorrhage was induced by withdrawing blood with a median of 4.8 mL [range 3.20–7.60], with no differences between crystalloid and plasma groups. Trauma resulted in shock, with low MAP, increased base deficit and increased lactate compared to sham (p < 0.05; Table 1, Table 2). Following trauma, MAP was decreased at all time points in comparison to sham (p < 0.05; Table 1).

Macro hemodynamic parameters during and after resuscitation

In rats allocated to plasma resuscitation, 6.6‐mL total volume was needed to restore MAP to 65 mmHg, which was significantly lower than the total volume used for crystalloid infusion if target MAP was reached (26.4 mL; p < 0.005). In the crystalloid group, not all animals reached the target MAP (Figure 1). During the 2 h of resuscitation, MAP was higher in the plasma group than in the crystalloid group (p < 0.05; Table 1).

Total blood volume used for resuscitation between the Trauma+Crystalloid and Trauma+Plasma groups. Data are mean ± SD, p < 0.005.

In the plasma group, heart rate increased significantly at T3 and T4 compared to the crystalloid group (p < 0.05). At T6, Hb was lower in the plasma group than in the crystalloid group, suggesting improved intravascular volume expansion. However, despite restoring macrocirculation, lactate levels remained higher in trauma groups at all time points in comparison to sham (p < 0.05; Table 1). After cessation of resuscitation when MAP of 65 mmHg was met, there was a gradual decrease in MAP in both trauma groups compared to sham (p < 0.05).

Microcirculatory parameters during and after resuscitation

At the baseline before trauma infliction, there were no differences in perfusion and diffusion (TVD, FCD, PPV, and tRBCp) between groups (Figure 2). After resuscitation (T3), perfusion and diffusion were decreased only in the trauma group that received crystalloids when compared to sham (Figure 2). tRBCp at T3 was significantly improved by plasma resuscitation compared to the crystalloid group (p < 0.05). At T6, TVD, FCD, and tRBC remained significantly higher in the trauma group that received plasma in comparison to the trauma group treated with crystalloids (p < 0.05; Figure 2). However, there was no significant difference in oxygenation and convection parameters (muscle mHbO2sat and mBF) between the groups (Figure 3). There was no correlation between the correction of microcirculatory parameters and systemic blood pressure in the plasma resuscitated group from T0 to T6 (Figure 4), which shows that even though MAP decreased, the effect of plasma on the microcirculation is long‐lasting.

Plasma transfusion significantly inhibited an increase in sVCAM‐1 levels compared to the crystalloid group (p < 0.01). DISCUSSION

In a polytrauma model, in which there is containment of bleeding but persisting shock, which would trigger resuscitation in the clinical setting, low volume of plasma resuscitation enabled restoration of both systemic arterial pressure and microcirculation, associated with less endotheliopathy. Importantly, the positive effects of plasma resuscitation on microcirculatory diffusion and perfusion were maintained even when MAP dropped over time. In contrast, crystalloids did not recruit microcirculatory dysfunction and did not inhibit endotheliopathy.

Shock results in microcirculatory alterations, with disturbed convection and diffusion parameters of sublingual microcirculation, translating into disturbed serosal microvascular perfusion 19 , 20 , 21 and leading to multiple organ failure. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 These effects have been observed in traumatic shock patients. 3 , 26 , 27 Importantly, the convection and diffusion parameters of sublingual microcirculation remain deteriorated in patients with trauma even after sustained restoration of arterial pressure and cardiac index for at least 72 h. 3 In this study, we confirm microcirculatory impairment after traumatic shock, as well as dissociation from macrohemodynamic parameters, as described before. 28

The present study also confirms that plasma is a more effective blood volume expander than crystalloids, as demonstrated by a reduced Hb to a mean of 8.5 g/dL after low amounts of plasma infusion, versus 10.5 g/dL following a 4 times higher volume of crystalloids. Most likely, crystalloids increase the gradient of fluid loss over the vessel wall into the interstitial space, resulting in a reduction of circulating volume with relatively higher Hb levels, whereas plasma remains in the circulation. Of note, lower Hb was present even 3 h after stop of resuscitation, suggesting a long‐lasting fluid expanding capacity of plasma. While a drop in Hb is unwanted per se, this is a consequence of blood volume expansion.

Our study further expands these previous findings by demonstrating that the blood volume expanding effect of plasma translates into correction of microcirculatory diffusion and perfusion parameters, which was absent when crystalloids were used. In line, crystalloids did not improve renal microvascular oxygenation after normalization of arterial pressure in a rat model of hemorrhagic shock. 21 , 29 It has also been shown that plasma, but not saline, can improve bowel perfusion. 30 Plasma resuscitation, but not crystalloids, resulted in an improvement in tRBCp, a perfusion parameter that combines the convective and diffusive functions of microcirculation. Furthermore, plasma results in a lasting improvement of peripheral microvascular diffusion and perfusion, even after stopping fluid infusion and despite recurrence of low blood pressure. This may render plasma an interesting option for the resuscitation of trauma patients with permissive hypotension.

Despite recruitment of the microcirculatory vessels, there was no difference between the plasma and crystalloid groups in terms of oxygen availability in muscle tissue in this study. In a previous experimental study, colloid solutions were shown to restore sublingual microcirculatory blood flow and mHbO2sat. 31 A possible explanation for an absence of improvement of tissue oxygen content might be that the skeletal muscle tissue was chosen for the spectrophotometric measurement. It can be speculated that myoglobin content in muscle can interfere with spectrophotometric measurements of oxygen saturation.

In this study, lactate levels were higher after plasma than after crystalloid resuscitation. This is not in line with other studies in hemorrhagic shock models. 32 We hypothesize that this difference is due to the model, as we also included fracture and clamping of the intestines to induce an endothelial stress response. Indeed, high lactate can also occur after metabolic derangements and after increased stress responses and hence is not a specific indicator of tissue hypoperfusion. Here, we think that the increased lactate level in the plasma group was likely due to an increased clearance of lactate from tissue into reperfused capillaries. Another feature of this model is the relatively low heart rate, probably caused by deep sedation.

Improved perfusion with plasma was associated with less sVCAM. We previously showed that plasma was associated with a decrease in syndecan‐1, 33 in line with previous studies. 33 , 34 Our finding that hyaluronan level was marginally decreased after balanced crystalloid resuscitation was unexpected and not in line with previous data. 35 , 36 This decrease may be due to dilution, which may specifically impact hyaluronan, but this remains speculative. Collectively, findings suggest improved endothelial condition. While we have not demonstrated a clear mechanism of this association, we speculate that plasma may induce less shear stress than clear fluids, thereby preventing destabilization of the cytoskeletal anchors of the glycocalyx. Alternatively, plasma may prevent dilution of proteins that contribute to glycocalyx stability. 37

To conclude, in this experimental model of traumatic shock with contained bleeding but in need of fluid resuscitation, low‐volume plasma resuscitation improves microcirculatory diffusion and perfusion with a long‐lasting effect, continuing even after resuscitation was stopped and MAP was decreasing. We further suggest that improved microcirculatory perfusion may limit endothelial dysfunction. All these effects are not found after crystalloids. Collectively, results point towards additional mechanisms of benefit of the use of plasma in trauma, which extend beyond the correction of coagulopathy. Further clinical studies are required to determine the place of plasma for trauma patients in need of volume outside the context of bleeding.
