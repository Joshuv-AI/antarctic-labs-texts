Synthesising evidence regarding artificial intelligence-generated radiological reports based on medical images: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Weibo Feng, Anthony Yazdani, Alban Bornet, Alexandra Platon, Douglas Teodoro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe increasing volume of radiological images and the associated workload of report generation necessitate efficient solutions, making artificial intelligence (AI) a crucial tool to streamline this process for radiologists. Recent years have seen a surge in research exploring AI-driven radiological report generation directly from images, particularly with the emergence of large vision language models. However, a comprehensive understanding of the current landscape, including specific limitations and the extent to which efforts move beyond abnormality detection to full textual report generation, remains unclear. This scoping review aims to systematically map the existing literature to provide an overview of the current state of AI in generating radiological reports from medical images, including the scope and limitations of existing research. To our knowledge, no prior scoping review has comprehensively mapped this landscape, especially considering recent advancements in foundation models in medicine and related AI architectures. Considering the explosive growth of related studies in recent years, a comprehensive scoping review will be significant in mapping the current research status and understanding relevant limitations.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines to map the literature on AI generating radiological reports from medical images. We will search PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science for peer-reviewed articles (January 2016 to March 2025) using keywords related to AI, radiological reports and medical images. Original research in English focusing on AI-driven report generation from images will be included and studies without report generation or not using medical images as input will be excluded. Two independent reviewers will perform a two-stage screening. Data extraction, guided by the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies checklist and focusing on study characteristics, AI methods, image modalities, report features, limitations and key findings, will be analysed using narrative and descriptive synthesis, with results presented in tables, figures and a narrative summary.Ethics and disseminationThis protocol describes a scoping literature review methodology that does not involve research on humans, animals or their data; therefore, no ethical approval is required. Following the review, the results will be considered for publication in a relevant peer-reviewed journal and may be shared with stakeholders through reports or summaries.

Introduction

Medical images, particularly radiological images such as CT, MRI and X-ray, play a crucial role in clinical screening, diagnosis and treatment.1 Typically, these images are presented alongside corresponding textual reports that explain basic patient information, clinical indications, findings and diagnostic conclusions for clinicians and, where appropriate, patients.2 3 A standard radiological report generally includes several sections for basic information, indication, description and conclusion.3 While the basic information and indication sections can often be automatically populated by the radiology information system (RIS), the description and conclusion sections require significant radiologists’ time and expertise. In clinical practice, radiologists routinely interpret numerous images by meticulously describing findings and abnormalities in the description section and subsequently synthesising a preliminary summary in the conclusion section. This process is often tedious, time-consuming and susceptible to errors.3 4 Given the rapid increase in the volume of radiological images in recent years, developing methods to assist radiologists in generating reports and alleviate their workload has become an important and challenging objective, especially in settings with limited radiologist availability and medical resources.5 6

Over the past decade, artificial intelligence (AI) has seen widespread adoption in medical imaging, especially in the radiology domain,7 yielding notable advancements.8 In particular, deep learning methodologies, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have demonstrated their effectiveness in processing large volumes of multimodal medical images and accurately detecting abnormalities.912 Furthermore, natural language processing (NLP), especially vision-language models (VLMs), has emerged as a powerful tool for integrating medical images and associated text, enabling the generation of medical image reports.13 14 Driven by the objective of assisting radiologists in alleviating workload and minimising diagnostic errors, several approaches leveraging NLP have been proposed for the automated generation of radiology reports from medical images.15 16

Despite promising advancements in current research, generating clinically readable and interpretable radiology reports with complex, lengthy structures using AI remains a significant challenge. While most existing AI models excel at identifying abnormalities or producing brief image descriptions,131720 their utilities for application in real clinical practice and assistance to medical doctors remain unclear. Furthermore, patients also require concise, interpretable and easily understandable summaries of radiological findings. Although various efforts are emerging to leverage AI for the direct generation of radiology reports to reduce radiologist workload and enhance patient understanding,1516 2123 the scope and robustness of this evidence currently require further investigation.

Although some recent reviews have provided initial summaries of AI applications in radiology, the breadth and landscape of their analysis remain restricted in certain areas. Many reviews have concentrated on specific subspecialties, such as nuclear medicine or musculoskeletal imaging,24 25 or have centred on particular AI models, including the Chat Generative Pre-trained Transformer (ChatGPT) and Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT),26 27 or have restricted their scope to specific radiological image types, such as X-rays.28 Another recent survey also outlined progress in VLMs for medical report generation, but their primary focus has been on VLM architectures and image captioning,29 without systematically synthesising the current application landscape within radiology, including data modalities, sub-specialties and report types. Consequently, a comprehensive overview of the application scope and coverage of AI-generated reports in radiology, detailing the current AI technologies, performance, dataset/image types and subspecialties, remains lacking. A new comprehensive scoping review may be helpful to address the current hindered clinical adoption of AI-generated radiological reports due to limited image types and inconsistent performance of AI models, providing research direction on how AI-generated reports could be further improved.

Sporadic studies related to AI and radiology can be initially traced back to 2016, while relevant publications are primarily concentrated between 2019 and 2025, especially with a rapid expansion in the last 3 years (online supplemental table 3). Considering the heterogeneity of the existing literature and rapid evolution in NLP, Transformer and other AI technologies, a scoping review is necessary to map the breadth of evidence and is suitable to address the identified knowledge gap. This scoping review aims to map the available literature from January 2016 to March 2025 concerning AI-generated radiological reports derived from medical images. Unlike previous related reviews, this work seeks to provide a comprehensive and up-to-date overview of how AI is integrated with radiological images to facilitate medical report generation, with specific attention to subspecialties, clinical context, use cases, algorithms, image types and reported performance metrics. In this scoping review, we try to maximise our coverage of all potential types of radiological images, texture reports, medical subspecialties and AI models, which may fill the gaps in previous relevant reviews. By doing so, we hope this scoping review will provide useful information regarding AI technologies in generating radiological reports, alleviating potential misunderstandings or resistance to medical AI among some older generations of radiologists, which might be another challenge for further AI-clinical adoption in the future.

Methods and analysis

In this scoping review, we will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines30 and use literature across both scientific journals and conference peer-reviewed literature from January 2016 to March 2025 in Pubmed, Embase and Web of Science. There are five stages in this study: (1) determining the research question, (2) search strategy, (3) inclusion/exclusion criteria, (4) data extraction and (5) analysis and presentation of the results. Details of the PRISMA-ScR checklist can be found in online supplemental table 1.

Source of evidence selection

For the source of evidence (SOE) selection, we will use the PRISMA-ScR guidelines30 to structure our selection process. BibTeX, Excel and other office tools will be used for screening articles identified during the search phase. Title and abstract screening will be conducted by two independent reviewers on all involved articles, who will categorise each article as ‘Yes’, ‘No’ or ‘Maybe’ according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria (see table 2). Articles with ‘Yes’ or ‘Maybe’ will be included for further full-text screening and extraction. If any discrepancies arise, another senior reviewer in our team will be consulted. Records that cannot be retrieved will be excluded. For those included in full-text screening, two research team members will independently review and categorise the articles as either ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Any uncertainties will be discussed by the whole team members. In this scoping review, we only include peer-reviewed original basic research studies in English. Grey literature, previous review and literature in other languages will not be included. In figure 1, the standard PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will be followed in the SOE selection.

Analysis of the evidence and presentation of results

Our data analysis will involve a descriptive numerical summary of the characteristics and distribution of the included articles, while critical appraisal will not be included in this scoping review. We will employ visual aids, such as bar charts, to illustrate the distribution of studies across key categories (e.g., subspecialties, AI algorithms) and tables (like our pilot findings in online supplemental table 3) to summarise the extracted data regarding AI architecture, model performance, image types, current clinical adoption and shortcomings. Alongside these methods, we will conduct a narrative synthesis to describe the scope and nature of the evidence on AI-generated radiological reports from medical images, addressing each of our research questions by synthesising the key findings from the included literature. Furthermore, we will also have a further clinically relevant discussion, including barriers to implementation and recommendations for future research based on our findings. By doing so, we hope this scoping review will be useful for future research in improving AI performance and providing a reference for clinical practice policy regarding how AI could be used to help radiologists.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients will not be involved in this scoping review. Patient consent is not applicable.

Limitations

This is a scoping review; evidence evaluation and critical appraisal are not involved in our study, which may lead to a potential bias. Meanwhile, grey literature and studies in other languages are not involved, and we may miss a small number of relevant studies.

Ethics and dissemination

This scoping review protocol is defined by the exclusion of research involving human or animal subjects, and data derived; consequently, ethical approval is not a prerequisite for this study. Subsequent to the publication of this protocol, the scoping review itself will be executed. The findings of this review will then be disseminated through publication in an open-access journal within the domain of biomedical informatics. Any deviations from the present protocol will be documented within the outcome document, including the date of the modification and a corresponding justification.
