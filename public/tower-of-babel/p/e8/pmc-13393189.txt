Free-Standing Polyaniline-Carbon Nanotube Films via Nonsolvent-Induced Precipitation–Filtration for Electromagnetic Interference Shielding
Authors: Yub Narayan Thapa, Bimal Rajchal, Deepshikha Karki, Sonam Tamang, Emre Kizilay, Ralf Lach, Ulrike Staudinger, Bhim Prasad Kafle, Rameshwar Adhikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Free-standing polyaniline (PANI)-multiwalled carbon nanotube (CNT) nanocomposite films were prepared via nonsolvent-induced precipitation followed by a vacuum filtration (NIP-F) method. The slow nonsolvent precipitation of PANI facilitated the formation of a well-interconnected PANI-CNT conductive network, with good electrical conductivity ranging from 19.0–60.7 S cm–1. The nanocomposite films (0.019–0.052 mm thick) demonstrated remarkable electromagnetic shielding effectiveness (SE) across the X-band region, with the thickest film achieving an average total shielding effectiveness (SET) of 33.1 dB. Notably, the thinnest film exhibited a specific SET of 1182.5 dB/mm, indicating high SE per unit thickness rather than an intrinsic shielding capability independent of thickness. The NIP-F approach yielded thin films with high electrical conductivity and electromagnetic interference shielding performance. These nanocomposite films with SE exceeding the 20 dB threshold required for commercial EMI shielding applications are suitable for electronic devices where lightweight and thin shielding are of primary concern.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of electronic and communication devices has made them an indispensable part of modern life. Their escalating use has imposed a major issue of electromagnetic radiation pollution, commonly referred to as electromagnetic interference (EMI). This poses significant challenges for both device functionality and human health. − This issue spans from everyday consumer electronics to critical aerospace, combat equipment, and telecommunications systems, establishing it as a global concern. − EMI shielding materials attenuate these radiations through three primary mechanisms: reflection loss, absorption loss, and internal multiple reflections. , Conventionally, metals such as copper, aluminum, and zinc remain the most widely used EMI shielding materials. However, their high density, substantial weight, susceptibility to corrosion, and processing challenges demand the development of alternative materials. , Furthermore, metal-based shields rely predominantly on a reflection mechanism, meaning that reflected radiation continues to pose significant risks to adjacent devices. , Metals like gold, silver, and copper possess a reflection loss of >99.99%. By contrast, polymer-based materials present distinct advantages, including flexibility, low density, and corrosion resistance, positioning them as viable alternatives to conventional metallic shields for advanced electronic devices. − They fall into two categories: (1) intrinsically conductive polymers (ICPs) such as polyaniline, polypyrrole, and polythiophene and (2) conductive polymer composites comprising insulating polymer matrices (e.g., polyurethane, polystyrene) filled with conductive materials including MXenes, carbon-based nanomaterials, and metal nanoparticles. Among ICPs, polyaniline (PANI) stands out owing to its good electrical conductivity, facile synthesis, and environmental stability. ,

Tan et al. reported the shielding effectiveness (SE) of PANI doped with various concentrations of hydrochloric acid (HCl), synthesized through chemical oxidative polymerization. They found a total SE ranging from 20 to 25 dB when using a HCl concentration between 0.5 and 2.0 M. The highest total SE was observed in the sample doped with 1 M HCl. Similarly, Saini and Arora synthesized doped PANI nanostructures via chemical oxidative polymerization in the presence of various organic sulfonic acids (dodecyl benzenesulfonic acid; DBSA, D-camphor-10-sulfonic acid; CSA, cardanol azophenylsulfonic acid; CDSA and lignin sulfonic acid; LSA). The sample doped with DBSA possessed the highest electrical conductivity of 2.11 S cm–1, also achieving the highest SE of −55.0 dB for a pellet with a thickness of approximately 2.7 mm. Similarly, other samples demonstrated SE values that were correlated directly with their electrical conductivity measurements.

Since SE primarily depends on electrical conductivity, incorporating CNTs offers a viable approach to enhancing performance. Various synthesis methods have been developed for PANI-CNT composites, including electrochemical polymerization, in situ chemical oxidative polymerization, inverse emulsion polymerization, interfacial plasma polymerization, and physical blending. Among them, the most common ones are the in situ chemical oxidative polymerization and physical blending. The in situ polymerization typically forms core–shell morphology where PANI nucleates and grows on the CNT surfaces. This offers intimate PANI-CNT contact, reducing interfacial contact resistance and improving electrical conductivity. However, a longer polymerization time increases the electrical resistance. Additionally, scale-up is also challenging. Pal et al. demonstrated enhancement in both electrical conductivity and SE by incorporating CNTs into PANI synthesized via in situ chemical polymerization. The neat PANI pellet (2 mm) exhibited a total SE of approximately 26 dB in the X band (8.2–12.4 GHz), while the PANI-CNT composite of the same dimension displayed a significantly improved SE of 48 dB with 8 wt % CNT loading. Similar observations were reported by Mostaani and co-workers, who demonstrated a direct correlation between enhanced EMI shielding and increased electrical conductivity achieved through higher CNT loadings in PANI-CNT composites. They recorded a total SE of approximately 60 dB in the X band for a 4 mm-thick pellet containing 7 wt % CNTs. On the contrary, the physical blending is a facile and scalable approach to the fabrication of blends and composites. Kumar et al. prepared functionalized f-MWCNT/PANI composites by a solution mixing approach and estimated a far-field SE of approximately 12 dB at 3.3 vol % f-MWCNT, based on shielding theory. Despite its simplicity and rapid process, the high agglomeration level is likely to result in nonuniform networks and poor EMI shielding performance.

Although these composites for EMI shielding have been widely investigated, existing approaches predominantly yield powdered materials requiring subsequent pelletization. PANI-CNT composites in pellet form suffer from several limitations, including poor mechanical integrity, lack of flexibility, space constraints, and limited processability. Integrating these composites into thermoplastic substrates effectively overcomes these limitations, and substantial progress has been made in this direction. For instance, Sobha et al. developed a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU)-based composite incorporating both PANI and CNT. Their 1 mm-thick composite film exhibited good mechanical properties and achieved a total SE of 31.35 dB in the X band (8–12 GHz) at an 8% filler loading. Similarly, Saini and Choudhary fabricated polystyrene (PS)/PANI/MWCNT composites through solution processing and achieved a total SE of −23.3 dB in the X-band at 7% MWCNT loading.

While incorporating PANI-CNT composites into thermoplastic substrates provides mechanical support and flexibility, this approach inherently suffers from reduced electrical conductivity because of the insulating nature of the polymer substrate. Substrate-free PANI-CNT films offer a promising alternative, maintaining high electrical conductivity while eliminating the need for an insulating substrate. Herein, we report a facile approach for fabricating free-standing PANI-CNT nanocomposite films as EMI shielding materials through nonsolvent-induced precipitation of a solution of presynthesized PANI followed by vacuum filtration (NIP-F). To the best of our knowledge, this approach to EMI-shielding materials has not been reported previously. It offers efficient removal of solvents with high boiling points, rapid processing, and direct production of mechanically robust, free-standing thin films that eliminate the need for pelletization or supporting polymer substrates. Additionally, it provides a scalable route to dense, well-connected conductive networks with a tunable microstructure and potentially enhanced shielding performance.

Experimental Section

Materials

Multiwalled carbon nanotubes (Nanocyl NC7000) with an average diameter of 9.5 nm, an average length of 1.5 μm, a carbon purity of 90%, and a surface area of 250–300 m2/g were obtained from Nanocyl SA (Sambreville, Belgium). Aniline (≥99.0%), ammonium persulfate (≥98%), and hydrochloric acid (≥37%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, and were used without further purification. The organic solvents acetone (≥99.5%), isopropanol (≥99.8%), and N-methyl-2-pyridilone (NMP) (≥99.0%) were also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, and used as received.

Synthesis of Polyaniline

Ammonium peroxydisulfate (APS) solution was prepared by dissolving 12.3 g (0.054 mol) of APS in 70 mL of 1 M aqueous hydrochloric acid (aq. HCl) solution. Similarly, an aniline hydrochloride solution was prepared by dissolving 5.0 g (0.054 mol) of aniline in 75 mL of 1 M aq. HCl solution. Both solutions were cooled in an ice bath to maintain the temperature below 5 °C. APS was then added dropwise into the aniline hydrochloride solution with constant stirring in a magnetic stirrer. The reaction mixture was stirred for 3 h at temperatures below 5 °C, and the resulting polyaniline emeraldine salt (PANI-ES) was filtered using a Büchner funnel. The precipitate was then washed with distilled water until the filtrate became clear followed by washing with acetone and drying under vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h. Consequently, PANI-ES was converted to the emeraldine base form (PANI-EB), for which, approximately 5.0 g of PANI-ES was treated with 100 mL of 24% (w/v) aqueous ammonia solution for 1 h. The resulting dispersion was filtered using a Büchner funnel, and the collected filter cake was washed with distilled water. The cake was partially dried on the funnel under suction for 30 min and then dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 48 h.

Preparation of the Nanocomposite Film via NIP-F

0.5 g of PANI-EB was dissolved by adding a little at a time in regular intervals in 100 mL of N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) with constant magnetic stirring for approximately 24 h. The dispersion was subsequently subjected to bath sonication (Sonorex TK 30 bath sonicator (Bandelin, Germany)) for 1 h followed by 5 min of ultrasonic horn dispersion at a 100% cycle and an amplitude of 50% using an UP400S ultrasonic processor (Hielscher Ultrasonics GmbH, Teltow, Germany) equipped with an H3 sonotrode. To separate the undissolved polyaniline particles, the dispersion was then filtered using folded filters of grade 1289 (Sartorius Stedim Biotech). Different amounts of CNTs were dispersed in 10 mL of PANI-EB solution by horn sonication for 5 min (100% cycles and 50% amplitude). A 100 mL mixture of acetone and isopropanol (optimized ratio of 6:4 by volume) as a nonsolvent was added slowly to the dispersion to induce precipitation. The whole mixture was further bath sonicated for 1.5 h followed by horn sonication for 5 min (100% cycles and 50% amplitude). The dispersion was filtered using a 1.2 μm pore size polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane. After the filtration, the wet nanocomposite in the membrane was sandwiched immediately between the Teflon sheet and filter papers and dried flat at room temperature overnight. The self-standing PANI-EB-CNT film was then peeled off from the membrane and placed between the Teflon sheets and further dried at 45 °C in a vacuum oven for 12 h to remove the residual solvent. Finally, the film was treated with 1 M aq. HCl solution to convert PANI-EB to PANI-ES. A schematic illustration outlining the stepwise synthesis of the nanocomposite film is presented in Figure . Hereafter, “polyaniline (PANI)” refers to PANI-ES unless stated otherwise. For comparison, a neat CNT film was prepared using the same procedure, but without adding PANI. The nanocomposite films were designated C x PANI, where x denotes the mass of CNTs (mg) added per 10 mL of 0.5% (w/v) PANI solution. Accordingly, C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI correspond to 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg of CNTs, respectively.

Schematic diagram of the synthesis of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films via the NIP-F method.

Characterization Techniques

Structural and Morphological Characterization

A Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Vertex80v) equipped with a golden gate diamond ATR unit (SPECAC) and an MCT detector was used to investigate the interactions between PANI and CNTs. Spectra were recorded over the range 4000–600 cm–1 at a resolution of 4 cm–1. Each sample was averaged over 100 scans to ensure a high signal-to-noise ratio and good reproducibility.

Raman spectra were acquired by using a WITec alpha 300 R confocal Raman imaging system. A 532 nm laser with a power of 0.5 mW was used for excitation. A 20× objective was used to focus the laser onto the sample and to collect the scattered light. Spectra were acquired with an integration time of 0.5 s and were averaged over 200 accumulations.

The crystal structures of the samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8 Discover) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1540 nm). Diffraction patterns were collected over 2θ values of 5–60° with a step size of 0.02°.

The morphologies of nanocomposite films were examined using a NEON40 scanning electron microscope (SEM; Carl Zeiss Microscopy Deutschland GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany) operated at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. Prior to imaging, the samples were sputter-coated with a 3 nm Pt layer to minimize charging. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were acquired by using a Talos F200i microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) operated at 200 kV. For this, the films were embedded in EpoFix epoxy resin (Struers, Ballerup, Denmark), and ultrathin sections, approximately 60 nm-thick, were obtained via ultramicrotomy operated at room temperature (UC6 Ultramicrotome, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) using a diamond knife.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

Thermal properties of the samples were studied using a TGA Q5000 analyzer (TA Instruments, Waters) equipped with a platinum high temperature (Pt-Ht) pan. TGA was performed by holding the sample isothermally for 10 min under a nitrogen flow of 100 mL min–1 followed by heating at 10 °C min–1 to 700 °C under nitrogen at a constant flow rate of 50 mL min–1.

Microhardness of Nanocomposite Films

The deformation behavior of nanocomposite films under load was evaluated by microindentation using a Fischerscope H100C microhardness tester equipped with a Vickers pyramidal diamond indenter. A maximum force of 2 mN was applied at a loading rate of 0.1 mN/s (with the same unloading rate). Surface hardness parameters were determined according to ISO 14577–1, including Martens hardness (HM), indentation modulus (E IT/(1 – ν2) (ν is Poisson’s ratio)), and maximum indentation depth (h max). For each specimen, 5–10 measurements were performed at randomly selected locations, and the median values were reported.

Electrical Conductivity

The volume resistivity of the neat CNT and the nanocomposite films was measured using a Keithley 2001 digital multimeter (DMM 2001) connected to a custom-built four-point probe fixture with in-plane gold electrodes (developed by IPF Forschungstechnik, Leibniz-Institut für Polymerforschung Dresden e.V., Dresden, Germany), applying a voltage of 1 V. For each measurement, 10 readings were recorded automatically and averaged. Four independent measurements were performed for each sample, and the arithmetic mean of the resulting resistivity values was used for subsequent analysis. The electrical conductivity of the nanocomposite films was calculated as the reciprocal of the resistivity. The powder conductivity of neat PANI-ES was measured by the in-house powder conductivity measurement setup, as described by Krause et al.

EMI Shielding Effectiveness Measurement

The electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding effectiveness (SE) of the nanocomposite films was measured in the X-band frequency range (8.2–12.4 GHz) using a N5230A PNA Series Network Analyzer. Two standard WR-90 waveguide coaxial launchers were used as the transmitter and receiver, and the films were placed between the waveguide sections using a copper sample holder. The system was fully calibrated before the measurements. The scattering parameters, namely, the reflection coefficient (S 11) and transmission coefficient (S 21), were directly recorded. The total shielding effectiveness (SET) was calculated from the measured transmission coefficient according to eq : SET=−20log10|S21|(dB) 1

Thickness measurements were performed at 10 different positions on each film using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo, 0.001 mm resolution), and the average values were used in the analysis.

Results and Discussion

Nonsolvent Precipitation of PANI

The precipitation rate and solvent exchange kinetics are critical in governing the resulting morphology and conductive pathways. The nonsolvent composition was carefully selected to control the precipitation of PANI from its solution. A higher acetone ratio in the mixed nonsolvent led to rapid precipitation (initiating at about 25 s for a 9:1 acetone:isopropanol ratio by volume). Such rapid precipitation likely provided insufficient time for PANI particles to organize within the CNT network. Moreover, rapid evaporation of the nonsolvent from the composite film surface caused fine cracks on it. In contrast, a 6:4 ratio provided slower precipitation (after about 30 min), allowing more controlled precipitation and a lower evaporation rate, thereby improving the film integrity. This optimized condition facilitated a better PANI-CNT conductive network. However, attempts to prepare a smooth, free-standing, pristine PANI film under identical conditions to the composite films were unsuccessful. The precipitated PANI after vacuum filtration could not be peeled off the PTFE membrane filter, unlike the composite films. This observation indicated that PANI alone lacks sufficient structural integrity, while CNT provides the necessary mechanical support for the formation of a free-standing film.

FTIR Analysis

The FTIR spectra of the PANI and a representative nanocomposite film, C20PANI, are depicted in Figure . The spectra exhibited characteristic stretching vibrations of the CC bond of PANI corresponding to the quinone structure (1576 cm–1) and benzene structure (1491 cm–1). , Moreover, the higher intensity band of benzenoid compared to the quinoid indicated the emeraldine salt form of PANI. The peak centered at 3229 cm–1 associated with N–H stretching vibration and a peak at 2928 cm–1 to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the aromatic C–H bonds were recorded, which is consistent with the literature. Similarly, the presence of a peak at 1300 cm–1 represents the stretching vibration of the C–N bond of the quinone structure. The doped state of PANI (emeraldine salt) was confirmed by a sharp band at 1134 cm–1 assigned to the NQN stretching vibration (Q denotes the quinoid ring). , This band indicated the formation of polarons or cation radicals, which are responsible for charge transport in PANI. The peak at 796 cm–1 corresponds to the out-of-plane aromatic C–H bending vibration.

ATR-FTIR spectra of PANI and the PANI-CNT nanocomposite films (C20PANI).

The spectrum of the nanocomposite film also exhibited the characteristic peaks of PANI with a slight change in the intensity and bathochromic shifts. The CC stretching vibrations of the quinone and benzene structures shifted by 20 and 12 cm–1, respectively. Similarly, NQN and out-of-plane aromatic C–H bending vibration shifted by 10 and 88 cm–1, respectively. These red shifts strongly indicate π–π interactions between CNTs and PANI backbones. Particularly, the π-bonded surface of CNTs interacts with the conjugated PANI structure through quinoid rings. These types of interactions promote electron delocalization, which weakens the internal PANI bonds and shifts their stretching and vibrational modes to lower frequencies (red shift).

FTIR analysis confirmed that the PANI present in the nanocomposite film is the emeraldine salt form, which forms strong π–π interactions with CNTs. However, the presence of CNTs is not evident in the FTIR because of their weak infrared activity; instead, it was confirmed independently by Raman analysis.

Raman Analysis

Figure displays the Raman spectra of pure PANI, pristine CNTs, and the representative nanocomposite film, C20PANI. The major bands of the polyaniline emeraldine salt of HCl are observed at 1349 and 1569 cm–1, representing C–N+ radical stretching vibration in protonated PANI and C–C stretching vibrations of quinonoid rings, respectively (inset, Figure ). Similarly, the characteristic D peak of disordered carbon at 1346 cm–1, the highly ordered carbon structure represented by the G peak at 1584 cm–1 and 2D band at 2693 cm–1 in pristine, CNTs were recorded. This result is consistent with the literature. All the nanocomposite films demonstrated similar peaks. The characteristic bands of PANI and CNTs were present in the nanocomposite spectra with only slight shifts in peak positions. The D band remained essentially unchanged (1346 cm–1 for CNT vs 1347 cm–1 for the nanocomposite film), whereas the G band showed a pronounced upshift from 1584 to 1599 cm–1. This shift suggests strong π–π* interactions between PANI and CNTs at the graphitic regions of the CNTs without indicating structural damage. The in-plane C–H bending of the quinonoid ring at 1175 cm–1 and a shoulder peak corresponding to the N–H vibration mode of protonated amine at 1497 cm–1 provided further evidence for the presence of PANI in the film. Moreover, the increased G band intensity in the nanocomposite indicated strong interactions between PANI and CNTs. The I D/I G ratio of the nanocomposite (0.89) was close to that of PANI (0.91) but lower than that of neat CNTs (1.30), suggesting that CNT defects were partially masked by the PANI coating. This supports the deposition of PANI onto the CNT network.

XRD Analysis

Figure shows the XRD patterns of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films and the corresponding patterns of pristine CNT and PANI in the inset. The diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 25.7° and 43° of the graphitic structure of CNTs correspond to (002) and (110) planes of CNTs. Similarly, PANI exhibited diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 8.9°, 15.2°, 20.7°, 25.2°, and 26.9°, which can be indexed to the (001), (011), (020), (200), and (121) planes, respectively, characteristic of polyaniline emeraldine salt. , The peak at 20.7° corresponds to the periodicity parallel to the polymer chain, whereas the peaks at 15.2° and 25.2° correspond to the periodicity perpendicular to the polymer chain. ,

X-ray diffraction (XRD) of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films; the inset shows the corresponding patterns of PANI and CNT.

The diffraction patterns of the nanocomposite films were dominated by CNT reflections, while PANI contributed only broad, poorly defined features. Similar behavior has been reported for PANI-CNT nanocomposites in the literature, irrespective of the synthesis route. Specifically, peak broadening in the ∼15–40° range suggests overlap between the amorphous PANI halo and the CNT reflections. Such broad diffraction peaks indicate the low crystallinity and largely amorphous nature of PANI. The dissolution–precipitation process likely disrupts initially ordered chain domains, rendering PANI more amorphous. Moreover, the intensity of the CNT diffraction peak at 25.7° increased progressively from C5PANI to C20PANI, reflecting the enhanced crystallinity with increasing CNT content.

Electron Microscopic Analyses

The SEM micrographs presented in Figure illustrate the arrangement of PANI and CNTs at the microstructural level and their interaction, leading to various properties. Additionally, an SEM image of the PANI powder is included in the Supporting Information (Figure S1) for morphological comparison. Well-dispersed CNTs forming a porous, continuous, and randomly oriented tubular structure were observed in the nanocomposite films. The precipitated PANI was deposited on the entangled CNTs, forming a cohesive nanocomposite film, as corroborated by the Raman analysis. The nanocomposite with a lower CNT content (C5PANI) exhibited a higher degree of PANI coverage around the CNT bundles, as expected; however, the deposition was not uniform throughout the network. As the CNT content increased while the PANI content was kept constant, the extent of PANI deposition decreased, as evidenced by the higher CNT density and smaller interfibril spacing observed in C20PANI. The deposited PANI likely promotes the formation of conductive bridges at the junctions within the CNT networks. This bridging morphology reduces the inter-CNT charge-hopping barrier and enhances electrical conductivity, which is further confirmed by the resistivity measurements. To further evaluate the internal microstructures of the composite films, TEM analysis was performed on C5PANI and C20PANI (Figure ). In both samples, dark interconnected filamentous structures corresponding to CNTs were observed. In addition, regions of weaker contrast surrounding the CNT filaments and bundles were visible and are tentatively assigned to PANI. These PANI-related regions appeared more pronounced in C5PANI than in C20PANI, qualitatively indicating a higher degree of PANI coverage at lower CNT loading. This observation is consistent with the higher PANI-to-CNT ratio in C5PANI, where a fixed amount of PANI is distributed over a smaller number of CNTs. STEM-EDX nitrogen elemental mapping (Figure S2) was further used to assess the spatial distribution of PANI within the composite films, as nitrogen is characteristic of PANI and is absent from the CNT network. The nitrogen signal was detected throughout the scanned area, confirming the presence of PANI across the CNT network and supporting SEM and TEM observations. However, variations in the nitrogen signal intensity indicate that precipitated PANI was not uniformly distributed within the composite.

Thermal Stability

The thermal degradation behavior of PANI, CNTs, and the nanocomposites was investigated by TGA. The weight loss profiles and their corresponding first derivative curves (DTG) are presented in Figure a,b, respectively. During the initial isothermal hold at room temperature under a nitrogen flow, PANI and the nanocomposite films exhibited a weight loss of 2.2–4.6 wt % (Figure a). Analysis of the TG curves revealed three prominent inflection points, indicating that decomposition in PANI and all nanocomposite films proceeds in three major steps up to 700 °C. These distinct stages are more clearly visible in DTG curves (Figure b). The temperatures at which the maximum rates of mass loss occur (T max), as obtained from DTG curves and the residual weight at the final temperature, are reported in Table . The first maximum decomposition at around 70 °C in all the samples is attributed to the loss of moisture contained in them. After the removal of moisture, the loss of dopants in the PANI took place up to 380 °C and contributed to the weight loss of 14.8%. This temperature in the nanocomposite films was around 300 °C with an average weight loss of 7.8%. The major weight loss in PANI occurred at 523 °C due to the thermal degradation of PANI chains. However, in the nanocomposite films, the major weight loss temperatures were lower than those of pristine PANI. Moreover, the nanocomposites C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI had weight losses of 21.7%, 11.0%, 19.8%, and 7.2%, respectively, at the final temperature of the third decomposition step. Clearly, there is no specific trend in weight loss amount and temperature as a function of CNT content in the nanocomposites. Different amounts of volatile impurities (components) contained in the samples are likely to cause this observed inconsistency. Thermally stable CNTs in the nanocomposites are expected to increase the T max. However, lower degradation temperature may be attributed to the residual catalysts being used during the synthesis of CNTs, which facilitated the degradation of the nanocomposites earlier.

Microhardness of Nanocomposite Films

Figure presents the surface hardness properties of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films obtained from microindentation measurements. Martens hardness (HM) median values of 3.0, 4.8, 18.7, and 2.8 N/mm2 were recorded for C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI, respectively. A similar trend was observed for the indentation modulus (E IT/(1 – ν2)), with median values of 94, 175, 557, and 73 MPa recorded for C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI, respectively. Regarding the observed consistency between Martens hardness and indentation modulus, it has to be pointed out that Martens hardness and indentation modulus were calculated by using very different parts of the load–indentation curves: Whereas the Martens hardness was calculated from the maximum load during loading, the indentation modulus was calculated from the slope of the load–indentation curves during unloading. Furthermore, all load–indentation depth curves exhibited qualitatively the same general shape. Consistent with hardness measurements, the maximum indentation depth (h max) median values were 4.8, 3.7, 1.9, and 4.9 μm for C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI, respectively.

Microhardness properties of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films with varying CNT content (median values and scatter bands).

The relatively poor mechanical properties of pristine PANI are typically improved by incorporating CNTs. , The enhanced HM observed for C5PANI, C10PANI, and C15PANI can be attributed to effective load transfer between the CNTs and the PANI matrix, enabled by the reinforcing effect of well-dispersed CNTs. , Especially for C15PANI, the favorable combination of optimum load transfer and well-dispersed CNTs results in the best mechanical performance (highest values of HM and E IT/(1 – ν2)) of all materials, as confirmed by the ANOVA (analysis of variance) test (significance level α = 0.05). However, at higher concentrations, CNTs tend to agglomerate due to strong van der Waals interactions, forming clusters that act as stress concentration points and weak zones. Additionally, agglomeration causes an inhomogeneous distribution, creating PANI-rich and CNT-rich regions. This leads to inconsistent, highly scattered (and strongly skewed, see Figure ) data, and, ultimately, degraded mechanical properties. This accounts for the reduced HM observed for C20PANI (similar to that of C5PANI). The lower h max of C15PANI indicates higher resistance to penetration, whereas the increased h max of C20PANI further confirms reduced mechanical reinforcement due to CNT agglomeration.

Electrical Conductivity

The powder conductivity of the synthesized neat PANI-ES used for the fabrication of the nanocomposite film was recorded to be 0.097 ± 0.01 S cm–1 at 30 MPa, confirming its conductive phase. The electrical conductivity of pristine CNT and nanocomposite films is presented in Figure . The pristine CNT film exhibited an electrical conductivity of 35.7 S cm–1, while C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI exhibited 19.0, 28.9, 42.2, and 60.7 S cm–1, respectively. Although C5PANI and C10PANI displayed lower conductivity compared to the pristine CNT film, a progressive increase in conductivity was observed with increasing CNT content, ultimately exceeding that of the pure CNT film. At lower CNT loadings, the precipitation of low-conducting PANI on the CNT network introduces higher contact resistance at the CNT junctions, thereby reducing the overall conductivity. Conversely, at higher CNT content, the relatively lower PANI proportion diminishes contact resistance, facilitating the formation of efficient three-dimensional (3D) charge transport pathways and enhanced electrical conductivity. PANI, when present at CNT interfaces, experiences interfacial ordering and planarization initiated by the intimate PANI-CNT contacts and dopant interactions. At the junction, π–π interactions between PANI quinoid units and the CNT sidewalls (as evidenced by Raman) reduce the effective tunnelling barriers at the junctions, allowing PANI to function as a conductive bridge. This conjugation facilitates charge delocalization across the PANI-CNT interface and enables rapid electron transfer throughout the interconnected network, thereby enhancing the electrical conductivity. −

EMI Shielding Performance

EMI shielding effectiveness (SE) describes the ability of a material to attenuate incident electromagnetic radiation and reduce its transmission. The total shielding effectiveness (SET) is commonly expressed as the sum of contributions from reflection (SER), absorption (SEA), and multiple internal reflections (SEM) , SET=SER+SEA+SEM 2

For ultrathin conductive films, the contribution from multiple internal reflections is generally negligible and SET can be reliably evaluated directly from the transmission coefficient.

Figure shows the frequency-dependent SET behavior of the nanocomposite films across the X band. All samples exhibited effective attenuation of the transmitted electromagnetic waves, and a clear improvement in shielding performance was observed from C5PANI to C20PANI over the entire frequency range. Based on the SET values averaged across the X band, C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI and C20PANI resulted in average SET values of 22.9, 26.8, 32.1, and 33.1 dB, respectively. This exhibited an increasing trend of absolute SET value, increasing from C5PANI to C20PANI with increasing CNT content. Additionally, thickness-normalized SET was also calculated to evaluate the material’s efficiency. The average film thickness of C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI and C20PANI was recorded to be 0.019, 0.035, 0.042, and 0.052 mm, respectively, with corresponding thickness-normalized average SET of 1182.5, 748.3, 748.3, and 629.1 dB/mm. The thickness-normalized SET exhibited a reverse trend, with C5PANI yielding the highest value owing to its very small thickness. The exceptionally high thickness-normalized SET of the thinnest C5PANI film is attributed to enhanced absorption within a shorter propagation path and favorable impedance matching at the measurement frequency range. This is a comparative metric and not an intrinsic property independent of thickness or testing configuration (frequency, geometry, mounting and measurement setup).

Frequency-dependent total shielding effectiveness (SET) of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films with varying CNT contents measured in the X-band.

The increase in absolute SET trend indicates a systematic enhancement in EMI shielding performance with increasing CNT content and film thickness. Higher CNT concentrations enhance electrical conductivity by introducing additional charge carriers that interact with and dissipate incident EM radiation, thereby improving SE. Moreover, the incorporation of CNTs increases the heterogeneity in the nanocomposites that enhances the dielectric permittivity, thereby improving the shielding. Further, an increase in CNT loading reduces the PANI layer between the CNTs, which is corroborated by the SEM micrographs. This facilitates a higher density of free electrical charges, which enhances the polarization within the nanocomposite’s dielectric layer and leads to enhanced SE.

Surface Resistance and Shielding Mechanism

The surface impedance of the composite films was extracted from the measured S parameters obtained using the WR-90 waveguide setup. For the dominant TE10 mode, the waveguide impedance was calculated as ZTE = ωμ0/β, where β = [k 0 2 – (π/a)2]­1/2, k 0 is the free-space wavenumber, and a is the broad-wall dimension of the WR-90 waveguide. The complex surface impedance was then estimated from the measured S 11 and S 21 parameters. The surface resistance and surface reactance were obtained from the real and imaginary components of the complex surface impedance, respectively, according to Z s = R s + jX s. Since surface resistance is a non-negative physical quantity, R s was taken as the absolute value of the real part of Z s, |Re­(Z s)|, while X s was obtained from the imaginary part of Z s, Im­(Z s).

The frequency-dependent surface resistance (R s) is presented in Figure . The average R s values were approximately 0.39, 0.37, 0.08, and 0.22 Ω/sq for C5PANI, C10PANI, C15PANI, and C20PANI, respectively.

Frequency-dependent surface resistance (R s) of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films of varying CNT content extracted from waveguide S-parameter measurements.

An inverse relationship between R s and SET was observed across the samples. Films with lower surface resistance generally exhibited higher SE, suggesting that surface-related conductive losses make an important contribution to the EMI shielding behavior of these ultrathin composite films. As a result, bulk dielectric and magnetic contributions are expected to be limited.

The EMI shielding mechanism can be better understood by considering how the incident electromagnetic wave interacts with the conductive film. When the wave reaches the film surface, it can induce surface currents within the conductive network. These currents are then dissipated as resistive Joule losses, contributing to the attenuation of the electromagnetic wave. Because the films are ultrathin, this interaction mainly takes place at the surface rather than within the bulk of the material. Therefore, surface-related conductive losses are considered to be an important part of the overall shielding behavior.

A transition from capacitive to inductive behavior was observed with increasing frequency. Contribution of Shielding Loss Components

The SE was further evaluated in terms of reflection (SER) and absorption (SEA) using an S parameter-based reflection/transmission coefficient approach to better understand the shielding mechanism. In this analysis, the reflection and transmission coefficients were first obtained from the measured S parameters using R = |S 11|2 and T = | S 21|2. The absorption contribution was then estimated as A=1−R−T 3

Since the films are very thin, with thicknesses between 0.019 and 0.052 mm, separating SET into its reflection and absorption components can be affected by small variations in the measured S parameters and by reference-plane effects. For this reason, SER and SEA were used only to support the qualitative discussion of the shielding mechanism, while SET was taken as the main quantitative parameter for comparing the films. To provide a more complete evaluation, the full S11 data and the corresponding SER and SEA curves are included in the Supporting Information (Table S1 and Figures S3 and S4, respectively).

Reflection is expected to contribute to the shielding response because of impedance mismatch at the film surface. However, absorption-related losses can also occur as the films have finite surface resistance and do not behave like perfect conductors. Therefore, part of the incident electromagnetic wave can still interact with the conductive network of the composites. The surface currents induced in this network are dissipated through resistive Joule losses, which contribute to the absorption-related part of the shielding. In addition, the air/film/waveguide structure includes multiple interfaces, which may lead to additional scattering and energy dissipation and, therefore, support the overall attenuation.

Although the thin and flexible films exhibited modest absolute SET within the X band, the thickness-normalized SET was exceptional compared to most of the nanocomposites reported in the literature. However, it does not represent a true intrinsic material property, as EMI shielding does not scale linearly with thickness due to skin depth and attenuation effects. Instead, this metric is used here to provide only a comparative parameter to account for thickness variations. These nanocomposite films with SE exceeding the 20 dB threshold required for general commercial shielding applications are suitable candidates for applications where thin and lightweight shielding is preferred. Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrates that nonsolvent-induced precipitation followed by a vacuum filtration (NIP-F) approach is an effective method for fabricating free-standing PANI-CNT nanocomposite films with strong PANI-CNT interactions.i.The efficient shielding performance (SET = 22.9–33.1 dB) achieved at electrical conductivity (19.0–60.7 S cm–1) suggests that the NIP-F method promotes the formation of an effective conductive network. The low surface resistance and high SE indicate that surface current dissipation is the dominant shielding mechanism in the thin films. Furthermore, the transition from capacitive to inductive behavior in the reactive response with increasing frequency indicates consistent electromagnetic performance of the films across the X band.ii.The superior thickness-normalized SE (1182.5 dB/mm) achieved at low CNT loading further supports that the shielding performance is not solely governed by electrical conductivity but also by the effective interaction of precipitated PANI with well-dispersed CNTs.iii.The findings broaden the EMI shielding potential of PANI-CNT nanocomposite films and establish NIP-F as an alternative strategy for fabricating lightweight, thin, flexible, and free-standing films for effective EMI shielding. Improving the mechanical robustness, optimizing the hierarchical CNT network, and conductive network density to achieve better impedance matching could enhance the efficiency of such nanocomposite films. Quantitative evaluation of mechanical flexibility and conductivity retention under repeated deformations remains an important direction for future work. Incorporating magnetic materials could be a promising route to enhance absorption-dominant EMI-shielding performance while reducing secondary EM radiation.iv.In addition, a systematic study of how the precipitation rate affects film morphology and electrical conductivity would help establish clearer structure–property relationships and guide the optimization of SE.
