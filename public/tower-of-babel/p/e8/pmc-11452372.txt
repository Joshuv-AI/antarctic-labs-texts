Effects of physical refining process on quality and stability of argan oil (Argania Spinosa (L.) Skeels)
Authors: Abderrahim Asbbane, Zahra Bousaid, Dominique Guillaume, Said El Harkaoui, Bertrand Matthäus, Zoubida Charrouf, Khang Wen Goh, Nanthida Srasom, Abdelahakim Bouayahya, Saïd Gharby
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubquality argan kernels are 30% cheaper than the regular kernels mandatory used to prepare edible argan oil. The use of these argan kernels for the preparation of argan oil intended to be a cosmetic ingredient, after bleaching and deodorization, is therefore particularly economically appealing. The oxidative stability of Argan oil prepared from subquality kernels is unknown. It was evaluated over a period of storage of 12 weeks at 60 °C, then compared with that of argan oil stored under the same conditions and originating from the same initial batch, but which had subsequently been simply bleached and deodorized (physical refining). Physical refining led to an increase in initial oil quality due the loss of free fatty acids (up to 30% for refined argan oil), primary and secondary oxidation products but also to a dramatic decrease of the oxidative stability of argan oil caused by the loss of tocopherols, witnessed by the up to 94% loss after 12 weeks under accelerated storage conditions. As a conclusion, the oxidative stability of argan oil prepared from subquality argan kernels remains difficult to be adequately and efficiently evaluated since the initial quality of the argan kernels is the subject of great variations.Keywords: Hydroperoxide, Oil preservation, Primary oxidation product, Schaal oven test, Secondary oxidation productSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry

Introduction

An argan grove sufficiently large to produce enough argan oil to meet global demand only grows in Morocco. Thus far, neither the endemic argan forest of the Hamada of Tindouf (Algeria), nor the argan trees introduced and cultivated in the regions of Ashkelon, Arava and the Negev (Israel), represent a production large enough to be considered as a key player on the global argan oil market, so far. Therefore, argan oil commercialized worldwide is exclusively produced from argan fruits harvested in Morocco. Over the past 30 years, the argan oil market has rocketed, and argan oil has grown from a cheap and low-quality oil exclusively sold locally to an extremely expensive and highly prized globally-sold oil1. Nowadays, because of its numerous beneficial nutritional, dermatological and cosmetic properties2,3, as well as its specific physicochemical profile compared to other recently introduced high-valued oils4,5, argan oil is particularly sought after in both the food and cosmetic markets. Any decrease in pressure on the argan kernel market should, at least temporarily, stabilize the price of edible and cosmetic argan oil.

Since January 2010, Argan oil has benefited from a Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) which specifies each single step of its preparation process: from, first, the quality of the usable fruits to, finally, the acceptable extraction techniques. Edible and cosmetic argan oils prepared in accordance with the PGI recommendations (PGI-oil or PGI-O) are extracted by cold-pressing of roasted or unroasted argan kernels, respectively, originating from harvested unpeeled argan fruits that have been subsequently cautiously air-dried6and mechanically peeled7. Fruits harvested partially or totally peeled, and particularly those peeled by goat-digestion must be mandatorily discarded. Edible argan oil is the basic ingredient of the Amazigh diet8, while cosmetic argan oil is intended to be applied directly to the skin or used as an ingredient in the cosmetics industry. The oxidative stability of cosmetic argan oil is inherently lower than that of edible argan oil9 since optimized kernel roasting has a positive impact on argan oil oxidative behavior10,11.

The kernel resource is inevitably limited so its price increases as demand increases. Therefore, the introduction of low-grade argan kernels (from broken, partially peeled, or goat-peeled fruits), which are currently resolutely discarded and do not enter into the PGI-O preparation circuit, and the price of which is 30 to 50% lower than that of PGI-grade argan kernels, has been suggested to prepare an argan oil intended exclusively to be incorporated as an ingredient by the cosmetics industry. Such suggestion is reinforced by the fact that manufacturers systematically refine argan oil before introducing it in cosmetics to remove its color and aroma12. Refining is a succession of treatments (physical and chemical) whose order and number, when correctly adapted, deliver an oil of defined degree of refining. An indiscriminate refining would possibly cause the total disappearance of substances essential for cosmetic activity (tocopherols, phospholipids, and phytosterols)13,14, and the appearance of unwanted substances (3-chloropropane-1,2-diol (MCDP) esters, trans-fatty acids)15,16. As a result, the refining process must ensure minimal loss of the bioactive components17,18and the cosmetics industry utilized selective refining conditions optimized for argan oil in terms of temperature19, pressure and contact time with filters that allow the discoloration/deodorization of argan oil while minimizing the loss of those essential substances.

Indeed, Argan oil is particularly rich in oxidation-sensitive unsaturated fatty acids. Immediately after its vapor-induced deodorization, argan oil prepared from subquality argan kernels presents a fatty acid profile and an oxidative profile similar to those of argan oil prepared from GPI-kernels12. However, the cosmetics industry frequently not only deodorizes argan oil but also bleached it prior to deodorization to avoid any coloring of cosmetic preparations. While refining may dramatically affect oil stability12,20, the impact of bleaching on argan oil preservation property is unknown although it is an essential industrial parameter.

To evaluate the impact of bleaching followed or not by a deodorization step on argan oil preservation, the aim of the present work was to prepare a batch of argan oil from subquality kernels. One third of the batch was isolated and analyzed as a blank sample, while two thirds were bleached. Half of the bleached batch was physically deodorized. The oxidative stability property of the three batches was monitored under Schaal oven test conditions at 60 °C over a 12-week period.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Argan oil samples used for this study were provided by Arganisme SARL (Essaouira, Morocco). It was prepared from an initial batch of subquality kernels (26 tons) purchased in 2022–2023. Oil extraction was achieved using a Komet DD 85 G press (IBG Monforts Oekotec GmbH & Co. KG, Mönchengladbach, Germany). Crude argan oil (CAO) was prepared by cold pressing argan fruit considered unsatisfactory for use to prepare PGI-O. Bleached and refined argan oils were prepared following the physical refining. This process takes place in two primary steps: bleaching and deodorization.

The bleaching process was carried out under industrial conditions by filtering CAO through 2 to 4% of a blend of hermit and smectite minerals (Pure-Flo Supreme Pro-Active). The temperature was maintained between 100 and 110 °C, with an average vacuum pressure of 625 mm Hg. The contact time between argan oil and the bleaching earths was up to 40 min. In addition, Celite 545 (10% w/w of the bleaching earth) was used as a filter aid21.

The deodorization process necessary to obtain refined argan oil (RAO, and bleached then deodorized CAO) consisted of injecting steam generated from 50 L of deionized water at 240 °C for 6 h into BAO under a vacuum of 1.5 mm Hg through 1200 L of oil. These conditions are exactly those used in the cosmetics industry12.

For each batch, some argan oil samples were immediately analyzed, the others were poured on 60 mL bottles (headspace volume 1.5) placed in a Memmert UF110 plus oven (Memmert GmbH, Schwabach, Germany) equipped with a Kimo KTT310-RF thermostat, and the temperature of which was kept constant at 60 ± 1 °C. Quality indices (free fatty acid, peroxide value, p-anisidine value, specific extinctions (K232 and K270) were measured every two weeks for 12 weeks. The fatty acid composition and the tocopherols content were qualitatively analyzed after 4, 8 and 12 weeks.

Analytical methods

Standards used for chromatographic analyses were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co (St. Louis, MO, USA). Other reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from Professional Labo (Casablanca, Morocco).

Physicochemical quality parameters, such as free fatty acid (FFA), peroxide value (PV) and p-anisidine value (AnV), and UV specific coefficients of extinction (K232 and K270) were determined according to the official analytical methods22–25 respectively. The FFA of argan oils was determined following ISO 660, 2020. Briefly, 10 g of oil were dissolved in ethyl alcohol and titrated with a 0.1 N NaOH solution, using phenolphthalein as an indicator. The result is expressed in goleic acid in mass percentage of oil (g/100g). PV was determined following ISO 3960, 2017. Firstly, 5 g of oil were dissolved in a 60:40 mixture of acetic acid and isooctane, then reacted with a supersaturated potassium iodide solution. After 5 min in darkness, the mixture was titrated with a 0.01 N sodium thiosulfate solution, using starch as an indicator. The result is expressed in milli-equivalents of active oxygen per kilogram of oil (mEq O2/kg oil). K232 and K270 were determined following ISO 3656, 2011. They were expressed as the specific extinctions of a 1% (w/v) solution of argan oil in cyclohexane (0.20 g of oil dissolved in 20 mL) measured in a 1 cm cuvette, using a SCILOGEX SP- UV1100 spectrometer. The AnV values were determined according to ISO 6885, 2016. In brief, 2 g of oil were dissolved in 25 mL of isooctane, and the absorbance was measured at 350 nm using isooctane as a blank. Then, 5 mL of the solution were mixed with 1 mL of AnV reagent (0.25%, w/v glacial acetic acid). After 10 min of reaction, the absorbance was again measured at 350 nm, using a blank of isooctane and anisidine reagent, in a 1 cm cuvette, using a SCILOGEX SP- UV1100 spectrometer.

Fatty acids composition

Fatty acid composition was determined following official analytical method26. Fatty acids were converted into their fatty acid methyl esters and FA composition was determined as their corresponding methyl esters by gas chromatography (Agilent 6890) on a CPWAX 52CB column (30 m x 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) using helium (He) as a carrier gas (flow rate 1 mL/min) and using a flame ionization detector (FID). One µL of sample was injected at 220 °C with split ratio of 1/50. The oven temperature was set at 180 °C for 5 min and was increased to 220 °C with a 15 °C /min slope. Detection was realized at 230 °C. Results are expressed as the relative percentage of area of each individual fatty acid peak.

Tocopherols content

Tocopherol composition was determined as previously reported27,28. Tocopherol content was determined by HPLC following the official analytical method29 using Shimadzu instruments equipped with a C18-Varian column (25 cm × 4 mm; Varian Inc., Middelburg, Netherlands). A fluorescence detector (excitation wavelength 290 nm, detection wavelength 330 nm) was used; eluent was a 99:1 isooctane/ isopropanol (v/v) mixture, flow rate 1.2 mL/min. Identification was based on retention time, and quantification of tocopherols was carried out using α, γ, and δ tocopherols (Sigma-Aldrich, St-Louis, USA) as external standards, at linear concentrations (r2 > 0.99) of 0.24-38, 0.26-41.2 and 0.26-42 µg/mL, respectively. Results are expressed as mg of tocopherols per kg of oil.

Phosphorus and phospholipids content

Phosphorus content was determined by mineralizing the sample in the presence of nitric acid. Then, the spectrophotometric measurement of phosphorus was performed at the end of the storage time using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 460 nm against a reagent blank. Five calibration curves were obtained using working solutions of KH2PO4 as reported by Szydlowska-Czerniak and Szlyk30. Once the phosphorus content determined, phospholipid content was calculated using the formula: Phospholipid content = Phosphorus content x 815/31.

Colors

Changes in color were determined by transmission measurements using a SCILOGEX SP-UV1100 spectrometer at λ = 420 nm using our previously reported method1.

Statistical analysis

Values reported are the mean values ± SD of three replications, then averaged using MS Excel 2019. The significance level was set at p = 0.05. Separation of mean values was performed by Tukey’s test at the same significance level. Statistical analysis and graphs were drawn using OriginPro 2019.

Results and discussion

The moisture content of the raw material was found to be between 2% and 5%, while the fat content ranged from 50 to 54%. Three types of argan oil were analyzed: crude argan oil (CAO) prepared from cold pressed subquality fruits unsatisfactory for the preparation of PGI-O, bleached crude argan oil (BAO) prepared by filtering CAO through bleaching earths, and finally refined oil (RAO) prepared by bleaching then deodorization of CAO. Analyses were repeated every 4 weeks for 12 weeks of storage at 60 °C. Bleaching was performed using bleaching earths whose sorptive properties allowed the efficient removal of phospholipids, carotenoids, or chlorophylls, as well as peroxides31,32. Deodorization, achieved through the introduction of vapor at 240 °C under a 1.5 mm Hg vacuum, led to the removal of volatile odoriferous components and free fatty acids as well12. The efficiency of the different steps of the refining processes was attested through the measurement of the transmission at λ = 420 nm for each type of oil. Initial transmission of CAO was measured to be 58.8 whereas that of BAO and RAO were measured to be 78.5 and 80.2, respectively. This evidenced the efficient removal of light-absorbing materials during the oil treatment. Interestingly, those values underwent very little variations during the 6 weeks of storage. Indeed, after 6 weeks at 60 °C, transmission of CAO, BAO, and RAO was found to be 56.5, 77.5, and 79.5; respectively.

Variations in free fatty acids content

The introduction of goat-depulped fruits to prepare argan oil is known to increase the free fatty acid content of the resulting oil33,34. The initial fatty acid content of CAO was calculated to be 1.31 g of oleic acid/ 100 g of oil (Fig. 1), about five times higher than the value previously reported for cosmetic PGI-O33,34. Such acid content would lead to a classification of CAO as fine virgin argan oil according to the Moroccan Norm 08.5.090 for argan oil35. During the 12 weeks of storage at 60 °C a progressive and almost linear increase in free fatty acid content of CAO was observed, attesting to a permanent and smooth triacylglycerol hydrolysis. A moderate acceleration of hydrolysis was observed between week-4 and -6. Then the hydrolysis continued to increase steadily, and the free fatty acid content of CAO finally reached 2.44 g of oleic acid/ 100 g of oil after 12 weeks at 60 °C (Fig. 1).

Free fatty acid content of argan oils.

Bleaching is not supposed to be efficient at eliminating free fatty acids. Those latter are eliminated through distillation during the deodorization process, which is consistent with the results of previous studies36,37. In general, bleaching acts on color and removes impurities38. Not surprisingly, BAO presented an initial free fatty acid content almost similar to that of CAO (1.35 g of oleic acid/100 g of oil). Over 12 weeks of storage at 60 °C, the acidity of BAO increased slowly during the first 4 weeks, then much faster between week 4 and week 6 to remain stable after that and finish at a free fatty acid content not significantly different from that of CAO after 12 weeks. The acidity profile of BAO was found similar to that of CAO indicating that bleaching did not significantly change neither the initial free fatty acid content, nor its final value even though the rate of triacylglycerol hydrolysis was found to be slightly increased, compared to CAO, after 4 weeks at 60 °C (Fig. 1).

The initial free fatty acid content of RAO was found to be 30% lower than that of CAO and BAO. The free fatty acid content of RAO reflected the presence of a limited amount of free fatty acids not eliminated during the deodorization process. After 12 weeks at 60 °C, the kinetic acidity of RAO was found to be parallel to that of BAO and CAO. Since in the experimental conditions, the kinetic of triacylglycerol hydrolysis is almost identical in CAO, BAO, and RAO, it indicates that the compounds eliminated during bleaching and deodorization have no influence on the kinetic of the triacylglycerol hydrolysis. The reduction of the content in free fatty acids after physical refining, particularly in deodorization step has been already frequently observed36,38,39.

Oxidative state of argan oils

Level of primary oxidation products

The peroxide value is the balance of two distinct mechanisms: an oxidation step leading to the formation of hydroperoxides, and a degradation step leading to the elimination of the hydroperoxide through formation of secondary oxidation products. Each step possesses its own kinetic40. CAO, BAO, and RAO all displayed an initial averaged peroxide value around 3.73 mEq O2/kg of oil (Fig. 2, full line), a value more elevated than that previously reported for PGI-O prepared from unroasted kernels33,34.

Peroxide value (full line, left axis) and p-anisidine value (dot line, right axis) of argan oils stored at 60 °C for a period of 12 weeks.

Under accelerated storage conditions, hydroperoxide formation in CAO reached a maximum value of 36.2 mEq O2/kg after 8 weeks (Fig. 2), a value that would be considered excessive in regular storage conditions. After this period, the breakdown of the peroxide to secondary oxidation products surpassed the peroxide formation process that was responsible for a decrease in the peroxide value. The final peroxide value in CAO was calculated to be 31.3 mEq O2/kg of oil (Fig. 2, full line).

The peroxide formation profiles of BAO and RAO were found to be highly similar (Fig. 2, full line). Such finding was not necessarily anticipated since physical deodorization has been shown to possibly lead to the decomposition of hydroperoxides to form secondary oxidation40. Peroxide formation in BAO and RAO increased much faster than in CAO between week-0 and week-6 to reach a maximum around 45 mEq O2/kg of oil after 6 weeks of storage at 60 °C. Then the peroxide value decreased. The reduction of the peroxide value of RAO was observed to be much more intense than that of BAO. The peroxide value kinetic profiles of BAO and RAO, compared to that of CAO, suggested that bleaching had led to the removal of substances that inhibit the hydroperoxide formation. However, after week-6, the observed decrease in peroxide value of RAO can be interpreted as an additional vapor-induced removal of substances inhibiting the decomposition of hydroperoxides during the deodorization process. Final peroxide indexes in BAO and RAO were calculated to be around 30 mEq O2/kg of oil, a value close to that of CAO (Fig. 2, full line). Therefore, even though the hydroperoxide formation was slower in CAO, all three oils were in a similar peroxide state after 12 weeks at 60 °C indicating that if bleaching and deodorization lead to a modification of the kinetics of hydroperoxide formation and decomposition, all three types of oils reach a similar final peroxidation state after a certain delay calculated to be 12 weeks for the used argan oil batch stored at 60 °C.

Level of secondary oxidation products

The peroxide value is a good marker to evaluate the formation of primary oxidation products, the p-anisidine value (AnV) gives information on the kinetics of formation of secondary oxidation products41. AnV of CAO (Fig. 2, dot line) was determined to be 0.5, a value not significantly different from than that of BAO (1.35) and RAO (0.74). AnV of CAO remained low for 6 weeks. After this delay, it increased slowly but constantly to reach 7 after 12 weeks. Interestingly, the peroxide value in CO at 6 weeks was already elevated (Fig. 2, full line) suggesting that a 2-week delay was necessary to significantly decompose primary oxidation compounds in secondary oxidation compounds. Accordingly, the strong increase of the AnV of CAO between week-10 and week-12 could be correlated with the maximum of primary oxidation compounds observed at week-8 confirming the stabilization of hydroperoxides in CAO. Chew et al., (2016) have also observed a decrease in PV, coupled with an increase in AnV42.

Initial AnV was found to be only slightly higher in BAO than in CAO (Fig. 2, dot line) suggesting that all secondary oxidation products as ketones or aldehydes likely catalytically produced during the bleaching step41, had not been removed during the vapor-assisted deodorization process. The AnV also revealed that secondary oxidation products accumulated 2 weeks earlier in BAO than in CAO (week-6 instead of week-8). Starting from week-4, the p-anisidine value of BAO increased linearly suggesting a limited stabilization of hydroperoxides in BAO. It is noteworthy that the AnV decreased after the deodorization, likely due to the volatilization of secondary oxidation products and/or their degradation43.

Initial AnV of CAO and RAO were similar. However, unlike that of CAO, the AnV of RAO immediately increased upon storage at 60 °C. The increase between week-2 and week-6 was found to be almost parallel to that of peroxide value, a fact suggesting the quasi-immediate transformation of primary oxidation products into secondary oxidation products and hence the lack of stabilization of the hydroperoxides. Secondary oxidation product formation continued to progress over the 12 weeks of the study suggesting the lack of stabilization of the hydroperoxides. Such phenomenon has already been reported42.

The AnV profile determined for the three oils is consistent with the hypothesis of the presence a class of components present in CAO that would slow down the hydroperoxide formation and, also their subsequent decomposition into secondary oxidation products. Those compounds would be unequally removed during the bleaching and deodorization steps.

To get a supplementary insight into the oxidation product formation in our oil samples, their specific extinction at λ = 232 and 270 nm was also examined.

Specific extinctions K232 and K270

Initial extinction coefficient at λ = 232 nm of CAO, BAO, and RAO was found to be similar to that reported in cosmetic argan oil33,34. Nonetheless, initial extinction coefficient at 232 nm of BAO was found to be slightly more elevated than that of CO and RO (Fig. 3, full line) suggesting the possibility of a rapid formation of primary oxidation products during the bleaching step that went unnoticed from the peroxide value determination. Initial extinction coefficient at 232 nm of CAO and RAO was found to be not significantly different with an average value lower than that of BAO (Fig. 3, full line). These extinction values confirmed the presence of similar amounts of primary oxidation products in CAO and RAO as already deduced from the peroxide value and indicated that deodorization allowed the elimination of a large portion of those primary oxidation products.

Specific extinction at λ = 232 (full line, right axis) and 270 nm (dot line, left axis).

In CAO, specific extinction profile at λ = 232 nm suggested that primary oxidation products started to importantly accumulate after 6 weeks of storage at 60 °C before to culminate after 8 weeks, then to decompose. Accordingly, secondary oxidation products detected through specific extinction at λ = 270 nm started to appear, after 6 weeks (Fig. 3). However, in CAO, the formation of secondary oxidation products was slow and progressive. These results are consistent with those observed from the peroxide value and using both methods, it shows that in CAO, formation of primary oxidation products occurs after a 6-week delay and those products decompose to afford secondary oxidation products.

Specific extinction at λ = 232 nm of BAO indicated that primary oxidation products were formed slightly more rapidly in BAO than in CAO during the first 4 weeks. Then primary oxidation products underwent a linear strong increase for 6 weeks before decreasing during the 2 final weeks of the study. Initial specific extinction at λ = 270 nm of BAO was found to be particularly elevated indicating some accumulation of secondary oxidation products, in line with the results observed during the p-anisidine value determination. The additional formation of secondary oxidation products started to be significant only after 8 weeks of storage at 60 °C suggesting a stabilization and accumulation of the primary oxidation products for 4 weeks.

Initial specific extinction at λ = 232 nm in RAO was lower than that of CAO confirming the elimination of primary oxidation products during deodorization as previously reported in olive oil44. Primary oxidation products in RAO increased very rapidly to reach a maximum after 6 weeks then decreased.

Initial extinction coefficient at λ = 270 nm of CAO, BAO, and RAO were found to be similar to that reported in cosmetic argan oil33,34. Secondary oxidation product formation determined by specific extinction at λ = 270 nm in RAO did not reflect this profile, they were formed quasi linearly for 12 weeks. Such kinetic indicated a very short lifespan of primary oxidation products in RAO and hence their almost slow transformation into secondary oxidation products. The formation of secondary oxidation products is accelerated at high temperatures40.

Interestingly, CAO is less sensitive to primary oxidation products followed by BAO, while RAO is the most sensitive to oxidation.

Variations in the chemical composition

Argan oil nutritional and cosmetic properties are attributed to its elevated content in unsaturated fatty acids and antioxidants2,7. Both types of compounds are known to be impacted by deodorization45,46. Therefore, we investigated the variations of these two families of components using our accelerated oxidation model.

The fatty acid composition of CAO presented the expected values47confirming that the use of subquality kernels did not influence the fatty acid composition of the resulting argan oil. Furthermore, CAO, BAO, and RAO displayed an initial fatty acid composition without significant differences indicating that neither the bleaching nor the deodorization step influences the fatty acid composition of the argan oil. Over 12 weeks at 60 °C, no significant change was observed in terms of relative fatty acid composition. Consequently, in accelerated oxidative conditions, argan oil fatty acid composition undergoes no modification. Other studies of vegetable oils; such as argan oil12, olive oil44, and hazelnut oil39, have confirmed that refining has no impact on the fatty acid composition.

Variation in the tocopherols content

Tocopherols constitute the second major class of compounds responsible for the properties of argan oil. They also actively participate in the preservation of argan oil against oxidative deterioration9.

The argan oil evaluated in the present study presented an initial tocopherol content different from that calculated in our previous study (Table 2)12. The possibility of such variation was anticipated because subquality kernels constitute a very heterogeneous group, and oxygen sensitive molecules such as tocopherols are likely to suffer various non-traceable oxidation events prior to kernel pressing. These oxidative events are not necessarily statistically averaged by the large number of kernels used for each batch of oil. Tocopherol content in subquality fruits is hence impossible to predict and depends on the quality of the fruits.

Deodorization of BAO led to an additional decrease in tocopherol resulting in a total tocopherol loss of 25% between CAO (414 mg/kg oil) and RAO (312 mg/kg of oil). Hence, deodorization was accompanied with the loss of a large quantity of tocopherols. Gamma-tocopherol appears to be more susceptible to degradation, particularly in the RAO sample, where its concentration decreases significantly (from 262 ± 12 to 15 ± 8 mg/kg of oil).

After 4 weeks at 60 °C, the tocopherol content in CAO had decreased by 7%. The decrease in total tocopherols was 22 and 51% in BAO and RAO, respectively. Therefore, the quick disappearance of tocopherols in BAO, and RAO was confirmed.

After 8 weeks at 60 °C, total tocopherol content in CAO had decreased by 32% compared to its initial value. In BAO, the loss was 54% and 74% in RO. Finally, after 12 weeks, CAO had lost 34% of its initial tocopherol content, BAO, and RAO had lost 69 and 92% of their tocopherol content, respectively.

The reduction in tocopherol content during refining has been already reported. Indeed, the content in tocopherols in the sunflower oils after bleaching and deodorization decreased by 52–53%36. There is also a reduction in tocopherols in physically refined rice bran oil48. Other researchers have reported a reduction in olive oil49.

Tocopherols are molecules that can be destroyed by the temperature and thus during physical refining50. Since such a dramatic loss in tocopherols had not been observed when argan oil is simply deodorized, our results suggest that bleaching may lead to the loss of molecules, such as phenolic compounds51, that protect tocopherols from oxidation. Once these molecules are removed, heat sensitive molecules52,53as tocopherol are quickly destroyed at 60 °C. Tocopherols participate in oil thermostability54and therefore, they actively participate in the preservation of argan oil. Because all tocopherols are not identically heat-degraded55,56the function of each tocopherol in argan oil preservation is difficult to identify. Loss of the synergistic protective effects induced by carotenes or phospholipids57, two classes of compounds removed during the physical refining process, and/or the elimination of other minor components58 may also explain the observed modified preservation properties of BAO, and RAO. To confirm this hypothesis, we measured the phosphorus and phospholipid content of our oil samples. Phospholipid content was found to be much lower in BAO and RAO than in CAO (Table 3) attesting the efficient removal of some phospholipids during the deodorization step and their additional removal during the subsequent refining step. At the end of the storage period, phospholipid content in CAO, BAO, and RAO was similar to the initial levels found for the same type of oil (Table 3), this demonstrated that the deodorization and refining steps were principally at the origin of the loss of phospholipids and not the storage conditions.

Even though other phenomena can be suggested to explain the limited oxidative stability of refined argan oil, compared to virgin argan oil, our study demonstrates that removal or destruction of tocopherols during the bleaching step using bleaching earths or during the vapor-induced deodorization may lead to a dramatic decrease of the preservation properties of physically refined argan oil.

Conclusion

This study complements our initial study on deodorized-only argan oil. The preservation properties of argan oil prepared from subquality argan fruits seem to be difficult to predict since it depends on the initial quality of the fruits. The preservation properties of argan oil seem to be directly linked to the oil tocopherol content of the oil, those molecules prevent the formation of primary oxidation products and their decomposition in secondary oxidation products. Even though deodorization does not seem to change the tocopherol content of argan oil too much, its association with bleaching, to produce refined oil, may have a dramatic impact on the resulting oil tocopherol content. The possible instability of refined argan oil suggests that, unless better vacuum distillation conditions are determined, refined argan oil must be used rapidly, or antioxidants that do not modify argan oil color should be added to increase its preservation profile.
