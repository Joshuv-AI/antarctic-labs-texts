Recent Advances in Analytical Platform for Single-Cell Microbial Electrochemistry
Authors: Zejing Xing, Ying Wang, Lingxia Zuo, Zixuan Chen, Jun-Jie Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The urgent need to mitigate global energy and environmental crises underscores the importance of developing clean and sustainable energy technologies. Microbial electrochemical systems (MESs) have emerged as a promising platform, exploiting the ability of electroactive microorganisms to exchange electrons with electrodes through extracellular electron transfer (EET). While conventional studies have primarily focused on community-level processes, they often overlook the intrinsic heterogeneity and dynamic behaviors of individual cells that ultimately govern MES performance. To overcome these limitations, single-cell analytical approaches are being developed, though their application to EET studies remains in its infancy. This review provides a comprehensive summary of current single-cell microbial electrochemistry studying techniques, beginning with the fundamental mechanisms of EET and progressing to electrochemical microelectrode methods and optical imaging strategies. Particular attention is given to both label-free and probe-labeled imaging approaches, each offering distinct advantages. Finally, we briefly discuss the interplay between EET and microbial behaviors at single-cell scale. These insights highlight emerging methodologies for probing single-cell EET, their applications in uncovering microbial physiology and behaviors, and the future opportunities they open for advancing microbial electrochemistry and the rational design of MESs.

Introduction

Human society is facing an unprecedented convergence of energy and environmental crises. The rapidly growing global population and continued industrial expansion have intensified the demand for energy, but the heavy reliance on fossil fuels has led to increasing greenhouse gas emissions, resource depletion, and severe ecological impacts. − These huge challenges underscore the urgency of transitioning from fossil fuel-dependent industry toward sustainable and environmentally clean energy technologies. Developing innovative platforms for clean energy production and carbon-neutral processes is thus not only a scientific priority but also a social task for addressing the dual pressures of environmental degradation and energy insufficiency.

Within this context, microbial electrochemical systems (MESs) have emerged as promising bioelectrochemical platforms capable of converting chemical energy from organic substrates into electrical energy or value-added products. , The operation of MESs fundamentally relies on extracellular electron transfer (EET). During EET process, electroactive microorganisms transport electrons derived from intracellular metabolic pathways across the cell envelope to external acceptors, such as electrodes or metal oxides. − Through this mechanism, microorganisms effectively link their respiration to external solid-state conductors, enabling the direct conversion of chemical energy into electrical energy. The earliest demonstration of this concept dates back to the early 20th century, when Potter first reported electricity generation from microbial cultures. After decades, the development of microbial fuel cells (MFCs) and their ability to harvest energy from organic wastewaters established a foundation for modern MES research. − Today, MESs is recognized not only as bioelectricity generators but also as versatile platforms with wide-ranging applications. They have been engineered for wastewater treatment, − where the dual functions of organic wastes degradation and current production provide an excellent approach to water purification. In bioremediation, MESs facilitates the detoxification of heavy metals and organic pollutants by coupling microbial metabolism with electrochemical reduction processes. − More recently, microbial electrosynthesis has attracted increasing attention, wherein microbes accept electrons from electrodes to catalyze the reduction of CO2 into organic chemical products. − Across these diverse applications, the mechanistic basis of EET remains central. Understanding how microbes transport electrons across insulating cell envelopes, regulate redox proteins, and establish conductive connections with electrodes is essential for improving efficiency and functional tunability of MESs.

To date, most studies of MESs have focused on the community or biofilm level, emphasizing macroscopic current output and reactor performance. − While such community-level investigations have provided much valuable information about overall system performance, they inevitably obscure the inherent heterogeneity of microbial populations. Individual cells within a biofilm often display significant differences in metabolic state, adhesion strength, motility, and electron transfer capability. These variations can collectively determine the efficiency and stability of electron flow within microbial communities. However, ensemble measurements are unable to obtain the information on such heterogeneity, limiting the ability to deeply resolve mechanisms. For instance, whether outer-membrane cytochromes operate uniformly across cells, or how appendages like pili and flagella contribute to both motility and electron transfer, cannot be fully demonstrated from bulk-level data. Single-cell-resolved approaches are thus essential for bridging this knowledge gap. By exploring microbial EET at the cellular and even subcellular scale, researchers can uncover fundamental insights into the diversity of microbial metabolic processes and behavioral correlation that underpins bioelectrochemical functionality.

In this review, we systematically summarize recent progress in single-cell studies of microbial EET. We first introduce the principles of direct and indirect electron transfer pathways, focusing on the structural and functional diversity of cytochromes, nanowires, and electron shuttles. We then highlight state-of-art micro/nanoelectrode strategies for probing the electrogenic activity of individual cells, followed by a discussion of label-free and probe-based optical imaging approaches that uncover dynamic redox processes, cell-electrode and cell-nanomaterials interactions. Finally, we explore how EET couples with microbial behaviors, linking electron transfer to adhesion, motility, and biofilm formation. By systematically integrating insights from these diverse approaches, this review aims to provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the dynamic, heterogeneous, and adaptive nature of microbial EET. Such knowledge not only enriches our fundamental grasp of microbial electrophysiology but also informs the rational engineering of next-generation MESs for sustainable energy and environmental applications.

EET Mechanisms in Electroactive Microorganisms

The operation of MESs fundamentally relies on microbial EET behavior, a complex, multistep process involving diverse proteins, metabolic pathways, and diffusible mediators. EET not only governs the efficiency and stability of electron exchange between microorganisms and external acceptors/donors, but also reflects the adaptive strategies that microbes have evolved to thrive in natural environments. Based on the electron transfer routes, EET is generally categorized into two principal modes: direct electron transfer (DET) and indirect electron transfer (IET). DET depends on structural components at the microbial cell surface, such as outer-membrane cytochromes or conductive nanowires, that directly contact extracellular acceptors/donors. In contrast, IET relies on soluble electron shuttles as intermediates that mediate electron exchange between cells and external electron sinks. This section systematically discusses the basic mechanisms of EET, focusing on both DET and IET, their key molecular players, and species-specific differences observed in different representative electroactive bacteria. DET

DET constitutes a central pathway by which electroactive microorganisms exchange electrons with external insoluble electron carriers, including metal oxides and electrodes. By avoiding the reliance on soluble mediators, DET enables efficient electron flow. Two major strategies have been established: cytochrome-mediated electron transport across the cell envelope and nanowire-based long-range conduction.

Cytochrome-Mediated DET

Among DET routes, the cytochrome-mediated pathway is the most thoroughly characterized and widely distributed. In S. oneidensis and G. sulfurreducens, DET is enabled by a network of membrane-spanning multiheme c-type cytochromes that form electron conduits. Despite their reliance on similar electron transfer pathways, periplasmic mediators, and outer-membrane cytochromes, species-specific variations in protein composition highlight divergent evolutionary strategies.

The DET mechanism in S. oneidensis represents a paradigmatic model. The process begins with intracellular metabolism under anaerobic or aerobic conditions, where organic substrates such as lactate are oxidized via glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle to generate reduced cofactors (e.g., NADH). Electrons from NADH enter the inner membrane quinone pool, composed mainly of ubiquinone or menaquinone, which acts as an electron reservoir. These electrons are then transferred to CymA, a tetraheme c-type cytochrome anchored to the inner membrane. CymA accepts electrons from reduced quinones and delivers them to soluble periplasmic redox proteins such as STC and FccA. These cytochromes or iron–sulfur proteins diffuse through the periplasm, functioning as mobile electron carriers. Subsequently, electrons are passed to the MtrABC complex at the outer membrane. Within this tripartite system, MtrA is a periplasm-facing c-type cytochrome that receives electrons from periplasmic mediators. MtrB, a β-barrel outer-membrane protein, acts as a structural scaffold. MtrC and OmcA, exposed to the extracellular environment, engage directly with insoluble electron acceptors such as Fe­(III) oxides or electrodes. , Through successive heme-to-heme electron transfer steps, electrons traverse the cellular envelope and are delivered to extracellular sinks, completing the DET pathway.

In G. sulfurreducens, DET proceeds through a similar hierarchical cascade but with species-specific protein components. The process begins with acetate oxidation under strictly anaerobic conditions, producing NADH equivalents that feed electrons into a menaquinone-based quinone pool. Instead of CymA, Geobacter employs MacA, a periplasm-facing c-type cytochrome, as the critical conduit from the quinone pool to the periplasmic electron carriers. MacA differs structurally and evolutionarily from CymA, reflecting adaptations to the obligate anaerobic lifestyle of Geobacter. In the periplasm, electrons are further relayed by c-type cytochromes such as PpcA and PpcD. The outer-membrane electron conduits in Geobacter are collectively termed outer-membrane cytochromes (OMCs). Distinct OMCs act different roles: OmcB and OmcS are central to Fe­(III) reduction by facilitating direct contact with insoluble minerals, , while OmcZ has been implicated in enhanced current production during biofilm growth on anode. Thus, while both Shewanella and Geobacter employ a quinone-cytochrome cascade, differences in inner-, outer-membrane, and periplasmic components underscore their evolutionary divergence.

Nanowire-Mediated DET

In addition to cytochrome-mediated DET, some electroactive microbes employ conductive pili, termed “nanowires,” to enable long-range electron transfer. Nanowires are filamentous appendages with diameters of 3–5 nm and lengths extending up to tens of micrometers, allowing microbial communities to bridge spatial gaps between cells and remote electron acceptors. These structures are particularly crucial in dense biofilms, where only surface layers are in direct contact with electrodes.

In G. sulfurreducens, nanowires are assembled from PilA proteins, the structural subunits of type IV pili, which extend outward from the inner membrane, interconnecting cells and forming conductive networks. Two main hypotheses have been advanced to explain their conductivity. Early models proposed that nanowires function through heme-to-heme hopping mediated by cytochromes such as OmcS localized on pili surfaces. However, more recent evidence highlights a π-π stacking mechanism involving aromatic amino acid residues (e.g., tryptophan, tyrosine) within the PilA C-terminal domain. These aromatic clusters form delocalized π-conjugated systems, reminiscent of organic semiconductors, which enable rapid electron transport. This paradigm shift has redefined our understanding of microbial nanowire conductivity and continues to be explored.

IET

Unlike DET, IET relies on soluble redox mediators, termed electron shuttles, to convey electrons between cells and external acceptors. IET is particularly important in heterogeneous environments, such as sediments or wastewater, where direct cell–substrate contact is limited. Electron shuttles can be either self-secreted by microbes or artificially supplemented.

Self-Secreted Electron Shuttles

Microorganisms can synthesize and release diverse redox-active molecules that function as natural electron mediators. Flavins are the most studied, especially in S. oneidensis. Both riboflavin (RF) and flavin mononucleotide (FMN) are actively secreted and participate in reversible electron transfer cycles (FMN/FMNH2, RF/RFH2). Reduced flavins diffuse into the extracellular environment and donate electrons to insoluble acceptors. The oxidized forms then re-enter the cell surface, where outer-membrane cytochromes such as MtrC rereduce them, thus completing the redox cycle. Notably, flavins not only function as free diffusible shuttles but also can bind to cytochromes, enhancing electron transfer through allosteric mechanisms.

Beyond flavins, microbes produce quinones, phenazines, flavocytochromes, and even gases such as H2 as shuttles. This redundancy ensures flexibility under fluctuating environmental conditions. However, shuttle biosynthesis and secretion require metabolic energy investment, suggesting that under resource-limited conditions, microbes may preferentially utilize DET routes.

Artificial Electron Shuttles

To enhance EET efficiency in engineered systems, exogenous mediators have been introduced. Classical examples include methyl viologen (MV), anthraquinone-2,6-disulfonate (AQDS), and neutral red (NR). MV, a quaternary ammonium cation, undergoes rapid reduction by microbial metabolism and efficiently transfers electrons to electrodes, facilitating fast start-up of MFCs. AQDS mimics natural quinone shuttles and has been employed in bioremediation, accelerating the reduction of toxic metals such as uranium and chromium. NR, a phenazine derivative, intercalates within microbial membranes, enhancing electron transfer efficiency. Artificial mediators offer tunability in redox potential and stability, but their toxicity and cost remain challenges. Current research aims to design biocompatible, low-cost analogues that balance efficacy with sustainability.

DET and IET represent complementary strategies in microbial electron transfer. While DET ensures high-efficiency direct conduction through membrane-bound proteins and nanowires, IET provides flexibility through diffusible mediators that extend the range of electron transfer beyond physical contact. Together, these mechanisms underpin the ecological success of electroactive microbes and their technological applications in MESs.

Micro/Nanoelectrode Approaches for Single-Cell Analysis of EET

The advent of micro/nanoelectrode technologies has opened a new frontier in electrochemistry by enabling highly sensitive and spatially resolved measurements under the microscopic scale. − Their small size confers distinct advantages, including rapid response times, reduced ohmic drop, and minimal perturbation of local chemical environments. These attributes make micro/nanoelectrodes particularly attractive for studying single-cell EET, where quantifying the coupling between microbial cells and electrode surfaces is crucial for understanding fundamental mechanisms and optimizing MESs. In recent years, a growing number of works have demonstrated the value of these platforms in unraveling single-cell EET dynamics, dissecting the relative contributions of direct and indirect pathways, and probing cellular heterogeneity in EET behaviors.

The pioneering work by Jiang and colleagues in 2010 marked the first application of microelectrode technology for probing single-cell EET. By combining optically transparent nanoelectrodes with optical microscopy (Figure A), they achieved simultaneous in situ imaging of cellular morphology and direct quantification of current produced by individual electroactive bacteria (Figure B). This approach revealed filamentous structures (∼4 nm in diameter) extending from the surface of S. oneidensis MR-1. However, correlated electrochemical measurements showed that these appendages did not contribute measurably to current production. Instead, current generation primarily relied on outer-membrane cytochrome-mediated DET. Moreover, comparative experiments with and without exogenous mediators demonstrated that the majority of current in MR-1 was attributable to IET via secreted flavins. Importantly, real-time imaging linked cellular motility to EET capacity, showing a negative correlation between migration and current output. These studies underscored the power of microelectrode-based assays in resolving single-cell behavior and the corresponding mechanism.

Using the same optical-nanoelectrode platform, further investigations were conducted on G. sulfurreducens DL-1. Current profiles from individual cells displayed two distinct phases: an initial fast-decaying peak attributed to rapid discharge of accumulated surface charges, followed by a sustained plateau representing steady-state current output. Simultaneous monitoring of multiple cells highlighted heterogeneity in current magnitudes, though with similar variation trends, thereby confirming that no cross-talk occurred between adjacent electrodes. Beyond single-cell measurements, microelectrode arrays facilitated the study of multicell interactions. These experiments revealed that even bacteria located away from direct electrode contact could contribute to current output via membrane cytochrome protein-mediated charge transfer. Notably, current densities at the single-cell level far exceeded those observed in optimized MFCs, providing the first quantitative evidence that MFC performance is limited not by the intrinsic electron-producing capacity of cells but rather by reactor-scale factors such as mass transport and biofilm organization.

A critical challenge in microelectrode studies lies in precisely positioning individual cells on microelectrode surfaces. To address this, Liu et al. have employed optical tweezer technology, which uses tightly focused laser beams to trap and manipulate microscopic objects. By combining optical tweezers with microelectrodes, single S. oneidensis cells could be accurately positioned onto electrode surfaces. Subsequent electrochemical measurements revealed a clear voltage-dependent response of single-cell current output, implicating dynamic regulation of OMCs. Specifically, the number of OMCs directly engaged with the electrode was shown to change with applied potential, representing the first evidence that Shewanella can reorganize its outer-membrane composition in response to the Fermi level of external electron acceptors. This finding not only deepened mechanistic understanding of DET but also highlighted the adaptive plasticity of microbial membranes in mediating extracellular respiration.

By combining real-time optical imaging, infrared optical tweezers, and indium tin oxide (ITO) microelectrode arrays fabricated via photolithography, Gross et al. created multifunctional platforms capable of both manipulating and studying single bacteria. These systems achieved current detection sensitivities below 100 fA, enabling quantification of extracellular respiration at unprecedented resolution. Measurements of S. oneidensis MR-1 revealed single-cell current outputs in the range of 15–100 fA, values that can serve as predictive benchmarks for estimating maximum power densities of MESs, including MFCs and microbial electrosynthesis. Furthermore, the designed platform suggests broad applicability in studying environmental perturbations, such as changes in nutrient availability, pH, or electrode potential, on single-cell EET behavior.

Without using complicated optical instruments, single-impact electrochemistry provides a powerful route to probe individual microbial collision events at ultramicroelectrodes. Langlard et al. reported current-step transients from single blocking impacts of Gram-negative bacteria, including S. oneidensis, on a 10 μm Pt ultramicroelectrode. Computer-assisted analysis enabled quantification of step magnitudes and revealed the influence of cell morphology and outer-membrane properties on the electrochemical response. The distinct behavior of S. oneidensis, governed by its outer-membrane redox proteins, highlights capability of the method to resolve strain-dependent phenotypes. Extending single-impact approaches to catalytic modes could further enable direct, single-cell interrogation of EET kinetics in electroactive bacteria.

Label-Free Optical Imaging Strategies for Probing Single-Cell EET

Optical imaging strategies for studying EET at the single-cell level can generally be categorized into label-free and probe-based approaches. Compared to probe-based methods, label-free imaging offers distinct advantages. For example, it eliminates the need to design or introduce exogenous probes, thereby reducing experimental complexity and avoiding interference with microbial metabolic activity. − These features ensure higher reliability in measured results. Over the past decade, a series of label-free optical imaging techniques has been employed to EET-related studies, including interrogating the dynamics of OMCs, revealing nanomaterial-microbe interfacial behaviors, and uncovering new mechanistic insights into microbial EET.

OMCs serve as primary conduits bridging intracellular electron carriers with extracellular acceptors, making them indispensable to microbial respiration and EET behavior. During electron transfer, OMCs undergo redox switching that induces conformational changes, which then alter the refractive index of their local microenvironment. Scattering-based imaging modalities, owing to their high sensitivity to refractive index variations, are therefore well suited to probe the OMC redox states dynamics. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) imaging is a representative scattering-based method. It exploits the excitation of plasmonic waves at metal-dielectric interfaces by incident light, and detects refractive index changes through alternations in reflected light intensity. By depositing single bacteria onto gold films and combining electrochemical modulation with real-time SPR detection, Liu et al. tracked conformational dynamics of OMCs during EET (Figure ). Quantitative analysis using Nernst equation fitting allowed extraction of fundamental electrochemical parameters, including formal potentials and numbers of electron transferred at the single-cell level. Importantly, these values were consistent with ensemble-level measurements, validating the accuracy of the approach. Further principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a negative correlation between formal potential and number of electrons transferred, uncovering previously inaccessible relationships within microbial EET.

Despite its utility, SPR imaging suffers from limited spatial resolution and sensitivity to environmental fluctuations. To address these challenges, structural color microscopy was introduced. This technique also relies on refractive index variations during OMC conformational switching but employs evanescent waves triggered by white light excitation on single-layer graphene, and analyzes scattering spectra instead of reflected intensity (Figure A). Compared with SPR, structural color microscopy offers superior spatial resolution (Figure B), enabling the observation of subcellular asymmetry in electron transfer. For instance, distinct scattering signatures were detected at the poles versus central regions of bacterial cells, suggesting that EET exhibits spatial heterogeneity correlated with asymmetric growth and division. Moreover, external voltage perturbation experiments quantified the kinetics of electron injection (ET-in) and extraction (ET-out), revealing faster kinetics for ET-in. Strikingly, synchronized spectral oscillations under UV stimulation demonstrated electrical communication between neighboring cells, offering compelling evidence for microbial electrocommunication at the single-cell level.

After that, the structural color microscopy further extends beyond OMC dynamics to probe other processes influencing refractive index. For example, gas evolution at cell surfaces based on EET process can be detected in real time, enabling monitoring of bubble nucleation, growth, and release. This capability provides mechanistic insights into gaseous product generation and transport in MESs. A particularly innovative application involves photoinduced EET in semiconductor-microbe hybrids. In these systems, semiconductor nanoparticles (e.g., CdS quantum dots) are interfaced with bacterial surfaces to inject photogenerated electrons into cellular metabolic pathways. Using structural color microscopy, real-time scattering spectra of CdS-modified E. coli under illumination were monitored (Figure C). Results demonstrated wavelength-dependent product generations, reflecting variations in enzymatic activity and light absorption capability. More interestingly, the so-called “bubble shading effect” was observed for the first time. As shown in Figure D, bubbles adhering to bacterial surfaces scattered incident light, decreasing photon flux reaching CdS quantum dots and thus reducing photoexcitation efficiency. Removal of these bubbles through physical or chemical treatments significantly enhanced hydrogen evolution, offering a simple yet effective strategy to improve microbial photofermentation efficiency.

Beyond serving as electron donors or acceptors, nanomaterials can actively modulate microbial EET efficiency. ,, Structural color imaging and scattering spectroscopy also have the capability to study these effects at the single-cell level. Single-layer graphene not only functions as an electrode substrate but also influences optical scattering through its Fermi level, which is tunable by surface charge density. During microbial EET, local changes in graphene charge density can alter scattering signals, enabling direct conversion of optical signals into electrochemical readouts. By correlating scattered light intensity with current, Xia et al. successfully reconstructed voltammetric curves for individual cells. This strategy was further applied to evaluate the effects of gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) on EET behavior of S. oneidensis MR-1. Results showed that Au NP modification decreased oxidation potentials, while has almost no impact on oxidation currents. This indicates that Au NPs act as “electronic plugs” to improve electron tunneling between cytochromes and electrodes, rather than simply serving as conductive bridges. Furthermore, it has also been found that smaller Au NPs exhibited stronger enhancement effects. These discoveries offer design principles for engineering optimized bacteria-nanoparticle hybrids.

Biosynthesized nanoparticles have been confirmed to enhance the EET efficiency. In addition, the interaction between microbes and metal precursors during nanoparticle biosynthesis is also an EET-driven process. , To unravel these mechanisms, surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) three-dimensional imaging has been employed. By using biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) as SERS substrates, researchers mapped molecular distributions within single cells during nanoparticle formation. Multivariate curve resolution (MCR) analysis revealed two distinct biosynthetic pathways: an intracellular NADH- and glutathione-dependent enzymatic reduction route, and a membrane-associated quinone-cytochrome C electron transfer chain-mediated route. These findings highlight the dual role of microbial EET both in respiration and in mediating nanomaterial biosynthesis.

Endogenous nanostructures generated through biomineralization can also be exploited as intrinsic SERS enhancers for probing microbial electroactivity (Figure A). The principle relies on the vibrational Stark effect. The effect can be briefly described that OMC redox changes during EET alter intracellular electric fields, shifting the vibrational frequency of endogenous CO at 2100 cm–1. By monitoring these shifts, as shown in Figure B, Chen et al. quantified the intensity of internal electric fields as indicator for electroactivity. Single-cell analyses revealed heterogeneity in redox activity, enabling the screening of highly electroactive bacterial subpopulations. Subsequent laser ablation and regrowth experiments confirmed that these subpopulations could be selectively isolated (Figure C), offering a novel route for enriching strains with superior performance in MFCs.

Label-free optical imaging has emerged as a transformative toolkit for studying single-cell EET. By avoiding perturbations associated with labeling, these approaches faithfully capture the intrinsic dynamic processes of microbial electron transfer. From revealing OMC kinetics, subcellular heterogeneity, and dissecting microbe-nanomaterial interactions and biosynthetic pathways, label-free imaging provides an unprecedented window into the living electrogenic cell.

Probe-Labeled Optical Imaging Approaches for Single-Cell EET

Label-free optical imaging strategies provide noninvasive and convenient tools for investigating microbial physiology. However, they are hampered by several intrinsic limitations, including low signal specificity, difficulties in distinguishing structurally similar molecules, and susceptibility to environmental interference. , In addition, their sensitivity is often insufficient to capture low-abundance species or subtle dynamic changes. , By contrast, probe-labeled optical imaging achieves high specificity and contrast by introducing carefully designed molecular or nanomaterial probes. This approach enables direct visualization of target metabolites, ions, or protein activities with high spatial and temporal resolution. Currently, probe-based imaging methods have wide applications across diverse biomedical fields such as oncology, neuroscience, and microbiology. − In the context of electroactive microorganisms, probe-labeled strategies now offer unique opportunities to study EET at the single-cell level. By selectively labeling targets, these methods provide dynamic and quantitative insight into the electron transport mechanism and its coupling with microbial physiology.

Single-particle tracking (SPT) combines high-speed imaging with fluorescence labeling to trace the movement of individual biomolecules in living cells at nanometer precision and millisecond temporal resolution. Using fluorescence microscopy, the trajectories of specifically labeled biomolecules are reconstructed through centroid localization, allowing analysis of diffusion modes, mobility rates, and molecular interaction dynamics. In such a context, SPT has now been adapted to probe microbial cytochrome proteins dynamics to reveal the electron transfer mechanisms. In pioneering work, quantum dots were used to selectively label outer-membrane c-type cytochromes on S. oneidensis MR-1. Tracking the fluorescence signals frame by frame revealed the diffusion dynamics of individual MtrC and OmcA proteins on the bacterial surface. From the observed results, two distinct diffusion modes were identified: a predominant slow-diffusion mode and a less frequent fast-diffusion mode. These observations demonstrated that cytochromes are not static but undergo dynamic lateral motion across the membrane. Moreover, cytochrome mobility was shown to facilitate intermolecular electron exchange, suggesting that diffusion behavior may represent a crucial determinant of long-range EET in electroactive bacteria.

Although quantum dots offer strong fluorescence and photostability, their relatively large size can perturb native protein behavior. To circumvent this limitation, researchers have employed genetically encoded fluorescent proteins fused to target proteins. This strategy allowed quantification of expression levels via fluorescence intensity while simultaneously tracking protein diffusion with single-molecule resolution. Through such molecular probes, it became possible to correlate protein abundance with dynamic behavior without the steric hindrance typically introduced by larger nanoparticle probes. Building on these advances, multimodal imaging platforms have been developed that integrate fluorescence microscopy with single-cell photocurrent measurement and single-molecule tracking analysis (Figure A–D). This combination provides a holistic perspective, linking protein dynamics with electrochemical performance.

Further extending the application of the platform, Fu et al. adapted it to study microbial-semiconductor biohybrids. For instance, cytochrome or hydrogenase proteins were labeled with fluorescent probes, enabling direct visualization of their dynamics while simultaneously recording single-cell currents. Work on a Rhodobacter strain, they revealed directional asymmetry in electron pathways: hydrogenases were essential for electron uptake but not required for electron efflux. Further analysis showed that distinct classes of hydrogenases, soluble hydrogenase (SH) and membrane-bound hydrogenase (MBH), play different roles in polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) biosynthesis (Figure E,F). These findings demonstrated that microbial EET pathways are not symmetric and that enzymes may play context-dependent roles depending on electron flow direction. By combining fluorescence imaging of proteins with quantitative electrochemical results, these platforms represent a powerful tool of dissecting complex biohybrid interactions at the single-cell level. In addition, the ability to resolve electron transfer in both uptake and efflux directions provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how microorganisms interface with inorganic materials.

Beyond protein dynamics, fluorescence imaging techniques have been applied to examine the impact of EET on microbial membrane potential and ionic transportation. As known to all, EET process is usually accompanied by electrical stimulation, which may perturb the balance of ion fluxes, altering membrane potential as a direct physiological consequence. By employing specific probes, it is possible to monitor these changes at single-cell resolution. Thioflavin T (ThT), whose fluorescence intensity correlates with membrane potential, was used alongside APG-2, a potassium-sensitive fluorescent dye. Then, time-resolved fluorescence microscopy revealed that EET currents were positively correlated with bacterial hyperpolarization. Moreover, hyperpolarization was found to be spatially localized within individual cells. Interestingly, this spatial restriction could be solved by the addition of exogenous electron shuttles such as riboflavin, which highlights their role not only as mediators of electron transfer but also as modulators of microbial bioenergetics. This line of research demonstrates that probe-based imaging can extend beyond protein labeling to provide functional readouts of microbial physiology. By capturing dynamic changes in membrane potential and ion flux, fluorescence probes offer insight into the coupling between electron transfer and cellular energy metabolism.

Beyond fluorescence microscopy, other imaging techniques can also be integrated with probe-labeling strategies, whereby the dynamic signals of specific probes are captured to indirectly elucidate cellular behaviors, while simultaneously overcoming some intrinsic limitations inherent to the respective imaging techniques. A significant limitation of conventional scattering-based imaging techniques such as SPR is their inability to provide quantitative information about electron transfer. Because the abundance of OMCs is not precisely known and multiple factors can induce their conformational changes, label-free SPR signals cannot be directly translated into electron flux.

To address this limitation, magnetite nanoparticles (MNPs) were employed as optical probes by attaching them to the surface of S. oneidensis MR-1. During EET, electrons transferred from the bacterium to the MNP induced reduction and partial dissolution of the nanoparticles, resulting in decreases in particle volume (Figure A). By monitoring scattering signal changes associated with nanoparticle shrinkage, Tang et al. was able to directly estimate the number of electrons transferred at the single-cell level (Figure B). The measured results led to the first discovery of “bio-current waves,” revealing that microbial EET does not occur as a continuous process but instead oscillates between active and dormant phases (Figure C). Importantly, asynchronous current waves were observed across different cells, indicating that the collective current output in MFCs is limited by temporal heterogeneity in single-cell activity. These findings provide a new conceptual understanding of microbial current production, demonstrating that temporal coordination, rather than intrinsic metabolic capacity, constrains system-level performance.

Probe-labeled optical imaging has emerged as a versatile and powerful approach for studying EET in electroactive microorganisms at the single-cell level. By enabling high-specificity, high-contrast visualization of proteins, ions, and nanoparticle probes, these methods complement the broader but less specific insights afforded by label-free techniques.

Studying Behavioral Correlates of EET at the Single-Cell Level

EET in electroactive microorganisms is not merely a metabolic pathway but is intricately coupled with microbial behaviors such as adhesion, motility, and proliferation. ,, These processes form a dynamic feedback network: while EET activity can reshape microbial physiology, microbial behaviors conversely regulate the efficiency and architecture of electron transfer pathways. Deciphering underlying mechanisms of these bidirectional interactions at the single-cell level is essential for linking energy metabolism with behavioral regulation, thereby offering fundamental insights for the rational design of high-performance MESs.

DET, particularly cytochrome c-mediated electron transport process, requires intimate contact between microbial surfaces and electrodes. To investigate how adhesion contributes to EET, researchers integrated electrochemistry measurement system with atomic force microscopy (AFM) and optical microscopy to develop an in situ electrochemical single-cell force spectroscopy platform (Figure A). This technique enabled real-time measurement of force–distance curves between individual bacteria and electrode surfaces within electrochemical environments. Using this approach, two distinct mechanisms governing bacterial adhesion during EET were uncovered. The first involves electrostatic interactions modulated by surface charges, while the second reflects conformational transitions of OMCs between coiled and extended states, dynamically altering adhesion strength (Figure B,C). Furthermore, variations in solution chemistry were also shown to influence adhesion forces, underscoring the important role of operational environment. By further constructing quantitative relationships between adhesion force and applied potential, this work provided a theoretical foundation for tuning bacterial adhesion, an essential determinant of stable DET pathways.

The interplay between EET and microbial physiology is reciprocal and dynamic. On one hand, EET profoundly influences cellular physiology; on the other, microbial growth, motility, and colonization behaviors also feedback to modulate EET efficiency. For instance, rapid proliferation and directional migration collectively govern biofilm initiation, spatial structure, and species composition at electrode interfaces. , Biofilms not only provide physical scaffolds for EET but also foster cooperative metabolic networks, thereby enhancing community-level electron fluxes. −

To probe these dynamics, high spatiotemporal resolution single-cell tracking technologies have been employed to study early cellular colonization events. Observations revealed that bacterial appendages such as pili and flagella, traditionally considered solely as motility organelles, also act as sensory structures. These appendages detect environmental cues and modulate cellular growth heterogeneity, thereby influencing the architectural evolution of biofilms. This discovery directly links behavioral sensing with metabolic phenotypes, reframing appendages as multifunctional regulators that integrate motility, adhesion, and metabolic adaptation. The recognition that pili and flagella mediate both motility and sensing has broad implications for biofilm development and community-level EET process. In detail, by shaping heterogeneity in growth rates and size stability during early colonization, these structures dictate the emergent three-dimensional biofilm architecture. Structured biofilms, in turn, enhance long-range electron transport, enable interspecies communication, and establish robust “electroactive networks” at the community scale. These findings highlight a key principle: the optimization of EET efficiency is not solely dependent on molecular electron carriers but also on collective behavioral traits that shape biofilm structure and intercellular connectivity.

Summary

In this review, we have systematically summarized recent advances in the mechanistic investigation of EET at the single-cell level in electroactive microorganisms. Beginning with fundamental EET pathways, including direct and indirect electron transfer, we highlighted the roles of multiheme c-type cytochromes, conductive nanowires, and self-secreted or artificial electron shuttles in mediating electron exchange with extracellular acceptors/donors. We then examined how the advent of micro- and nanoelectrode platforms enabled quantitative electrochemical measurements at the resolution of individual bacteria, uncovering heterogeneity in current output, adaptive reorganization of outer-membrane cytochromes, and potential-dependent electron flux. Complementary to these approaches, we discussed optical microscopy techniques. First, label-free optical imaging strategies such as SPR imaging and structural color microscopy, which have provided real-time visualization of cytochrome dynamics, subcellular spatial heterogeneity, and microbial-nanomaterial interactions without introducing exogenous perturbations. Probe-labeled imaging further expanded this toolbox, allowing targeted tracking of proteins capable of transferring electrons, mapping of ion fluxes and membrane potentials, and nanoparticle-assisted quantification of single-cell currents. Finally, we addressed the interplay between EET and microbial behaviors, demonstrating that adhesion, motility, and biofilm formation are dynamically coupled with electron transfer efficiency. Together, these advances facilitate comprehensively understanding the contribution of molecular mechanisms, physiological dynamics, and behavioral regulation to microbial EET.

Looking forward, several technological directions will likely define the next stage of studying single-cell microbial electrochemistry. First, a promising avenue lies in the integration of label-free and probe-labeled techniques into multimodal platforms. Label-free imaging excels at capturing intrinsic molecular dynamics with minimal perturbation, while probe-based approaches offer high specificity and sensitivity for targeted molecules or events. By combining these complementary strengths, multimodal strategies could give more comprehensive insights into microbial EET. Second, experimental designs must address the need for strict environmental control. Many electroactive bacteria are obligate anaerobes, while others must require oxygen for metabolism. Therefore, for long-term and high-resolution monitoring of EET, it demands measurement chambers equipped with rigorously sealed gas pools to maintain defined atmospheres. The incorporation of microfluidic architectures capable of delivering stable gas and nutrient gradients will further extend the duration and reliability of single-cell experiments. Third, moving closer to realistic operational conditions for MESs requires measurements in complex chemical environments. Natural waters and wastes contain salts, organics, and suspended solids that may interfere with both electrochemical and optical signals. Developing robust platforms capable of isolating microbial signals from such background noise will be critical for translating single-cell studies into practical contexts. Strategies may include improved surface chemistries for electrodes, or adaptive optical correction coupled with advanced filtering algorithms. Finally, the combination of single-cell electrochemical technique with machine learning will create powerful new capabilities. By training algorithms on large data sets of current outputs, spectral signatures, and physiological parameters, predictive models could be developed to rapidly identify highly active electroactive strains or even predict microbial performance under fluctuating environment. Such approaches could accelerate the screening of desired microbes and inform the rational engineering of next-generation bioelectrochemical systems.

From the perspective of the obtained information about EET mechanisms, several frontiers remain open. One critical challenge is the mechanistic understanding of nanowire-mediated electron transfer. Although conductive pili and cytochrome arrays have been implicated in long-range electron conduction, the precise molecular basis remains under unknown. Resolving these mechanisms will require the development of techniques with much higher spatial resolution, potentially integrating current single-cell electrochemical measurement techniques with conductive AFM or near-field optical probes. Another area deserved to explore is the interaction between individual cells and biofilms during EET. Current studies often isolate single bacteria from their community, but in real MESs, biofilm architecture and intercellular cooperation profoundly influence electron flow. There is thus a need for methodologies capable of resolving single-cell electron transfer process within intact biofilms. Such approaches would shed light on how individual behaviors make effect on community-level conductivity and stability. Furthermore, a longstanding challenge is the simultaneous correlation of electron transfer events with intracellular metabolic dynamics. At present, electron flux is typically monitored independently of metabolic products distribution, redox cofactors, or enzymatic activity. Multimodal approaches that couple electron transfer-related information with spectroscopic or imaging readouts of intracellular metabolites will be essential to bridge this gap. For example, integrating Raman or fluorescence imaging with single-cell electrochemical probes could allow real-time mapping of metabolic pathways alongside electron transfer activity. These developments would fundamentally enrich our understanding of how energy transition is coordinated across molecular, cellular, and even community scales.

In conclusion, the rapid development of single-cell microbial electrochemical research marks a transformative step toward unraveling the fundamental principles of MESs. By moving beyond bulk-level measurements to the resolution of individual cells, researchers are now able to capture the dynamic heterogeneity, adaptive plasticity, and behavioral complexity that underpin EET process. The impact of these efforts will be not limited in academic inquiry. By providing mechanistic clarity and predictive capability, single-cell microbial electrochemical studies will inform the rational design of microbial strains, electrode interfaces, and reactor architectures. This will in turn accelerate the development of MESs as reliable technologies for renewable energy generation, environmental remediation, and sustainable biomanufacturing. Ultimately, advancing single-cell microbial electrochemistry is not only pivotal for deepening our understanding of microbial electrophysiology but also indispensable for adapting the full potential of microorganisms in addressing the current global challenges of energy and the environment.
