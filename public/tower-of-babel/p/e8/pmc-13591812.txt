Effectiveness of virtual reality disaster education on the performance of nursing students: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chalermkwan Madsong, Arbkhanok Tongtham, Teerapon Dhippayom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisaster education is essential in nursing students to prepare students for crises. Virtual reality (VR) technology offers a promising training approach.ObjectiveTo estimate the difference in performance, time to complete performance, cognitive learning, and self-efficacy of nursing students in VR disaster education compared to traditional education only.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, CENTRAL, CINAHL, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global were searched from inception to 22 January 2025. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs that investigated the effect of VR disaster education on performance, time to complete performance, cognitive learning, and self-efficacy. The Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions, Version 2 (ROBINS-I V2) was used to assess risk of bias. A random-effects model was used to pool the effect estimates across studies. I2 was used to quantify statistical heterogeneity among included non-RCTs. The protocol of this study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420250652860).ResultsOf the 307 articles identified, two quasi-experimental studies (non-RCTs) were included (n = 220). The time to complete the performance was significantly lower in the VR disaster education compared to the control group (mean difference [MD]: -45.18 s; 95% confidence interval [CI]; -52.06 to -38.30), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). No significant difference was observed between VR and the control group for performance score (MD: -0.14; 95%CI; -1.98 to 1.70; I2 = 96%) and cognitive learning (MD: 3.56; 95%CI; -3.12 to 10.25; I2 = 99%), both showing considerable heterogeneity. No safety concern was reported in any of the included studies.ConclusionsVR-based disaster education has the potential to improve completion times in decontamination scenarios, which may contribute to enhanced nursing students’ efficiency in emergency response. However, its effects on performance scores and cognitive learning remain inconclusive, due to non-significant pooled estimates and considerable heterogeneity across studies. Further high-quality RCTs with longer intervention durations are needed to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09792-6.

Background

Disasters are unpredictable events that can lead to significant human suffering and extensive infrastructural damage, including fatalities, injuries, and long-term disabilities [1]. During such emergencies, the primary need of affected individuals is to receive timely and effective healthcare services. This underscores the urgent necessity for a nursing workforce that is adequately prepared to respond to crises [2]. Consequently, it is essential to integrate disaster education as a core component of nursing curricula. This education should not only impart theoretical knowledge but also foster critical competencies, such as analytical thinking, clinical judgment, and rapid decision-making under pressure.

However, implementing these competencies through traditional disaster preparedness training methods often presents various challenges. Live drills and on-site simulations can encounter logistical issues, such as difficulties with scheduling and resource allocation. Additionally, live drills pose safety concerns due to physical risks and stress. They also present financial limitations regarding equipment and personnel costs [3].

In this context, virtual reality (VR) technology presents a promising alternative to traditional simulation-based training. Its capacity to offer immersive, repeatable, and risk-free learning experiences enables nursing students to engage in realistic emergency scenarios without the associated risks of real-life exposure. VR systems, which vary in cost from less than $100 to over $1,000, provide scalable solutions that can be tailored to align with institutional resources and educational objectives [4]. VR-based training allows learners to practice procedures repeatedly, receive immediate feedback, and engage in self-directed learning at their own pace [5, 6]. This technology has been effectively employed in various contexts, such as coal mine disaster simulations and emergency room preparedness, showcasing its potential to enhance learning outcomes and skill retention [7]. By leveraging immersive technologies, educational institutions can better equip nursing students to navigate the complexities of emergency response [6].

Importantly, VR-based disaster education enhances learning transfer, which is the ability to apply knowledge and skills gained in a simulated environment to real-world situations [8]. Immersive VR scenarios closely mimic actual disaster conditions, increasing the chances that nursing students will successfully transfer critical decision-making and performance skills to emergency practice [9, 10]. From a theoretical perspective, the added value of VR lies in its alignment with experiential learning and learning transfer theory. Unlike traditional lecture‑based instruction or limited‑scale drills, VR provides immersive, context‑rich learning experiences that integrate cognitive, psychomotor, and decision‑making processes, offering a stronger theoretical foundation for translating educational experiences into effective performance in real disaster situations [11].

Moreover, VR has shown positive outcomes across multiple domains. These include knowledge acquisition [12], disaster preparedness [13], confidence levels [13, 14], cognitive learning [14–16], performance [13–17], task completion time [14–17], and overall satisfaction [17]. A previous systematic review [10] indicated that VR significantly enhances knowledge retention, practical skills, and academic satisfaction among nursing students. However, it suggested that the effectiveness of VR was highly context dependent.

In recent years, the increasing frequency and complexity of disasters including large‑scale fires, extreme weather events, mass casualty incidents, and global public health crises. For instance, the COVID‑19 pandemic have underscored the critical need for effective disaster preparedness among nurses [18]. These events have exposed limitations in traditional disaster education, which often relies on didactic instruction or small‑scale drills that are insufficient to replicate the dynamic, high‑pressure conditions of real emergencies [19].

Unlike prior reviews that broadly examine simulation or VR applications in general nursing education, this study focuses specifically on VR‑based disaster education, where contextual realism and emergency preparedness are particularly critical, thereby addressing an important gap in the current evidence base. In addition, inconsistencies reported in previous findings highlight the need for targeted and rigorous evaluation of VR’s role in disaster nursing education. Accordingly, the present study seeks to assess the effectiveness of VR-based disaster education specifically designed for nursing students. By drawing comparisons with traditional educational methods, this review aims to provide evidence-based insights into the integration of VR within nursing curricula and its potential to enhance preparedness for real-world emergencies.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review according to the Cochrane Collaboration guidelines for a systematic review of interventions [20] and following the PRISMA 2020 statement [21]. The protocol of this study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420250652860). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Search strategies

We conducted a comprehensive search in the following databases: PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Central Register of Clinical Trials (CENTRAL), the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, from the inception of each database to January 22, 2025. Our search focused on three main keywords: “nursing student,” “virtual reality” or “mixed reality,” and “disaster.” A complete list of search terms utilized in each database can be found in Appendix 1. To ensure we identified all relevant studies, including those not indexed in the aforementioned databases, we also screened the reference lists of the included studies and used Scopus to track articles that cited them.

Selection criteria

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized controlled trials (Non-RCTs) were included in this study if they met the following criteria: (1) the participants were nursing students; (2) the intervention group received disaster education using virtual reality (VR); (3) the control group received traditional education methods (e.g., written materials, videos, or online learning); and (4) the studies measured at least one of the following outcomes: performance, time to complete performance (measured in seconds), cognitive learning, and self-efficacy. CM and AT independently screened the titles and abstracts of the search results to identify studies that assessed the effectiveness of VR in disaster education among nursing students. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were subsequently reviewed independently by CM and AT and verified by TD. Any disagreements or uncertainties regarding inclusion were resolved through discussions with TD.

Data extraction

CM and AT extracted data by using a pre-specified data extraction form. Data extracted included: setting; number of participants; participant characteristics such as age, gender, previous disaster training, disaster experience, VR experience, online gaming experience; detail of the intervention and control such as detail of VR, type of training, duration, platform, provider; outcome measurement; and funding. TD verified data extraction. The outcomes of interest were performance, time to complete performance in sec, cognitive learning and self-efficacy.

Quality assessment

We used the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions, Version 2 (ROBINS-I V2) assessment tool to evaluate the quality of included studies [22]. CM and AT independently assessed the quality of included Non-RCTs on an Excel tool to implement ROBINS-I V2. The overall judgment based on the seven risks of bias domains was classified into four categories, i.e., critical, serious, moderate, and low risk of bias. Disagreements between the reviewers were settled through discussion, and a consensus was reached with the third reviewer (TD). We presented the traffic light plot of the domain-level judgments for each Non-RCTs by using a visualization tool web application, robvis [23].

Analysis

We conducted a pairwise meta-analysis to determine the pooled effect estimates and 95% confidence interval (95%CI). We calculated the mean difference (MD) between intervention groups for performance, time to complete the tasks, and cognitive learning to compare the effectiveness of VR. We employed a random-effects model (the Dersimonian and Laird) for all analyses [24]. We assessed statistical heterogeneity among included trials by using the Chi-squared test and I2 [25]. All analyses were conducted by using RevMan version 5.4.

Results

Search results

A total of 144 articles were retrieved from electronic databases. In addition, 163 studies were identified from other searching techniques. Among those, only two studies were eligible for inclusion in this systematic review [14, 15] (Fig. 1) with a total of 220 participants.

Study characteristics and details of interventions

Two included studies were quasi-experimental [14, 15]. Most participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 33 years old (86.8%) and 18–25 years old (73.3%). Both studies used VR in disaster education in decontamination training, plus web-based learning modules as the intervention of interest compared to traditional education (i.e., written instructions) as a control group. The VR in a study by Smith et al. [15] used a 3D environment with ocular and hand tracking to simulate an emergency room (ER) setting. This allowed participants to demonstrate decontamination skills, such as donning personal protective equipment (PPE), selecting equipment by physically reaching out, and removing contaminated clothing. On the other hand, the study by Farra et al. [14] employed a similar approach but utilized two-dimensional (2D) VR technology with camera tracking to monitor movements. Both studies provided participants with a web-based module before beginning the VR training. Participants engaged in VR activity for 10 min and then completed a post-test immediately following the intervention. Additionally, a mannequin (i.e., dummy) was used to conduct pre-tests and post-tests to evaluate performance and the time taken to complete tasks. The post-test assessment was administered right after the intervention. Both studies measured performance (in score), time to complete performance (in seconds), and cognitive learning understanding (in score) (Table 1).

Effects on performance

Both included studies assessed performance in decontamination scenarios, which included tasks such as donning personal protective equipment (PPE), removing patients’ clothing, and conducting patient cleansing procedures. Performance was evaluated using a 17-item Decontamination Checklist as the measuring tool [14, 15]. The pooled MD was − 0.14 score (95%CI; -1.98 to 1.70). One study reported lower performance scores in the VR intervention group compared to traditional education, which served as the control [14]. In contrast, the second study found slightly higher scores in the VR group [15] (Table 2). These opposing trends contributed to the considerable statistical heterogeneity observed (I² = 96%) (Fig. 4). This suggests that there is no difference in performance between VR intervention and traditional education.

Effects on time to complete performance

Both studies reported the duration used to complete the decontamination procedures (measured in seconds). The pooled MD of VR in the disaster education group compared to the control group on time to complete decontamination procedures was − 45.18 (95%CI; − 52.06 to -38.30) with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. 5). This indicates that the VR intervention facilitates the completion of contamination procedures more rapidly than traditional educational methods.

Effects on cognitive learning

Two studies reported cognitive learning as an outcome of interest, measured by the FEMA IS-346, a 20-item assessment tool [14, 15]. One study showed a higher cognitive learning score in the VR intervention group compared to traditional education [14]. The second study found no difference between the VR intervention group and the traditional education group [15]. Moreover, none of the studies showed a significant difference in cognitive learning outcomes related to decontamination performance when comparing VR to traditional education (p > 0.05) (Table 2). The pooled MD of VR in disaster education compared to control on cognitive learning was a 3.56 score (95%CI; -3.12 to 10.25) with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 99%) (Fig. 6). This suggests that the effect of VR on cognitive learning is not different from traditional education.

Effects on self-efficacy

Only one study reported self-efficacy as an outcome immediately after the intervention [14]. The self-efficacy for the VR in disaster education group was 50.00 ± N/A, while self-efficacy for the control group was 19.25 ± N/A; p = 0.172.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive summary of current evidence regarding the effects of VR disaster education on nursing students. The findings indicate that VR training significantly reduces the time required to complete decontamination scenarios when assessed using a mannequin, compared with traditional training methods. This suggests that VR may enhance procedural efficiency in emergency situations. However, the evidence does not support a positive effect of VR on performance, cognitive learning, and self-efficacy.

Both included studies demonstrate consistent improvements in task completion time in the VR group. These improvements are supported by pooled estimates with homogeneous data (I² = 0%). Importantly, both groups were assessed using a mannequin to evaluate decontamination performance completion time, ensuring consistency in the outcome measurements. This outcome represents the strongest evidence in our meta-analysis. It highlights the potential of VR-based disaster education to enhance procedural efficiency, which is essential in situations that require quick action, such as disaster response training. By streamlining the execution of specific procedures, VR training could greatly improve response times in critical situations, ultimately leading to better outcomes in real-world emergencies. However, this positive impact contrasts with the findings of Lochmannová [26], who observed slower task completion times among paramedic students trained with VR during mass casualty incident simulations. The delay was attributed to participants’ unfamiliarity with the VR interface, whereas the control group trained with mannequins and real-world equipment. Similarly, Smith et al. [17] found that participants trained with mannequins completed tasks more quickly than those in the VR group post-intervention. Notably, the present study differs in its control condition, using traditional education rather than mannequin-based simulation, which may influence comparative outcomes.

However, despite VR’s potential to improve task completion time, as evidenced by consistent improvements in completion time, its effect on performance scores and cognitive learning remains inconclusive. One possible explanation relates to learning transfer theory. Learning transfer occurs when knowledge or skills acquired in one context influence performance in another, which can be either positive or negative. Transfer can be categorized into near transfer, which involves closely related contexts and tasks, and far transfer, which involves more distinct contexts and tasks [8]. VR-based disaster education seems to support far transfer from virtual practice to real disaster response; however, several limitations were identified in the included studies, such as short exposure time (10 min), lack of scenario diversity, and cognitive load from unfamiliar VR interfaces, which may have restricted learners’ ability to generalize skills beyond the simulation environment. Moreover, variations in VR technology may trigger reflexive or low-road transfer. This reliance on reflexive transfer may explain why performance and cognitive learning outcomes showed negative transfer, as participants focused on operating the system or repeating familiar tasks rather than applying deeper conceptual understanding. These findings underscore the need for future research to design VR interventions that foster conditions for mindful (high road) transfer through longer exposure, diverse scenarios, and strategies that reduce cognitive load. Consistent with previous systematic reviews of VR‑based nursing education, brief exposure durations and limited scenario diversity have been associated with mixed effects on higher‑order outcomes such as performance and cognitive learning [10, 27]. While short VR sessions may improve task efficiency, they appear insufficient to support far and mindful (high‑road) learning transfer required for complex disaster decision‑making [3, 11]. Restricted scenario variability and cognitive load from unfamiliar interfaces may further limit learners’ ability to generalize skills beyond the simulated environment [28]. Accordingly, future VR‑based disaster nursing research should prioritize longer or repeated exposure, greater scenario diversity, and instructional strategies that reduce extraneous cognitive load to better facilitate sustained performance gains and cognitive learning transfer [29, 30].

The considerable heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 96%) and the inconsistent findings regarding performance may be attributed to differences in VR technology: i.e., a desktop-based or 2D system with camera tracking [14], and a more immersive three-dimensional (3D) environment that used eye tracking through head-mounted displays (HMDs) with ocular and hand tracking [15]. The immersive 3D environment is likely to create a stronger sense of presence and realism for participants. Such immersive experiences can enhance engagement, leading to more effective simulation-based learning. This intrinsic difference in technology and experience could account for the divergent findings observed in the two studies, highlighting the need for further exploration into the impact of VR modalities on training outcomes. Similarly, studies by Kiegaldie and Shaw [31] and Farra et al. [32] found that higher levels of VR immersion tend to enhance student experience and engagement in nursing education. However, due to the substantial heterogeneity in the pooled performance outcomes, these results should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, the VR systems used in 2015 and 2018 such as Kinect-based 2D VR and basic head-mounted displays offered limited immersion, low-resolution visuals, and rudimentary motion tracking. In contrast, modern VR platforms e.g., Oculus Quest, HTC Vive, and Microsoft HoloLens, which feature high-resolution displays, precise 6-DoF motion controllers, haptic feedback, AI-powered scenarios, and cloud/remote capabilities [33]. These advancements deliver significantly higher spatial presence, interactivity, and simulation fidelity, which are crucial in areas like surgical training [34], anatomy education [35], and emergency response in medical education [36]. Thus, further studies utilizing VR technology, are required to validate the findings. In addition, future trials should standardize the reporting of key hardware specifications, such as frame rate, field of view, and level of interactivity. This would facilitate clearer comparisons across studies and help isolate the technological factors that may influence cognitive and performance outcomes.

Our findings indicate that VR did not significantly enhance cognitive learning in disaster education. One possible reason for this outcome is the limited exposure time, as participants only engaged with the VR content for 10 min. This short duration may have hindered their ability to fully immerse themselves in the experience and engage in deeper learning processes. Longer VR sessions up to 45 min may improve perceived learning and competence; however, the benefits may diminish due to user fatigue [37]. Additionally, the lack of diversity in the scenarios presented during the VR session may have restricted opportunities for critical thinking and contextual understanding. Immersive VR environments are most effective when they offer varied and interactive scenarios that stimulate both emotional and cognitive learning pathways [38]. Moreover, the cognitive load imposed by navigating VR environments, particularly for users who are unfamiliar with the technology, could have interfered with learning [39]. Participants may have focused more on operating the system rather than absorbing the educational content. It is imperative to note that the pooled effect for cognitive learning exhibited significant heterogeneity (I² = 99%), indicating that these findings necessitate cautious interpretation. Furthermore, validation through additional high-quality research conducted over extended periods is essential. Therefore, these findings should be viewed as exploratory rather than definitive.

Self-efficacy was assessed only in Farra et al. [14], which found higher scores in the VR group, though not statistically significant. This suggests that while there was a noticeable trend favoring the VR group, the results were not strong enough to draw definitive conclusions. The limited evidence highlights the need for further investigation into the emotional and psychological outcomes of VR-based disaster education programs, specifically how these programs may impact participants’ confidence and preparedness in emergency situations. Additionally, broader research in nursing education consistently shows that VR training significantly improves students’ self-confidence and perceived competence. A study by Cho and Kim [11] for example, found that VR-based simulations had a large positive effect on nursing students’ competency and self-confidence. This improvement is largely attributed to VR’s ability to provide repeated, hands-on practice and immediate feedback, allowing learners to refine their skills in a safe, controlled environment. As a result, students often feel more prepared and confident when transitioning to real-life clinical settings.

Our findings present a contrast to those of Liu et al. [10], who performed a systematic review of 12 RCTs involving nursing students. Their results showed significant improvements in practical skills, following VR-based training. In contrast to our study, which employed short, disaster-specific VR modules lasting only 10 min. Liu et al. focused on longer, multi-session VR programs that were integrated into comprehensive nursing curricula. Their interventions aimed to bolster general competencies, including clinical decision-making and procedural skills, often within combined academic and clinical environments that utilized both VR and traditional teaching methodologies. Conversely, the studies in our review primarily utilized VR as an independent tool with limited integration into pedagogical frameworks. Additionally, Liu et al.‘s findings were influenced by methodological variability and heterogeneity, suggesting that the effectiveness of VR is likely contingent upon the specific context, design, and implementation of the training programs.

In our review, VR-based disaster education did not demonstrate statistically significant differences compared to traditional methods in terms of performance and cognitive learning outcomes. This inconclusiveness may be attributed to the limited number of existing studies, which hinders the ability to capture the comprehensive impact of VR intervention. Nonetheless, recent research has identified notable advantages associated with immersive learning technologies, particularly in enhancing learner engagement, knowledge retention, and skill acquisition within healthcare training environments, especially in nursing education [10, 40]. These insights align with the Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025, which emphasizes the importance of integrating digital health competencies into the educational curricula for all health professionals and allied workers [41]. Furthermore, digital health education is integral to improving preparedness and resilience in the event of future public health challenges [42]. A recent study from Australia highlighted that developing a proficient digital health workforce requires educational frameworks that keep pace with emerging technologies, thereby enabling graduates to drive innovation while ensuring digital safety [43]. Importantly, emerging evidence from the COVID‑19 pandemic highlights that the successful implementation of simulation‑based and immersive learning systems is shaped not only by the sophistication of the technology itself, but also by broader systemic factors, including institutional digital readiness, instructor competency, and equitable access to digital infrastructure and resources [44–47]. During the pandemic, healthcare institutions rapidly adopted digital health and remote learning technologies out of necessity, accelerating digital acceptance and revealing significant variation in organizational preparedness and capacity. These contextual factors continue to influence the sustainability and effectiveness of VR‑based education in the post‑COVID era. Accordingly, future research and policy development should account for institutional readiness, workforce digital literacy, and health equity considerations to maximize the long‑term impact and scalability of immersive learning approaches in healthcare education.

Moreover, the lack of positive effects may be attributed to the considerable variability among the studies included, brief training sessions, limited scenarios, and technological constraints. Additionally, participants may have needed more time and practice to become familiar with the VR tools. Furthermore, the focus on decontamination tasks may have restricted broader learning outcomes. To address these challenges, VR‑based disaster education should be integrated into nursing curricula through phased or blended approaches, beginning with low‑cost, high‑impact scenarios and supplemented by conventional teaching methods. While VR has the potential to enhance training efficiency, its educational impact remains uncertain. Future studies should employ rigorous designs, using study designs that are methodologically comparable and closely aligned with contemporary VR technologies, and extending the training duration, diversify scenarios, and evaluate cost‑effectiveness to better inform sustainable curricular implementation in nursing disaster preparedness.

Implications for practice

To effectively utilize VR in nursing education, it should complement traditional teaching methods rather than replace them. Training sessions should allow for repeated practice and extend beyond brief exposure. Institutions should invest in high-fidelity VR systems that provide immersive experiences. Scenarios should cover a variety of emergency situations, and learners should have opportunities to repeat tasks and receive feedback. Moreover, the integration of VR in training programs must be continuously evaluated using performance metrics and learner feedback to ensure effectiveness. Future studies should focus on incorporating real-world performance measures, such as longitudinal tracking of clinical decision-making and simulated disaster drills, to assess how well skills learned through VR translate to practical application. These initiatives help bridge the gap between virtual training and real-world readiness, enhancing the impact of VR in nursing education. Although evidence is limited, this review contributes by identifying critical gaps and offering preliminary guidance for integrating VR into disaster education for nursing students.

Limitations

A key limitation of this analysis is the small number of studies included, as the pooled estimates were based on only two studies. Moreover, two outcomes demonstrated substantial heterogeneity, which reduces the consistency of the evidence and limits the strength of the conclusions. Nonetheless, these findings provide a foundational basis for future well-designed studies to further validate and expand upon these results. The included studies were conducted in 2015 and 2018, a period when VR technologies primarily relied on 2D systems or early HMD platforms with limited immersion, interaction, and motion‑tracking capabilities. In contrast, contemporary standalone 6‑DoF VR systems offer substantially higher levels of spatial presence, interactivity, and realism through advanced tracking and immersive hardware. These technological differences may influence learning experiences and outcomes, thereby limiting the generalizability of the present findings to current VR‑based nursing education contexts. Additionally, the inclusion of non-RCTs with a moderate risk of bias could impact the credibility of the findings. Another limitation of our systematic review is that only one study reported self-efficacy, which is one of the vital outcomes in VR training. The limited information regarding this outcome may result in inconclusive findings concerning the efficacy of VR in disaster education. Nonetheless, the single study that addressed self-efficacy indicated that participants in the VR group demonstrated superior self-efficacy levels. Lastly, all studies included in our review administered posttests shortly after the intervention, specifically immediately following a 10-minute VR training session. This duration for intervention may adversely affect the findings. A longer training duration and follow-up period could potentially yield different results.

Conclusion

This systematic review suggests that VR-based disaster education has been explored as an approach that has the potential to improve completion times in decontamination scenarios, which may contribute to enhanced efficiency among nursing students during emergency response. However, the interpretability of these findings is limited due to the small number of studies, substantial heterogeneity, short intervention duration, and outdated technological capabilities. Therefore, strong conclusions regarding its effectiveness in improving nursing students’ performance, cognitive outcomes, or self-efficacy are not warranted. Rather than demonstrating definitive effectiveness, the available evidence highlights important gaps in the current literature, and any indications of improved procedural efficiency and preparedness should be interpreted with caution. Future research should prioritize rigorous randomized controlled trials, incorporate extended and repeated VR exposure, diverse scenario-based learning, and leverage advanced immersive technologies to better evaluate the educational impact of VR in disaster preparedness for nursing students.
