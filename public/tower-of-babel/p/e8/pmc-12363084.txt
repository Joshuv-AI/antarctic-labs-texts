Health worker perspectives on, and health system-level facilitators and barriers to early infant male circumcision for HIV prevention in a traditionally non-circumcising community, Northeastern Uganda: a cross-sectional qualitative study
Authors: Michael Ediau, Stephanie Brodine, Kimberly C. Brouwer, Anita Raj, Eyal Oren, Susan M. Kiene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVoluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) remains one of the recommended HIV prevention measures in heterosexual men in selected countries, including Uganda. While most VMMCs are on adolescent and adult males, its longer-term potential population-level impact might be realized through the inclusion of early infant male circumcision (EIMC). However, EIMC’s successful rollout requires understanding the perspectives of health workers, health system-level facilitators, and barriers to EIMC. Following EIMC pilot/project implementation, we explored health workers’ individual-level perspectives on, and health system-level facilitators and barriers (to EIMC) in a traditionally non-circumcising setting in Northeastern Uganda.MethodsIn April 2022, we conducted a qualitative study involving 26 in-depth interviews. Participants included seven health workers who participated in EIMC and seven who did not, two heads of hospitals that conducted EIMC, and six District health officials, four representatives of national-level partners who supported EIMC implementation in the study area. Data were coded and analyzed using inductive (for individual-level perspectives) and deductive (for health system-level barriers and facilitators) thematic analyses.ResultsHealth workers who had participated in EIMC reported its comparative advantages, including performance simplicity, faster wound healing, and relatively lower cost. Some health workers who had not participated in EIMC expressed concerns regarding the safety of the procedure and the potential associated pain. Health system-related facilitators for EIMC included leadership support and availability of funding and supplies for initial implementation and health workers’ capacity building through EIMC training. This support was limited to the project’s period and later ended. Therefore, barriers to EIMC implementation, including a lack of supplies and funding, were reported.ConclusionEIMC was perceived as a comparatively beneficial intervention for possible HIV prevention because, compared to VMMC, it is technically simpler, safer, potentially low-cost, and wound healing is faster. Nevertheless, some health workers raised concerns about EIMC. Leadership support, funding, health workers’ EIMC training, equipment, and supplies availability facilitated EIMC. Some facilitators were short-lived as a lack of continued funding and supplies stock-outs were major EIMC barriers. Successful implementation requires sustained EIMC financing and addressing the concerns of some health workers, in addition to the identified health system-related barriers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13330-3.

Background

Three randomized controlled trials demonstrated that voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) is approximately 60% effective in reducing HIV acquisition in heterosexual men in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1–3]. Hence, the World Health Organization (WHO) and The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS (UNAIDS) urged the inclusion of VMMC to the comprehensive HIV prevention package in countries where the prevalence of heterosexually transmitted HIV infection was high, the levels of male circumcision (MC) were low, and populations at risk for HIV were large [4] like Uganda. Thus, in 2010, Uganda set a target of 80% VMMC coverage [5]; however, only 54.1% of men in the country are circumcised, and only 32.4% of men reported having had VMMC. In addition, there is significant regional variation in the VMMC coverage, ranging from as low as 17.0% in the Northeastern (Karamoja) region to as high as 47.2 in Kampala. Only 19.2% of eligible men in the Northeastern (Teso) region have had VMMC [6].

Studies have identified early infant male circumcision (EIMC), which is performed within the first 60 days of life as a potential HIV prevention intervention for further sustaining the prevention gains anticipated through VMMC, albeit when the infants get older and become sexually active [4, 7, 8] and it could be implemented alongside VMMC in countries with high HIV prevalence and low MC coverage like Uganda [4]. EIMC offers relative benefits. It offers long-term protection from HIV acquisition [9]. It is technically simpler to perform and offers faster wound healing than MC in adolescents/adults [10–12]. EIMC can be safely performed [13–15] by nurses and clinical officers with high (up to 100%) parental satisfaction in developing country settings [14, 15], including Uganda [15] and Kenya [14]. Under some assumptions, evidence suggests that EIMC could also be less expensive than adult or adolescent MC [10], although more recent and conclusive evidence is still needed.

The WHO issued guidelines for EIMC for HIV prevention [12]. However, it further recommended that countries planning to include EIMC as a strategy for achieving sustainable VMMC coverage carefully consider risks and benefits, as well as acceptability issues, ethics, human rights, cultural preferences, and the resources available to deliver EIMC safely [12, 16]. Indeed, evidence also shows that culture, religion, and age influence MC [17, 18]. Thus, rates of MC vary across social, cultural, and religious groups. MC is not traditionally practiced in most areas globally, including Uganda [19]. Unlike in other regions of Uganda, MC is generally not practiced for cultural or religious reasons in the northeastern region [20]. Recent data suggests that only up to 4.4% of males had nonmedical MC in this region [6]. A realignment of the health system to respond to the contextual uniqueness of the target population to promote EIMC suggests the need for studies targeting such service planners and providers as health workers to understand their perspectives on EIMC and the health system-level facilitators and barriers.

Varied perspectives have also been reported among key EIMC stakeholders, such as health workers [15, 21, 22]. Some of these perspectives suggest facilitators for service delivery and the uptake of EIMC for HIV prevention [14, 21, 22], while others are barriers [21]. Therefore, while evidence generated elsewhere has explored perspectives on EIMC [14, 15, 21, 22], it may not be generalizable to Ugandan settings due to the varying contexts. Nevertheless, as a potential facilitator, quantitative research in Rakai, Uganda, demonstrated that nurse midwives and clinical officers (equivalent to physician assistants) were competent in conducting EIMC [15]. However, understanding stakeholders’ perspectives regarding EIMC in the broader context of health systems and in historically non-circumcising communities is warranted for expanding the EIMC intervention for HIV prevention [23]. Specifically, there is a lack of evidence on health systems-related perspectives, yet such evidence is necessary for the introduction and scale-up of EIMC. Additionally, no studies have employed a theoretical framework that incorporates health system-level factors to investigate health workers’ perspectives on EIMC. Yet, such a framework would enable an effective exploration of elements and perspectives at both the health system and community levels around EIMC and how they are interlinked, thus facilitating a better understanding of the potential facilitators and barriers. This study explored the perspectives of health workers on, and the health system-level facilitators and barriers to, EIMC for HIV prevention in Katakwi district and Soroti city, traditionally non-circumcising communities in northeastern (Teso) Uganda, to inform future scale-up efforts.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional qualitative study collected data through in-depth individual interviews (IDIs) with selected health workers and representatives of partners who supported EIMC in the study districts. It was conducted in April 2022. It aimed to explore the individual-level perspectives of health workers and the health system-level facilitators and barriers to EIMC for HIV prevention in a traditionally non-circumcising community, particularly in Soroti city and Katakwi district in Northeastern (Teso) Uganda. The northeastern (Teso) region had not widely practiced MC until the introduction of VMMC. EIMC was implemented in selected hospitals in these districts from March to June 2019 with support from the Ministry of Health and other partners.

The study was conducted at Soroti Regional Referral Hospital (SRRH), Princess Diana Memorial Health Center IV, and Katakwi General Hospital, which implemented EIMC. Soroti Regional Referral Hospital is situated in Soroti City, and is located 320 km northeast of Kampala, the capital of Uganda. It serves as the referral hospital for at least 10 districts including Soroti, Katakwi, Serere, Amuria, Kaberamaido, Kapelebyong, Kumi, Ngora, and Bukedea. The Princess Diana Memorial Health Center IV is a public hospital located in the West Division of Soroti City, Soroti District. It is also located approximately 322 km northeast of Kampala, the capital of Uganda. It serves the population of Soroti West Division and other surrounding communities. Katakwi Hospital is a public general hospital in Katakwi town, Katakwi district. It is approximately 358 km northeast of Kampala, the capital city of Uganda. All two hospitals (SRRH and Katakwi general hospital) and Princess Diana Memorial Health Center IV provided routine VMMC services for adolescents and adults at their static (in-hospital) and outreach sites. UNICEF funded EIMC procedures, which were performed using the Mogen clamp.

Conceptual framework

The present study utilized the Health System Dynamics Framework [24] (Fig. 1) to guide the assessment of health workers’ perspectives, including information on elements of the health system, and to understand the dynamic interactions between these elements of the framework such as human resources, infrastructure, financing, and service delivery, how these ultimately would affect the implementation of EIMC and the desired HIV prevention benefit.Fig. 1Health worker and health system levels barriers and facilitators to EIMC for HIV prevention in northeastern Uganda mapped on the J. van Olmen et al., 2012 health system dynamics framework

The framework is suited to guide the study because it not only helps capture the health systems framework components or blocks [25], but it also takes into account the context, which is important in this case. In this case, the context is that the study area is traditionally non-circumcising with very low MC coverage [6]. The framework also has the population component. All these contextual components were crucial and needed to be taken into account during the study. The health system dynamics framework has also been utilized in various studies to clarify concepts and enhance understanding of the underlying mechanisms in health systems research and strengthening. In Uganda, one study applied it to assess the dynamic interactions driving the sustainability of antiretroviral therapy scale-up implementation [26]. Another qualitative study titled “If donors woke up tomorrow and said we can’t fund you, what would we do?” A health system dynamics analysis of the implementation of prevention of mother-to-child transmission of HIV (PMTCT) option B + in Uganda also applied it [27]. Similarly, in Zambia, a qualitative study used this framework to understand the frontline healthcare workers’ experience of the PMTCT policy changes and to explore the health system’s readiness to respond to rapid PMTCT policy changes [28]. In addition to its application to HIV research, the health systems dynamics framework has also been applied to research on noncommunicable diseases [29, 30].

As presented in the framework (Fig. 1), excellent leadership is essential for introducing and scaling EIMC services. It is vital to develop policies and guiding documents, provide enabling environments, and forge strategic partnerships with all key stakeholders, including financiers and implementers. Financing is equally essential since it is required to facilitate all other components. Furthermore, human resources for health, as well as commodities and equipment for EIMC, and the EIMC health information system are all critical resources for EIMC service delivery. These, however, interact within the context that the area is traditionally non-circumcising and has a very low MC coverage.

Participants and selection criteria

In Katakwi district, Katakwi General District Hospital was included by default because it was the only hospital that provided EIMC. In Soroti city, Soroti Regional Referral Hospital and Princess Diana Memorial Health Center IV, which provided EIMC, were randomly selected using simple random sampling from three possible hospitals that provided EIMC. In total, 26 participants were interviewed, all of whom were 18 years or older. These included seven health workers who participated in EIMC (EIMC health workers) and another seven who were not directly involved in EIMC (non-EIMC health workers) but were involved in delivering maternal, newborn, and child health services, selected using snowball sampling. Snowball sampling was used to help easily identify the participants who met the inclusion criteria. This is because only a few health workers (among all health workers) in the selected hospitals met the inclusion criteria, and they were more likely to be familiar with one another. Two heads of hospitals that conducted EIMC (n = 2), district health officers (n = 2), and assistant district health officers (n = 2) in charge of maternal and child health, and the district health educators for the two districts (n = 2) were included and interviewed by default because only one person occupied each of their positions. Four interviews were conducted with representatives of non-governmental organizations that supported the implementation of the EIMC in the two study districts, as well as with EIMC trainers. Saturation of themes - a situation where the collection of qualitative data reaches a point where no new information emerges [31–34]- was achieved with this number of participants (N = 26). Subsequently, data collection was stopped.

Participant recruitment, data collection tools, and procedures

Recruitment scripts (Appendix 1) were developed, each tailored to aid the recruitment of participants in a specified category. In addition, with the guidance of the recruitment script, the study team ensured that only participants aged 18 years or older were interviewed (Appendix 1), using the appropriate interview guide (Appendix 2). The study used semi-structured IDI guides (Appendix 2) to collect participant data from all participants. Three IDI guides were developed in English (Appendix 2) to collect data from specific participant categories as follows: (1) IDI guide specifically for healthcare workers who participated in EIMC, (2) IDI guide specifically for healthcare workers who had not participated in EIMC, and (3) IDI guide specifically for hospital in-charges, District Level Health Managers, and National level Stakeholders. Each IDI was conducted with only individual participants. The lead researcher (ME) conducted the IDIs with the support of an experienced research assistant (RA) who received additional training on qualitative interviewing techniques and adherence to ethical considerations. All IDIs were audio-recorded and conducted in the English language using a face-to-face method at the participants’ workplace. IDIs took about 40 min.

Analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, removing any identifiable information, and entered into NVivo (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia), a qualitative data storage and retrieval program. We employed inductive thematic analysis to examine the individual-level perspectives of health workers regarding EIMC. In this analysis, six transcripts were initially reviewed to identify themes and sub-themes and develop a coding framework. Then we used this framework to code all the remaining transcripts, although more sub-codes were added to the coding framework as they emerged. We employed a deductive thematic analysis technique to examine the data on health system-level facilitators and barriers for EIMC. This was performed in line with the health system dynamics framework [24] (Fig. 1). Within the framework, the key blocks of the health system dynamics framework formed predetermined themes. They included leadership and governance, financing for EIMC, human resources for EIMC, EIMC health management information system, commodities and equipment for EIMC, EIMC service delivery, and the population. (Fig. 1). The lead researcher and an experienced RA coded transcripts separately using the modified coding frameworks and noted emerging codes. Thus, the coding framework was revised in accordance with any agreed-upon changes, particularly regarding the health workers’ individual-level perspectives on EIMC. Coding disagreements were resolved through discussion. In presenting results, themes and sub-themes were illustrated with representative quotes.

Results

Perspectives of health workers towards EIMC

In speaking with health workers who had EIMC experience and those without, we found important differences in perceptions surrounding EIMC, as presented below.

Perspectives of health workers who participated in EIMC

Health workers reported mixed perspectives on EIMC, including changes in their opinions before and after training, as well as after performing EIMC. They reported that before training and participating in EIMC, they had anxieties and concerns regarding EIMC, including its safety, the pain the male infants go through, and the risks of circumcision wound infection. According to them, these anxieties and concerns cleared when they attended the training and conducted EIMC. These new perspectives, which reinforced the value of EIMC, facilitated their participation in service delivery. One of them said: My thoughts about it changed because before I participated in the training and actual procedures, I knew it was good, but I didn’t support it. I imagined the child in too much pain, crying. I would also imagine if the wound gets infected, it will cause abnormalities in the child. But when I attended it and conducted circumcision, I was like, this thing is safe because I could see mothers come after two weeks, and the babies were okay. (EIMC health worker).

Another health worker reinforced: My thoughts about infant circumcision greatly changed because I realized the procedure was very safe given the method we were using, the Mogen clamp, and the anesthesia you just apply on top. You don’t inject. You just rub on the penis of the baby. Then the care, there was not much care needed. The cosmetic part of it was also really very impressive, and performing it was great. So, after the training, I think it opened up my mind, and things have moved on well. (EIMC health worker).

Knowledge and perspectives on the comparative benefits of EIMC were also important facilitators, as most participants welcomed EIMC benefits, citing its expected future HIV prevention benefits and perceived comparative advantages to adolescent/adult MC, like faster wound healing, safety, a simpler procedure, less costly, less pain, and easy circumcision wound care. It is beneficial and should be conducted because you prevent the child from getting diseases in the future or in their adult life, like sexually transmitted diseases such as HIV. It also helps address conditions like phimosis. (EIMC health worker).

Another participant concurred by saying: The younger age is okay because the circumcision wound heals together with the umbilical cord, under the mother’s care, so hygiene is maintained well. It heals very fast compared to the big children who want to play around like adolescents, where the healing would not be faster. (EIMC health worker).

About the pain, one health worker explained: I really feel there’s not much pain that these babies go through. I was trained, and we have done a number of these procedures. I think it’s just because during circumcision, the babies are held in the same position, and yet they want to move their limbs, so they cry. But there are some who, when held very well, will not even make any noise, meaning the procedure appeared painless. (EIMC health worker).

Perspectives of health workers who did not participate in EIMC

Discussions with health workers who had not participated in EIMC revealed views both in favor and against EIMC. For instance, while some raised concerns about the age and safety, they also believed that the pain from the procedure was manageable and that the procedure was simple and quick, which they found beneficial. For the ones I’ve seen, the age, especially the very young, I feel it is risky, but then there’s less pain during that time because what I know is it heals so fast, and it’s not something that takes so long. It’s one of the quickest procedures I have ever seen. (Non-EIMC health worker).

Some discussions revealed that some health workers were skeptical about the HIV prevention benefit of EIMC. On the side of HIV prevention, I am still reluctant to say it will protect an infant from HIV infection. No, we expect this to happen later in life when this child becomes an adult and engages in unprotected sexual intercourse. But by the time this child grows to that point, do we think the HIV prevention effectiveness of circumcision will still be that high? Or almost not there? Subjecting the child to circumcision, including pain and risks when this child cannot get involved in sexual activity, is unnecessary. Allow the child to reach adolescence or adulthood, then subject him to circumcision. I think that would be better. (Non-EIMC health worker).

Health system-level facilitators and barriers

Our study also explored the perspectives of all study participants regarding the six health systems building blocks in delivering EIMC, including governance and leadership, financing, human resources for health, service delivery, medical supplies and equipment, and health information systems as presented in the Health System Dynamics Framework [24] and the WHO health system building blocks [25]. Additionally, we explored views on the sustainability of EIMC. These are also included in the subsections below.

Leadership and governance for EIMC

Leadership and governance support for EIMC

Interviews highlighted leadership-will at the national, district, and health facility levels, as well as at the community level, as an essential facilitator and a sustainability factor for the implementation of EIMC in Soroti and Katakwi. One participant elaborated: There is a leadership will. We have the policy that supports EIMC in place, and we have the EIMC national training curriculum and the WHO manual for EIMC under local anesthesia. These are critical guiding documents for the rollout of EIMC in Uganda. (National-level participant). A health worker who participated in EIMC described how hospital leadership supported EIMC: As a team, we were permitted by the hospital director to do this in the hospital. There was support from the leadership, and then also, by that time, we had the supplies, so doing the procedure was so simple. We didn’t have to look for things to use. (EIMC health worker).

Financing for EIMC

Availability of financing for EIMC during the pilot intervention

Participants reported that UNICEF, in collaboration with implementing partners and the Ministry of Health, funded the piloting of EIMC in select health facilities in Soroti and Katakwi.

….It was good, UNICEF facilitated us monetary wise. (EIMC Health Worker).

Human resources for EIMC/health

Effective health workers’ capacity building through training to perform EIMC

Discussions suggested that training health workers, such as clinical officers, midwives, and nurses in EIMC was key to promoting the procedure. The hospitals, therefore, had teams trained and could conduct EIMC. This was also considered a potential sustainability factor for EIMC, given that the hospital was government-owned (public). As a facility, we are proud of the Ministry of Health for training our health workers who can now ably do these procedures. You know, before that training, people could go to look for surgeons in Soroti, or even in Mbale for these pediatric circumcisions. But now, if a mother comes with a child, the child is worked on. Actually, we have a ready team; whenever an opportunity comes, we don’t let it go. (Hospital in-charge). Another health worker also emphasized how they were well-trained on EIMC and motivated: As a team, yeah, we got training. It was good, and we were well facilitated not only monetary but like the people who trained us made it more hands-on, which in turn made sure that we were able to deliver a very good service and when we delivered a very good service, the results were good. Participants could look at the circumcision outcome and be like oh wow! This is good. So if they had not trained us well, maybe our work could have been messy. (EIMC health worker).

The simpler nature of the EIMC procedure and the availability of health workers who were already trained in, and were conducting VMMC

According to the participants, the EIMC procedure is simpler and therefore the available human resources for health particularly nurses and clinical officers who already had been conducting adult and adolescent VMMC could efficiently conduct it (EIMC). They particularly emphasized that since medical doctors are relatively scarce and have many competing priorities, the use of such cadres like clinical officers, particularly those who are well qualified, to scale up EIMC should be encouraged. One national-level participant also explained: The procedure is much simpler in that it can be performed by trained midwives, nurses, clinical officers, and doctors at any time after birth or when thought appropriate. Given the scarcity of medical doctors and other competing health priorities that they need to attend to, using clinical officers and other cadres like registered midwives should be encouraged and it is what we are using in scaling up adult and adolescent male circumcision (National level participant). The national-level discussions also confirmed and further explained this: We first trained those who were already familiar with the circumcision of adults to make it easy for them. (National-level participant).

Commodities and equipment for EIMC

Availability of medical supplies and equipment for EIMC

The participants reported that during the EIMC pilot, the hospitals received supplies, including Mogen clamps, surgical blades, antiseptic with chlorhexidine, cotton wool, sterile gauze, emla cream for topical anesthesia, and Vaseline jelly. One participant had this to say …we received supplies and equipment after the training. The ministry left us with some instruments, particularly the Mogen clamps. (EIMC health worker).

Health information systems

Discussions revealed that EIMC is integrated into the health management information system. One health worker explained The same system the HMIS has provision for it. It is already integrated, but only that it’s not performed, and it is always reported as 00, but it is already integrated into the system, so the reporting system is already there. (EIMC health worker).

Population/parents’ willingness to embrace EIMC

At the population level, the discussions revealed that, despite being a traditionally non-circumcising population or region, there was generally little resistance to EIMC among the population (parents). The discussions showed that some parents were already willing to have their sons undergo EIMC, and these were circumcised. One health worker said: There is generally a better community perception of circumcision compared to earlier on. (District Health Manager). Another health worker expounded by saying: ……some mothers were already willing to bring their babies to be circumcised, and within that short time, we managed to circumcise some babies (EIMC Health Worker).

EIMC service delivery

Integration of EIMC into maternal and child health services

Most participants emphasized the integration of EIMC into maternal and child health services, as well as adult MC services, as a vital approach for delivering EIMC. For most of the infants we circumcised, we got them from the postnatal clinic and the maternity unit. Very few came from outside. So integration is happening, and it would be the way to go. It would work because, as they are coming for their postnatal visits, they would access circumcision, or as they are coming for maybe a vaccination, they could also be educated, and so integration of early infant male circumcision into other services was critical. (Hospital in-charge).

Health system barriers to EIMC

During the discussion, study participants reported some health system-level barriers to EIMC in the study area or sites. These are elaborated below.

Financing for EIMC

Lack of financing for continued EIMC

Participants revealed that, while EIMC was piloted in Soroti and Katakwi, among other districts in Uganda, this was for a limited time. At the time of this study, the Uganda Ministry of Health was not rolling out EIMC. Therefore, when the pilot of EIMC ended, the funding support also ended. By the time of this particular study, the funding had already ended. One participant had this to say No, there’s no funding. In the first place, the funding was there but only for the camp that was for a short period, and it ended. After the camp, there was no more funding, but since the public already had the information, we continued doing the work until we hit a rock when the supplies got done. (EIMC health worker).

Another participant further explained that while health workers were trained to provide EIMC, they lacked funding to provide other necessities to perform EIMC: Financially, we are not okay to facilitate EIMC. So, we need some financial backup. We do not have a budget for it (District health manager).

Commodities and equipment for EIMC

Lack of EIMC supplies when the pilot intervention ended

According to the participants, while the EIMC pilot intervention involved the provision of EIMC and supplies, including surgical blades, antiseptic with chlorhexidine, cotton wool, sterile gauze, emla cream for topical anesthesia, and Vaseline jelly, the stocks for these got finished. Thus, hospitals mainly offered EIMC services to infants whose parents requested and could afford them. The supplies we received after the training got finished. Supplies have been a problem. You find that we have only circumcised children whose parents can afford the supplies. Yet other parents are interested but cannot afford them. (EIMC health worker).

Discussion

This study was designed to address the limited evidence regarding the individual health worker perspectives and health system-level facilitators and barriers to the implementation of EIMC in traditionally non-circumcising settings.

Our findings contribute to the existing body of research knowledge elsewhere in SSA, highlighting diverse perspectives among EIMC stakeholders, including health workers and policymakers [22, 35]. Some perspectives facilitate the uptake of EIMC [8, 14, 21, 22, 36, 37], while others impede it [21, 36, 38]. Specifically, the study revealed some positive perspectives regarding EIMC, particularly among health workers who had participated in it. They believed that EIMC offers faster wound healing and is simpler to perform. Nonetheless, some health workers who had not participated in EIMC also expressed doubts about the perceived health value of EIMC as well as the risk and pain associated with it. While such concern may suggest the need to sensitize such health workers, it may also be legitimate and calls for further studies to, for example, predict and estimate the future benefit of EIMC, particularly given the rapidly changing trends in HIV prevention and care.

Beyond reinforcing prior findings [8, 14, 21, 22, 36–38], our study highlighted the effectiveness of EIMC training and hands-on experience in changing the perspectives of health workers. Specifically, health workers who participated in EIMC reported initial anxieties about the safety and difficulty of the EIMC procedure, the pain that children go through, and their age. Their perceptions, however, changed in support of EIMC with training and practice. Conversely, health workers who had not participated in EIMC maintained concerns about EIMC, particularly the safety of the procedure, pain, and the very young age of the infant at the time of MC. The findings imply that during the scale-up of EIMC, implementers need to ensure health workers understand its benefits. Thus, awareness campaigns need to be designed in such a way that they effectively explain the benefits of EIMC by addressing the risk of HIV and the non-HIV advantages of EIMC compared to the adult or adolescent MC. There is also a need to explain how EIMC is performed, including the fact that the procedure does not require sutures and it involves non-life-threatening or minimal bleeding, faster healing, and easy wound care, especially relative to MC procedures for adolescent and adult men [9–13, 15, 39–42]. This may alleviate some of the health workers’ concerns that may act as barriers to the scale-up of EIMC.

On the other hand, a study on the cost-effectiveness of VMMC for HIV prevention across SSA, which used five independent models, revealed that VMMC remains a cost-effective and cost-saving prevention intervention in SSA for at least the next five years [43]. In addition, Serwadda and Kigozi (2023) asserted that a rapid decline in HIV incidence projected in the optima model between 2020 and 2030 [44] could make VMMC less cost-effective for HIV prevention. This projection likely indicates a need to reassess the cost-effectiveness of rapidly declining HIV incidence [44]. While we note that our study is qualitative and may not be very comparable with these previous modeling studies [43, 44], it suggests some level of consistency in that our findings also reveal that some of the health workers, particularly those who had not participated in EIMC, also questioned the need for EIMC for HIV prevention, given that this benefit is realized later in life when the children reach adulthood. Suggesting that it (EIMC) may not be required once the child gets older and becomes sexually active. This is a likely future scenario and, thus, a potentially legitimate concern that calls for further research to establish the likely HIV prevention effect of EIMC in decades to come. This is particularly a viable concern given that the scale-up of VMMC for HIV prevention is for countries where the prevalence of heterosexually transmitted HIV infection remains high, the levels of MC are low, and populations at risk for HIV are large [4]. However, EIMC remains relevant to the current Ugandan context.

Notwithstanding the participants’ concerns, the present study further identified health system-level facilitators for initiating and scaling up EIMC and potentially sustaining it. We found that the following factors facilitated the implementation of EIMC: favorable leadership support at the national, district, and hospital levels; the availability of health workers and required training; and the availability of funds (which was only during the pilot implementation). In addition, integrating EIMC into other maternal and newborn health services, ongoing adult and adolescent MC, and good community mobilization also facilitated the delivery and uptake of EIMC in this specifically traditionally non-circumcising region in Uganda. These findings reinforce the existing literature, particularly in the SSA region, where studies have shown that health workers believe robust support from hospital, regional, and national leadership [36, 45], donor funding, and integration into the existing medical infrastructure [8, 14, 21, 45] are vital for EIMC success.

Previous studies have also documented a mix of health system-level barriers to the introduction and scale-up of EIMC for HIV prevention [21, 36, 38, 46]. Similarly, our study revealed that the delivery of EIMC services in the study area was not without barriers, which could also affect the potential future scale-up if these barriers are not addressed. The major barriers that participants were concerned about were the need for more EIMC financing and support for supplies to facilitate and sustain its scale-up. This is particularly important because, in Uganda, the national scale-up of male circumcision for HIV prevention programs is mainly financed by development partners (external donors). More importantly, this study’s findings reveal that, at the time of this study, development partners only supported adult and adolescent MC, but not EIMC. In addition, the government of Uganda, through the Ministry of Health, prioritized adult and adolescent MC and not EIMC. This implies the need to ensure funding partners and government buy-in to support the scaling up of EIMC and HIV prevention interventions. This study also specifically found that the financial and supplies support for implementing EIMC in Soroti and Katakwi districts was limited to the project period. This necessitates the strategic coordination of funding sources to support EIMC, indicating a need for enhanced leadership involvement. It also implies the need to develop a model to support this impactful intervention without necessarily relying on external funding, but certainly being mindful of other national health competing priorities [8, 47].

Strengths and limitations of the study

One strength of this study was the diversity of participants, including in-depth interviews with health workers at various health facilities and districts, as well as national-level participants from EIMC-supporting organizations. Specifically, the study included health workers who participated in EIMC and those who did not. Thus, concordance among data obtained from different participants reinforced the likely validity of the results. In addition, many of the issues presented here also arose in similar qualitative studies designed to investigate stakeholders’ perspectives on EIMC.

The potential limitation is that the data were limited to IDIs. Thus, the lack of triangulation of data collection methods possibly limited the understanding of perspectives around EIMC. However, there was largely concordance between data obtained from different participant categories, highlighting the likely validity of the results. This study also does not include perspectives from community members and parents, yet they could have influenced the EIMC in the study area. Our study findings may also not be very generalizable to other traditionally non-circumcising regions. In addition, the study did not collect and report on participant characteristics.

Conclusions

This study adds to the evidence gap on health workers’ perspectives regarding facilitators and barriers to the introduction and scale-up of EIMC, particularly in traditionally non-circumcising settings. Health workers who participated in EIMC believed that EIMC was a safe, easy-to-perform it and it offered faster wound healing. However, some health workers who had not participated in EIMC feared that the procedure might be harmful or painful. Leadership and governance support, the availability of funding, health workers’ training on EIMC, the provision of EIMC equipment and supplies, as well as the delivery of EIMC in a more integrated approach, all contributed to the implementation of EIMC in the study area. The support on these was, however, limited to the period when EIMC was being implemented. Thus, despite its potential HIV prevention benefits, the lack of continued funding and unavailability of supplies to support continued EIMC were key health system-level barriers to EIMC. These findings will inform the design of interventional strategies to support the broader and more sustainable adoption of EIMC.
