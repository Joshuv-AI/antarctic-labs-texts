Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a genetic disorder that disproportionately affects African populations, with over 75% of global cases found in sub-Saharan Africa.1 SCD continues to be the most common cause of death for all age groups in the WHO Africa area, accounting for 3.68 deaths per 100 000 people.2 Each year, approximately 300 000 infants are born with SCD in Africa, and as of 2021, there were 12.18 fatalities per 100 000 children before age 5 due to inadequate diagnosis and curative treatment.2 3

Several medications, including hydroxyurea and voxelotor, are available to improve clinical outcomes for patients. Hydroxyurea has been approved by the FDA since 1998 as a modifying drug therapy for SCD, and it has shown efficacy in reducing complications of SCD. Hydroxyurea improves erythrocyte deformability, lowers haemolysis and decreases circulating leukocytes and reticulocytes.4 Small molecule drugs like voxelotor, L-glutamine and crizanlizumab have also demonstrated effectiveness in improving quality of life in patients.1 5 6 However, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) offers a curative solution by replacing defective bone marrow with healthy stem cells. Its application in Africa is limited by high costs, lack of infrastructure and donor compatibility challenges.1 7 Only seven African countries offer HSCT, and many patients must travel abroad, often without access to proper post-transplant care on return.8 Given these limitations, the central question emerges: What lies beyond HSCT for Africa in the fight against SCD? This paper examines innovative therapies, assesses barriers to their implementation and proposes strategies for their scalable adoption.

Limitations of HSCT in Africa

A curative option for SCD is HSCT, which involves the infusion of hematopoietic stem cells from a matched donor into the patient following the administration of a preparative conditioning regimen.9 Despite its curative potential, HSCT remains largely inaccessible in Africa. As of recent data, only seven African countries: Algeria, South Africa, Tanzania, Morocco, Nigeria, Egypt and Tunisia have established HSCT programmes.1 9 These centres are concentrated in urban areas and serve a small, often elite population, with the cost of the procedure ranging from $100,000 to $200,000 per patient.10

However, the procedure requires specialised expertise and infrastructure, which are scarce across the continent.7 Lack of donor matching has been a major hurdle, especially in regions with high prevalence of infectious diseases and lack of bone marrow registries.11 Following transplantation, the post-transplant care is critical but often unavailable locally, leaving patients who undergo HSCT abroad frequently returning to health systems unequipped to manage complications or provide long-term follow-up.1 This inequity underscores the need for alternative, scalable solutions tailored to Africa’s realities.

Emerging and innovative therapies

Given the growing success of these therapies globally, it is critical to assess their feasibility, comparative advantages over HSCT and potential impact within African healthcare systems. Biotechnological innovations have introduced promising alternatives to HSCT, offering new avenues for curing SCD in Africa. Gene editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9 and exa-cel therapy, aim to correct the genetic mutation responsible for SCD by modifying autologous hematopoietic stem cells ex vivo, followed by a conditioning regimen before reintroducing the genetically modified cells into the patient (figure 1).12 13 The uptake of gene-based therapies in Africa could address many limitations of the availability of matching donors associated with HSCT and reshape curative care for SCD. Unlike HSCT, which requires matched donors and carries risks of graft-versus-host disease and transplant-related mortality,1 gene editing therapies, such as exa-cel and CRISPR-Cas9, use autologous cells, thereby eliminating donor dependency and reducing immunological complications.12

Key stages in the process of gene therapy.

Although these therapies have demonstrated success in clinical trials, their implementation remains constrained by high costs and infrastructure demands.12 Gene silencing approaches, such as siRNA-based treatments, suppress harmful haemoglobin variants, while gene addition strategies introduce functional β-globin genes to restore normal haemoglobin production.14 Allogeneic cell therapies and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models offer additional avenues for regenerating healthy blood components.15

Globally, many of these therapies have received regulatory approval or are in advanced clinical trials. Their potential for adaptation in African contexts is significant, provided that necessary infrastructure and policy frameworks are established.7 12 Although these therapies are currently expensive and infrastructure-intensive, clinical trials and regulatory approvals in the United States and Europe affirm their safety and efficacy, with durable increases in fetal haemoglobin and reduced vaso-occlusive episodes.12 Countries including the United States, United Kingdom and France have begun implementing these therapies under compassionate use and commercial pathways, offering models for global adaptation.12 15

Low- and middle-income countries such as India are making efforts towards innovative therapies for SCD. The National Sickle Cell Elimination Mission in India (2023–2047) was launched in 2023 to screen 70 million people, enable early diagnosis and expand access to treatment. While gene therapy is not yet available in India, significant groundwork is underway to make it a reality.6 Institutions such as CSIR-IGIB, C-CAMP and the IITs are developing cost-effective gene-editing tools, while biotech companies are focusing on the local production of CRISPR components and viral vectors to reduce costs. Furthermore, government-sponsored clinical trials are paving the way for gene therapy to become accessible to Indian patients in the near future.6

In African contexts, adopting these curative approaches could yield substantial clinical, economic and social benefits. Chronic SCD management imposes a significant financial burden on families and health systems, especially where access to hydroxyurea and transfusion services is inconsistent.3 11 Gene therapies, though costly, offer a one-time intervention that could eliminate lifelong treatment needs, reduce hospitalisations and improve productivity and quality of life.7 8 Moreover, integrating these therapies into national health strategies could catalyse broader genomic medicine infrastructure, benefiting other conditions as well. Through strategic investment, regional manufacturing and policy reform, African nations can begin to build sustainable access to advanced SCD care.7 11 12

For instance, Tanzania, a member of the SickleInAfrica consortium, is collaborating with stakeholders across the continent to develop SCD registries, implement newborn screening programmes, improve access to hydroxyurea and enhance clinical care initiatives to strengthen the healthcare system.8 In 2021, the Sickle Cell Programme (SCP) at Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS), in collaboration with various local and international partners, spearheaded the launch of the Sickle Cell Disease Advanced Therapy Programme (SCD-ATP). This initiative builds on the progress made by the SCP, aiming to enhance the capacity of researchers, healthcare professionals and patient advocates to foster and strengthen partnerships dedicated to advancing therapy interventions for SCD in Tanzania. The programme was established in response to identifying gaps in advanced healthcare services for patients experiencing severe SCD complications.8

Challenges to uptake in Africa

The adoption of advanced SCD therapies in Africa faces numerous barriers.

Cost

Cost remains the most significant obstacle, with gene therapies priced far beyond the reach of most African health systems.10 Current gene therapy methods necessitate ex vivo gene modification after mobilised autologous CD34+cells are collected by apheresis. The patient receives another infusion of the genetically altered stem cells following myeloablative conditioning. Patients experience severe cytopenias for several weeks as a result of this procedure, which puts them at risk for opportunistic infections and necessitates regular transfusion support. Long-term hospital stays at specialised transplant facilities are therefore required. In the US, recently approved therapies like Lyfgenia and Casgevy cost $3.1 million and $2.2 million, respectively. These reflect only the direct cost of gene therapy. The cost is further increased with hospital stays and supporting care, making access to the therapy nonexistent or severely restricted.16

Many individuals in African countries primarily rely on out-of-pocket funding and/or external funding for healthcare and lack health insurance schemes. The majority of SCD patients are unable to cover their healthcare expenses, not to mention gene therapy for SCD.17 The health system operates within resource constraints, and most countries shoulder a burden of infectious diseases, including malaria, HIV and tuberculosis, causing substantial funding to be diverted towards the relief of these diseases.18

Regulatory framework

Regulatory capacity is also limited, with many countries lacking frameworks to evaluate and approve complex biologics.3 Most African countries lack guidelines addressing gene therapy and its research. This might pose a risk of overlooking important ethical issues. Moreover, existing regulatory guidelines in some countries, like South Africa, may not yet accommodate the long-term follow-up requirements, management of off-target effects, or quality assurance of locally manufactured or imported gene therapy products. The absence of clear pathways for clinical trial approval, product registration, and ongoing pharmacovigilance can delay or even prevent patient access to potentially curative treatment.17

Inadequate infrastructure

Manufacturing and distribution pose logistical challenges as cold chain requirements and weak supply chains limit the accessibility of advanced therapies.11 Manufacturing necessitates the procurement of high-quality raw materials, potentially involving human and animal-derived materials. Due to the potential absence of laws governing importation regulations, sourcing of these materials outside national borders could pose challenges, thereby compromising the safety and affecting the quality of the products. The local workforce must also be trained and educated to manufacture and formulate cell and gene therapies using specialised facilities and methodologies.19 The health system readiness is another concern, with most facilities lacking the infrastructure for genetic testing, cell processing, and long-term monitoring, which hinders the promise of innovative therapies.1

Technical expertise

Human resources are insufficient, with a shortage of trained clinicians, genetic counsellors and lab technicians.8 These advanced therapies require the expertise of professionals trained in specialised areas, including advanced biotechnologies.18 These trainings may require workshops locally and/or abroad, and there is a constraint of inadequate funds to train healthcare professionals in specialised areas.9 Countries also face challenges in developing the skilled personnel necessary for manufacturing, quality assurance, and regulatory compliance, further impeding the translation of gene therapies from bench to bedside.19

Associated risk to benefit consideration

Exposure to myeloablative chemotherapy with people undergoing gene therapy may infrequently still be at risk for cancer, infertility, and organ toxicity. This may also continue to put them at risk of experiencing a poor quality of life because of residual organ damage from SCD and its after effects (such as osteonecrosis, nephropathy, cardiomyopathy, impotency and stroke). The dangers associated with gene therapy are a major worry for adult patients and the majority of parents of children with SCD, and many do not think these risks, when combined, justify the possible advantages. Gene therapy for SCD is still a ‘high-risk, high-reward therapy’, despite being licensed by the Food and Drug Administration, restricting the acceptability of gene therapy.20

Opportunities for scalable uptake

Despite these challenges, several opportunities exist to scale innovative SCD therapies in Africa. The scope and sustainability of newborn screening for sickle cell disease (SCD) in sub-Saharan Africa and other resource-constrained regions worldwide are limited by the high cost, complexity, and reliance on electricity, specialised equipment, and supplies associated with conventional diagnostic methods, as well as the sustainability of SCD curative intervention.21 Point-of-care screening tests offer an inexpensive, dependable, and user-friendly way to screen for sickle cell disease, guaranteeing the earliest diagnosis feasible, the highest level of participant follow-up, access to treatments locally (such as hydroxycarbamide, pneumococcal vaccines and penicillin prophylaxis), and efficient preventive measures regionally (such as transcranial doppler for stroke risk). Reducing sickle cell disease mortality and morbidity in sub-Saharan Africa and other high-prevalence nations is a top priority.22 23

To bridge the gap of ethical and regulatory oversight, harmonised frameworks should take into account the African research and clinical environment while adhering to international norms. This includes regulatory systems for the production or approval of gene therapy products in Africa and the availability of comprehensive healthcare for sickle cell disease. African national medical regulatory bodies, health research institutions, research ethics committees, pharmaceutical companies, patient support/advocacy groups, and international health organisations, like the WHO would need to work together to develop such comprehensive guidelines.17

African countries can leverage existing networks and partnerships, such as the African Vaccine Regulatory Forum (AVAREF) established by WHO in 2006, to build skills in clinical trial assessment, regulatory compliance, and ethical oversight, creating a foundation for future gene therapy. AVAREF has been instrumental in aligning regulatory standards, advancing vaccine evaluations, and improving equitable access to essential medical products. By facilitating collaboration between national regulatory authorities and ethics committees, the forum has transformed the regulatory environment, establishing a foundation for strengthened clinical trial oversight and faster product approvals.24

Another initiative that can be leveraged is the SickleInAfrica consortium, which advances equitable access to advanced therapeutic services and has established an ethically and legally approved sickle cell disease registry, enrolling over 13 000 participants.25 Also, public-private partnerships can facilitate local manufacturing and tiered pricing models, making treatments more affordable.3 15 Strategies to ensure this include leveraging government incentives such as tax relief and expedited regulatory approvals, while engaging multinational pharmaceutical companies through co-financing schemes and technology transfer agreements. These approaches help reduce production costs and encourage local investment in SCD treatment infrastructure.3

Implementing task-shifting strategies, training nurses and mid-level providers can expand clinical capacity. Establishing clinical trial sites in Africa would not only improve access but also leverage the continent’s genetic diversity for research.7 12 This diversity is critical for developing therapies that are more effective across varied genetic profiles, and it allows researchers to identify biomarkers and treatment responses unique to African populations. Moreover, local trials reduce logistical barriers and foster trust among communities, which can accelerate recruitment and improve long-term engagement with care systems.7

Governments in countries with a high burden of SCD have a critical role in formulating policies that promote access to comprehensive SCD care, including the integration of SCD services into existing health systems. There is a pressing need to establish SCD Centres of Excellence across Africa to strengthen capacity for gene therapy delivery, while also serving as hubs for advanced research and workforce training. From expanding newborn screening initiatives to reinforcing comprehensive care services and enabling equitable access to curative therapies, collaborative efforts can have a transformative impact. By pooling resources, expertise and institutional capacity, partnerships can address gaps in healthcare delivery and improve outcomes for individuals with sickle cell disease in Africa.26

Health financing reforms, such as insurance coverage and donor support, can reduce out-of-pocket costs.10 Community engagement is equally vital, as educating patients and families about new therapies builds trust and improves uptake, especially when delivered through culturally sensitive outreach and trusted local health workers.27 In addition, addressing stigma through storytelling and peer-led education can empower affected individuals and reduce social isolation, as seen in initiatives by the Sickle Cell Foundation of Ghana and similar organisations.28 Creating community networks and support groups also fosters shared learning and resilience, while behavioural science-informed messaging, such as framing health behaviours in terms of social norms and emotional relevance, can enhance participation and adherence to treatment plans.29

In response to declining external aid, Africa CDC, in partnership with African Union Member States, has revised long-term health financing strategies to strengthen the sustainability of healthcare and research systems across the continent. The strategy calls for renewed commitment to the Abuja Declaration, urging governments to allocate at least 15% of national budgets to health, while introducing innovative domestic financing mechanisms, including solidarity levies on airline travel, alcohol and mobile services. It also explores leveraging Africa’s substantial diaspora remittances to support national health priorities, alongside the use of blended finance to mobilise public and private investment in health infrastructure, digital health and local manufacturing of vaccines and medical products.30

Implementation is structured in phases, with the initial phase (2025–2026) focusing on revising national health financing plans, piloting new revenue mechanisms and enhancing transparency through performance dashboards. The subsequent phase (2026–2030) aims to scale effective models, enabling at least 20 countries to finance 50% or more of their health budgets through sustainable domestic sources. To ensure accountability and measure progress, Africa CDC introduced an African Health Financing Scorecard to track performance, improve donor coordination and enhance the efficiency of domestic health spending.30

Since gene therapy may carry risks that many patients find intolerable, children under the consent-giving age should be well-informed and able to give their consent before receiving therapy, and parents and guardians should also be well-informed so they can make the best choice.20 Therefore, for patients and parents considering gene therapy, clinicians should provide current, interactive and age-appropriate teaching materials. A joint decision-making process and ‘checking-in’ regarding treatment alternatives over time may foster trust and enthusiasm in gene therapy for individuals for whom it is highly recommended. Furthermore, while addressing patients’ and parents’ concerns regarding this promising, potentially curative therapy, discussions involving both a patient’s haematology team and the gene therapy/transplant team may be beneficial in building trust.20 31

By aligning these strategies with national health priorities, Africa can create a sustainable pathway for integrating advanced SCD treatments. Collaboration among governments, academia, industry and civil society will be key to success.14

Call to action

To move forward, African governments must prioritise SCD in their national health agendas. This includes allocating funds for research, treatment and infrastructure development.3 The African Union and health bodies like the Africa CDC should lead efforts to build regulatory systems capable of evaluating and approving advanced therapies.11 Investment in local biotechnology through grants, incubators and academic partnerships is essential.15

Global pharmaceutical companies and donors must adopt equity-based access models. This includes differential pricing, licensing agreements and support for local production.7 12 Together, these actions can transform the landscape of SCD care in Africa, ensuring that innovative therapies reach those who need them most.

Conclusion

SCD continues to impose a heavy toll on African populations. While HSCT offers hope, its limitations necessitate a broader exploration of emerging therapies. By addressing systemic barriers and leveraging strategic opportunities, Africa can position itself for scalable uptake of innovative treatments. The time to act is now, through collaboration, investment and commitment to health equity.
