A curcumin-enabled ZIF8 nanosystem enables ovarian cancer treatment by inducing mitochondrial disruption
Authors: Zhishan Zhang, Genghui Luo, Xiaoxia Tang, Xiubin Ma, Weitao Huang, Bin Wang, Xueying Meng, Caiyan He, Xiaoman Cai, Ping Zhang, Shiyou Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer is a common malignancy affecting the female reproductive system. Curcumin (Cur) demonstrates potential as a treatment for ovarian cancer, but creating an effective curcumin-based drug delivery system remains challenging. The advent of the Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework (ZIF) system offers a novel approach for antitumor drug delivery. In this study, effective drug delivery to ovarian cancer cells was accomplished through the construction of a ZIF-based nanodrug delivery system, ZIF-Cur, that encapsulates curcumin. We verified that Cur delivered by ZIF enhanced mitochondrial ROS accumulation in tumor cells, thereby killing tumor cells. The system showed the well biosafety profile, offering a insight therapeutic perspective for ovarian cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-025-04380-x.Keywords: Curcumin, ZIF8, Ovarian cancer, ROS, Mitochondria

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is a common and highly lethal malignancy affecting the female reproductive system [1, 2]. The primary therapeutic modalities for ovarian cancer include surgical intervention and chemotherapy. Surgical procedures aim to excise the maximum feasible amount of tumor tissue; however, surgical resection alone frequently proves insufficient for optimal therapeutic outcomes [3]. Consequently, combination drug therapies are commonly employed in treatment regimens for ovarian cancer. Cisplatin-based chemotherapeutic agents constitute the most frequently utilized first-line treatment. The use of antiangiogenic agents or PARP inhibitors has been demonstrated to enhance patient survival rates [4–6].

In recent years, curcumin has garnered significant attention from researchers as a potent antitumor agent. Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound from turmeric, exhibits significant antitumor potential, especially against ovarian cancer [7, 8]. Curcumin exerts its anti-ovarian cancer effects through multiple mechanisms, including interfering with cellular interaction factors essential for energy metabolism, metastasis, and recurrence in ovarian cancer cells and increasing the expression of proapoptotic proteins. It can also modulate the production of molecules like cytokines, transcription factors, enzymes, protein kinases, and growth factors [9–12].

In terms of interference with tumor mitochondria, current research indicates that curcumin exerts multifaceted regulatory effects on tumor cell mitochondria. These effects primarily inhibit tumor growth through mechanisms such as disrupting mitochondrial function, inducing oxidative stress, and promoting apoptosis. Curcumin inhibits mitochondrial electron transport chain complexes I and II, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. Tumor cells already have elevated ROS levels, and curcumin further exacerbates oxidative stress, leading to mitochondrial membrane lipid peroxidation and DNA damage, thereby triggering tumor cell apoptosis [13, 14]. Additionally, curcumin can downgrade antioxidant enzymes within tumor cells (such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)), thereby weakening their ability to clear ROS [15, 16].

However, curcumin faces the challenges of low bioavailability and poor water solubility in clinical applications. These findings prompted us to further explore the means for the effective delivery of curcumin. Zeolite imidazole framework (ZIF8) is a metal‒organic framework material composed of zinc ions and dimethylimidazole organic linkers [17, 18]. It has attracted much attention because of its excellent specific surface area, microporous structure, excellent chemical stability, and sensitivity to acidic environments. Moreover, in vitro experiments confirmed that ZIF8 can maintain its stability for more than 15 days at a physiological pH of 7.4. In addition, ZIF8 can be degraded in acidic solutions, which helps to promote the release of encapsulated drugs. These properties make ZIF8 an ideal carrier for designing smart drug delivery systems. Notably, the acid-responsive degradation property of ZIF8 is particularly prominent in the acidic microenvironment of tumors. Owing to the acidic nature of the tumor microenvironment, ZIF8 is able to control drug release in this specific environment, resulting in longer-lasting and stable therapeutic effects.

In this study, we developed a one-pot synthesis method to prepare a quinolone-loaded ZIF8 drug delivery system (ZIF-Cur). It effectively aggregated in the ovarian cancer region through the nanosize EPR effect. Compared to traditional Cur nanoparticles, the constructed ZIF-Cur nanoparticles exhibit controllable drug release in acidic microenvironments and excellent biocompatibility. Additionally, compared to other carriers, ZIF demonstrates a higher encapsulation efficiency for Cur [19, 20]. After Cur is released in response to the acidic microenvironment of the tumor, the mitochondria of the cells in the tumor area are destroyed, which greatly limits the ability of the tumor to proliferate and migrate, thereby effectively killing ovarian cancer cells. This nanosystem provides a new strategy and possibility for targeted therapy of ovarian cancer (Scheme 1).

Methods

Synthesis and characterization of ZIF8 and ZIF8-Cur

ZIF8 was synthesized by dissolving 4.00 mmol (1.19 g) of Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O in 10 mL of methanol and stirring the solution for 20 min. Subsequently, 10 mL of a methanol solution containing dimethylimidazole (16.00 mmol, 1.54 g) was gradually added. The resulting mixture was stirred overnight, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with alcohol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. In the synthesis of ZIF8-Cur, curcumin (2.00 mmol, 0.74 g) was stirred with Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O for 20 min, maintaining all other procedural steps unchanged. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to examine the morphological characteristics of the samples. A Malvern analyzer was used to measure particle size and zeta potential. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy were employed to further elucidate the crystal structure and chemical composition, respectively.

Experiments were conducted involving the grouping of mice, administration of treatments, and measurement of tumor volumes.

Twenty mice, each with an average tumor volume of 90 mm³, were randomly assigned to four groups: the control (Con), ZIF, Cur, and ZIF-Cur groups. Each group was administered tail vein injections every four days, totaling four injections. The control group was given 200 µL of saline, while the ZIF, Cur, and ZIF-Cur groups received 200 mg/kg of their respective substances. The experiment ended on day 16, with tumor volumes calculated using the formula V = 4π/3ab², where ‘a’ and ‘b’ are the tumor’s long and short diameters, respectively. The subjects were evaluated, and upon the conclusion of the experiment, the mice were humanely euthanized. The major organs were subsequently harvested for further analysis. The tumor regions were photographed and weighed to assess the outcomes. All animal experiments were performed following the ethical protocol approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Longgang Center Hospital of Shenzhen (No. 202501045). All procedures strictly followed the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and fully conformed to the Animal Welfare Act.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 18.0 (IBM, USA) software. Data were conducted via analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (95% confidence interval). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and analysis of ZIF8-Cur

ZIF8 is renowned for its outstanding stability, biocompatibility, and effective cellular uptake. ZIF8’s intrinsic characteristics make it an ideal candidate for diverse applications, notably in drug delivery and biomedical engineering, where it can significantly enhance therapeutic outcomes. In this research, a one-pot synthesis method was used to load ZIF8 with Cur, thereby forming novel nanoparticles with multiple effects. The polyhedral structures of both ZIF8 and ZIF8-Cur were clearly observed via TEM (Fig. 1A). A Malvern analyzer was subsequently employed to precisely determine the dimensions of the nanoparticles and thereby facilitate effective EPR.As shown in Fig. 1B, the particle sizes of ZIF8 and ZIF8-Cur were 57.27 ± 2.96 nm and 54.52 ± 2.03 nm, respectively. Additionally, their zeta potentials were 9.79 ± 1.25 and 10.30 ± 1.17, indicating the ability to further increase their enrichment in tumors while reducing their effect on normal tissues. Furthermore, a lower PDI indicates that the system has not experienced particle size distribution drift or drug crystallization (Fig. 1C). XRD and FTIR analyses of ZIF-Cur were performed with reference to published literature. Studies indicate that the loading of Cur has a limited effect on the stability and crystallinity of the ZIF framework, with no significant alterations observed. Therefore, this study only performed the corresponding characterization on ZIF and ZIF-Cur [19, 21]. FTIR spectral analysis of ZIF8 and ZIF8-Cur demonstrated a clear correspondence between their characteristic spectral peaks (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the crystal morphology of ZIF8 and its derivative ZIF8-Cur was further analyzed via XRD, and the results revealed that the structure and morphology of the synthesized ZIF8-Cur were similar to those of ZIF8 (Fig. 1E). The findings suggest that Cur doping preserved the original structural properties of ZIF8.

Cytotoxicity and cellular uptake capacity of the ZIF-Cur

To determine the optimal concentration of ZIF-Cur for in vivo treatment, we cocultured it with SK-OV-3 cells. After a 72-hour coculture period, cell viability was assessed via the CCK-8 assay across various concentrations of ZIF-Cur (Fig. 2A). A concentration of 200 µg/mL significantly inhibited SK-OV-3 cell proliferation, while 100 µg/mL showed no notable effect on cell viability. Additionally, Cur at 100 µg/ml exhibited cytotoxic effects at 3 days, due to its higher drug concentration compared to the Cur content in ZIF-Cur (Fig. 2B). These findings were further corroborated through a cell proliferation assay (Fig. 2C). The proliferative capacity of tumor cells significantly decreased at a ZIF-Cur concentration of 200 µg/mL. Consistently, the live-dead staining of tumor cells also yielded similar results (Fig. 2D). These results collectively demonstrate the effective inhibition of tumor cell proliferation by the constructed ZIF-Cur.

The pronounced endocytosis of ZIF-Cur by tumor cells is pivotal to its efficacy in tumor therapy. To confirm endocytosis at the cellular level, we labeled ZIF-Cur with CY3 and subsequently cocultured it with SK-OV-3 cells and observed its uptake (Fig. 2E). The results indicated that prior to coculture, the cells presented no fluorescence intensity. However, following 24 h of coculture, SK-OV-3 cells displayed bright red fluorescence within the cytoplasm, demonstrating the efficient endocytosis of ZIF-Cur. Notably, the red fluorescence intensity persisted even after 72 h of coculture, suggesting the sustainability of the endocytosis process. This observation was consistent with previous findings. In vivo imaging effectively revealed the distribution and metabolism of ZIF-Cur in major organs and tumor regions in mice. The therapeutic effectiveness and potential side effects of nanomedicines are significantly influenced by their in vivo distribution. In this study, we administered FITC-labeled ZIF-Cur via tail vein injection and subsequently harvested all major organs and subcutaneous tumors 24 h postinjection (Fig. 2F). The findings showed that ZIF-Cur primarily accumulated in the liver, kidney, and tumor areas, indicating a potential metabolic pathway. The accumulation observed in the tumor region is probably due to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, influenced by the size of ZIF-Cur. These results are crucial for developing in vivo therapeutic strategies.

Therapeutic effect of ZIF-Cur on the SK-OV-3 subcutaneous tumor model

We investigated the therapeutic efficacy of ZIF-Cur on tumors via a subcutaneous SK-OV-3 human ovarian cancer cell model in nude mice. Subcutaneous tumor volume was calculated from bidaily measurements of tumor length and diameter, and both pooled and individual tumor growth curves were plotted (Fig. 3A). The data showed that subcutaneous tumors in both the control and ZIF groups grew more quickly, with average tumor volumes reaching 1214.2 mm³ and 1125.1 mm³, respectively, by the treatment’s end. Although the Cur treatment had a favorable therapeutic effect in the early stages, more rapid tumor growth was observed once the tumor volume exceeded 300 mm³, ultimately reaching a final volume of 714.7 mm³. This observation suggests the limited efficacy of the free drug in treating larger tumors, likely due to the poor penetration and lack of retention of the Cur monomer. In contrast, the ZIF-Cur group presented a significantly lower final average tumor size of 322.7 mm³, with no evidence of accelerated tumor growth throughout the treatment period. These findings underscore the superior antitumor efficacy of ZIF-Cur.

At the conclusion of the treatment, the researchers euthanized the mice, excised the subcutaneous tumors, and photographed them (Fig. 3B). The observed trend in tumor treatment was consistent with the previously documented tumor growth curve. Notably, tumor growth in the Cur and ZIF-Cur groups was significantly inhibited, indicating a reduction in local tumor size. The therapeutic effect was most pronounced in the ZIF-Cur group. Conversely, the ZIF group did not exhibit any therapeutic effect. Finally, we measured the tumor weights in each group and calculated the tumor inhibition rate (Fig. 3C and D). The findings demonstrated a notable decrease in tumor weight in both the Cur and ZIF-Cur groups relative to the control group. The ZIF-Cur group exhibited a statistically significant reduction in tumor weight compared to the Cur group. These findings are consistent with the previously discussed data.

Given the proven effectiveness of ZIF-Cur in inhibiting tumors in vivo, we utilized hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess pathological differences in tumor areas among different experimental groups (Fig. 3E). In both the Con- and ZIF-treated groups, tumor cells showed dense growth with well-defined, prominently stained nuclei. Conversely, in the Cur-treated group, the tumor cells appeared more dispersed and sparser than those in the aforementioned groups. More than half of the cells within the field of view presented solidified nuclei and signs of cell death. In the ZIF-Cur group, large areas of necrotic tissue were noted in the tumor regions of the mice, suggesting significant tumor cell death.

ZIF-Cur disrupts tumor mitochondrial function by increasing mitochondrial ROS levels in tumor cells

To explore the underlying mechanism of the antitumor activity of ZIF-Cur, we focused on monitoring changes in the mitochondria-related properties of tumor cells. First, we observed tumor cells costained with MitoTracker and DAPI under a 400× fluorescence microscope (Fig. 4A). The tumor cells in the Con and ZIF groups clearly contained many mitochondria, which were evenly distributed and had intact contours. In contrast, the Cur and ZIF-Cur groups presented a significant decrease in mitochondrial density, with localized aggregation observed, indicating mitochondrial damage in these cells. As the mitochondrial membrane potential is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial function, we validated mitochondrial membrane potential polarization via JC-1 staining (Fig. 4B). In the Con group, we clearly observed red clusters, indicating that these mitochondria were in a healthy polarized potential state. However, after Cur and ZIF-Cur treatment, this polarized potential was severely disrupted, manifested predominantly as green fluorescence. Finally, we directly observed the mitochondrial morphology in the tumor region by TEM (Fig. 4C). The results revealed that in healthy Con group tumor cells, the mitochondrial membranes were intact, and the internal mitochondrial cristae were regularly folded. However, in the presence of Cur or ZIF-Cur, the normal structure of the mitochondria was severely disrupted, resulting in mitochondrial membrane rupture, twisted and broken cristae, and even leakage of the mitochondrial contents. Furthermore, to achieve more accurate JC-1 quantitative analysis, JC-1-stained cells were subjected to flow cytometry. By analyzing changes in mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 monomer and JC-1 polymer assays, the results demonstrated a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential in cells treated with Cur or ZIF-Cur (Fig. 4D). These findings provide valuable insights into the mechanism by which ZIF-Cur exerts its cytotoxic effects on tumor cells. The energy supply is critical for tumor cells, which are highly metabolically active, and disruption of the mitochondrial structure inevitably impairs their proliferation and migration capabilities. We believe that such interference with local energy metabolism in tumors can effectively achieve tumor therapy.

In vivo safety evaluation of ZIF-Cur

We assessed the biosafety of the ZIF-Cur. The body weight of each mouse group was consistently monitored and recorded during the experimental period, resulting in the generation of corresponding weight curves (Fig. 5A). The study found no significant changes in mice body weights, regardless of Cur or ZIF-Cur treatment. This finding highlights the material’s well biocompatibility. We used the previously established administration method to monitor the mice’s survival cycle. The treatment with ZIF-Cur significantly extended the survival of mice compared to those in the control and ZIF groups (Fig. 5B). These findings confirmed that ZIF-Cur can effectively extend the expected lifespan of patients while exhibiting antitumor activity and that this process is safe. Furthermore, our previous observations indicated that ZIF-Cur is metabolized primarily by the liver and kidneys. We utilized hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining on tissue sections to evaluate the health of vital organs in each mouse group (Fig. 5C). Moreover, to strengthen the basis for the biosafety of ZIF-Cur, the blood biochemistry of the mice was analyzed within 14 days post-administration to evaluate the biosafety of various nanosystems. The results demonstrated no statistically significant differences in hepatic biochemical indicators, such as alanine aminotransferase and alanine transaminase, or in renal biochemical markers, including creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, between the treatment and control groups. The results indicated that there were no pathological signs of organ damage in the major organs across all three groups of mice. These findings further corroborate the exceptional biosafety profile of the ZIF-Cur. These findings strongly endorse the clinical translational potential of the material.

Conclusion

In this study, a ZIF nanocarrier system incorporating Cur was successfully synthesized. The resulting ZIF-Cur complex demonstrated significant accumulation in the tumor region and efficient endocytosis. In vivo experiments indicated that ZIF-Cur effectively targeted tumor regions passively, resulting in substantial cytotoxic effects on tumor tissues. The local mitochondrial energy metabolism function of tumors is greatly disrupted by the presence of Cur. This innovative design offers a promising new approach for the development of comprehensive therapeutic strategies for ovarian cancer.
