Sex Differences in Exercise Capacity and Outcomes Following Outpatient Cardiac Rehabilitation
Authors: Joshua R. Smith, Jose R. Medina‐Inojosa, Audry S. Chacin Suarez, Jenna L. Taylor, Karen M. Fischer, Amanda R. Bonikowske, Ray W. Squires, Randal J. Thomas, Thomas P. Olson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac rehabilitation (CR) leads to increased exercise capacity (VO2peak) and risk reduction of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Women exhibit blunted VO2peak improvements following CR. The purpose was to examine sex differences in the clinical predictors of VO2peak improvement and odds of MACEs following CR.MethodsFrom 1999 to 2017, all consecutive patients undergoing cardiopulmonary exercise testing before and after CR were included. Sex differences in peak exercise metrics in response to CR were compared. Multiple logistic regression models were fit to assess clinical predictors of VO2peak improvement (defined as >0% from pre‐CR) and MACEs following CR for men and women.ResultsA total of 513 men and 158 women were included. Men had greater increases in VO2peak than women following CR (P=0.001). For men, independent predictors of VO2peak improvement following CR included higher CR session attendance (odds ratio [OR], 1.04 [95% CI, 1.02–1.06]), peripheral artery disease (OR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.25–0.86]), and pre‐CR VO2peak (OR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.97–0.99]) (all, P<0.02). Independent predictors of lower odds of MACEs following CR in men included higher number of CR sessions (OR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.95–0.99]), pre‐CR VO2peak (OR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.97–0.99]), surgical CR indication (OR, 0.34 [95% CI, 0.18–0.68]), and no history of peripheral artery disease (OR, 2.94 [95% CI, 1.60–5.38]) (all, P<0.02). For women, the selected clinical characteristics were not statistically significant independent predictors of VO2peak improvement or odds of MACEs (except for diabetes) following CR.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that clinical predictors of VO2peak improvement and odds of MACEs following CR are sex specific.

In the United States, cardiovascular disease is 1 of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity for both men and women. 1 Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a cornerstone of secondary prevention for patients with cardiovascular disease (class 1 recommendation). 2 , 3 A fundamental core component of CR is supervised exercise training. 2 , 3 , 4 To this point, CR participation is associated with improvements in aerobic exercise capacity (ie, peak oxygen uptake [VO2peak]). Sex differences are present in the outcomes of several components of CR. 4 In this context, female sex has consistently been associated with smaller improvements in VO2peak following CR. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 CR attendance (ie, attending more CR sessions) is associated with greater odds of VO2peak improvement and lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) following CR. 9 , 10 , 11 However, it is unclear whether there are sex‐specific differences in predictors of VO2peak improvement (CR attendance or other clinical characteristics) and odds of MACEs following CR. This is highly relevant as ~40% of eligible CR patients are women. 12 , 13 Identification of sex‐specific clinical predictors of CR outcomes is necessary to tailor CR programming and optimize benefits for all patients. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to investigate if sex differences exist in the clinical predictors of VO2peak improvement and MACEs following CR.

METHODS

Study Design

The study design was previously described in detail. 9 This historical cohort included consecutive patients who participated in outpatient CR (for at least 1 CR session) between June 1999 and July 2017 at Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN). All patients were residents of Olmsted County, aged ≥18 years, had a clinical indication for CR, and completed cardiopulmonary exercise tests (CPETs) pre‐CR and post‐CR (within 12 months) with a peak respiratory exchange ratio of ≥1.0 for both CPETs. 9 , 14 Of the 1264 sets of exercise stress tests (ie, pre‐CR and post‐CR), 874 sets were CPETs and initially included. A total of 203 sets of CPETs were excluded as they (1) involved patients who enrolled in multiple CR programs during this time period (n=70) and (2) did not meet the peak exercise respiratory exchange ratio criteria (n=133). Therefore, 671 patients were included in the cohort of the present study. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board and, per Minnesota statute, only patients who had provided authorization to use their medical records for medical research were included. Data are available on reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Patient Characteristics

The Rochester Epidemiology Project is a record linkage system that captures clinical data of all Olmsted County residents 15 , 16 , 17 and was used to obtain demographic and clinical characteristics for this historical cohort. International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9), and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), codes were used to electronically extract clinical diagnoses, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes (both type 1 and 2 diabetes included), and peripheral artery disease (both symptomatic and asymptomatic included). This approach has previously been validated. 18 , 19 , 20 For this historical cohort, validation was performed by 2 physician investigators (A.C.S and J.M.I) reviewing a random sample of these variables in duplicate, with excellent interobserver agreement (all κ>0.85).

Cardiac Rehabilitation

The Mayo Clinic outpatient CR program is a multidisciplinary chronic disease management program based on American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation guidelines. 21 The Mayo Clinic CR program incorporates core components focused on exercise training and physical activity, weight management and nutrition, stress management, medication management and adherence, cardiovascular risk factor control, and sleep hygiene. In general, patients were prescribed 36 supervised exercise sessions, 2 to 3 days per week for 12 to 18 weeks. Each patient received an individualized exercise prescription that included 30 to 45 minutes of supervised aerobic exercise and 10 to 15 minutes of strength training.

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing

Patients performed a symptom‐limited CPET pre‐CR and post‐CR, with the CPETs completed closest to CR enrollment and CR completion used for analysis. The CPETs consisted of an incremental exercise protocol composed of increasing 2 metabolic equivalents every 2 minutes until volitional fatigue. 9 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 The CPETs were performed on the treadmill or cycle (with the modality matched at pre‐CR and post‐CR). Medications were not withheld before exercise testing. VO2 and other pulmonary gas exchange and ventilatory variables were measured (MGC Diagnostics, St Paul, MN), and the last 30 seconds of data during exercise were averaged and reported as peak values. Heart rate and rhythm were monitored continuously with a 12‐lead ECG. During the CPET, blood pressure was measured via manual sphygmomanometry, and ratings of perceived exertion were recorded. Oxygen (O2) pulse was calculated by dividing VO2 by heart rate. In men, a subset of patients had systolic blood pressure (n=509), diastolic blood pressure (n=507), and oxygen saturation (n=486). In women, a subset of patients had systolic blood pressure (n=155), diastolic blood pressure (n=154), and oxygen saturation (n=151). VO2peak improvement was defined as >0% in VO2peak from pre‐CR to post‐CR. A sensitivity analysis was also performed, with VO2peak improvement defined as ≥1 mL/kg per minute from pre‐CR to post‐CR.

Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events

Incidence of MACEs was defined as a new diagnosis of (1) acute coronary syndrome, including ST and non‐ST myocardial infarction and unstable angina, (2) coronary revascularization through percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting, (3) stroke (including nontraumatic brain hemorrhage or infarction and transient ischemic attack), (4) decompensated heart failure, (5) ventricular tachycardia that required hospital admission, ventricular fibrillation, or cardiac arrest, or (6) death from any cause. 10 , 28 , 29 , 30 MACE outcome data were abstracted by 2 physician investigators (A.C.S and J.M.I) who were blinded to baseline characteristics. All‐cause mortality assessment status was obtained directly from the Rochester Epidemiology Project, which retrieves status from state vital statistics offices and the National Death Index. Patients were followed up passively through electronic medical records review in the records‐linkage system through January 1, 2021.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were completed using SAS statistical software (SAS Institute Inc). Pre‐CR patient characteristics are reported as mean±SD for continuous variables and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. Pre‐CR and post‐CR peak cardiopulmonary exercise data are reported as mean±SD. The pre‐CR and post‐CR variables were analyzed within and between men and women. Patients with missing systolic and diastolic blood pressure as well as oxygen saturation were not included in this analysis. The change from pre‐CR to post‐CR for each outcome was compared between sexes using least square mean difference to calculate ANCOVA models with the covariate of the pre‐CR value included as a covariate in the model. Paired t‐tests were used to analyze variables from pre‐CR to post‐CR within each group. Associations between number of CR sessions (as well as selected patient characteristics) and VO2peak improvement and MACEs were assessed with univariate logistic regression for men and women. Age, pre‐CR VO2peak, and number of CR sessions were selected as covariates for the multiple logistic regression model based on previous findings showing that age and pre‐CR VO2peak are predictors of VO2peak improvement and mortality following CR. 5 , 6 , 7 , 31 , 32 , 33 We also included CR session attendance as a covariate as it was the primary focus of the present study. In addition, any statistically significant factors (P<0.05) in the univariate analysis were added as covariates to the multiple logistic regression model for men and women. One male patient was missing the surgical/nonsurgical grouping and was not included in the full multiple logistic regression models. A sensitivity analysis with a change in relative VO2peak of ≥1 mL/kg per minute was also performed using logistic regression models for men and women. Odds ratios and their 95% CIs are reported. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 shows patient characteristics. Women had a lower body mass index than men, whereas age was not statistically different between groups. Women were more likely to have a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease than men, despite the fact that they were less likely to report tobacco use. Women were less likely to have a history of dyslipidemia. Women had higher use of calcium channel blockers and diuretics, but lower antiplatelet use than men. The CPET modality was primarily treadmill for men (95%, n=485) and women (95%, n=150), with no statistical difference between sexes (P=0.847). There was no statistical difference in the number of CR sessions attended.

Sex Differences in Peak Exercise Responses

Test time, VO2peak, O2 pulse, heart rate, and systolic blood pressure were higher post‐CR compared with pre‐CR for both men and women (Table 2). There was no statistical difference in the frequency of men and women who achieved an increase in VO2peak of ≥1 mL/kg per minute following CR (women: 62% [n=98] versus men: 67% [n=343]; P=0.263). Men had greater increases in relative VO2peak and O2 pulse post‐CR compared with pre‐CR than women (both, P≤0.001) (Figure1), whereas no differences were present in heart rate (P=0.631). In addition, men had greater increases in test time and systolic blood pressure post‐CR compared with pre‐CR than women (both, P<0.005). Changes in VO2peak (as percentage predicted), heart rate (percentage predicted), respiratory exchange ratio, diastolic blood pressure, and O2 saturation pre‐CR to post‐CR were not statistically different between men and women (all, P>0.110).

Sex Differences in Predictors of VO2peak Changes Following CR

For men, significant univariate predictors of VO2peak improvement (defined as >0% change in VO2peak from pre‐CR to post‐CR) following CR included younger age, no history of peripheral artery disease, and lower pre‐CR VO2peak (Table 3). For men, multivariable analysis identified a higher number of CR sessions, younger age, no history of peripheral artery disease, and lower pre‐CR VO2peak as significant independent predictors of VO2peak improvement following CR. For women, the selected clinical characteristics investigated herein were not statistically significant predictors of VO2peak improvement (defined as >0% change in VO2peak from pre‐CR to post‐CR) following CR with univariate or multivariable analyses (Table 3). A sensitivity analysis was performed, with VO2peak improvement defined as ≥1 mL/kg per minute from pre‐CR to post‐CR (Table 4). The multivariable predictors of VO2peak improvement were similar for women and men when defining VO2peak improvement as ≥1 mL/kg per minute and >0% change from pre‐CR to post‐CR.

Sex Differences in MACEs Following CR

During a mean±SD follow‐up period of 4.4±3.0 years following CR, 138 (21%) patients had at least 1 documented MACE occurrence. The occurrence of MACEs was not statistically different between men (n=113, 22%) and women (n=25, 16%) (P=0.092). For men, significant univariate predictors of lower odds of MACEs following CR included younger age, surgical CR indication, as well as no histories of peripheral artery disease, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and dyslipidemia (Table 5). Independent predictors of lower odds of MACEs following CR in men via multivariable analysis included higher number of CR sessions, younger age, higher pre‐CR VO2peak, surgical CR indication, and no history of peripheral artery disease. For women, significant univariate predictors of lower odds of MACEs following CR included younger age as well as no histories of peripheral artery disease, hypertension, and diabetes (Table 5). For women, multivariable analysis identified no history of diabetes as a significant independent predictor of the odds of MACEs following CR.

DISCUSSION

There were several novel findings of the present study. First, women exhibited attenuated increases in relative VO2peak following CR compared with men when adjusting for the well‐known baseline sex differences in VO2peak. Furthermore, women had attenuated improvements in peak O2 pulse following CR compared with men, with similar improvements in peak heart rate between groups. Second, we found in our multivariable model that the independent predictors of the VO2peak improvement following CR for men were CR session attendance, younger age, peripheral artery disease, and lower pre‐CR VO2peak. Third, and in contrast to the men, CR session attendance along with the selected clinical characteristics investigated herein were not predictive of VO2peak improvement following CR for women. Fourth, we found in our multivariable analysis that the independent predictors of lower odds of MACEs following CR for men included a higher number of CR sessions, younger age, no history of peripheral arterial disease, higher pre‐CR VO2peak, and surgical indication for CR. Last, CR attendance and the selected clinical characteristics investigated in this study were not predictive of odds of MACEs following CR for women, with the exception of history of diabetes. Taken together, our findings suggest higher CR attendance elicits benefits for VO2peak improvement and lower odds for MACEs for men, but not women. These findings support sex‐specific tailoring of CR programming to optimize VO2peak improvement and MACE benefits for women.

An increase in VO2peak of ≥1 mL/kg per minute following CR is associated with a 10% reduction in all‐cause mortality. 7 In the present study, 62% of women and 67% of men exhibited an increase in VO2peak of ≥1 mL/kg per minute following CR. Female sex has been consistently reported as a negative predictor of VO2peak improvement following CR. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, minimal data are available comparing the VO2peak responses to CR between the sexes. 8 In the present study, women had smaller increases in relative VO2peak following CR compared with men when adjusting for pre‐CR VO2peak. Importantly, men and women completed a similar number of CR sessions, suggesting that women elicit smaller increases in VO2peak per CR session compared with men. These findings are highly consistent with a recent study by Rengo et al that also reported smaller increases in VO2peak for women than men following CR. 8 A novel finding of the present study was the blunted increases in O2 pulse following CR in women, suggesting sex differences may be present in cardiac adaptations (eg, stroke volume) in response to CR. Consistent with this notion, previous studies in older women have reported blunted cardiac adaptations to exercise training compared with older men and younger women. 34 , 35 , 36 The blunted increase in O2 pulse following CR in women is hypothesis generating, and future studies investigating sex differences in cardiac adaptations to CR are necessary.

The primary novel finding of the present study was sex differences were present in the clinical predictors of VO2peak improvement following CR. Specifically, the independent predictors of VO2peak improvement for men included younger age, no history of peripheral artery disease, a lower pre‐CR VO2peak, and CR session attendance. To this latter point, each CR session was associated with a 4% increase in the odds of VO2peak improvement for men. These predictors of VO2peak improvement are largely consistent with previous studies. 5 , 6 , 7 , 9 These previous studies included primarily men in their cohort (73%–93%). An important finding of the present study was that CR attendance was not an independent predictor of VO2peak improvement in women. Potential explanations for the observation that CR attendance was not predictive of VO2peak improvement in women include attenuated cardiac adaptations to CR in women and/or women performing CR sessions at lower exercise intensities compared with men. 8 , 34 , 35 , 36 Furthermore, the other selected clinical characteristics investigated herein were not predictive of VO2peak improvement following CR for women. We were surprised age and pre‐CR VO2peak were not predictive of VO2peak improvement for women as these variables are commonly reported predictors of VO2peak. An important consideration of the present study is the relatively smaller sample size of women in relation to identifying clinical predictors of VO2peak improvement following CR. Future studies incorporating a larger sample size and novel clinical characteristics are necessary to determine predictors of VO2peak improvement in women in response to CR.

An additional novel finding was sex differences were present in CR attendance as a predictor of MACEs following CR. In men, each CR session attended was associated with a 3% lower odds of MACEs. These findings are supported by previous studies reporting CR attendance is associated with lower risk of MACEs following CR in cohorts of predominately men. 10 , 11 Other independent predictors of lower odds of MACEs following CR in men included younger age, no history of peripheral artery disease, higher pre‐CR VO2peak, and surgical indication for CR. Along these lines, a recent study found that cardiac surgery was associated with a lower risk of mortality following CR in Medicare beneficiaries. 37 Previous studies have found that women exhibit greater mortality benefit compared with men following CR. 38 , 39 Therefore, we expected CR attendance to be predictive of MACEs following CR in women. In contrast, CR attendance along with other selected clinical characteristics were not statistical predictors of the odds of MACEs following CR in women, except for history of diabetes. Our findings should be interpreted in the context of the relatively small MACE outcomes for men and women in the present study.

The findings of the present study provide important insights for future studies to develop tailored CR strategies for women. For example, emerging evidence suggests that female‐focused CR programming may be superior for multiple domains of quality of life and morbidity for women enrolled in CR. 40 Future studies specifically tailoring telerehabilitation may also be a strategy to optimize CR outcomes for women. 4 , 41 However, it is unclear if these alternative CR program delivery models result in enhanced MACE benefits. Additional clinical factors need to be investigated in predicting VO2peak improvement and MACE risk following CR for women, such as psychosocial factors. Furthermore, the role of dose (ie, CR attendance) and intensity of exercise on VO2peak improvement in the CR setting appears to be equivocal for women in particular. 8 As such, the optimal exercise prescription for women in the CR setting is unclear. Taken together, our findings support the necessity for future studies to investigate sex‐specific tailoring of CR to optimize CR‐related improvements for women.

Our study has methodological considerations that should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, this study is a retrospective, single‐center study and, thus, causation cannot be implied. Selection bias may have occurred as only patients who provided prior research authorization for the use of their medical records for medical research were included. By design, only patients with pre‐CR and post‐CR CPETs were included in the present study. Although the underlying factors contributing to some patients not performing pre‐CR or post‐CR CPETs are unclear, the inclusion of only patients who performed pre‐CR and post‐CR CPETs may have resulted in selection bias and could limit the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, this historical cohort included patients from 1999 to 2017, during which time CR programming, exercise prescription strategies, and medical therapies have evolved. As such, the temporal heterogeneity of this historical cohort may have influenced our outcomes. Confounders not measured in the present study may affect VO2peak improvements and MACEs. Our findings should be interpreted in the context of the relatively small sample size, particularly for women, as well as relatively small MACE outcomes for men and women in the present study. To this point, the null findings that the selected clinical characteristics investigated in the present study were not significant predictors of VO2peak improvement and odds of MACE following CR in the female group should be interpreted with caution and warrant additional studies with larger sample sizes to confirm the findings presented herein.

In conclusion, higher CR attendance is associated with greater improvements in VO2peak and lower odds of MACEs following CR in men. In women, CR attendance is neither associated with VO2peak improvement nor odds of MACEs following CR. As traditional predictors were not statistically significant for women in the present study, the role of physiological, psychosocial, and health care delivery–related factors on VO2peak improvement and odds of MACEs following CR needs to be investigated. These findings add to the cumulating evidence demonstrating sex differences in the spectrum of CR enrollment, delivery, programming, and outcomes. 4
