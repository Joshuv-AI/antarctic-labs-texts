Comparison of early versus late tracheostomy in trauma patients: a multicenter propensity score-matched cohort study
Authors: Soon Ki Min, Jin Young Lee, Seung Hwan Lee, Sebeom Jeon, Kang Kook Choi, Min A Lee, Byungchul Yu, Gil Jae Lee, Youngmin Kim, Jayun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTracheostomy is commonly performed to facilitate airway management in critically ill trauma patients. It may also reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation (MV); however, the optimal timing remains uncertain. This study compared clinical outcomes between early and late tracheostomy in trauma patients. A total of 220 adult patients who underwent tracheostomy following endotracheal intubation at two regional trauma centers between January 2019 and December 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were categorized into early (≤ 7 days after intubation) and late (> 7 days) tracheostomy groups. Outcomes included MV duration, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LOS), hospital LOS, ventilator-free days (VFDs), and in-hospital mortality. Baseline differences were adjusted for using propensity score matching (PSM). After PSM, early tracheostomy was associated with shorter hospital LOS (median [interquartile range], 44.0 [26.5–68.5] vs. 59.0 [29.0–78.5]; P = 0.038), ICU LOS (22.0 [15.0–37.8] vs. 28.0 [20.0–38.5]; P = 0.047), and MV duration (11.5 [6.3–18.5] vs. 20.0 [14.0–27.8]; P < 0.001), as well as more VFDs (13.5 [0.0–20.0] vs. 4.0 [0.0–13.0]; P = 0.002). In-hospital mortality did not differ significantly (20.0 vs. 20.0%; P = 1.000). Introduction

Tracheostomy is commonly performed in critically ill patients who require prolonged airway support. While endotracheal intubation remains the standard initial method for airway management, prolonged use can lead to complications, including pharyngeal or laryngeal injury, increased infection risk, and impaired swallowing or speech1. Tracheostomy offers an effective alternative for long-term airway management, delivering several clinical advantages such as improved patient comfort and communication, simplified nursing care, reduced sedation requirements, shortened intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital lengths of stay (LOS), and expedited weaning from mechanical ventilation (MV)1–6.

In cases of trauma, tracheostomy is often indicated, particularly among patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI), cervical spine injuries, or thoracic trauma necessitating extended ventilatory support3,7–9. Establishing the optimal timing for tracheostomy in this population remains a key clinical challenge. The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) recommends early tracheostomy—typically within 3–7 days—for individuals expected to require MV for more than 7 days10. Similarly, the Brain Trauma Foundation advocates for early intervention but does not specify a timeframe11. In contrast, other leading trauma organizations, including the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) and the Western Trauma Association (WTA), have not issued explicit guidance on timing12. Furthermore, certain critical care guidelines advise against tracheostomy before the fourth day of MV13, while others refrain from recommending early tracheostomy5. Consequently, a unified consensus regarding the ideal timing for this procedure is still lacking.

Given these discrepancies, further research is warranted to elucidate the clinical implications of early versus late tracheostomy in trauma cases. This study seeks to evaluate whether early tracheostomy confers clinical benefits compared with delayed intervention in adult patients with trauma.

Methods

Study design, population, and setting

This multicenter retrospective study was conducted at two regional trauma centers in South Korea: Gachon University Gil Medical Center and Chungbuk National University Hospital. Both institutions are classified as level 1 trauma centers, and each has a 20-bed ICU dedicated to trauma care. The study included all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) in the trauma units who underwent tracheostomy following endotracheal intubation at either center between January 2019 and December 2022 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient selection.

All protocols complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of Gachon University Gil Medical Center approved the study (GBIRB2024-276). Due to its retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Data collection and definitions

The following variables were collected for eligible patients: age, sex, injury mechanism, tracheostomy technique, operator type, initial systolic blood pressure (SBP), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Injury Severity Score (ISS), Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score by body region, reason for tracheostomy, initial 24-h red blood cell (RBC) transfusion requirements, and prior surgery under general anesthesia (GA) before tracheostomy. AIS body region variables were dichotomized using an AIS ≥ 3 threshold to reflect clinically meaningful injury severity. Tracheostomy techniques were classified as either percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) or traditional surgical tracheostomy (ST). PDT was performed using bronchoscopic guidance or anatomical landmarks14.

Tracheostomy timing was guided by institutional practice but was individualized by the patient’s clinical condition and anticipated need for prolonged MV. Early tracheostomy was generally considered within 7 days after intubation in patients requiring prolonged airway protection or those considered unlikely to undergo successful early extubation, particularly patients with severe TBI or upper cervical spinal cord injury. Tracheostomy was deferred in patients with a high likelihood of early extubation or ongoing clinical instability. The decision on timing was made through multidisciplinary discussion among trauma surgeons and the critical care team, rather than by an individual operator performing the procedure. In this study, early tracheostomy was defined as tracheostomy performed within 7 days after intubation, whereas late tracheostomy was defined as tracheostomy performed after 7 days, consistent with definitions used in previous trauma and critical care studies15–18.

The primary reason for tracheostomy was classified as either a central nervous system (CNS)-related cause or a respiratory cause, based on the dominant clinical indication for anticipated or ongoing need for prolonged ventilatory support. Each patient was assigned to a single category based on the predominant indication when multiple factors were present. CNS-related causes included conditions requiring airway protection due to impaired consciousness or neurological dysfunction, such as TBI or cervical spinal cord injury. Respiratory causes included conditions primarily affecting pulmonary function, such as lung injury, pneumonia, or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), including both trauma-related injuries and ICU-acquired complications. Tracheal stenosis—a tracheostomy-related complication—was defined as clinically significant narrowing of the trachea (confirmed by bronchoscopy or imaging) accompanied by symptoms, such as exertional dyspnea and/or stridor.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included hospital and ICU LOSs, ICU-free days, MV duration, ventilator-free days (VFDs), and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). Additional evaluations included tracheostomy-related complications, such as stoma infection, bleeding, vocal cord palsy, tracheal stenosis, granulation tissue at the stoma site, pneumothorax, and tracheoesophageal fistula, as well as procedure-related events such as accidental decannulation and cuff rupture.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers (%) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce baseline imbalances and control for potential confounding between the early and late tracheostomy groups. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model where early versus late tracheostomy was the dependent variable. Covariates with clinically relevant imbalance before matching, defined as standardized mean difference (SMD) > 0.2, were included in the propensity score model. The following baseline covariates were included: mechanism of injury, tracheostomy technique, operator type, initial SBP, ISS, abdominal AIS ≥ 3, reason for tracheostomy, and prior surgery under GA before tracheostomy. Patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance between groups was assessed using SMDs, with values < 0.2 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Survival outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared with the log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and PSM was conducted using R version 2.12.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 220 patients were included in this study, with a mean age of 61.5 ± 19.3 years, and 75.9% (n = 167) were male (Table 1). Among them, 61 patients underwent early tracheostomy and 159 underwent late tracheostomy. The early tracheostomy group underwent the procedure at 4.1 ± 2.3 days after endotracheal intubation, whereas the late group underwent tracheostomy at 14.6 ± 5.6 days (P < 0.001). PDT was more frequently performed in the late tracheostomy group (54.1% versus 14.8%; P < 0.001), whereas ST predominated in the early group. Trauma surgeons performed most tracheostomies (70.9%), carrying out 78.0% of late tracheostomies and 52.5% of early procedures. Residents more often participated in early tracheostomies (34.4%).

Clinical outcomes

Before matching, median MV duration, hospital LOS, and ICU LOS were significantly shorter in the early tracheostomy group than in the late tracheostomy group (9.0 versus 21.0 days, P < 0.001; 41.0 versus 58.0 days, P = 0.003; and 22.0 versus 28.0 days, P = 0.001, respectively). The early group showed greater VFDs (14.0 versus 3.0 days, P < 0.001) and ICU-free days (3.0 versus 0.0 days, P = 0.003). After PSM, these differences persisted. MV duration, hospital LOS, and ICU LOS remained significantly shorter in the early group (11.5 versus 20.0 days, P < 0.001; 44.0 versus 59.0 days, P = 0.038; and 22.0 versus 28.0 days, P = 0.047, respectively). The early group showed greater VFDs (13.5 versus 4.0 days, P = 0.002; Table 3). In-hospital mortality and VAP incidence did not differ significantly between the early and late tracheostomy groups.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses showed similar results among patients with CNS injuries and those with respiratory-related indications. Following PSM, median MV duration remained significantly shorter in the early tracheostomy group across both CNS injury and respiratory indication subgroups (11.0 versus 22.0 days, P < 0.001; and 12.0 versus 20.0 days, P = 0.003). Median VFDs were significantly greater in the early group for CNS and respiratory subgroups (12.0 versus 3.0 days, P = 0.022; and 15.0 versus 4.0 days, P = 0.028), supporting consistent findings across major indications for prolonged intubation (Table 3).

Survival and complications

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in in-hospital mortality between the early and late tracheostomy groups, both before (log-rank test, P = 0.097) and after matching (log-rank test, P = 0.600; Fig. 2). Tracheostomy-related complications and procedure-related events did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 4).

Discussion

The optimal timing of tracheostomy in trauma cases remains a subject of debate7–9,12,15,16,19–22. Recent meta-analyses have shown that performing tracheostomy early does not significantly enhance survival outcomes7,15,19,21,23. The findings of this study align with this emerging evidence, as no differences in hospital mortality were detected between early and late tracheostomy groups, even after PSM.

In trauma cases, the primary factors influencing mortality are injury severity, refractory hemorrhage, and secondary organ injury: survival may be less directly influenced by the timing of tracheostomy alone9,10,12. Rather, tracheostomy timing may exert a greater impact on downstream critical care aspects, including MV duration, sedative exposure, and ICU resource use. This view is consistent with trauma-focused meta-analyses reporting neutral effects on mortality but noting improvements in other clinical outcomes7,15,22,23.

In this context, this study’s findings support the notion that the principal benefit of early tracheostomy relates to ventilatory outcomes rather than survival. Early tracheostomy was associated with significantly shorter MV duration and a greater VFD; these effects persisted after controlling for baseline differences. These findings are consistent with recent randomized clinical trials highlighting improvements in ventilatory parameters4,17.

Subgroup analyses revealed consistent effects across major indications for prolonged intubation. Patients with CNS injuries, including TBI and spinal cord injury, demonstrated reduced MV durations and more VFDs following early tracheostomy. This outcome likely reflects enhanced airway management, fewer pulmonary complications, and improved neurological assessment, rather than direct effects on neurological recovery. Similarly, those intubated for respiratory conditions such as lung injury or ARDS experienced improved ventilatory outcomes without changes in mortality, consistent with previous studies6,24.

Early tracheostomy was associated with shorter ICU and hospital LOS. Earlier conversion to tracheostomy may facilitate reduced sedative use, improved pulmonary hygiene, and more efficient ICU turnover, potentially translating to improved resource utilization and earlier rehabilitation initiation, even in the absence of a survival benefit2,5,12.

No notable differences were detected between early and late tracheostomy groups in VAP or tracheostomy-related complications. Although conflicting results have been reported regarding the association between tracheostomy timing and VAP incidence4,6,15,17,20–23,25, most studies show no significant differences in procedure-related complications8,18,22,23,25.

Our observations and previous findings suggest that early tracheostomy primarily improves ventilatory outcomes and ICU efficiency, not survival4,7,8,15–17,21. Nonetheless, international guidelines acknowledge its potential benefits without providing clear recommendations on optimal timing5,11. In this context, the current multicenter cohort study offers real-world insights that clarify how timing affects clinical outcomes and support guideline interpretation.

A methodological strength of this study is the use of PSM to reduce potential confounding. Significant baseline differences were observed between the two groups, particularly in operator type, with a higher proportion of resident-performed tracheostomies in the early tracheostomy group. This imbalance may reflect institutional practice patterns and educational roles rather than a direct clinical determinant of tracheostomy timing. Accordingly, PSM was performed to adjust for these baseline imbalances and minimize potential confounding effects.

Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. First, its retrospective design cannot fully eliminate selection bias and unmeasured confounding, despite the use of PSM. Second, tracheostomy timing was determined by clinician discretion rather than random assignment, which may introduce treatment selection bias. Third, although conducted across multiple centers, variations in tracheostomy technique, operator experience, and ICU management protocols may have affected outcomes, limiting the findings’ generalizability. Fourth, ventilator weaning was generally performed following standard ICU practices, including daily spontaneous awakening trials and spontaneous breathing trials. However, detailed data on these processes were not consistently available because of the study’s retrospective design. Therefore, respiratory-related outcomes were assessed using surrogate measures, including MV duration and VFD. Fifth, detailed information on the duration of resuscitation and vasopressor use was not consistently available. Although initial 24-h RBC transfusion requirement, initial SBP, and ISS were considered markers reflective of early resuscitation status and clinical severity, residual confounding related to hemodynamic instability cannot be completely excluded. Sixth, only short-term clinical endpoints were examined; long-term functional recovery, neurological outcomes, and quality-of-life measures were not assessed.

In summary, early tracheostomy in trauma patients did not reduce in-hospital mortality, but significantly shortened MV duration as well as ICU and hospital stays with no increase in complication rates. Accordingly, early tracheostomy should be considered to expedite ventilatory independence and enhance ICU efficiency in trauma cases anticipated to require prolonged airway protection. Prospective studies should focus on refining patient selection criteria and establishing standardized, diagnosis-specific guidelines for tracheostomy timing in trauma care.
