Deep learning for precision medicine: Guiding laser therapy in ischemic retinal diseases
Authors: Ting Fang Tan, Shelley Yin-His Chang, Daniel Shu Wei Ting
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In this issue of Cell Reports Medicine, Zhao and colleagues1 report a multi-tasking artificial intelligence system that can assist the whole process of fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) imaging and reduce the reliance on retinal specialists in FFA examination.

Main text

Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven precision medicine has revolutionized the field of medicine. Along the continuum from screening, diagnosis, treatment, to monitoring, care can be tailored to the individual. Deep-learning systems (DLSs) have been extensively applied to ophthalmology over the past decade, a field where clinical practice is predominantly guided by multimodal imaging.2 However, beyond disease diagnosis and classification, increasing work has focused on personalizing patient care.3 In diabetic retinopathy (DR), predicting progression based on clinical and demographical variables and color fundus photographs (CFP) has been demonstrated at 2–5 years from baseline.4 This potentially translates to personalizing DR screening intervals beyond the current 1- to 2-yearly fixed intervals to maximize resource capacity in the face of the increasing disease prevalence. Furthermore, large language models enable unsupervised self-learning on vast amounts of unlabeled data in medicine.5,6 The recent release of RETFound, a foundational model from millions of unlabeled retinal images, achieved accurate diagnosis and prognostication of ocular, as well as systemic, diseases.7

Retinal ischemia is important in retinal vascular diseases to prognosticate disease trajectory and visual outcome in each patient. Ischemic eyes are at an increased risk of complications, including neovascularization, neovascular glaucoma, vitreous hemorrhage, and cystoid macular edema.8 Therefore, the identification and quantification of ischemia is fundamental in guiding principles of management and frequency of follow-up of retinal vascular diseases. In these ischemic retinal vein occlusion (RVO) eyes, careful observation with frequent follow-up monitoring was recommended to perform prompt laser photocoagulation when neovascularization develops. For DR, peripheral retinal ischemia was found to be strongly associated with proliferative DR.9

To ascertain the extent of retinal ischemia, fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) remains the gold standard in assessing the extent of ischemia. In eyes with RVO, the Central Vein Occlusion Study (CVOS) defined eyes with central RVO as ischemic when 10 or greater disc diameters of retinal non-perfusion were noted on 7-field FFA; while the Branch Vein Occlusion Study (BVOS) defined eyes with branch RVO as ischemic when 5 or greater disc diameters of retinal non-perfusion were noted.7 Although optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), including the widefield OCT-A, has emerged as an effective alternative to FFA, these newer imaging techniques could still be limited by motion artifacts and difficulty in distinguishing non-specific signal voids from non-perfusion areas (NPAs). The advantages of FFA, as compared to OCT-A, include a wider field of view of the retina peripheries using widefield imaging and evaluation of leakage and vascular filling time.

The key challenge of FFA, however, lies in its need for manual interpretation by human experts, which is time consuming, subjective, and variable. Furthermore, existing work on automating FFA evaluation using DLS has been limited to single retinal disease or solely for diagnosis. To tackle this clinical challenge, Zhao et al. developed a DLS (Ai-Doctor) to automate the clinical workflow from diagnosis to treatment planning of DR and branch RVO based on FFA.1 This multi-step DLS included phase identification and disease diagnosis on FFA and segmentation of NPAs to derive their proposed clinically applicable ischemia index (CAII), intended to suggest the need for laser therapy. Ai-Doctor achieved AUC of >0.90 in FFA phase identification and disease diagnosis. Similar performance was further maintained in external datasets. For NPA segmentation, >85% dice similarity coefficient (DSC) was demonstrated in internal and external datasets. Notably, Ai-Doctor consistently segmented NPAs in separate datasets of unencountered retinal vascular diseases (Central RVO, retinal vasculitis) with >80% DSC. Finally, optimal thresholds of CAII values (based on the proportion of NPA over total visualized retinal area on 55° FFA) were determined to guide likelihood for laser therapy.

The Ai-Doctor illustrated the ability to overcome the clinical challenge of FFA assessment across several retinal vascular diseases, with its large labeled training dataset and generalizability of NPA segmentation to other retinal vascular diseases unencountered in model training. Furthermore, the multi-step workflow expands its capability beyond disease diagnosis to influence treatment decision with its proposed CAII as a quantitative threshold to guide the potential need for laser therapy and monitor progression during follow-ups.

To translate Ai-Doctor into the real-world clinical practice, several areas could be considered for future work. First, this study was tested primarily on the Chinese population and a specific widefield FFA imaging device, and its generalizability to other populations and FFA imaging devices still remains uncertain. Second, it would be interesting to evaluate the health economic analyses to assess the clinical impact in real-world settings.10 Third, future studies could also consider using the more economical, accessible, and non-invasive fundus imaging to evaluate the extent of retinal ischemia, comparing them with the FFA results. Should these prove to be clinically effective, the AI technology potentially could have a wider reach in real-world settings.

Prevention is better than cure. Given the increasing prevalence of diabetes globally, more populations potentially may suffer from ischemic retinal diseases. It is prudent, therefore, to harness the power of cutting-edge AI techniques, for example, deep learning and large language models, to focus on preventive and precision medicine. The use of generative AI and large language models has certainly created a tremendous interest worldwide. Given the surge in performance, this would be another potential powerful AI technology to be considered to improve patients’ clinical outcome and experience. Lastly, it is also important to consider the health economic, trustworthy, and explainable AI to improve the clinical translation and adoption of these technologies in real-world settings.

In summary, the management of ischemic retinal diseases requires both systemic and ocular management. The use of telehealth and state-of-art digital and AI technologies could help personalize patients’ care, streamlining clinical workflows and optimizing resource capacity to improve patients’ clinical outcome and experience.
