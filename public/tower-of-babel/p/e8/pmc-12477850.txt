Illuminating the therapeutic potential of Schisandrin a against rheumatoid arthritis by targeting ferroptosis: An integrated bioinformatics and experimental study
Authors: Zhifang Yang, Xiaojuan Yin, Sha Yang, Huimin Li, Huimin Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by chronic inflammation and hyperplasia of the synovial membrane. This study aimed to elucidate the anti-inflammatory mechanisms of Schisandrin A (SCHA), a bioactive component from Schisandra chinensis, on RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs). Integrated bioinformatics analysis was performed on transcriptomic datasets from RA and normal FLSs as well as SCHA-treated RA FLSs. Enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes between groups were significantly enriched in the ferroptosis pathway, suggesting SCHA may exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting ferroptosis. Protein-protein interaction network analysis identified Txnrd1, lpcat3 and slc7a11 as key hub genes with pivotal roles in mediating SCHA's effects, and molecular docking and dynamics simulations demonstrated that SCHA directly binds to these proteins with favorable binding affinities through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, with stable complex formation confirmed over 100-ns molecular dynamics trajectories. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflamed RA FLSs, SCHA significantly decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β at both protein and mRNA levels, restored antioxidant enzymes GSH-Px and SOD levels, and reduced lipid peroxidation product MDA, Fe2+ and ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner Transmission electron microscopy confirmed SCHA's dose-dependent protective effects against ferroptosis-induced mitochondrial damage. Our study demonstrates inhibition of ferroptosis via targeting TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 mediates the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA on RA FLSs. These findings reveal a novel mechanism for SCHA's therapeutic potential against RA by modulating ferroptosis.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a debilitating autoimmune disease characterized by chronic inflammation of the synovial membrane, leading to progressive joint damage, pain, and loss of function, which severely compromises patients’ quality of life [1]. The etiology of RA is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and aberrant immune responses that culminate in synovial inflammation and hyperplasia [1,2]. Central to the pathogenesis of RA is the fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs, also known as synovial fibroblasts) a cell type that contributes significantly to the inflammatory milieu and joint pathology [3].

FLSs in RA are pivotal in orchestrating the inflammatory cascade, through the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and matrix-degrading enzymes. These cells not only sustain inflammation but also facilitate the invasion of the synovium into adjacent cartilage and bone [4,5]. The activation of FLSs is the key factor in the persistent synovitis observed in RA patients. Hence, interventions targeting the inflammatory responses of FLSs offer a promising strategy for ameliorating disease progression [4].

The current therapeutic arsenal for RA consists primarily of anti-inflammatory agents, immunosuppressants, and biologics targeting immune cells or cytokines [6]. However, these medications often demonstrate limited efficacy and considerable adverse effects [7]. In contrast, traditional Chinese medicine has exhibited several advantages, including enhanced safety, increased pharmacological potency and improved patient compliance [7]. Schisandra glaucescens Diels (SGD), known as "Huaxueteng” or "Xiangxueteng” in traditional Chinese medicine, has been utilized for the treatment of various ailments, including RA [8]. Schisandrin A (SCHA), a key lignan constituent of Schisandra, has been shown that it can protect knee joints in a rat model of RA from erosion, damage and deformation, and ameliorate tissue inflammatory injury [9]. Shuo Ni et al. found that SCHA can inhibit osteoclast inflammatory activation by suppressing ROS production and activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway [10]. These findings indicate that SCHA may be the active constituent mediating the therapeutic efficacy of Schisandra chinensis for RA, and its mechanisms may be associated with anti-inflammatory actions. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which SCHA exerts its effects remain to be fully understood. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of SCHA in improving inflammation in RA FLSs is of significance for the treatment of RA and the development and utilization of Schisandra medicines.

In the present investigation, we aimed to elucidate the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA on FLSs in the context of RA pathology. An integrated approach, combining bioinformatics tools with rigorous experimental protocols, is employed to uncover the molecular mechanisms underpinning SCHA's influence on the inflammatory processes characteristic of RA. This study aims to provide theoretical support for the therapeutic potential of SCHA in RA management and to broaden our comprehension of its modulatory impact on synovial inflammation.

Methods

Data download and preprocessing

The mRNA expression profiles of GSE181614 and GSE205795 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geoprofiles/) [11]. GSE181614 is transcriptomics data based on GPL16956 platform, including 5 FLS samples from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients (FLSs-RA) and 5 normal control samples (FLSs-NC). The GSE205795 dataset is transcriptomics data based on the GPL24676 platform, containing 3 normal rheumatoid arthritis FLS samples (FLSs-RA) and 3 SCHA-treated rheumatoid arthritis FLS samples (FLSs-SCHA). GSE181614 and GSE205795 datasets were jointly used in this study to screen for genes associated with the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA on FLSs.

Data preprocessing was performed using R limma package. Raw data was background corrected to eliminate systematic biases inherent in microarray technology. Median normalization was then implemented. Finally, the probes were annotated and probe IDs were converted to gene symbols.

Differential expression analysis

The R limma package was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between FLSs-RA and FLSs-NC in GSE181614 dataset, as well as between FLSs-SCHA and FLSs-RA in GSE205795 dataset. DEGs were filtered with criteria of |fold change| > 1.5 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method) to account for multiple comparisons and reduce false positives. Common DEGs in both datasets were considered as genes associated with the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA on RA FLSs.

Functional enrichment analysis and PPI network analysis

Metascape (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) [12] was used for functional enrichment analysis of the common DEGs, including GO analysis (Biological Processes, Molecular Functions, Cellular Components) and pathway analysis (WikiPathways, Reactome Gene Sets, KEGG Pathway). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then performed using R GSEA package. The normalized enrichment score (NES) was calculated to evaluate the enrichment of key pathways in different sample groups. NES<0 and P < 0.05 indicate significant downregulation compared to control, while NES>0 and P < 0.05 indicate significant upregulation.

The String database (https://string-db.org/) [13] was used to predict protein-protein interactions among the common DEGs, and results were imported into Cytoscape for visualization. In the constructed PPI network, the degree and clustering coefficient of each node were calculated. Combined with network density and centrality analysis, key interactive nodes were identified.

Density functional theory calculation and molecular docking

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the electronic structure and frontier molecular orbital properties of Schisandrin A (SCHA). The three-dimensional conformation of SCHA (PubChem CID: 10066) was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [14] and subjected to geometry optimization using Gaussian 16 software package with the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-31G(d) basis set. The molecular structure was fully optimized, and frequency calculations were performed to confirm the optimized geometry. Frontier molecular orbital analysis was conducted to determine the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies, with the HOMO-LUMO energy gap calculated to evaluate molecular stability and chemical reactivity.

Molecular docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina software (v1.1.2) to predict the binding affinity and potential binding modes of SCHA with target proteins TXNRD1, LPCAT3, and SLC7A11. High-confidence predicted structures for these proteins were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database [15], except for TXNRD1 and SLC7A11, where crystal structures with PDB IDs 7 × 1R [16] and 7EPZ [17], respectively, were utilized. The UniProt ID for LPCAT3 was Q6P1A2. For TXNRD1 and SLC7A11, docking was constrained to experimentally reported binding sites. Specifically, the reference binding residues for TXNRD1 included K95, H96, W98, C189, I212, and L214, while for SLC7A11, the residues I52, I53, Q191, L252, F254, V259, I266, A269, and I270 were targeted. The docking grid boxes were centered on these reference binding sites, with the grid box parameters for TXNRD1 set to a spacing of 0.500 Å, grid dimensions of 50 × 50 × 50 points, and a center at coordinates (71.637, 70.446, 104.536). For SLC7A11, the grid box parameters were set to a spacing of 0.500 Å, grid dimensions of 50 × 50 × 50 points, and a center at coordinates (148.132, 149.046, 120.267). The protein structures were pre-processed using AutoDockTools (ADT), including the addition of hydrogen atoms and assignment of charge states. The optimized SCHA structure from DFT calculations was used as the ligand input, with hydrogen atoms added and partial charges assigned using the Gasteiger method. For LPCAT3, the docking grid was set to encompass the entire protein due to the absence of known binding site information. A total of 100 independent docking runs were conducted for each target to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the predictions. The docking conformations with the lowest binding free energies were selected for further analysis. Molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, were analyzed and visualized to characterize the binding modes.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.4 to evaluate the dynamic stability of SCHA-protein complexes. Take the optimal docking conformation as the initial structure. The AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was used for proteins and GAFF for SCHA, with ligand topology generated by ACPYPE. The complexes were placed in cubic boxes with a minimum edge length of 1.0 nm, solvated using the TIP3P water model, and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. Energy minimization was performed using steepest descent for 50,000 steps. Two-phase equilibration included NVT (100 ps, 310 K) and NPT (100 ps, 310 K, 1 bar) with V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Position restraints (1000 kJ/mol/nm2) were applied to heavy atoms during equilibration. Production simulations ran for 100 ns under NPT conditions at 310 K and 1 bar. Periodic boundary conditions were applied with PME for long-range electrostatics (1.0 nm cutoff) and LINCS constraints for hydrogen bonds (2 fs time step). Coordinates were saved every 10 ps. Trajectory analysis included root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and SASA calculations using GROMACS tools. Hydrogen bonds were analyzed with 3.5 Å distance and 30°angle cutoffs. Calculate the interaction energies between proteins and SCHA using GMX energy. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate conformational dynamic characteristics, where the first two components (PC1 and PC2) used for trajectory projection and variance analysis.

Cell culture and inflammatory model establishment

The human FLS cell line MH7A was obtained from the Cell Resource Center of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). MH7A cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and maintained at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator. To establish an inflammatory cell model of RA, MH7A cells were stimulated with 10 μg/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 6 h [18].

Cell viability assay

MH7A cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well. After LPS stimulation for 6 h, different concentrations (15, 30, 60, 120, 240 μM) of SCHA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added to normal MH7A cells and LPS-induced cells for 24 h. 10 μl of CCK-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

ELISA assay

MH7A cells were divided into control, model (10 μg/ml LPS), low dose (60 μM), medium dose (120 μM) and high dose (240 μM) SCHA treatment groups, as well as the positive control group treated with ferroptosis inhibitor Erastin (10 μM [19]) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After LPS induction, corresponding drugs were administered for 24 h. Cell culture supernatants were collected and the levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β were determined using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Ferroptosis biomarkers detection

After treatment with different concentrations of SCHA (60, 120, 240 μM) and ferroptosis inhibitor Erastin (10 μM) for 24 h, cells from each group were collected. Cell lysates were prepared and centrifuged at 10000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were obtained for biochemical assays (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Appropriate amount of supernatants were reacted with detection reagents and absorbance was measured to calculate the relative levels of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and ferrous iron (Fe2+) in each group. In parallel, for ROS detection, single cell suspensions were prepared from each group and incubated with 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA probe (Solarbio, CA1410) solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing twice with serum-free medium, fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry to determine the relative reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels.

For ultrastructural analysis, cells were harvested after treatment and fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by post-fixation with 1 % osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Subsequently, samples were dehydrated through graded ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were then cut, mounted on copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, sections were examined under transmission electron microscope at 80 kV. Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated based on mitochondrial volume, matrix electron density, membrane integrity, and cristae structure.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from each group of cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) per manufacturer's protocol. RNA quantity and purity were assessed via NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). One μg total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) on an ABI 7500 Fast System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). PCR reactions (20 μL) contained 10 μL 2 × SYBR Premix Ex TaqII, 0.8 μL each primer, 2 μL diluted cDNA, and 6.4 μL nuclease-free water. The PCR cycling conditions were set as follows: An initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s for denaturation and 60 °C for 30 s for annealing and extension. GAPDH was used as an internal control. Relative mRNA levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Western blotting analysis

RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a cocktail of protease inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was employed to lyse MH7A cells and extract total protein. The BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was utilized for the quantification of protein concentrations. Proteins, each quantified at 30 μg, were resolved by 10 % SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Post-transfer, membranes were blocked with 5 % skimmed milk for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies specific to TXNRD1, LPCAT3, SLC7A11, and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, dilution 1:1000)) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequent to primary antibody incubation, membranes were thoroughly rinsed with TBST followed by a 1 h incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, dilution 1:5000) at ambient temperature. The protein bands were developed using an ECL Substrate Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and the signal intensity was quantified with ImageJ software. GAPDH was employed as the loading control to ensure equal protein loading across all samples.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software. Differences between two groups were analyzed by unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Differences between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test. P less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Density functional theory analysis and molecular docking

To gain insights into the electronic structure and chemical reactivity of SCHA prior to molecular docking studies, frontier molecular orbital analysis was performed using DFT methods. The DFT calculations revealed that the HOMO energy was −5.367 eV, while the LUMO energy was −0.003 eV, yielding a HOMO-LUMO energy gap of 5.365 eV (Fig. 4A). According to frontier molecular orbital theory, the HOMO serves as an electron donor while the LUMO acts as an electron acceptor, with the energy difference reflecting the molecular reactivity characteristics. The relatively large HOMO-LUMO energy gap indicates high thermodynamic stability and low chemical reactivity of SCHA. The HOMO energy level at −5.367 eV suggests moderate electron-donating capacity, while the relatively high LUMO energy level (−0.003 eV) indicates weak electron-accepting ability, further confirming the molecular stability. The electronic structure parameters revealed that the C24H32O6 compound possesses a unique hexamethoxy-substituted tricyclic structure that confers distinctive electronic distribution characteristics. The multiple methoxy substituents, acting as electron-donating groups, effectively stabilize the molecular electronic structure through conjugative effects, influencing the frontier molecular orbital energy distribution. Based on these electronic structure insights, molecular docking studies were performed to investigate the potential binding modes and affinity of SCHA with three key ferroptosis-related proteins TXNRD1, LPCAT3, and SLC7A11. The binding free energies were calculated to be −5.33 kcal/mol, −7.24 kcal/mol, and −6.42 kcal/mol for SCHA complexed with TXNRD1, LPCAT3, and SLC7A11, respectively. The negative binding free energies suggest favorable intermolecular interactions between SCHA and the target proteins, with SLC7A11 showing the strongest binding affinity. Detailed binding conformations and molecular interactions are shown in Fig. 4B–D, revealing that SCHA forms multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts with key amino acid residues in the active sites of all three target proteins. The molecular stability revealed by DFT analysis, combined with the favorable docking scores, provides structural basis for SCHA's potential therapeutic effects through ferroptosis pathway modulation.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To further evaluate the dynamic stability of SCHA binding, 100 ns MD simulations were performed for each SCHA-protein complex system. The characteristic results of MD simulation are shown in Table 3 and Fig. 5. The convergence of the RMSD of the protein backbone atoms within 0.21–0.64 nm and low residue fluctuations in the binding site regions indicated by the RMS) analysis validated the stability of the MD trajectories (Fig. 5A–C). The subtle fluctuations of Rg within 2.11–3.49 nm signified the protein complexes maintained compact conformations (Fig. 5A–C). The solvent accessible surface area (SASA) values ranging from 250.66 to 415.86 nm2 reflected appropriate complex packing (Fig. 5A–C). Additionally, hydrogen bonds formed between SCHA and key residues in the binding pockets further stabilized the interactions (Fig. 5A–C). The binding was facilitated by van der Waals interactions including π-π stacking and hydrophobic contacts, which contributed optimal binding energies between −90.79 and −172.08 kJ/mol. The electrostatic interactions provided energies ranging from −8.26 to −26.41 kJ/mol (Fig. 5D–F). To assess the conformational stability during the simulation process, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the 100 ns MD trajectories (Fig. 6). The PCA results demonstrated good conformational stability across all three systems. The TXNRD1-SCHA complex displayed PC1 and PC2 explaining 97.8 % and 1.6 % of the variance, with the conformational trajectory presenting several relatively concentrated cluster distributions, suggesting ordered transitions between multiple stable conformational states (Fig. 6A). For the LPCAT3-SCHA system, PC1 and PC2 contributed 65.3 % and 6.6 % of the variance, respectively, showing reasonable conformational flexibility while maintaining overall stability (Fig. 6B). The SLC7A11-SCHA complex exhibited the highest stability, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 90.1 % and 6.5 % of the conformational variance, respectively. Discussion

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA on RA FLSs using integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. By comparing transcriptomic data from normal and RA patient FLSs as well as untreated and SCHA-treated RA FLSs, we identified common DEGs associated with SCHA. Enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were mainly involved in inhibiting the ferroptosis pathway, suggesting SCHA may exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the upregulated ferroptosis mechanisms in RA FLSs. PPI network analysis identified the ferroptosis-related genes TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 as key nodes, implying they may mediate the anti-inflammatory activity of SCHA.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death distinct from other cell death modalities like apoptosis, necrosis and autophagy. It is characterized by redox imbalance resulting in lipid peroxidation and eventual cell death [20]. In RA, the ferroptosis process is hyperactivated, leading to excessive lipid peroxidation products like MDA that further stimulate inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α to propagate joint inflammation and synovial hyperplasia [21]. Meanwhile, ferroptosis also depletes cellular antioxidant defenses like glutathione and reduces activities of antioxidant enzymes like SOD, in addition to increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS), collectively exacerbating oxidative stress [21,22]. Moreover, ferroptosis can activate matrix metalloproteinases to degrade the extracellular matrix of synovial cells and promote inflammatory cell infiltration [23]. Therefore, inhibiting ferroptosis not only alleviates lipid peroxidation and inflammatory cytokine secretion in synovial cells, but also protects them from oxidative damage, thus having dual anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects.

To validate the role of ferroptosis mechanisms in SCHA's amelioration of FLS inflammation, we performed in vitro experiments. The results showed that SCHA could significantly suppress LPS-induced increases in MDA, Fe2+ and ROS as well as decreases in GSH-Px and SOD in FLSs. This demonstrates SCHA can improve LPS-induced FLS inflammatory responses by inhibiting ferroptosis activation. These findings align with emerging evidence that ferroptosis plays a critical role in rheumatoid arthritis pathogenesis, where activated FLS exhibit proliferative features similar to tumor cells and contribute to cartilage erosion through iron-dependent lipid peroxidation mechanisms [1]. The protective effects of SCHA mirror those observed with other ferroptosis inhibitors in synovial inflammation models. For instance, icariin (ICA) has been shown to protect LPS-induced synoviocytes from ferroptosis by activating the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, resulting in decreased MDA levels, reduced ferroptosis, and elevated GPX levels [24] Furthermore, the consistency between protein and mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines further validates the transcriptional regulation of SCHA on inflammatory responses. This dual confirmation at both transcriptional and translational levels strengthens the evidence that SCHA's anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through direct gene expression modulation rather than post-translational modifications alone.

The ultrastructural analysis by transmission electron microscopy provided direct morphological evidence of SCHA's protective effects against ferroptosis-induced mitochondrial damage. The dose-dependent preservation of mitochondrial cristae structure and membrane integrity aligns with previous findings that ferroptosis primarily targets mitochondrial function through lipid peroxidation [25]. These morphological changes serve as reliable indicators of ferroptosis inhibition, as mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae loss are hallmark features of this cell death pathway. The restoration of normal mitochondrial morphology by SCHA treatment provides compelling visual evidence supporting the biochemical markers of ferroptosis inhibition observed in our study.

Using molecular docking and dynamics simulations, we explored the interactions between SCHA and key ferroptosis-related genes TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 from the PPI network. SCHA could form stable complexes with TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, providing structural insights into its direct interactions with ferroptosis-regulating proteins. Additionally, we examined the effects of SCHA on the expression of these key ferroptosis genes in the FLS inflammatory model. LPS markedly decreased TXNRD1 and SLC7A11 expression but increased LPCAT3 expression in FLSs, while SCHA reversed the aberrant expression changes in these genes induced by LPS. TXNRD1 is a selenoprotein that provides reducing equivalents to oxidized thioredoxin, thereby maintaining thioredoxin in an antioxidant reduced state to suppress lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [26,27]. Lemarechal et al. [28] found that under oxidative stress conditions, the activity of TXNRD1 is significantly impaired in human rheumatoid arthritis synovial cells [28]. SLC7A11 is a cystine/glutamate transporter that imports extracellular cystine for glutathione synthesis to enhance antioxidant capacity and inhibit ferroptosis [29,30]. In RA, downregulation of SLC7A11 expression leads to exacerbated oxidative stress and occurrence of ferroptosis in FLSs [31,32]. LPCAT3 is an enzyme incorporating polyunsaturated fatty acids into phospholipids, increasing lipid peroxidation susceptibility and promoting ferroptosis [33,34].

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that SCHA suppresses inflammation in RA FLSs by inhibiting ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death pathway. We identify TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 as key mediators of the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA, through coordinated regulation of ferroptosis. By upregulating the negative regulators TXNRD1 and SLC7A11 and downregulating the positive regulator LPCAT3, SCHA suppresses ferroptosis activation, thereby alleviating lipid peroxidation, oxidative stress and inflammation in RA FLSs. Our findings support the therapeutic potential of SCHA for RA by targeting ferroptosis. Future studies should investigate the in vivo anti-arthritic effects of SCHA in RA animal models. An in-depth understanding of SCHA's anti-inflammatory mechanisms will facilitate its pharmaceutical development into an effective disease-modifying agent for RA therapy.

Conclusion

In this study, we elucidated the mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory effects of SCHA on rheumatoid arthritis fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA FLSs). Integrated bioinformatics analysis of transcriptomic data identified ferroptosis inhibition as a potential mechanism through which SCHA alleviates inflammation in RA FLSs. TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 were predicted as key mediators regulating the anti-inflammatory activity of SCHA. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed that SCHA could directly interact with these proteins. Experimental validation in LPS-induced inflamed RA FLSs demonstrated that SCHA significantly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines and ferroptosis biomarkers in a dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, SCHA reversed the aberrant expression changes of TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 induced by LPS. Collectively, these results demonstrate for the first time that SCHA alleviates inflammation in RA FLSs by coordinately regulating TXNRD1, LPCAT3 and SLC7A11 to inhibit ferroptosis activation. Our study elucidates a novel mechanism underlying the anti-RA effects of SCHA and provides a scientific basis supporting its therapeutic potential for RA management by targeting ferroptosis.
