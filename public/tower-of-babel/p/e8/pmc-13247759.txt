Hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance—is it time to update clinical practice?
Authors: Jessica Yang, Jacob George
Published: 2026

Ultrasound (US) surveillance is universally recommended for patients at high risk of developing hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), as supported by international guidelines from the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL), the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) (1-3). However, the sensitivity of US for detecting HCC varies considerably, ranging from 28% to 100%, with a pooled sensitivity of 84%. For early-stage HCC, sensitivity drops significantly, with a pooled sensitivity of only 47% (4).

The diagnostic performance of US is strongly influenced by liver visualisation. Son et al. demonstrated that patients with no sonographic visualisation limitations (Figure 1) [as defined by the Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) visualisation score (VIS)-A] had a false-negative rate of 57%, compared with 64% with moderate limitations (LI-RADS VIS-B), and 86% with severe limitations (LI-RADS VIS-C) (7). Despite its importance, it is probable that the inclusion of the US VIS in radiology or sonography reports is not yet standard practice globally.

The US VIS is a critical component of HCC surveillance as it communicates whether the entire liver parenchyma has been adequately evaluated for lesions suspicious for an HCC and thus the examination’s expected sensitivity. US visualisation may be impaired by intrinsic factors (e.g., cirrhosis, parenchymal heterogeneity, hepatic steatosis) or extrinsic factors (e.g., rib or lung shadowing, obesity, bowel gas, or limited patient compliance with breathing or positioning instructions) (5). In their most recent update, both LI-RADS and AASLD strongly recommend that US surveillance reports include the visualisation assessment as this information will affect clinical follow-up and recall procedures (3,5). This is an important step forward, and we advocate for the LI-RADS VIS to become routine in clinical practice for radiologists and hepatologists involved in HCC surveillance.

The 2024 update to LI-RADS recognises the limitations of US surveillance and addresses the need for management guidance for patients with poor US visualisation, particularly those with LI-RADS VIS-C. For patients with risk factors for VIS-C—such as cirrhosis from metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), alcohol-related cirrhosis, Child-Pugh class B or C cirrhosis, a body mass index (BMI) ≥35 kg/m2, or repeated VIS-C assessments—the recommendation is that an alternative imaging strategy should be considered (5). However, the specific alternative strategies are not defined within the update.

Various alternative surveillance strategies have been proposed in the literature, including multiphase contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), full-sequence magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with extracellular or hepatocyte-specific contrast agents, and abbreviated MRI (aMRI) using non-contrast or limited contrast-enhanced protocols, with extracellular or hepatocyte-specific contrast agents. Notably, the MIRACLE-HCC trial demonstrates that non-contrast aMRI is a viable alternative to US for HCC surveillance in patients with cirrhosis (8). These findings are supported by prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses, many of which were retrospective in nature (9,10), as well as by prospective trial such as that by Kim et al. (11).

Non-contrast aMRI (NC-aMRI) offers several advantages: it does not require patient cannulation and intravenous contrast, involves no ionizing radiation and has a shorter acquisition time. In the MIRACLE-HCC trial, the protocol total scanning time is approximately 7–10 minutes (8). NC-aMRI is currently the most patient-friendly and economical alternative to US surveillance. Importantly, the MIRACLE-HCC study showed comparable dropout rates between MRI and US, further supporting its feasibility (8).

A significantly higher false-positive rate in the US group, as demonstrated in the MIRACLE-HCC trial, is unsurprising due to the variability in image quality arising from patient- and operator-dependent factors. In contrast, MRI demonstrates consistently low false-negative rates. The MIRACLE-HCC trial found no false negatives in the MRI arm. Similarly high specificity/high negative predictive values were found in prior studies, particularly those that incorporated diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (9-11). This difference likely reflects MRI’s resilience against technical and anatomical factors that commonly degrade US image quality, such as large patient body habitus, heterogeneous liver parenchyma due to chronic liver disease and hepatic steatosis. It is worth noting that in the MIRACLE-HCC study, the size criterion of lesion detection on both US and MRI is >1 cm, in line with the LI-RADS US surveillance algorithm (5).

The superior performance of MRI is further highlighted by its lower surveillance failure rate (4% for MRI vs. 26% for US) and the greater sensitivity for detecting Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage 0 tumors (8). Given MRI’s high specificity, low false-negative rate, and lower surveillance failure, extending the surveillance interval to eight or nine months may be reasonable, aligning with the known doubling time of HCC (12). Kim et al. showed that even annual non-contrast MRI outperformed biannual US surveillance in sensitivity (71% vs. 45.2%, P=0.077) (11). The ability to increase surveillance interval will challenge the concerns regarding cost-effectiveness and accessibility to MRI. A future research focus can consider assessing the diagnostic performance of MRI surveillance with a slightly increased surveillance interval than the currently recommended biannual interval.

Cost and accessibility are often cited as barriers to widespread MRI surveillance. However, universal implementation is not necessary. Instead, MRI should be selectively employed for patients with poor US visualisation. In the MIRACLE-HCC trial, we note that few patients had a BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (fewer than 4% in the US group and less than 1% in the MRI group), and hence we assume that the proportion of patients that were VIS-C was low (8). In a systematic review by Lee et al., of 39,166 US examinations, 59.7% were classified as VIS-A, 32.5% as VIS-B, and 7.8% as VIS-C. Obesity, NAFLD, and cirrhosis were significantly associated with VIS-C classification (6). While the proportion of VIS-C patients is relatively small, they are precisely the group that will most likely benefit from alternative surveillance strategies such as MRI, which is much less affected by visualisation quality compared with US.

The cost-effectiveness analysis of NC-aMRI is highly complex and depends on the inputs and outputs for any model and in the country in which it is to be implemented. These variable costs are region/country-specific and can vary enormously. To properly assess the cost-effectiveness of abbreviated MRI vs. US, region/country-specific analysis would need to be undertaken prior to any implementation.

In Western populations and increasingly worldwide, the prevalence of metabolic liver disease is rising, along with the associated cancers. In a meta-analysis and systematic review by Crane et al., metabolic dysfunction associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) accounted for 12% of HCC globally as the sole aetiology. Moreover, MAFLD was a contributing factor in 40% of hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related, 54% of hepatitis C virus (HCV)-related, and 64% of alcohol-related HCC. Overall, 49% of individuals with HCC had MAFLD as a component of their liver disease (13). These data highlight the growing relevance of metabolic liver diseases in HCC epidemiology and underscores the urgent need for effective surveillance strategies in this population.

The role of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) in HCC surveillance has been hotly debated. At a population level, blood-based biomarkers, if sufficiently sensitive, are preferable in terms of cost, infrastructure and convenience, with imaging reserved as a confirmatory second-line test. At present, AFP is recognised as imperfect for this purpose, while studies using newer algorithms such as GALAD [Gender, Age, Lens culinaris agglutinin-reactive fraction of AFP (AFP-L3), AFP, Des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP)] (14) and GAAD [Gender, Age, AFP, and Des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP)] (15) await further prospective evaluation and replication. In future, providing cost-effectiveness can be demonstrated for the population undergoing surveillance, liquid biomarkers such as AFPL3, PIVKA-II and algorithms such as GAAD and GALAD may complement imaging markers including NC-aMRI for early HCC detection. In MIRACLE-HCC, in the US group, 30% of HCCs were positive by AFP only, and 26% were false-negative, while in the MRI group, 8% were positive by AFP only, and none were false-negative. Thus, while the NC-aMRI was more sensitive, some cancers would have been missed; whether this would impact outcomes awaits further research.

Limitations of the MIRACLE-HCC study include that it is a trial from a single centre with a homogeneous population, and a dominant background liver disease (80% of patients having hepatitis B). The authors could have also considered recording the US VISs from their surveillance US.

In conclusion, MRI is an effective alternative to US for HCC surveillance in patients with poor US visualisation. The 2024 LI-RADS update appropriately addresses this need in patients with VIS-C or other contributing factors such as MAFLD and alcohol-related cirrhosis. Among available options, non-contrast MRI currently represents the most accessible, efficient, and patient-centred alternative to US surveillance in appropriately selected patients.
