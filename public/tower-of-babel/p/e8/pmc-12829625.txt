AI-assisted rapid on-site evaluation ROSE of EUS-FNA cytopathology for pancreatic solid lesions: A two-stage deep learning approach
Authors: Yuan Tian, Jixin Zhang, Xiaolong Rao, Bin Zhou, Xudong Zhao, Yan He, Haiqiang Sheng, Weidong Nian, Yunlong Cai, Long Rong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEUS–guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) combined with rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) is crucial for diagnosing pancreatic solid lesions. However, the widespread application of ROSE faces challenges, particularly the limited availability of specialized cytopathologists for immediate interpretation. Furthermore, the inherent variability in EUS-FNA sample quality (e.g., cellularity and debris) and suboptimal smear preparation during procedures can impede reliable automated analysis.This study aimed to develop and validate a novel 2-stage deep learning–based diagnostic model for the automated analysis of EUS-FNA cytological images of pancreatic solid lesions captured using mobile phones during the ROSE process, with the goal of automating diagnosis and improving efficiency.MethodsWe retrospectively collected 882 EUS-FNA cytological images from 92 patients with pancreatic solid lesions at Peking University First Hospital, captured using mobile phones, during ROSE. A 2-stage deep learning model was developed. The first stage utilized YOLOv8n-p2 for robust tissue cell detection, addressing background complexity, and enabling preprocessing steps such as cell size normalization. In the second stage, DenseNet201 was employed to classify the detected cellular regions as malignant or normal. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing sets. The model performance was evaluated using a dedicated test set consisting of whole images.ResultsOn the independent test set, the proposed 2-stage method achieved high performance in classifying images for malignancy, yielding an accuracy of 93.3%, an area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR) of 0.958, and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.962.ConclusionThe diagnostic accuracy of the proposed AI method is comparable to that of traditional expert-involved ROSE, while offering the potential to significantly reduce diagnostic time and dependency on immediate pathologist availability. This 2-stage deep learning approach effectively analyzes variable mobile phone-captured EUS-FNA images, demonstrating its potential as an automated, AI-assisted diagnostic system for the ROSE process. It holds significant potential for clinical application, particularly in facilitating timely pancreatic cancer diagnosis in resource-constrained settings and in scenarios where on-site pathological expertise is limited.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most aggressive malignancies, characterized by high mortality rates and limited early diagnostic options, particularly in its early stages.[1] Early diagnosis and accurate subtyping are crucial for improving patient prognosis.[2,3] EUS–guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) has emerged as a reliable and minimally invasive technique for obtaining tissue samples to diagnose pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and other solid masses.[4,5] Recent meta-analyses have shown that EUS-FNA can reliably diagnose pancreatic masses with an accuracy of 80%–86%.[6,7] However, the diagnostic accuracy of EUS-FNA in real-world practice is influenced by various factors, such as the number of needle passes and operator experience, leading to significant variability in the diagnostic yield.[6–8]

Rapid on-site cytopathological evaluation (ROSE) improves the diagnostic accuracy and efficiency of EUS-FNA by providing real-time feedback on sample adequacy, thereby reducing the need for repeat biopsies.[9,10] However, the widespread adoption of ROSE is limited by the scarcity of specialized cytopathologists as well as the associated high costs.[11] To address the resource limitations of ROSE, rapid online evaluation (ROLE) has emerged, allowing endoscopists to obtain preliminary diagnoses from cytopathologists remotely.[12] However, the inconsistent quality of images captured using mobile phones during the ROLE process poses challenges for remote pathological diagnosis. Additionally, issues such as the high demand for specialized skills from endoscopists, the continued need for pathologist involvement, and feedback delays persist. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, is increasingly being applied to pancreatic cancer diagnosis,[13,14] especially within the context of EUS-FNA coupled with ROSE. Research indicates that AI models, notably deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs), can effectively analyze cytopathological images, achieving accurate and reproducible assessments and offering the potential to enhance diagnostic workflows by reducing the reliance on immediate expert availability.[15–17]

However, the translation of this potential into widespread clinical practice faces significant hurdles. First, developing robust models typically requires large, meticulously annotated datasets, the creation of which is resource-intensive and a key barrier.[18] Second, EUS-FNA cytological samples exhibit considerable variability in quality, including inconsistent cellularity, high blood content, and necrosis, which can complicate automated analysis.[19] Furthermore, smears prepared directly during procedures by endoscopists may contain more artifacts than optimally processed laboratory slides, which hinders accurate cell recognition. Finally, the inherent complexity or “black box” nature of some deep learning models can impede clinical trust and adoption.[20] This underscores the critical need for AI solutions that are not only reliable but also interpretable. Ideally, such systems should offer intuitive visual outputs (such as heatmaps indicating the probability or intensity of malignancy), enabling clinicians to understand and verify the basis of AI assessments, thereby fostering greater confidence and supporting clinical integration.

To overcome these specific challenges, particularly the issues of data requirements and sample heterogeneity encountered in real-world ROSE settings, this study introduces and evaluates a novel 2-stage deep learning–based diagnostic model. By leveraging cutting-edge AI technologies, we aim to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of pancreatic diseases during real-time evaluation while addressing resource limitations. Our proposed model first utilizes the YOLOv8n-p2 object detection model to accurately identify tissue cells within complex cytological images captured directly using mobile phones during ROSE. This initial stage effectively filters out background debris and other noncellular elements that are common in real-world smears. Subsequently, the DenseNet201 classification model was used to analyze these selected cellular regions to determine malignancy, focusing its detailed feature analysis only on pertinent areas. This architecture strategically combines the strengths of object detection and image classification, making it particularly suitable for improving target cell identification accuracy and potentially streamlining the ROSE workflow, particularly in resource-limited scenarios.

We hypothesized that this method, incorporating preprocessing steps such as cell size normalization, could achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to conventional expert-involved ROSE while significantly reducing the reliance on immediate pathologist availability and associated time costs.

METHODS

Study design overview

This study employed a retrospective design to develop and evaluate a 2-stage deep learning–based method for analyzing cytological images of pancreatic solid lesions obtained through EUS-FNA and captured using mobile phones during the ROSE process [Figure 1].

Overall study workflow illustrating data processing, model training, and evaluation.

Data acquisition and patient selection

Data were sourced from EUS-FNA cytological images of pancreatic solid lesions captured by mobile phones during the ROSE process at Peking University First Hospital between January 2022 and August 2023, as part of a research initiative integrated into clinical practice. These images were acquired specifically for research purposes to facilitate AI-assisted analysis, and the images were transmitted to computers for processing. All images contained only cytological content without any patient-identifying information. Measures to protect patient privacy and data security were implemented in accordance with institutional data protection policies and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.[21] Image acquisition was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval numbers: 2020-251 and 2024-412), and all patients provided written informed consent. A total of 882 high-resolution, large-sized cytological images were collected from 92 patients with pancreatic solid lesions, including 74 malignant and 18 noncancerous cases. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age >18 years, (2) pancreatic lesions confirmed by at least one diagnostic method, (3) provision of written informed consent, (4) EUS-FNA performed at the Endoscopy Center of Peking University First Hospital, and (5) availability of high-definition cytological images captured under the microscope. The exclusion criteria were designed to prioritize cases in which EUS-FNA could directly inform clinical management, thereby minimizing selection bias toward irrelevant or low-yield lesions. Specifically, cases were excluded if (1) severe coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia increased procedural risks, (2) intervening blood vessels precluded safe EUS-FNA, or (3) EUS-FNA results were unlikely to influence clinical management, such as incidental low-risk cystic-solid lesions or cystic lesions with benign features (e.g., simple cysts without concerning imaging findings). This approach ensures that the study cohort represents diagnostically challenging pancreatic solid lesions, enhancing the applicability of our AI model to high-impact clinical settings. To ensure reproducibility and image quality, smartphones with a minimum resolution of 12MP and optimized for high-definition photography (e.g., YN450) were used. These devices were connected via a fixed adapter to the trinocular head port of a standard trinocular microscope, enabling stable wireless transmission of images while maintaining clarity and consistency in the capture conditions (Figure 2 for an illustration of the image acquisition setup).

Smartphone-microscope imaging setup and data privacy workflow. The setup consisted of a ≥12-megapixel smartphone (YN450, Yongnuo, Shenzhen, China) mounted to the trinocular port of a standard light microscope via a fixed optical adapter to ensure stable, high-resolution imaging. The images were transferred to a secure local workstation. Only cytological content without identifiable patient features was captured. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional policies and approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval numbers: 2020-251 and 2024-412), Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

EUS-FNA procedures and sample processing

EUS-FNA procedures were performed by 2 experienced endoscopists (each performing >100 procedures/year) using a linear echoendoscope (Olympus) for lesion visualization and color Doppler to identify regional vasculature. Under endosonographic guidance, FNA was performed using needles from either Cook Medical or Boston Scientific. The sampling technique, either slow-pull stylet removal or vacuum aspiration, was selected based on the lesion characteristics. Specimens were immediately processed for rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE). Visible tissue fragments (“cores”) or aspirated fluid was smeared onto glass slides, air-dried, and stained using Diff-Quik. Slides were promptly examined microscopically to assess specimen adequacy and cellularity, and representative images were captured when diagnostic material was present.

Image labeling

Image annotation for this study was performed collaboratively by experienced endoscopists and 2 senior pathologists. Initial bounding box annotations were made by endoscopists and subsequently reviewed by pathologists. Any discrepancies in the annotation were adjudicated by a third, highly experienced senior pathologist to ensure consensus. Two distinct labeling protocols were used. The first protocol, applied to 41 original EUS-FNA images, involved delineating all identifiable tissue cells using bounding boxes. This dataset was used to train the tissue cell detection model. The second protocol, applied to the full cohort of collected EUS-FNA images, required only the annotation of malignant cells with bounding boxes. These annotations were used to generate a dataset of malignant cell image patches for training the malignant cell classification model. Notably, owing to time and resource constraints, some images received incomplete annotations, where only a representative subset of malignant cells was labeled. These partially annotated images were utilized exclusively for model training.

Dataset construction and division

This study involved the construction and utilization of 2 distinct datasets for sequential model training and evaluation: a tissue cell image dataset and a malignant cell image block dataset. The data construction process is illustrated in Figure 3, which details the division and annotation of the original dataset.

Construction and partitioning of image datasets. A total of 882 EUS-FNA cytology images were collected from 92 patients, comprising fully and partially annotated images. Forty-one fully annotated images with complete cell-level labels formed the cell detection dataset (2487 annotated cells in total) used to train and evaluate the YOLOv8n-p2 model. The malignant cell patch dataset was derived from the full set of 882 images (including the aforementioned 41) following cell size normalization, patch extraction, and removal of edge artifacts. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets for patch classification, with 60 fully annotated whole-slide images designated as independent whole-image test sets.

The tissue cell image dataset was developed primarily to train the initial cell detection model. It comprised 41 images, where all cells (tumor and nontumor, totaling 2487 cells) were annotated by expert pathologists with bounding boxes derived from the original unprocessed EUS-FNA images acquired using mobile phones during the ROSE process. This dataset was subsequently divided into a training set (33 images containing 1895 annotated cells) and a test set (8 images containing 592 annotated cells).

The Malignant Cell Image Block Dataset was created for training and validating the malignant cell classification model, derived from a larger pool of 882 EUS-FNA images captured similarly during ROSE, which included the 41 original EUS-FNA images used previously. Based on annotation completeness by expert pathologists, these source images were categorized as either “completely labeled” (all discernible malignant cells annotated) or “incompletely labeled” (only a representative subset of malignant cells annotated). To generate the final image blocks, specific processing steps were applied: cell size normalization, image block extraction (using cell-centered cropping for labeled malignant cells and a sliding window approach for background/normal regions in completely labeled images), and removal of blocks near image edges from the completely labeled set. For incompletely labeled images, only blocks centered on the annotated malignant cells were extracted.

The source images for block generation were allocated as follows: the training set utilized blocks derived from 329 completely labeled images and all 439 incompletely labeled images (leveraging all available malignant cell annotations). The validation set used blocks derived from 54 distinct, completely labeled images. After block extraction, the resulting dataset composition was as follows:

To align with the clinical task of assessing overall image malignancy, the test set for evaluating the final classification model consisted of 60 separate, completely labeled whole EUS-FNA images (distinct from those used for training/validation block generation). These test images were evaluated directly by the model without prior conversion into image blocks. Model architecture and training

The 2-stage model in this study consisted of a YOLO model and a DenseNet model. YOLOv8n-p2, a small object detection model,[22] is used to detect all tissue cells in each image; DenseNet201, a classification model,[23] is used to determine whether each image block contains malignant cells.

YOLOv8n-p2 model

YOLOv8n-p2 is a lightweight, high-precision small object detection model. The model is a variant of YOLOv8n, and its network structure consists of 3 parts: the backbone, neck, and head networks. The backbone network adopts a lightweight CSPDarknet architecture, stacking cross-stage partial (CSP) connections to effectively extract multi-scale features while maintaining computational efficiency, making it suitable for detecting tissue cells in mobile phone-captured images. The neck network utilizes a combination of Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) and path aggregation networks (PAN) to enhance cross-layer feature fusion capabilities, enabling it to capture the details of small target cells at different resolutions. The head network is based on the classic design of the YOLO series and is responsible for predicting the object bounding boxes, classes, and confidence scores.

Our chosen YOLOv8n-p2 model optimized YOLOv8n for the detection of small objects. In the original YOLOv8n model, although features for small objects, such as cells, were captured in the backbone network, they were not directly utilized in the neck and head networks. The suffix “-p2” indicates that an extra detection head is spliced into the feature pyramid. Thus, the captured features for small objects are passed to the neck network and used by the head network. Consequently, the new model improves the accuracy of detecting small-scale tissue cells.[24]

The model was trained on a self-constructed tissue cell image dataset using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 (24.0 GB GPU memory) with 1000 training epochs and an image size of 640 × 640. The training parameters included a batch size of 64, an early stopping patience of 20, the AdamW optimizer for the first 10,000 iterations followed by the SGD optimizer, and an initial learning rate of 0.01. The loss functions were BCEWithLogitsLoss for classification, CIoU Loss for localization, and Distributional Focal Loss (DFL) for fine-grained predictions. DenseNet201 model and data augmentation

DenseNet201 offers advantages in terms of feature reuse and gradient propagation. Its network structure significantly improves the feature transfer efficiency through dense connectivity. The core component is the Dense Block, where each layer is directly connected to all preceding layers within the Dense Block, achieving feature reuse through concatenation, thereby reducing parameter redundancy and enhancing the representation of fine-grained features in cell image blocks. Transition layers are located between adjacent Dense Blocks and consist of convolution and pooling operations, which are used to reduce the dimensionality and manage model complexity while maintaining the spatial resolution of the features. This structure is particularly suitable for the fine-grained classification task of cell image blocks, as it effectively captures the microscopic texture and morphological differences of tissue cells, thus improving the classification accuracy.[25]

Various data augmentation methods were implemented during training to improve the generalization performance and robustness of the model. These included random rotation (±180 degrees), translation (±0.1 of the image size in both horizontal and vertical directions), scaling (±0.1 of the image size), vertical flipping (with a probability of 0.5), and horizontal flipping (with a probability of 0.5). The model was trained on a self-constructed malignant cell image dataset using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 with 24.0 GB GPU memory. The training process consisted of 1000 epochs with an input image size of 224 × 224. Other specific training parameters included a batch size of 32, early stopping patience of 50, Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-5, and cross-entropy loss as the loss function. Two-stage model inference framework

This study employed a 2-stage inference framework to analyze EUS-FNA cytological images. Stage 1 comprised cell detection and size normalization. Initially, the YOLOv8n-p2 object-detection model identified tissue cells and generated corresponding bounding boxes. To standardize the cell appearance for subsequent analysis, the dimensions (width, height, and area) of each detected cell were calculated. The central 70% of the cells, ranked by area, were selected to compute the average cell width. This average was compared with a predefined target width of 50 pixels to determine a scaling factor, which was then applied to resize both the original image and bounding box coordinates. Stage 2 comprised block classification and heatmap generation. Following scaling, 256 × 256-pixel image blocks were extracted and centered on each detected cell’s adjusted coordinates. These blocks were resized to 224 × 224 pixels, which is the required input size for the pretrained DenseNet201 classification model. The classifier assigned a probability score (malignant vs. normal) to each input block. These individual block probabilities were then spatially reassembled based on their original locations to generate a preliminary heatmap. To ensure smooth transitions, the probability values in the overlapping regions were averaged. This heatmap was subsequently resized to match the original image dimensions and masked using the initial YOLOv8n-p2 detection results, effectively retaining probability information only within the boundaries of detected tissue cells. Finally, for clinical interpretation, this cell-specific probability map can be postprocessed by applying a user-defined threshold to delineate the regions classified as malignant. Evaluation metrics

This study primarily used accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, F1 score, area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) as evaluation metrics for model performance.

Accuracy

Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly predicted samples to the total predictions. In EUS-FNA cytological image analysis, high accuracy indicates that the model can effectively differentiate between malignant and normal cell image blocks, providing a reliable basis for clinical diagnosis of pancreatic cancer.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity represents the proportion of correctly identified malignant cell image blocks to the actual malignant cells. High sensitivity is crucial for reducing the rate of missed malignant cell diagnoses and is a core metric for evaluating the clinical utility of this model.

Specificity

Specificity represents the proportion of correctly identified normal cell image blocks to the actual normal cells. High specificity reduces false positives, thereby minimizing unnecessary follow-up examinations and patient anxiety.

F1 score

F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, which comprehensively measures the accuracy and completeness of the model’s predictions. A higher F1 score reflects the model’s ability to balance precision and coverage when identifying malignant cells.

AUC-PR

In situations where the proportion of malignant cells is low and the consequences of missed diagnoses are severe, AUC-PR is a key metric. A high value indicates that the model effectively reduces missed diagnoses while maintaining a high precision.

AUC-ROC

AUC-ROC evaluated the model’s ability to distinguish between malignant and normal cell image blocks. A high value indicates that the model can maintain a high true positive rate and a low false-positive rate across different thresholds, demonstrating excellent clinical applicability.

Model performance

The effectiveness of the first stage of our framework, the YOLOv8n-p2 small object detection model, was evaluated for its primary task of identifying tissue cells using the dedicated tissue cell image dataset. The model exhibited strong performance for accurately locating the locations of tissue cells. The precision-recall (PR) curve analysis resulted in an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.880, corresponding to a mean average precision (mAP) of 0.880 at an intersection over union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 [Figure 8A]. This indicates effective cell detection with a good balance between the precision and recall. DISCUSSION

This study developed and validated a novel 2-stage deep learning method for analyzing mobile phone-acquired EUS-FNA cytological images obtained during ROSE for pancreatic solid lesions. The method demonstrated robust performance in diagnosing malignant cells, with key metrics, including Accuracy, AUC-PR, and AUC-ROC, approaching those achieved with traditional expert participation in ROSE.[16] Its diagnostic accuracy is comparable to that of other recently reported AI-assisted systems for EUS-FNA cytology. More importantly, these results highlight the promise of this method as a potential substitute for real-time interpretation by an on-site pathologist during ROSE, offering a solution to mitigate the constraints imposed by the limited availability of pathologists for immediate on-site evaluation.

A key strength of our approach lies in its 2-stage architecture, which synergistically combines the distinct advantages of YOLO for object detection and DenseNet for classification, thereby creating an efficient system for identifying malignant cells in complex cytological images. First, the YOLOv8n-p2 model addressed a critical challenge in cytological smear analysis by performing robust object detection. It effectively isolates potentially diagnostic tissue cells from confounding elements, such as blood cells and background debris. This capability aligns with recent findings by Karol et al.,[26] who demonstrated YOLO’s proficiency in medical image object detection, particularly in separating targets from intricate backgrounds. Second, once the tissue cells are localized, the DenseNet201 classifier leverages its densely connected network structure and potent feature reuse mechanisms. As supported by the work of Madani et al.,[27] who explored DenseNet in cancer detection, this architecture excels at capturing the subtle morphological features necessary for accurately distinguishing malignant cells from benign ones within the selected regions, thereby significantly enhancing the classification accuracy. This hierarchical identification strategy not only has the potential to reduce the workload for pathologists but also aims to improve diagnostic consistency and reduce false-positive rates. Consequently, this method offers a reliable AI-assisted tool for clinical cytopathology, holding considerable value, particularly in resource-limited health care environments, where immediate expert pathological interpretation may be challenging. Furthermore, this study specifically addressed practical application scenarios involving mobile phone image acquisition during ROSE. Recognizing the inherent variability in the image scale introduced by different devices and capture distances, we incorporated a cell size adjustment algorithm. This preprocessing step effectively normalizes cell sizes across the dataset, mitigating inconsistencies related to image capture, thereby enhancing the robustness and generalizability of the downstream deep learning models. Finally, additional preprocessing strategies contributed to the model performance. The adoption of image blocking centered on detected cells focuses the classifier’s attention on pertinent cellular information, whereas the removal of blocks containing edge artifacts minimized the influence of incomplete or distorted cells, which are often found near image borders. These steps likely reduced background noise and artifact interference, further refining the data used for classification and contributing positively to the model’s overall performance.

The integration of our proposed diagnostic method with ROSE holds potential not only to assist endoscopists by providing real-time diagnostic insights but also to serve as a valuable tool for training, potentially accelerating the learning curve for less experienced practitioners through immediate, objective feedback. Notably, this study achieved performance comparable to existing large-sample studies using a relatively small dataset (882 images from 92 patients).[16,17] A key innovation enabling this efficiency is our data processing strategy, which maximizes information from limited cases. Detecting individual cells (via YOLO) and generating centered image blocks transformed the initial images into a large dataset of cellular patches for effective classifier training. This shifts the model’s focus to high-resolution, background-reduced cell features, enhancing diagnostic efficiency and substantially reducing the dependency on acquiring numerous patient cases for AI training. This principle of leveraging patch-based processing to improve data utility aligns with findings from previous studies, demonstrating its effectiveness for training robust deep learning models with fewer initial medical image samples.[27] Thus, this data amplification strategy is central to the performance of our method and offers a pragmatic approach for AI development, especially in resource-constrained settings.

Furthermore, AI-assisted ROSE may enhance procedural efficiency and safety by potentially reducing the number of necessary EUS-FNA needle passes. Although ROSE typically reduces passes compared to procedures without it (e.g., 3–5 vs. 5–7 passes often recommended[28,29]), our average under ROSE was 3.17 passes. The rapid feedback of AI on sample adequacy, particularly identifying likely malignant cells, could empower endoscopists to confidently conclude sampling earlier, possibly after only 1–2 passes. This potential reduction could further improve efficiency and lower the risks associated with multiple needle insertions, such as bleeding. Therefore, AI-assisted ROSE holds promise for streamlining the EUS-FNA workflow and potentially enhancing safety margins.

Moreover, this cell-centric, block-based approach inherently adapts to the typical characteristics of cytology smears, which are often rich in cells but with diagnostically relevant cells that are potentially sparsely distributed, ensuring that the model effectively learns from pertinent features while balancing analytical efficiency and diagnostic accuracy. Therefore, our findings suggest that the proposed method can effectively extract and leverage detailed image features for accurate classification, even from a limited initial cohort, demonstrating its significant application potential.

Despite these promising results, this study has some limitations. First, its single-center design restricts immediate generalizability, although some mitigation is provided by intracenter procedural variability (multiple endoscopists) and increasing ROSE process standardization. Nevertheless, rigorous multicenter external validation is essential to confirm the broader applicability of these findings. Second, although our patch-based strategy maximized data use, the base cohort of 92 patients was relatively modest. This may constrain the model’s peak performance and full generalization potential, necessitating future studies with larger, ideally multi-center, patient populations. Third, the variability inherent in mobile phone image acquisition (e.g., device differences, lighting, and angles) poses a challenge, although it is partially addressed by size normalization. Future efforts should target improved image standardization or the development of models that are more inherently robust to real-world variations.

The 2-stage methodology proposed in this study holds potential for broader applications. This method can be extended to the image analysis of adenocarcinoma cells in other organs or transferred to different types of oncological scenarios (such as the identification of malignant cells in squamous cell carcinoma cytology). Rigorous comparison with pathologist diagnoses in these varied applications will be essential to fully establish its clinical utility.

In conclusion, this study successfully developed and validated a 2-stage deep learning framework for the automated analysis of EUS-FNA cytological images acquired using mobile phones during ROSE. This method demonstrates robust diagnostic performance and offers a novel solution to facilitate automation within the ROSE workflow. This approach holds significant promise for facilitating broader application of EUS-FNA technology, particularly in resource-limited settings or clinical scenarios where immediate on-site pathologist availability for ROSE is constrained. By potentially enabling rapid and objective cytological assessments, this AI-driven tool could help improve the efficiency and accessibility of early pancreatic cancer diagnosis.
