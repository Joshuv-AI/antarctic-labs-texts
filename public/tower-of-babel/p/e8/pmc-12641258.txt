CRISPR/Cas12a2 enables ultra-sensitive amplification-free RNA detection
Authors: Hyowon Jang, Ju-Eun Kang, Hansol Kim, Jae-Rin Kim, Junsoo Park, Seong-Ryeong Go, Young-Hoon Lee, Hyunju Kang, Yeonkyung Park, Sunjoo Kim, Yongwon Jung, Seung Jun Kim, Eun-Kyung Lim, Juyeon Jung, Eui-Jeon Woo, Kwang-Hyun Park, Taejoon Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) technologies have led to the use of diverse CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins in diagnostic applications. Herein, we present a CRISPR/Cas12a2-based amplification-free RNA detection method that exhibits sub-attomolar sensitivity and substrate versatility. Cas12a2, a recently characterized RNA-guided nuclease, uniquely integrates bimolecular recognition through CRISPR RNA (crRNA)-target complementarity and protospacer flanking sequence identification, enabling highly specific trans-cleavage of single-stranded DNA, double-stranded DNA, and RNA. We have optimized key biochemical parameters, including pH, ionic strength, and temperature, to enhance the catalytic efficiency of Cas12a2. Based on the optimal activity conditions of Cas12a2, we have achieved ultra-sensitive viral RNA detection with a limit of detection of 46.7 aM through the strategic design and cooperative activation of crRNAs targeting conserved regions of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) genome. The diagnostic accuracy of the Cas12a2-based assay has been demonstrated for 26 SARS-CoV-2 variants, and it has further resulted in the definitive diagnosis of 317 clinical samples. This work establishes Cas12a2 as a promising molecular diagnostic tool that provides an amplification-free, rapid, and versatile solution for RNA detection. The adaptability and simplicity of the platform render it particularly well suited for point-of-care applications, paving the way for next-generation CRISPR diagnostics.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of molecular diagnostic technologies has significantly enhanced the capacity to detect and monitor infectious diseases and genetic anomalies. At the forefront of this progress are clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated (Cas) systems [1], which have expanded the frontiers of biomolecular detection beyond the conventional methodologies of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and isothermal amplification techniques [2]. Among the Cas proteins, Cas12a and Cas13a have significantly broadened the versatility of CRISPR-based diagnostic platforms [3]. Each of these proteins exhibits distinctive substrate preferences and molecular mechanisms, thereby enabling the development of bespoke applications for the detection of DNA or RNA molecules [4]. For example, Cas12a and Cas13a have been successfully employed in platforms such as DNA Endonuclease Targeted CRISPR Trans Reporter (DETECTR) [5], Specific High-sensitivity Enzymatic Reporter unLOCKing (SHERLOCK) [6], and Self-amplified and CRISPR-aided Operation to Profile extracellular vesicles (EVs) (SCOPE) [7], enabling highly sensitive detection of pathogens and biomarkers, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), Zika virus, and EV messenger RNA (mRNA) [8]. The recent discovery of novel Cas proteins has further transformed the landscape of CRISPR diagnostic technologies. One example is Cas12j, a compact protein with robust trans-cleavage activity on short single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), which has been applied to microRNA detection [9]. The continuous expansion of the Cas protein repertoire enhances the adaptability and effectiveness of CRISPR diagnostics, equipping researchers with versatile tools to address an increasingly wide range of diagnostic challenges [10, 11].

Cas12a2, a recently characterized Cas protein, represents a mechanistically distinct component of prokaryotic adaptive immunity. Its molecular architecture contains both a WED domain and a RuvC domain, which are shared with Type V single effectors. However, it exhibits only 10%–20% sequence similarity with other members of this group [12]. Interestingly, Cas12a2 necessitates dual molecular recognition for activation in contrast to RNA-specific nucleases such as Type VI Cas13a and Type III Csm/Cmr complexes [13]. This distinctive mechanism integrates target RNA binding with the identification of the protospacer flanking sequence (PFS), triggering conformational changes that expose the RuvC nuclease domain. The dual molecular recognition mechanism of Cas12a2, which requires both RNA target binding and PFS identification, offers an additional degree of specificity, thereby minimizing off-target effects and enhancing assay reliability. Upon activation, Cas12a2 initiates broad-spectrum nuclease activity, cleaving indiscriminately ssDNA, double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), and RNA, resulting in comprehensive nucleic acid degradation [14]. Moreover, Cas12a2 exhibits a rapid reaction speed, allowing for fast signal amplification and detection, which is beneficial for time-sensitive diagnostic applications [12]. These distinctive biochemical properties of Cas12a2 suggest its potential as an adaptable, rapid, and robust tool for the advancement of CRISPR-based diagnostics. However, the application of Cas12a2 in molecular diagnostics is rarely investigated. A significant challenge hindering the widespread adoption of Cas12a2 in the diagnostic field pertains to the limited understanding of its activation dynamics and cleavage efficiency under physiological conditions. To apply the characteristics of Cas12a2 to a diagnostic workflow, extensive optimization is necessary.

Herein, we systematically optimized the biochemical parameters of Cas12a2 and developed an amplification-free RNA detection method. First, we optimized key biochemical parameters, including pH, ionic strength, temperature, and reducing conditions, to establish robust operational protocols. Under these optimized conditions, Cas12a2 demonstrated higher trans-cleavage activity compared to Cas13a. Second, we designed multiple CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) targeting conserved severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) genomic regions with appropriate PFS elements, achieving enhanced sensitivity through cooperative Cas12a2 activation. The quadruplex Cas12a2/crRNAs system exhibits a thousand-fold enhanced sensitivity in comparison with single crRNA approach. The limit of detection (LOD) of Cas12a2-based assay was in the sub-attomolar range. Furthermore, we validated the assay across 26 SARS-CoV-2 variants and 317 clinical samples, demonstrating its potential as a reliable and scalable diagnostic tool. The findings of our research endeavor to facilitate the broader implementation of this CRISPR effector within the domain of molecular diagnostics.

Protein expression and purification

The Cas12a2 enzyme used in this study is derived from the sulfur-oxidizing ε-proteobacterium Sulfuricurvum sp. PC08-66 (SuCas12a2). The gene sequence was confirmed based on the information reported by Dmytrenko et al. [14] and codon-optimized for Escherichia coli expression prior to synthesis. The synthetic open reading frame was cloned into the pET-28a(+) vector to express Cas12a2 with an N-terminal 6× His tag, and the resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli recombinant inbred line cells for protein expression. The cultures were grown at 37°C in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.5–0.7. Protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranosid and continued at 18°C for 18 h. Following incubation, cells were harvested, resuspended in lysis buffer (2M NaCl, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and 1 mg/ml lysozyme), and lysed by sonication. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation, and the supernatant was diluted 1:1 with salt-free buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 7.2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM imidazole, and 10% glycerol). The soluble protein fraction was applied to a pre-equilibrated HisTrap HP 5 ml affinity column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) in A buffer (500 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM imidazole, and 10% glycerol). Proteins were eluted using B buffer (500 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.2, 2 mM MgCl2, 250 mM imidazole, and 10% glycerol), followed by further purification via size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 26/600 column (Cytiva) equilibrated in SEC buffer [150 mM NaCl, 25 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazine ethanesulfonic (HEPES) acid, pH 7.2, 2 mM MgCl2, and 5% glycerol]. The purified protein fractions were concentrated to 8 mg/ml using centrifugal filtration (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Ribonucleoprotein preparation

To promote hairpin formation, the crRNA was heated at 95°C for 3 min and subsequently cooled to 25°C. The Cas12a2 protein was diluted to 50 μM in SEC buffer, while the crRNA was diluted to 50 μM in Ultrapure DNase/RNase-free distilled water (DEPC-W). Both components were combined in a 1:1 ratio and incubated at 25°C for 30 min.

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis

A 10 µl reaction mixture was prepared, consisting of 2 µl each of ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, target RNA, 5× reaction buffer [250 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 50 mM MgCl2, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 500 µg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 25% glycerol], DEPC-W, and reporter probe. The final concentrations were 1 µM for the RNP complex, 250 nM for the target RNA (I, II, or III), and 250 nM for the FAM-labeled ssDNA, dsDNA, or RNA reporter. The reactions were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, 1 µl of 25 mg/ml proteinase K (Bioneer) was added to the reactions, followed by further incubation at 37°C for 30 min. Subsequently, 11 µl of 2× RNA loading dye (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA) was added to each reaction, and the mixtures were heated at 95°C for 5 min. The reactions were immediately placed on ice to stop the reaction. The reaction mixture resolved on a 10% Novex™ TBE Gels (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) at a constant voltage of 110 V for 120 min, using 1× tris-borate-ethylene-diamine-tetra acetic acid as the running buffer. Following electrophoresis, the gel was stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain, and images were acquired using a GelDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

In vitro trans-cleavage activity assay

For comprehensive analysis of Cas12a2 trans-cleavage activity, a 10 µl reaction mixture was prepared, consisting of 2 µl each of RNP complex, target RNA, 5× reaction buffer, DEPC-W, and reporter probe. The final concentrations of RNP, target RNA, and reporter probe (ssDNA, dsDNA, and RNA) were all adjusted to 250 nM. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 20 min. To optimize the assay conditions, the pH of the reaction buffer was varied between 7.0 and 9.0 using Tris–HCl, NaCl concentrations were adjusted from 50 to 250 mM, different cofactors including MgCl2, CoSO4, CaCl2, CuSO4, and NiCl2 were compared, and the chosen MgCl2 concentrations were adjusted from 0 to 20 mM. Additionally, DTT concentrations were varied between 0 and 20 mM, and BSA concentrations were tested from 0 to 600 μg/ml. The final buffer conditions for the reactions at 1× were 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8), 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 100 µg/ml BSA, and 5% glycerol. Thermal dependency was assessed over a temperature range of 25°C–65°C.

For comparative analysis of Cas12a2 and Cas13a trans-cleavage activity across target BA.1 and XBB.1.9.1, assays were performed using different reporter nucleic acid types. Additionally, the impact of the presence or absence of a PFS on the cleavage efficiency of Cas12a2 and Cas13a was compared. Assay specificity was evaluated by introducing four nucleotide mutations into 24 distinct target sequences. After the reaction, samples were diluted 10-fold with a buffer containing 50 mM NaCl and 10 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0, and fluorescence intensities were measured using Infinite M200 Pro (Tecan, Mäennedorf, Switzerland) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm, using black 96-well microplates (Thermo Scientific™) for the analysis.

For a more rigorous comparative analysis, we employed two Cas13a orthologs (LwaCas13a and LbuCas13a). Each crRNA comprised a Cas13a-specific handle sequence and a spacer region complementary to SARS-CoV-2 RNA. To ensure equivalency in target recognition, the spacer sequences were designed to match those of Cas12a2 crRNAs 6, 7, 9, and 10. The complete sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For single crRNA-based comparisons, the experimental conditions were identical to those used in the previous comparative analysis targeting BA.1 and XBB.1.9.1, with the sole modification that 100 pM of SARS-CoV-2 gRNA was used as the target in this study. For multiple crRNA-based comparative analysis, the SARS-CoV-2 gRNA was serially diluted 10-fold. The diluted SARS-CoV-2 gRNA (1 µl) was combined with the other reaction components (9 µl), consisting of four kinds of RNP complex, reaction buffer, DEPC-W, and reporter probe. The final concentrations used were 20 nM RNP (5 nM of each RNP), 100 nM reporter, and 1× reaction buffer (50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 100 µg/ml BSA, and 5% glycerol). Subsequently, the mixture was incubated at 37°C, and the fluorescence signal was monitored at 1 min intervals using a CFX Opus 96 RT-PCR system (Bio-Rad).

The reporter saturation assay was conducted in a total reaction volume of 30 µl, consisting of 6 µl each of RNP complex, target RNA, 5× reaction buffer, DEPC-W, and reporter probe. For reporter tests, the final concentrations used were 20 nM RNP, 40 nM target RNA, and 100 nM reporter. Reactions were carried out in 384-well microplates (Thermo Scientific™), and fluorescence measurements were recorded every minute for a total of 30 min using Infinite M200 Pro (Tecan) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm.

Kinetic assays were performed in a total reaction volume of 30 µl, consisting of 6 µl each of RNP complex, target RNA, 5× reaction buffer, DEPC-W, and reporter probe. For assays using ssDNA or RNA reporters, final concentrations of 500 pM RNP and 1 nM target RNA were used. For dsDNA reporters, final concentrations of 25 nM RNP and 50 nM target RNA were used. Reporter concentrations ranged from 1000 nM to 0 nM, with 10 serial 1/2 dilutions. Reactions were carried out in 384-well plates (Thermo Scientific™), and fluorescence measurements were taken every minute for 1 h using Infinite M200 Pro (Tecan) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm. The fluorescence data were then converted into reaction velocities by numerical differentiation. Selection of optimal crRNAs for SARS-CoV-2 detection using Cas12a2

The wild-type (WT) SARS-CoV-2 gRNA was employed in the optimization experiment. Amplification-free detection of SARS-CoV-2 using Cas12a2

To assess the sensitivity, the SARS-CoV-2 gRNA was serially diluted 10-fold. The diluted SARS-CoV-2 gRNA (1 µl) was combined with the other reaction components (9 µl), consisting of four kinds of RNP complex, reaction buffer, DEPC-W, and reporter probe. Diagnosis of COVID-19 using amplification-free Cas12a2 assay

A total of 277 clinical specimens (nasopharyngeal swabs) from individuals suspected of having SARS-CoV-2 infection were obtained from Gyeongsang National University College of Medicine and stored in universal transport media at −70°C. The viral RNA was extracted using the ExiPrep™ 96 Viral DNA/RNA Kit (Bioneer) with the Bioneer extractor system in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For the RNA extraction-free test, 5 µl of the clinical sample was mixed with 2 µl of 5× lysis buffer [500 mM Tris–HCl,pH 8, 25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 500 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP)] and 3 µl of DEPC-W, then the sample was reacted at 55°C for 5 min and 95°C for 5 min. The extracted RNA or lysed solution (1 µl) was combined with the reaction components (9 µl) and incubated following the aforementioned protocol. The fluorescence signal was measured using a CFX Opus 96 RT-PCR system (Bio-Rad) or the portable isothermal nucleic acid amplification device (Revosketch, Daejeon, Korea).

For the blinded evaluation, 40 de-identified clinical samples were newly obtained from Gyeongsang National University College of Medicine. Viral RNA was extracted following the same protocol used in the previous diagnostic assessments. The Cas12a2 assay was then performed on these samples, and the results were interpreted independently, without prior knowledge of the corresponding PCR outcomes. To facilitate a comparative analysis of this detection platform, a PCR was conducted using the Takara One-Step TB Green® PrimeScriptTM RT-PCR Kit II, in accordance with the protocol delineated by the manufacturer. In brief, the extracted RNA (2 µl) was combined with a reaction mixture (18 µl) comprising a gene-specific primer set (0.5 μM each), 1× One Step TB Green RT-PCR Buffer, and PrimeScript 1 Step Enzyme Mix. The samples were initially reverse transcribed for a period of 5 min at 42°C, after which they were amplified by means of a PCR procedure comprising 39 cycles. Each cycle consisted of a 5 s period at 95°C, followed by a 30 s period at 60°C. The fluorescence intensities of the samples were measured at each cycle using a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) based on MIQE guidelines [15]. The MIQE checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Results

Comprehensive analysis of Cas12a2 trans-cleavage activity

Cas12a2 is characterized by its strict dependence on PFS and its ability to indiscriminately degrade a variety of nucleic acid substrates (ssDNA, dsDNA, and RNA) upon target RNA-mediated activation (Fig. 1A) [14]. To investigate the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a2 in detail, we performed evaluations for each substrate under various reaction conditions. These evaluations provided insights into the substrate-specific catalytic activity of Cas12a2. Consequently, these evaluations enabled the optimization of biochemical conditions for Cas12a2 and the development of a highly sensitive, amplification-free RNA detection platform.

Initially, we investigated the PFS dependence of Cas12a2 by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis and found that only purine (adenine, guanine) PFS sequences can induce trans-cleavage activity, whereas pyrimidine (uracil) PFS sequences or the absence of PFS did not activate Cas12a2 (Fig. 1B and C). Subsequently, we quantitatively assessed the substrate-specific degradation activity of Cas12a2 under various conditions using fluorescence-based assays with FAM and quencher-labeled nucleic acid substrates. Initial observations indicated that pH exhibited a marginal effect on the substrate preference of Cas12a2, with ssDNA activity peaking at pH 7.5–8.0, dsDNA activity increasing up to pH 9.0, and RNA activity remaining stable over pH 7.0–9.0 (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1A). Furthermore, an analysis of ion strength demonstrated that Cas12a2 exhibited significant activity even at physiological salt concentrations (~154 mM NaCl), with a modest decline in cleavage efficiency at higher salt concentrations (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1B). In addition, an essential role of the Mg2+ ion in Cas12a2 activity was identified, with trans-cleavage activity saturating at Mg2+ concentrations above 5 mM (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S1C and D). The presence of the reducing agent DTT enhanced trans-cleavage activity for all substrates, especially RNA cleavage, suggesting the importance of maintaining reduced cysteine residues for optimal Cas12a2 function (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S2A). The supplementation of BSA led to a significant enhancement in Cas12a2 activity, with optimal cleavage observed at concentrations above 100 μg/ml (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. S2B). The temperature stability assay demonstrated robust trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a2 over a wide range (25°C–65°C) (Fig. 1I and Supplementary Fig. S2C). This broad thermal stability emphasizes the potential application of Cas12a2 in a variety of diagnostic settings.

Under the optimized Cas12a2 conditions (Supplementary Table S3), time-course analysis revealed rapid degradation of both ssDNA and RNA, with fluorescence signals reaching saturation within 3 min. In contrast, dsDNA exhibited slower reaction kinetics (Fig. 1J). The results of the kinetic analysis demonstrated that Cas12a2 exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for ssDNA (8.66 × 105 M−1s−1), followed by RNA (4.85 × 105 M−1s−1), and the lowest efficiency for dsDNA (1.64 × 104 M−1s−1). It is noteworthy that dsDNA exhibited the highest substrate affinity (Km = 61.01 nM), yet the lowest maximum reaction rate (Vmax = 0.033 nM/s) (Fig. 1K and L). These observations suggest that Cas12a2 exhibits a pronounced preference for single-stranded nucleic acids, particularly ssDNA, and imply potential structural constraints in processing dsDNA substrates. This distinctive substrate specificity profile offers insights into the unique molecular mechanism of Cas12a2 and its potential biotechnological applications.

Comparative analysis of Cas12a2 and Cas13a trans-cleavage activities and effect of target RNA mismatch on Cas12a2 trans-cleavage efficiency

To elucidate the functional distinctions between RNA-targeting CRISPR/Cas systems, a comparative analysis of Cas12a2 and Cas13a trans-cleavage activities was conducted. In this experiment, two target RNAs originating from the SARS-CoV-2 BA.1 and XBB.1.9.1 variants were used (Supplementary Table S1). Cas12a2 exhibited robust cleavage activity across various substrate types, including ssDNA, dsDNA, and RNA in both target RNAs, with the peak efficiencies observed in ssDNA and RNA (Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S3A). In contrast, Cas13a exhibited cleavage activity exclusively on RNA substrates when the same target RNAs were present, with negligible activity against DNA reporters (Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S3B). Intriguingly, Cas12a2 exhibited a stronger fluorescence signal than Cas13a for the same target RNA. This suggests that Cas12a2 may provide better sensitivity compared to Cas13a, which is the most widely used protein for CRISPR-based RNA detection [16]. The effect of PFS on the activity of these two enzymes was also analyzed (Fig. 2C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S3C). It was found that, in the presence of PFS, Cas12a2 exhibited a significant enhancement of trans-cleavage activity. In contrast, Cas13a activity showed minimal PFS dependence, with ~1.3-fold higher activity when the target RNA had a PFS compared to when it did not. Collectively, these observations offer insights into the selection of target RNA sequences and the type of substrates for the optimal Cas12a2 function.

Subsequently, a systematic examination was conducted to assess the tolerance of Cas12a2 to mismatches within its target sequence (Fig. 2E and F, and Supplementary Fig. S4). F value is the fluorescence intensity in the presence of the target RNA and F0 value is the fluorescence intensity in the absence of the target RNA. For target BA.1, Cas12a2 exhibited the highest F/F0 value for the ssDNA reporter when the crRNA exhibited a perfect match (PM) to the target sequence. More importantly, the decrease in fluorescence intensity due to sequence mismatch (MM) between the crRNA and target RNA was more pronounced closer to PFS, and trans-cleavage activity was completely suppressed in the MM4, MM5, and MM6 regions. When target XBB.1.9.1 was used, a similar pattern was observed. We further evaluated the mismatch tolerance of Cas13a using a setup identical to the Cas12a2 assay against XBB.1.9.1 (Supplementary Fig. S5). MM1–5 suppressed the trans-cleavage activity of Cas13a, while MM6 caused only a slight reduction. This pattern of suppression differs significantly from that of Cas12a2. These findings suggest that Cas13a lacks a PFS recognition mechanism and relies exclusively on direct crRNA–target RNA complementarity, particularly within the seed region. This highlights the importance of careful crRNA design for Cas-protein-based sequence-specific target recognition.

Amplification-free detection of SARS-CoV-2 using Cas12a2

Based on the optimized Cas12a2 reaction conditions, we tried to develop the amplification-free detection method for SARS-CoV-2 gRNA. Initially, the regions of the SARS-CoV-2 genome, where the PFS sequence of Cas12a2 is present, were identified. Then, 11 target regions were randomly selected (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we designed crRNAs for each target region and used the 11 Cas12a2/crRNAs to detect SARS-CoV-2 gRNA (100 pM), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6). Briefly, SARS-CoV-2 gRNA was mixed with Cas12a2/crRNA and ssDNA reporter probe, and the mixture was incubated under fluorescence monitoring. The results demonstrated that all Cas12a2/crRNA systems exhibited enhanced fluorescence signals in the presence of SARS-CoV-2 gRNA. However, it remained challenging to reliably detect picomolar levels of SARS-CoV-2 gRNA using a single type of Cas12a2/crRNA. Therefore, we decided to increase the sensitivity of viral RNA detection by incorporating multiple crRNAs (Fig. 3B). Previous literature has shown that multiple Cas/crRNA RNPs can bind to different regions within a single target RNA to increase detection sensitivity [17]. We adopted this strategy for amplification-free RNA detection using Cas12a2.

For the selection of optimal crRNAs for SARS-CoV-2 detection using Cas12a2, we meticulously investigated the combination of crRNAs. Initially, crRNAs 5 and 11 were excluded due to their weak fluorescence signal intensity and large standard deviation in RNA detection (Supplementary Fig. S7). The remaining nine crRNAs were then evaluated for their viral RNA detection performance by combining them in pairs (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Figs S8 and S9). The results demonstrated that combinations containing crRNA 6, 7, 9, or 10 exhibited high average fluorescence change (AVG F/F0) values in the presence (F) or absence (F0) of SARS-CoV-2 gRNA. Conversely, the combination containing crRNA 8 exhibited the lowest AVG F/F0 value, therefore, crRNA 8 was excluded from the subsequent optimization process. Next, we performed SARS-CoV-2 gRNA detection experiments with three combinations of eight crRNAs in a similar manner to the previous experiment (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Figs S10 and S11). Analogous to the double crRNA combination test result, the combination containing crRNAs 6, 7, 9, and 10 exhibited the most significant average fluorescence change between the presence and absence of RNA. The combinations containing crRNAs 1 and 2 demonstrated substantially lower AVG F/F0 values and were thus excluded from further testing.

With the exception of crRNAs 1, 2, 5, 8, and 11, the remaining six crRNAs were evaluated in combinations of 4, 5, and 6, respectively (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S12). The outcomes of the tests demonstrated the higher F/F0 values with five or six crRNA combinations than with four crRNA combinations. This phenomenon can be attributed to the activation of more Cas12a2 collateral cleavage with an increased number of crRNAs, resulting in an enhancement of the fluorescence signal [18–20]. However, the most effective combination for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA was found to be that consisting of only crRNAs 6, 7, 9, and 10. The high trans-cleavage efficiency of these crRNAs was consistently demonstrated in all tests conducted. Consequently, the decision was taken to proceed with the amplification-free detection of SARS-CoV-2 gRNA using these four crRNA combinations.

Lastly, a comparative analysis for all combinations of the selected crRNAs 6, 7, 9, and 10 was conducted (Supplementary Fig. S13). The highest F/F0 values were obtained when all four crRNAs were used simultaneously. Notably, while the single Cas12a2/crRNA allowed detection of up to 4 pM of viral RNA (Supplementary Fig. S14), the quadruplex Cas12a2/crRNA assay allowed detection of RNA at concentrations as low as 1 fM (Fig. 4A and B). To further explore the quantitative detection capability of the amplification-free Cas12a2 assay, we plotted the fluorescence signal as a logarithmic function of SARS-CoV-2 gRNA concentration (Fig. 4C). The resultant plot exhibited a linear relationship (R2 = 0.99), and the LOD was calculated to be 46.7 aM. This sensitivity is superior to the existing Cas13a-based RNA detection methods (Supplementary Table S4) [17, 19, 21–25]. The sensitivity of Cas12a2 assay is even comparable to the nucleic acid amplification combined Cas assays (Supplementary Table S5) [26–33].

To evaluate the versatility and specificity of the developed assay, experiments were performed with different SARS-CoV-2 variants (WT, beta, zeta, epsilon, eta, iota, gamma, kappa, delta, mu, BA.1.1, BA.2, XBB, BA.2.3.20, XBA.2.75.5, XAY.1, XBC, BN.1, XBB.1.5, CH.1.1, XBJ, XBF, XBK, CJ.1, XBB.1.9.1, and XBB.1.16), human coronaviruses (NL63, XOC43, 229E, and HKU1), influenza virus (H3N2), and MERS-CoV. As shown in Fig. 4D, all types of SARS-CoV-2 viruses showed high fluorescence signals, while other viruses showed weak fluorescence signals similar to the negative control. This suggests that the developed platform possesses the capability to selectively detect SARS-CoV-2 genes. A comparison was conducted between the amplification-free Cas12a2 assay and PCR for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 variants. A strong correlation was observed between fluorescence and Ct0–Ct values for all types of SARS-CoV-2 viruses (Supplementary Fig. S15). Ct0 represents the cycle threshold for NC and Ct denotes the cycle threshold for SARS-CoV-2. Overall, the Cas12a2 assay can detect SARS-CoV-2 variants versatilely but no other viruses, as the selected crRNA is designed to target conserved gene regions of SARS-CoV-2.

Diagnosis of COVID-19 using amplification-free Cas12a2 assay

An amplification-free Cas12a2 RNA assay was used to detect SARS-CoV-2 in clinical samples. Between the third quarter of 2022 and the fourth quarter of 2023, a total of 245 nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected, stored at −70°C, and applied to the assay (Fig. 5A). The Cas12a2 assay accurately identified the clinical samples as either positive (158 samples) or negative (87 samples) for SARS-CoV-2, proving its capacity for precise diagnosis of SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. 5B). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, a statistical tool used to assess the performance of diagnostic tests, revealed an area under the curve (AUC) value of 1.000, indicating the assay’s remarkable accuracy in identifying patient infection status (Fig. 5C). A comparison was made between the fluorescence intensity of the amplification-free Cas12a2 assay obtained for all clinical samples and the Ct value of RT-PCR (Fig. 5D). The resultant data revealed an inverse correlation between the fluorescence signal value and the Ct value. Considering that the Ct values increase as the amount of viral RNA decreases, this result proves that the Cas12a2-based assay exhibits a diagnostic performance comparable to that of RT-PCR, the gold standard for virus diagnosis [34]. In addition, a comparison was made of the diagnostic results according to the period at which the clinical samples were collected. As demonstrated in Fig. 5E, the diagnostic results obtained through RT-PCR in hospital and the results obtained through the Cas12a2 platform were 100% consistent, irrespective of the clinical samples collected period. However, a negative correlation was found between the average fluorescence signal and the collection time, with older samples exhibiting lower fluorescence signal values (Fig. 5F). As proven above (Fig. 4D), the fluorescence signal of the Cas12a2 assay remains stable across different viral variants. Therefore, the observed decrease in the fluorescence signal with prolonged storage duration can be attributed to sample degradation. Importantly, negative samples exhibited no time–dependent change in fluorescence, whereas positive samples archived for longer durations showed reduced signals. This pattern indicates loss of target RNA during extended storage rather than assay drift. To ensure accurate diagnosis of infectious diseases, it is imperative to perform diagnostic tests promptly after sample collection [35].

To further validate the clinical performance of the assay, an additional blinded evaluation was conducted using 40 de-identified clinical samples. The results demonstrated 100% concordance between the Cas12a2 assay and RT-PCR outcomes, indicating that the assay can deliver accurate and unbiased diagnostic results under blinded conditions (Supplementary Fig. S16). The Cas12a2-based RNA detection method has the potential to serve as a valuable diagnostic platform for infectious diseases, as it facilitates straightforward RNA detection without the need for gene amplification.

Discussion

This study establishes Cas12a2 as a transformative platform for direct RNA detection, underpinned by its molecular mechanisms and substrate versatility. The dual regulatory mechanism of Cas12a2, which combines crRNA-target complementarity with purine-rich PFS dependency, distinguishes it from other CRISPR systems, such as the PFS-independent RNA targeting of Cas13a [36] and the protospacer adjacent motif-dependent mechanisms of Cas9 [37] and Cas12a [38]. This distinct feature, coupled with robust enzymatic stability across a wide range of physiological conditions (pH 7.0–9.0, salt concentrations up to 250 mM NaCl, and temperatures from 25°C–65°C), could position Cas12a2 as an efficient tool for molecular diagnostics.

A major advancement in this study is the implementation of a quadruple-crRNA system, which achieves sub-attomolar sensitivity without the need for amplification steps. This represents a substantial improvement over single-crRNA approaches, achieving a sensitivity enhancement of up to 1000-fold. Notably, the experimental results demonstrated that sensitivity improved as the number of crRNA combinations increased from one to six, with the exception of the four specific crRNAs combination (6, 7, 9, and 10). Given that the only variable other than the number of crRNA is the sequence of the crRNA, it was hypothesized that improvement in sensitivity could be attributed to the change in thermodynamic properties caused by the difference in sequence. To verify this hypothesis, the GC content, melting temperature, Delta G, and the number of U bases in the entire target recognition sequence of each crRNA were examined. Upon comparison of these characteristics, no significant difference in the Delta G value was detected, however, higher values of GC content and melting temperature were observed in the four specific combinations employed (Supplementary Table S6). Furthermore, a comparison of the thermodynamic properties of more significant regions was deemed worthwhile. We hypothesized that the MM4, 5, and 6 sequences (Fig. 2) might also exert a substantial influence on enhancing sensitivity. To this end, we conducted a subsequent analysis of the thermodynamic properties of the 12 bases from the 3′ end of the crRNA corresponding to MM4, MM5, and MM6. The analysis revealed that the GC content and melting temperature were elevated in the optimal crRNA combination, and the number of U bases was notably high (Supplementary Table S7). While this finding alone does not provide a definitive explanation for the superiority of a specific combination of crRNAs, further research of the influence of U base and identifying the relevant interactions may establish the foundation that could enhance diagnostic performance.

Another key finding of this study is that Cas12a2 exhibits superior performance compared to Cas13a. Previous attempts to enhance detection without nucleic acid amplification, including Cas13a-based multiple crRNA targeting strategies [17, 22], have shown limited clinical utility. These methods consistently fail to detect samples with Ct values above 22 [17], underscoring the need for sensitive detection systems. In this context, the Cas12a2 platform addresses these limitations by offering high sensitivity, providing accurate diagnosis of COVID-19. Its superior performance was further substantiated through direct head-to-head comparisons with two Cas13a orthologs, LbuCas13a and LwaCas13a. For these assays, four newly designed crRNAs were employed, each targeting the same genomic regions as Cas12a2 crRNAs 6, 7, 9, and 10, respectively. Initially, three kinds of single crRNA approaches were compared with WT SARS-CoV-2 gRNA as the target. In all cases, the Cas12a2 system exhibited higher F/F0 fold changes relative to both Cas13a orthologs (Supplementary Fig. S17). Consistent results were obtained in the assays employing four crRNAs simultaneously, where the Cas12a2 platform demonstrated ~100-fold greater sensitivity than LwaCas13a and 10-fold greater sensitivity than LbuCas13a (Supplementary Fig. S18). Moreover, the strategic design of crRNAs targeting highly conserved genomic regions enabled versatile detection across 26 SARS-CoV-2 variants. Comprehensive clinical validation spanning Q3 2022 to Q4 2023 demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy, despite the emergence of 13 distinct viral variants in the period [39]. These features underscore its potential for widespread application in clinical diagnostics and further public health surveillance.

Statistical validation of clinical studies can establish the clinical utility of the diagnostic platform. In this regard, cross-validation was conducted using a modified version of the Python script. For this validation, the dataset comprising 245 clinical samples was partitioned into six folds, and ROC curves were generated for each fold as well as for the aggregated mean. The results showed an average AUC of 0.9949 for the mean ROC curve, while each individual fold yielded an AUC of 1.0000 (Supplementary Fig. S19). This slight discrepancy is attributable to interpolation steps employed during cross-validation, which allow for more precise estimation of the AUC. To further ensure statistical robustness of our assay, we assessed whether the clinical sample size was sufficient to substantiate claims of clinical utility. Sample adequacy was evaluated in accordance with relevant U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance for diagnostic accuracy studies and through prospective calculations using MedCalc (MedCalc Software Ltd.) [40, 41]. Notably, the 245 clinical samples analyzed in this study exceed both the minimum sample size of 120 recommended by the U.S. FDA for diagnostic evaluations and the required sample size of 95 calculated using conservative assumptions in the MedCalc statistical software (Type I error = 0.01, Type II error = 0.01, expected AUC = 0.75, null hypothesis AUC = 0.5, and a negative-to-positive sample ratio of 0.55). Collectively, these analyses highlight the potential of our assay for broad implementation in clinical diagnostics and public health surveillance.

In molecular diagnostics, a challenge lies in adapting assays for point-of-care testing by simplifying or integrating the entire detection process. To address this, we evaluated the potential of Cas12a2 assay to streamline the detection steps. First, we assessed the feasibility of bypassing nucleic acid extraction. We demonstrated that RNA can be directly detected from clinical samples pretreated with TCEP and EDTA, thereby eliminating the need for kit-based RNA extraction (Supplementary Fig. S20). Second, we investigated the applicability of the assay for use in portable diagnostic platforms. The results showed that the assay can be successfully integrated into portable devices for the detection of clinical samples (Supplementary Fig. S21). Overall, this Cas12a2-based approach simplifies diagnostic workflows, reduces the risk of contamination, and shortens detection times, making it well suited for point-of-care testing applications.

Despite the encouraging results, there are several aspects that require further investigation to maximize the diagnostic utility of Cas12a2. Further structural analysis of the Cas12a2/multi-crRNAs complexes may elucidate the molecular basis of the observed activation, which could pave the way for the rational design of crRNAs, tailored to optimize target site accessibility and enhance cleavage efficiency. Furthermore, the broad nuclease specificity of Cas12a2, which enables its use with both DNA and RNA substrates, presents opportunities for the development of various molecular diagnostic strategies.
