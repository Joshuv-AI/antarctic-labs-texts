Thalidomide memorial activity: pedagogies of creativity, joy and immersion in medical education
Authors: Anuradha Joshi, Dhruv Prajapati, Kuldeepsinh Parmar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough medical education has advanced rapidly over the past few decades, literature reveals lack of interest and curiosity of students in medical classrooms. Considering this, one of the major prerequisites in medical education is to, encourage the use of creative and novel pedagogies in medical curriculum.MethodsAn educational intervention study with a post-test only design was conducted at a medical school in Western India. The study involved 150 second-year MBBS students and utilized a student-centric “Thalidomide Memorial” activity. The intervention included the creation of multimodal physical and digital exhibits—such as plaques, 3D models, posters, collages, short films, and narratives—to teach drug safety and pharmacovigilance. Data were collected from 125 participants (83.3% response rate) using a validated feedback questionnaire (Kirkpatrick Level1) and a post-event knowledge test (Kirkpatrick Level 2).ResultsOn the basis of Kirkpatrick’s model level 1, student perceptions on the making of Thalidomide Memorial (Based on a 5-point Likert scale) revealed “Excellent” to very good ratings for most of the components i.e. Narratives (83.8%), Poster making (82.7%), History and Timeline of events (80.8%), Trigger film (80.8%), Newspaper clippings (80.8%), Clinical trial models (79.8%), Drug alert Posters (76.9%), and Thalidomide memorial plaques (75%). Kirkpatrick’s model level 2 evaluation on a 10-item post-event knowledge test indicated that the mean scores were 8.14 out of 10 with a median score of 8.0. Analysis of the individual scores exhibits that 94 students (75.2%) scored 8 or higher on the test. Analysis yielded an Odds Ratio (OR) of 6.41, indicating that students who rated the activity as “Excellent” were over six times more likely to be “Good Performers” (score 8–10) than those who rated it lower. Moreover, Cramer’s value denoted a moderate relationship between student performance and feedback for the overall event.ConclusionThus, this study demonstrates the first 2 levels of Kirkpatrick’s model displaying the perceptions and occurrence of learning and knowledge acquisition. This study also demonstrates the effective application of Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles—Engagement, Representation, and Expression—in medical education. Overall this creative approach helped students identify and understand key elements of drug safety and ethical issues in an interesting way. In addition, it helped build empathy and critical thinking. Creative teaching-learning methods being the need of hour, can transform medical education, making complex topics more relatable and engaging for medical learners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09058-1.Keywords: Creativity, Thalidomide, Medical education, Pedagogies, Universal design for learning (UDL)

Introduction

“There are no really safe biologically active drugs. There are only safe Physicians.” This profound observation by Harold A. Kaminetzsky sets the context for modern pharmacovigilance education. In a constantly advancing medical world and concerns regarding lack of engagement and curiosity in medical classrooms, one of the major pre-requisite in medical education is to practice creative and innovative pedagogies in the given curriculum [1]. Although traditionally didactic lectures have been informative and instruction based, current trends reveal lectures to be more of a monologue rather than a bimodal activity [2]. While continuous passivity during lectures hinders student participation and engagement, students report medical teaching as a boring and monotonous process [3, 4].

Moreover, lectures often miss immersing students in the process of building empathy, making them curious, out of box and critical thinkers during the teaching-learning process [5]. In context to this a few scholars and unconventional thinkers like Shri Jiddu Krishnamurthi, Shri Anil Pradhan, Sir Bertrand Russell etc. stress the importance of creativity and diverse pedagogies over rigid curricula [6–8]. Together, all these thinkers highlight the need for education to be a transformative and inspiring journey rather than a mechanical process. Need of the hour is introduction of creative pedagogical tools in higher education. In regard to this, department of Pharmacology at one of the western medical colleges in India conducted a student centric activity i.e. Making of “Thalidomide memorial”. Thalidomide was chosen for the activity as it was a landmark drug in the history of Pharmacovigilance. The current pedagogy was conceived to impart the knowledge of what, why and how of Thalidomide crisis and its role in Pharmacovigilance in a creative and an unconventional way, guided by the principles of inclusive and diverse learning. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to design, implement, and evaluate the effectiveness of this novel, creative pedagogy in teaching the concepts of drug safety, teratogenicity, and clinical trial regulations, while simultaneously assessing student satisfaction and its correlation with learning outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an educational intervention study utilizing a post-test only design to assess knowledge acquisition. The effectiveness of the intervention was evaluated using the first two levels of Kirkpatrick’s model: Level 1 (Reaction) and Level 2 (Learning). The Kirkpatrick Model is a four-level framework used in education to evaluate the effectiveness of sessions, assessing participant Reaction to the learning, the Learning that occurred, the Behavior changes, and the final results in terms of broader goals or impacts. By applying these levels, educators can gain insights into the quality of the session and make data-driven decisions to improve future learning experience.

Study site

The study was conducted at the Department of Pharmacology at Parul Institute of Medical Sciences and Research, a medical college in Western India.

Study population and participants

The study population consisted of batch of 150 second-year MBBS students. This specific cohort was selected because the given batch was an immediate recipient of Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) curriculum which emphasizes on shifting medical education from a teacher-centered, knowledge-based model to a learner-centered approach. The study employed a universal sampling method, where the entire batch of 150 students was invited to participate (census sampling). The final sample size for analysis was 125 students (83.3% participation rate), consisting of those who were present on the day of the activity, provided written informed consent, and completed both the feedback and the post-test. As no students withdrew from the session or submitted incomplete data, zero participants were excluded from the final analysis.

Data collection tools

Two primary tools, validated by subject experts, were used to collect data. First, to assess Kirkpatrick’s Level 1 (Reaction), a structured feedback questionnaire was administered using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Poor) to 5 (Excellent). This tool recorded student perceptions of the overall learning experience and the effectiveness of specific exhibits. Second, to assess Kirkpatrick’s Level 2 (Learning), a 10-item multiple-choice question (MCQ) post-event test was designed to measure the recall of factual information regarding the history of the tragedy, teratogenic mechanisms, and pharmacovigilance regulations. Due to the novelty of the specific historical details presented, which are not covered in standard textbooks, baseline knowledge was assumed to be minimal, and a pre-test was not conducted.

The educational intervention was considered successful if: (1) More than 75% of students achieved a score of ≥ 80% on the post-event knowledge test, and (2) More than 80% of students rated the overall activity as “Excellent” or “Very Good” on the feedback scale.

Study process

The study was executed in three distinct phases over a period of three months from March to May 2024. The first phase involved pre-execution and sensitization, where the activity was conceptualized by the lead faculty and a blueprint was prepared. A one-hour acclimatization session was conducted to orient the students to the background and objectives of the “Thalidomide Memorial,” and the batch was divided into eight groups supported by faculty mentors. The second phase was the intervention itself, a half-day student-centric event where students served as the primary creators. Under faculty facilitation, student sub-groups presented physical exhibits, such as the central ‘Thalidomide Memorial Plaque’ and 3D thermocol models demonstrating clinical trial phases. They also presented digital exhibits, including a student-scripted ‘Trigger Film’ to provoke discussion and a ‘Narrative Film’ documenting the lived experiences of survivors. Additionally, graphic exhibits like timelines, newspaper collages, and drug alert posters were displayed. The final phase involved post-execution analysis, where key reflections were compiled and the evaluation of learning and reaction was executed.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze demographic details and Likert scale feedback. The 10-item knowledge test scores were categorized into Good (8–10), Standard (4–7), and Poor (0–3). To determine the relationship between student satisfaction and learning outcomes, the Chi-square test was applied, and the strength of the association was measured using Cramer’s V. Additionally, an Odds Ratio (OR) was calculated to quantify the likelihood of high academic performance based on student satisfaction levels. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 125 out of 150 s-year medical students (83.3%) participated in the given activity. Out of this, 71 (56.8%) were females while 54 (43.2%) were males. Their age ranged between 18 and 22 years. 67 students (53.6%) belonged to CBSE Board while 58 students (46.4%) were from state board. The activity was evaluated using Kirkpatrick level wherein first 2 levels i.e.: Reaction (how participants felt about the training), Learning (what participants learned) were evaluated. Participants rated their overall learning experience and the effectiveness of each exhibits on a 5-point Likert scale (wherein 1 = Poor, 5 = Excellent). The learning experience for the entire activity received a mean score of 4.89 out of 5, with 92% of students rating the activity as “Excellent” (5/5). While overall the activity was well received by the students, the feedback indicated positive responses for the different pedagogical methods. While all the exhibits received mean scores above 4.65, indicating a uniformly positive perception among students, “Narratives of Phocomelia sufferers” was the highest-rated component, with a mean score of 4.85.

Event-wise participant experience

To objectively measure the learning achieved through the activity, a 10-item post-event knowledge test was administered. The results confirm that the event was highly effective in imparting key factual information.

The class achieved a mean score of 8.14 out of 10 with a median score of 8.0. The scores ranged from 4 to 10, and an analysis of the individual scores shows that 94 students (75.2%) scored 8 or higher on the test. This high level of performance indicates that such pedagogical approaches lead to significant knowledge acquisition. Detailed breakdown of the results of 10-item post-event test is presented in Fig. 2.

Results of 10-item post-event knowledge test

For the purpose of analysis, student performance on the 10-item knowledge test was categorized into three levels: “Good” (scores 8–10), “Standard” (scores 4–7), and “Poor” (scores 0–3). The results demonstrated a moderate to high level of recall and knowledge acquisition, with no students scoring in the “Poor” performance range. Therefore, for clarity and brevity, this empty category was omitted from the final table.

To quantify the strength of the association between student satisfaction and learning outcomes, an Odds Ratio (OR) was calculated. For this analysis, feedback ratings were collapsed into two categories: “Excellent” versus “Non-Excellent” (Very Good & Good) as shown in Tables 3 and 4. The analysis yielded an Odds Ratio of 6.41 (95% CI: 2.58–15.89). This indicates that students who rated the event as “Excellent” were over six times more likely to be “Good Performers” (scoring 8–10 on the knowledge test) compared to those who rated it lower. This strong statistical link reinforces the premise that high engagement and satisfaction in creative medical pedagogies are significant predictors of academic success.

Discussion

Activity 1. using thalidomide memorial plaques

In this study, the creation and display of ‘Thalidomide Memorial Plaques’ was a key component of the event, receiving a mean student rating of 4.66 out of 5, with 72.8% of participants rating it as “Excellent”, which in turn, is good. This highlights the power of using commemorative artifacts to bridge historical events with emotional understanding. In context to this one of the studies conducted by Shapiro et al. emphasizes on integrating arts and humanities to cultivate empathy—a core professional competency [9]. Another study by Krathwohl et al. on importance of affective domain in medical education states the use of memorials as a powerful tool for engaging students in the emotional and humanistic dimensions of a topic, not just the scientific facts [10]. Additionally such pedagogical approaches are supported by other educators like Ansbach and Jennifer, Marreez et al. who feel that engaging with memorials helps build empathy and critical thinking [11]. Prof Marreez & colleagues highlight ‘the role of medical museums in contemporary medical education’, wherein many medical institutes incorporate visits to pathology museums to provide students with a tangible connection to the history of disease and patient suffering [12]. Thus medical museums and memorials are described as powerful tools in contemporary education that provide students with a tangible connection to the historical, ethical, and humanistic dimensions of medicine—aspects that cannot be fully appreciated through textbooks alone.

While direct comparisons are difficult, the high rating achieved for the plaques in our study also aligns with the work by Monrouxe and Rees on humanizing a medical topic through a symbolic tribute, hence making the lesson more interesting and memorable than a textbook description alone [13].

In our study, the plaque served not just as a summary of facts, but as a focal point for remembrance and ethical reflection, reinforcing the idea that behind every medical error are real human lives. This finding suggests that even simple, student-created memorials can be a highly effective, low-cost method for instilling a sense of empathy and ethical responsibility in the next generation of health care professionals (Fig. 3).

Activity 2. poster making on history and timeline of events

The activity titled “Poster making on history and timeline of events” showcased all the important landmark events related to the drug in the form of different posters that were made by the students. This activity received a mean student rating of 4.80 out of 5 with 84% of the students rating it as “Excellent”. This shows that majority of the students found this mode of presentation effective and useful.

In context to this a study by Ismail N et al. states the importance of posters in teaching and learning. Through the academic poster competitions, students are given the opportunity to work in groups to produce posters as an added value to the knowledge gained during lecture sessions in class [14].

Also a study by Khatri et al. on using posters as a powerful educational tool stresses posters to be an innovative teaching and learning tool for both academic and soft skills. Almost 90% of study participants responded positively, agreeing to the statements about poster activity helping them to-hone their soft skills [15].

Another study on poster making by Tasleem S, Anjum V, Ali SI states that posters can be used as a novel, innovative, interest-generating teaching-learning tool to encourage students and fortify their learning [16].

All these studies as well as positive responses from the students highlights that using pedagogical tools like poster making serve as an effective and engaging learning medium and leave a long lasting effect on the students (Fig. 4).

Activity 3. Making a trigger film(audiovisual) on Thalidomide disaster

This activity received a mean student rating of 4.81 out of 5. While 84.8% students regarded the Trigger film making activity as excellent. Trigger films (TFs), also called trigger videos, are brief (3–10-minute) clips that are used to provoke reflection, stimulate discussion, help learners confront their feelings, and give learners practice in responding to challenges [17, 18] Our findings are in syn with a recent study by Khaliq et al. on using Trigger film in medical teachings wherein 82% of students found making and watching trigger films (TFs) to be an innovative and interesting tool for learning in medical education [19]. Furthermore TF’s fostered reflective attitudes and connected learning to emotive experiences. Overall TF’s act as a powerful stimulus for affective learning [20] (Figs. 5 and 6).

Activity 4. using collage of newspaper clippings

Activity on “Using collage of newspaper clippings” received a mean student rating of 4.79 out of 5 with 82.4% of the students regarding it as excellent. Majority believe that collage can make learning easier and should be widely promoted. As stated by Barron in their paper that collages can help in structuring, developing, analyzing, and presenting issues or experiences which might be difficult to express verbally or manually alone [21]. Other authors like Ramluggun et al. found that through the assembly of pictures along with visual expressions, collages foster self-expression and translation of thoughts and emotions into a visual medium [22]. Literature also reports that artistic approaches are recognized as valuable methods within health research especially as innovative means of both knowledge production and knowledge dissemination [23] (Fig. 7).

Activity 5. displaying posters for teratogenic drugs

The second activity “Displaying posters of teratogenic drugs,” was also well-received. This exhibit, earned a mean rating of 4.83 out of 5, with 85.6% of students rating it as “Excellent.” This finding underscores the value of active, visual learning methods in simplifying and reinforcing complex biomedical concepts. This active learning approach resonates with the work of Shapiro, Rucker, and Beck, who in their study found that using student- centric activities promotes a shift from surface memorization to deep learning [9]. Furthermore, the innovative use of fruits to symbolize fetal growth is a form of analogy-based learning. This technique aligns with the findings of Ramos et al. in their paper “Fables in Anatomy,” which highlights how analogy-based teaching can make difficult-to-visualize concepts—like the stages of fetal development—more concrete and relatable for learners. In this study 92% students agreed that such innovative teaching methods helped in remembering the topics better [4].

The high student ratings for this activity align with educational theories emphasizing the power of visual aids in teaching pharmacology and embryology. For example, a study by Pandey and Zimitat on medical students’ learning of anatomy concluded that visual tools can significantly improve students’ ability to recall complex information and understand dynamic processes [24]. By combining the creative act of poster making with a tangible, memorable analogy, this exhibit transformed the abstract concept of teratogenicity into a powerful and lasting lesson on the importance of medication safety during pregnancy. This suggests that such hands-on, visual pedagogies are not just engaging but are also highly effective for teaching foundational scientific principles, particularly in conceptually difficult subjects like pharmacology, an observation which also echoes in the work of Baños et al. in their work on “Beyond lectures and practical courses using imaginative pedagogical tools.” [5] (Fig. 8).

Activity 6. use of drug alerts

Drug alert activity received a mean student rating of 4.74 out of 5, while 78.4% rated it as excellent. As we are aware drug alerts play an important role in strengthening Pharmacovigilance activities, by helping both drug users and healthcare professionals about potential risks. Students engaging in ADR-reporting or alert-analysis tasks gain a vantage point of patient harm, system risk, alert/responsibility. Our findings resonate with a study conducted by Mohan et al. on usefulness of training of Pharmacovigilance for medical students which states that Medical and pharmacy students recognize alerts as important learning tools, suggesting that early exposure to drug-safety concepts is valuable for future practitioners [25].

Another study by Thota et al. documents the role of drug-safety alerts issued by NCC-PvPI (National Coordination Centre for the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI) in Pharmacovilance activities in India [26]. Such activities should be applied in hospitals & medical because most of the clinicians and medical students are unaware of how to access or interpret drug safety alerts (Fig. 9).

Activity 7. using narratives of phocomelia sufferers

The activity involving student-created narratives of phocomelia sufferers was the highest-rated component of the entire event, achieving a mean score of 4.85 out of 5, with 88% of students rating it as “Excellent.” This powerful response underscores the profound impact of narrative medicine—using patient stories to understand the human experience of illness—as a core pedagogical tool. The exhibit, which included compiling “Thalidomide Files” and a live, role-played portrayal of a patient, was exceptionally effective in cultivating empathy.

The foundational concept of narrative medicine, as described by pioneers like Charon (2001) in their works on narrative medicine emphasize the significance of a well-established discipline that helps healthcare professionals recognize, absorb, and be moved by the stories of illness, a practice that is critical for developing patient-centered care [27]. By researching and writing these narratives, students moved beyond the clinical signs of phocomelia to engage with the lived realities of the victims—their struggles, resilience, and emotional turmoil. This process aligns with Milota et al.’s systematic review on “narrative competence,” needed for building a strong therapeutic alliance with patients [28].

Furthermore, the inclusion of a student volunteer portraying a live patient introduced the element of role-playing, which Nestel and Tierney in their study “Role-play for medical students learning about communication”, have established as a powerful tool for developing communication skills and empathy. Here 96.5% students reported that role-play had been helpful for learning in their post-session questionnaire [29]. This method allows students to safely explore the patient’s perspective in a simulated environment, leading to a deeper and more personal understanding of the emotional and social consequences of a medical condition. The outstanding student feedback validates this approach as researchers Das Gupta and Charon impress upon the value of personal illness narratives, and voicing out-patient stories, which in turn connects them with the patients on a deeper level. This process of using personal illness narratives is a powerful method for transforming an abstract lesson into a formative ethical experience and for cultivating empathy [30] (Figs. 10 and 11).

Activity 8. constructing thermocol models and posters to display role of clinical trials

The final activity analyzed involved students constructing thermocol (polystyrene) models and posters to explain the phases and ethical importance of clinical trials. This hands-on, kinesthetic approach was also highly effective, earning a mean student rating of 4.78 out of 5, with 82.4% of participants rating it as “Excellent.” This result strongly supports the inclusion of tactile, model-making activities in medical curricula to demystify complex, abstract concepts.

Medical education has traditionally relied on didactic teaching, but kinesthetic learning—or learning by doing—is increasingly recognized for its ability to improve comprehension and long-term retention, a point emphasized by Graffam in a paper on implementing active learning strategies in medical education [31]. When students physically build a model representing concepts like Therapeutic Index, ED50, and LD50, they engage different cognitive pathways than when they simply read about them. This process of constructing a physical representation of an abstract idea, as described in a study by Pant et al. on exploring physiology through model preparation, forces a deeper level of engagement and clarifies the relationships between different variables. In this study, 35% students strongly agreed and 52% students agreed that model making was a useful exercise and helped them in understanding Physiology [32].

The effectiveness of 3D models as educational tools is well-documented, in study by Bui et al. on three-dimensional visualization modalities. The study confirms they are highly effective for improving the spatial understanding of complex structures compared to traditional 2D resources [33]. Our findings suggest this benefit extends to more theoretical subjects like pharmacology and clinical research ethics. The Thalidomide tragedy, which was a direct result of inadequate clinical trials, provided a powerful real-world context for this activity. By creating these models, students were not just learning the phases of a trial; they were actively internalizing the reasons why each phase is critically important for patient safety, a topic reviewed by Pathak et al. in their work on clinical trial phases in India [34]. This active, hands-on approach transforms abstract regulatory requirements into tangible, unforgettable lessons in medical ethics (Fig.12).

Universal design for learning (UDL) in medical education

While each of the eight activities described above functioned effectively on its own, their collective impact is best understood through the lens of inclusive pedagogy. When viewed holistically, the “Thalidomide Memorial” represents a practical application of the Universal Design for Learning (UDL) framework.

A significant pedagogical contribution of this study is its alignment with the Universal Design for Learning (UDL) framework. UDL is an educational approach based on research in learning theory and cognitive neuroscience that guides the development of flexible learning environments to accommodate individual learning differences. By moving beyond traditional didactic lectures, the “Thalidomide Memorial” activity instinctively adopted the three core principles of UDL:

Multiple Means of Engagement: UDL emphasizes tapping into learners’ interests to motivate them. The choice of the Thalidomide tragedy—an emotive and ethically charged topic—served as a powerful “hook.” As noted in our results, the use of “Narratives of Phocomelia sufferers” and memorial plaques engaged the students’ affective networks, fostering empathy and ethical reflection. The student-led nature of the event allowed learners to engage with the material in ways that felt personally meaningful, whether through artistic creation or scientific analysis.

The student-led nature of the event allowed learners to engage with the material in ways that felt personally meaningful, whether through artistic creation or scientific analysis.

Multiple Means of Representation: UDL calls for providing learners with various ways of acquiring information and knowledge. This study moved away from the “monologue” of traditional lectures to a multimodal approach. Students accessed information through a distinct combination of physical and digital exhibits, including historical archives (newspaper clippings), visual storytelling (digital trigger films), and tactile experiences (physical thermocol models of clinical trials). This variety ensured that information was accessible to students with different learning preferences, reinforcing the “what” of learning.

Multiple Means of Expression: Finally, UDL advocates for providing alternatives for demonstrating knowledge. Instead of assessing understanding solely through a written test, students expressed their comprehension through diverse creative outputs. Building 3D models to explain ED50/LD50, acting in role-plays, and designing drug alert posters allowed students to demonstrate the “how” of learning in ways that a standard exam could not capture.

Our application of UDL aligns with recent calls to integrate inclusive frameworks into health professions education [35, 36]. As noted by Sethuraman (2025), UDL serves as a practical vehicle for ‘Compassionate Pedagogy,’ ensuring that the learning environment proactively reduces stress and removes barriers for diverse learners [37]. By offering multiple means of engagement, we adhere to this principle of treating learners as whole persons.

The strong statistical association between student satisfaction and test performance (OR 6.41) suggests that when barriers to learning are reduced through such inclusive design, students not only enjoy the process more but also achieve higher learning outcomes. This theoretical alignment suggests that the success of the Thalidomide Memorial was not merely due to the novelty of the topic, but because the pedagogical design inherently catered to diverse cognitive and affective learning needs.

A limitation of this study is the absence of a pre-test, which restricts the ability to quantify the exact gain in knowledge. However, the high mean score of 8.14 out of 10 on the post-event knowledge test is particularly noteworthy and indicates significant knowledge acquisition, especially considering that the specific historical and regulatory details covered in this activity are not found in standard medical textbooks.
