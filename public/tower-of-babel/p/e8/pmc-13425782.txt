Symptoms of antenatal depression and associated factors: a multicenter cross-sectional analysis at primary care clinics in Mexico
Authors: Martín Paredes-Cruz, Ingrid Patricia Martínez Vega, Svetlana V. Doubova, Ana Lucia Rosado-Valenzuela, Alma Lucila Sauceda-Valenzuela, Diana Perez-Moran, Claudio Quinzaños-Fresnedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntenatal depression is a common mental disorder during pregnancy, affecting maternal and infant health. Research on this issue in Latin America is limited, particularly in Mexico. This study assessed symptoms of depression among pregnant Mexican women and identified associated factors.MethodsWe analyzed baseline data from 1390 pregnant women (ages 18–49) enrolled in an eCohort at the Mexican Social Security Institute. Depressive symptoms were identified using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), with a score of 8 or higher indicating the presence of symptoms. We used descriptive statistics to characterize pregnant women and a chi-square test to compare the characteristics of women with and without symptoms of depression. Multivariable Poisson regression was conducted to identify associated factors.ResultsThe study found that 6.4% of pregnant women attending their first antenatal care visit with their family physician presented symptoms of antenatal depression. The factors significantly associated with symptoms of antenatal depression were intimate partner psychological violence (adjusted Prevalence Ratio (aPR) = 3.08; 95% Confidence Intervals (CI): 1.96, 4.83; p < 0.001), a previous diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety (aPR = 3.63; 95%CI: 1.98, 6.66; p < 0.001), a medical history of pregestational noncommunicable chronic diseases (except depression) (aPR = 1.97; 95% CI: 1.29, 3.03; p = 0.002), and common pregnancy-related discomforts (aPR = 3.15; 95% CI: 1.45, 6.85; p = 0.004).ConclusionThe prevalence of antenatal depression symptoms among pregnant women was 6.4%, which is below global estimates. Associated factors included intimate partner psychological violence, pregestational chronic diseases, history of depression and/or anxiety, and common pregnancy-related discomfort. Integrating mental health screening and assessing factors linked to depressive symptoms into antenatal care is essential for supporting maternal mental health.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Depression during pregnancy is a mental health disorder that negatively affects a woman’s mood and daily life, including her self-care and the care of others, which implies a risk to her health and that of the baby. When women are left untreated, symptoms of depression can lead to preterm birth and maternal morbidity, including postpartum depression and suicide [1, 2]. In infants, maternal depression is associated with an increased risk of stillbirth, low birth weight, use of neonatal intensive care, and developmental delays [3–5].

Antenatal depression is one of the most common mental health disorders during pregnancy, with an overall prevalence ranging from 22% to 30%, depending on the country and the trimester of gestation evaluated, with a higher prevalence in South American countries in the third trimester of pregnancy [6]. Evidence from Latin America is limited; most studies have been conducted in Brazil and report a prevalence of antenatal depression varied between 11.4% and 73.6% [6, 7].

Antenatal depression is influenced by multiple risk factors. These include sociodemographic factors such as being a single mother [8, 9], experiencing domestic or intimate partner violence [9–11], low or no social support [8–12], low educational attainment [13, 14], risky health behaviors [13, 15], poverty, or economic hardship [8, 10–12]; medical factors, including a history of chronic diseases [16] and depression or anxiety before pregnancy [8–12, 14], complications from previous pregnancies [8, 9, 11, 14], unwanted pregnancy [8–10, 12, 14], pregnancy-related somatic symptoms [1, 17, 18], and finally, stressful situations, such as fear of childbirth, work demands or unemployment, natural disasters, or epidemics [1, 9, 11, 12].

In Mexico, research studies evaluating depression in the antenatal period are scarce [19]. They were carried out in specific places in the country and focused mainly on women without social security, in addition to evaluating limited risk factors. These studies reported a prevalence of depression between 27.1% and 39.7% in pregnant women attending a psychology clinic [20], a tertiary care hospital, or a gynecological specialty clinic for women [21]. The risk factors for depression they found were having previous depressive symptoms, lack of social support, and not having a partner [21]. Despite the high prevalence of depression identified in research studies, the Ministry of Health reported in 2023 that 75% of pregnant women with depression are undiagnosed and do not receive treatment [22].

Knowing the prevalence of depressive symptoms in pregnant women and its risk factors in a larger sample that includes women residing in several states of Mexico will allow a better understanding of the epidemiology of depression and identify subgroups of women with a higher probability of developing it. This information will inform the design and implementation of interventions to reduce the incidence and severity of antenatal depression, optimize its diagnosis and treatment, and consequently, improve the health of pregnant women and their babies.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to estimate the prevalence and factors associated with symptoms of depression in pregnant women in Mexico.

Materials and methods

Design

We performed a secondary analysis of baseline cross-sectional data from a maternal cohort at the Mexican Institute of Social Security (IMSS) [23], collected between August 10 and December 15, 2023.

The IMSS is the largest public health institution in Mexico. It provides medical care through an extensive national network of primary, secondary, and tertiary care facilities. In addition to medical services, IMSS offers economic and social benefits to workers in the formal labor market and their families. Currently, it serves approximately 77 million people, which is equivalent to 57.7% of the country´s total population [24].

Population

The study population included pregnant women between 18 and 49 years old who attended their first antenatal visit with their family physician at IMSS. Women with intellectual or hearing disabilities that prevented them from responding to the survey were not included.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the single-population proportion formula, based on an anticipated prevalence of 27% for antenatal depression, as reported in a meta-analysis of 88 studies across 48 countries [6]. A 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level were applied, resulting in a minimum sample size of approximately 505 participants. Furthermore, the criterion for multivariable regression analysis, which requires at least 10 participants per covariate [25], was considered. Given that the regression model includes 24 covariates, a minimum of 240 participants is required for this analysis.

Settings and sampling

Mexico comprises 32 states. To achieve broad geographic representation, eight states were selected across four regions (Western, North, Southeast, and Central), with two states per region. Within each region, states were chosen based on convenience, prioritizing those with a higher number of childbirths. The selected states included Aguascalientes and Jalisco in the Western region, Coahuila and Nuevo León in the North, Veracruz and Yucatán in the Southeast, and the State of Mexico and Mexico City in the Central region (Fig. 1). In each state, six IMSS family medicine clinics were selected according to size (two large, two mediums, and two small, based on the number of affiliates) and a high number of previously registered antenatal care visits. This sampling strategy aimed to include women from diverse sociodemographic and cultural backgrounds within Mexico, thereby enhancing the geographic representativeness and generalizability of the study results. The methodology used for the selection of states, clinics, and participating women is detailed in a previous publication [23].

Data source and study variables

The data analyzed in the present study were collected through a validated questionnaire [23] that included information on sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history, medical history, and symptoms of antenatal depression.

The main outcome variable of this study was the presence of symptoms of antenatal depression, which was measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) validated in Mexican Spanish [26]. This instrument consists of nine questions, each rated on a scale from 0 (never) to 3 (almost every day), resulting in a total score between 0 and 27. In the general population, PHQ-9 scores are typically categorized as follows: 0–4 (minimal), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), 15–19 (moderately severe), and 20–27 (severe). However, since certain items, such as sleep disturbances and fatigue may overlap with pregnancy symptoms and potentially overestimate depression, a pregnancy-specific cutoff is necessary. As no validated threshold exists for pregnant Mexican women, the Peruvian cutoff of 8 or higher was adopted, as it maximizes sensitivity and specificity for detecting depressive symptoms during pregnancy [27]. Consequently, a total PHQ-9 score of 8 or more was classified as indicative of depressive symptoms, while a score below 8 indicated their absence.

The independent variables were selected from a literature review with emphasis on the factors associated with the presence of symptoms of antenatal depression [8–18]. These included: (i) sociodemographic characteristics such as age (18–24, 25–34, and > 35 years), educational attainment (secondary or less, and high school or higher), occupation (with and without paid work), marital status (married/common-law union and single/widowed/divorced/separated) and region of residence (Western, North, Southeast, and Central). (ii) Health-related risky behaviors were assessed based on whether the woman reported smoking or consuming alcohol during pregnancy. A combined variable was created to indicate the presence of either of the two behaviors. Additional variables included (iii) pregnancy planning (categorized as planned or unplanned) and (iv) intimate partner violence, which was assessed using a violence scale validated in Mexico [28]. This instrument consists of 19 items that evaluate three domains: psychological, physical, and sexual violence. Each domain was treated as a separate variable and classified as ‘with violence’ if at least one affirmative response was recorded in the respective domain, or ‘without violence’ if none were reported. A general variable for intimate partner violence was defined as present if there was at least one affirmative response in any of the three domains [28]. (v) family/social support network, measured as the number of close people identified by the pregnant woman as a source of emotional, instrumental, or affective support during pregnancy. This variable was categorized as a small network if zero or one person was reported, and as extensive if two or more were reported; (vi) Medical history focused on chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, among others) and previous mental disorders (depression or anxiety), each classified as present or absent; (vii) obstetric history included parity (primigravid and multigravida), history of complications in previous pregnancies (obstetric hemorrhage, preterm delivery, fetal deaths, abortions), categorized as present or absent, and the trimester of pregnancy (first, second, or third trimester); (viii) physical discomfort during the current pregnancy (such as dizziness, nausea, vomiting, paresthesia, or low back pain) was classified into two categories: with and without discomfort.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the National Research Committee and the Research Ethics Committee of the IMSS (R-2022-785-064). Before participating in the study, all women signed an informed consent. Women with a score ≥ 5 on the PHQ-9 were referred to the second level of care to the psychology or psychiatry service.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize pregnant women. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute frequencies and percentages. For the numerical variable family/social support network, median with interquartile ranges was used since it did not have a normal distribution, determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Subsequently, the bivariate analysis was conducted, comparing the characteristics of women with and without symptoms of depression, using the chi-square test. Finally, we performed a multivariable Poisson regression analysis with robust variance to identify the factors associated with the presence of symptoms of antenatal depression. We used Poisson regression with robust variance because Poisson regression provides more stable, interpretable, and accurate estimates than logistic regression, as odds ratios may overestimate risk when outcomes are common. The robust variance option was used to control for any possible violation of the Poisson distribution assumption [29] and to correct Type I error, even in the case of the possibility of incorrect model specification [30]. The modeling strategy was based on the criteria proposed by VanderWeele [31], who recommend including all covariates with conceptual and clinical relevance to control minimal levels of confounding. In addition, we adjusted for the standard errors of the regression model considering the cluster sampling approach, using for this adjustment the primary care clinics where the women received their first antenatal visit. The results of the model were expressed as adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Statistically significant results were considered with a p < 0.05. The analysis was performed using the statistical software Stata version 14 (StataCorp LP; College Station, TX).

Results

Of the total of 1,390 pregnant women included in the analysis, 89 (6.4%) had symptoms of depression. Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, most were between 25 and 34 years old (52.8%), had a high school educational attainment (64.3%), and had paid work (61.0%). Similarly, about 82.1% were married or lived in a common-law union. No statistically significant differences were observed for these sociodemographic characteristics between those with and without symptoms of depression. At the same time, pregnant women with depressive symptoms had a higher prevalence of intimate partner violence (31.5%) than those without symptoms (8.1%), with psychological violence being most common. Most participants reported having an extended social support network, defined as two or more individuals providing emotional, instrumental, or affective support. However, there was no statistically significant difference in support networks between the two groups. Risky behaviors, such as alcohol and tobacco use, were almost similar between women with and without depressive symptoms. A higher prevalence of women with symptoms of depression was observed in western region (Table 1).

Table 2 presents the medical history and obstetric characteristics of the participants. Pregestational noncommunicable chronic diseases, including diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disorders, gastrointestinal conditions, and other diseases, were more prevalent among participants with symptoms of depression (31.5%) than among those without symptoms (13.0%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, 27.0% of women with symptoms of antenatal depression had a prior diagnosis of anxiety or depression, compared with 4.2% in the group without symptoms (p < 0.001).

Among the participants, 36.6% were primigravid, and 48.8% reported that their pregnancy was planned. These proportions did not differ significantly between groups with and without symptoms of antenatal depression. A higher proportion of women with symptoms of depression were in their first trimester of pregnancy (56.2%) compared with those without depression (45.1%) (p = 0.060). The incidence of previous obstetric complications, including hemorrhages, miscarriage or fetal death, and preterm deliveries, was similar between the two groups. Physical discomforts commonly associated with pregnancy, such as nausea, dizziness, paresthesia, and low back pain, were reported by 92.1% of women with depressive symptoms, in contrast to 71.9% of women without depressive symptoms (p < 0.001). Discussion

The present study found that 6.4% of pregnant women receiving care at IMSS presents symptoms of depression. The main factors associated with an increased likelihood of these symptoms were: experiencing psychological intimate partner violence, having a history of depression and/or anxiety, having pregestational chronic diseases, and experiencing common physical discomforts of pregnancy.

Depression among pregnant women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICS) represents a significant public health concern, characterized by high prevalence and adverse outcomes for both mother and child. The prevalence of depressive symptoms identified in the present study is lower than rates reported in countries with similar income levels in Latin America, including Brazil (17.4% for women with a history of depression and 15.3% for those without) [7] and Peru (9.9%) [27], as well as LMICs such as Pakistan (18.0%) [32] and Bangladesh (18.0%) [33]. Variations in prevalence may be attributable to differences in screening instruments or cut-off thresholds. Previous research in Mexico reported a 17.0% prevalence of depression during pregnancy [34, 35]; however, these studies focused on women without social security, a group that typically experiences greater socioeconomic vulnerability due to informal employment and lack of social protection.

Numerous studies have identified intimate partner violence as a significant factor associated with depressive symptoms during pregnancy [9–11]. In the present study, 31.5% of participants exhibiting depressive symptoms reported experiencing intimate partner violence during pregnancy. This prevalence exceeds that reported in high-income countries, including the United States (6.5%) [36], Spain (7.7%) [37], and Japan (15.9%) [38], but is comparable to findings from Latin America, where prevalence rates can reach up to 44% [39]. These results are also consistent with international literature from LMICs, which indicates that intimate partner violence during pregnancy is associated with depressive symptoms [39, 40] This association may reflect the psychological impact of violence, the disruption of support networks, and the activation of physiological mechanisms of chronic stress. Therefore, this finding supports the need to implement protocols for early detection of intimate partner violence during pregnancy [39, 40]. This could enable timely identification of at-risk women and the implementation of psychosocial interventions to prevent or mitigate depressive symptoms, ultimately reducing the negative impact on the mother’s physical and mental well-being. Validated tools such as the Woman Abuse Screening Tool are useful in antenatal care, especially when complemented with psychosocial interventions and the strengthening of support networks [41]. In addition, it is essential to train health personnel for the proper management of these cases and to develop information campaigns to raise awareness about this problem.

Currently in Mexico, the Ministry of Health, through the National Medical Care Protocol (PRONAM), recommends assessing the mood and the support network available to pregnant women in risky situations (e.g., teenage women) at each antenatal visit [42]. However, at IMSS, the comprehensive care protocols for pregnancy, childbirth, and puerperium address mental health exclusively in the postnatal period, leaving mental health unattended during pregnancy [43]. Consequently, the use of validated instruments is recommended in the IMSS as a screening strategy to facilitate the timely identification of intimate partner violence and emotional alterations. These interventions are aligned with the World Health Organization (WHO) Guideline for the Integration of Perinatal Mental Health into Maternal and Child Services, which emphasizes reducing childbirth-related risks and improving maternal health outcomes [44].

Having a diagnosis of noncommunicable chronic diseases prior to pregnancy, such as asthma, diabetes, thyroid and cardiovascular disease, was also associated with an increased likelihood of depressive symptoms during antenatal care. This association is supported by studies indicating that pregnant women with pre-existing medical conditions experience greater emotional challenges, due to the demands of self-care and concerns about potential pregnancy complications [16]. During antenatal care visits, it is recommended that women with pre-existing medical comorbidities be identified as a high-risk group. These women should receive interdisciplinary care that integrates family medicine, obstetrics, internal medicine, and psychology services. Implementing these services is expected to enhance the quality of antenatal care and prevent emotional and physical complications, thereby safeguarding maternal and fetal well-being.

Women with a history of depression and/or anxiety were more likely to experience depressive symptoms during pregnancy. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Spain, which reported that 15.6% of pregnant women with a history of depression and/or anxiety exhibited depressive symptoms, compared with 3.8% of those without such a history. Additionally, women with a history of anxiety had a 27.9% prevalence of depression, compared with 10.3% among those without previous anxiety [45]. Other studies further corroborate these results [8–12]. Consequently, it is essential that women with a history of depressive symptoms receive mental health screening, guidance, and care during pregnancy. Early detection and intervention support timely treatment and help prevent complications related to emotional health.

Consistent with other studies [17, 18], common physical discomforts during pregnancy, such as dizziness and nausea, were linked to a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms in pregnant women. This connection can be understood by recognizing that frequent and severe pregnancy discomforts can adversely impact a woman’s emotional well-being. Therefore, it is important for healthcare providers to address these pregnancy-related physical symptoms, especially when they are moderate to severe.

Although several studies have identified factors such as single motherhood, educational attainment, risky health behaviors, and pregnancy planning as being associated with depressive symptoms during pregnancy [8–14], in the present study, these factors did not demonstrate a significant relationship with the presence of depressive symptoms. Our findings are consistent with a study conducted in China, which also found no association between educational attainment or marital status and the likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms [46]. However, this does not imply that such factors are not of clinical or epidemiological importance, especially when analyzed in conjunction with other psychosocial variables.

This study has certain limitations that are important to note. First, all the data in the study was self-reported by the participants, which is subject to two possible biases: the memory bias and the social desirability bias. A second limitation concerns the PHQ-9. This questionnaire is self-reported, whereas structured psychiatric interviews are considered the “gold standard” for distinguishing between major depressive episodes and subclinical manifestations. While such interviews would have provided a more accurate diagnosis, their implementation is time-consuming and requires the presence of a psychiatrist, which was not feasible in our study. In addition, the PHQ-9 was not originally developed for use during pregnancy. Some items (particularly items on sleep disturbances, fatigue, and changes in appetite) may reflect common physiological symptoms of pregnancy rather than depressive symptomatology, potentially leading to an overestimation of prevalence. However, PHQ-9, is widely utilized among pregnant women, including pregnant women in Latin America [27], demonstrating strong psychometric properties [47]. Another limitation is the lack of a validated PHQ-9 cutoff for depression screening during pregnancy in Mexico. Existing studies use various cutoffs, usually between 5 and 10 [27, 48, 49]. In this study, a cutoff of ≥ 8, validated in pregnant women in Peru [27] was chosen because Peru and Mexico are Latin American countries; therefore, the Peruvian cutoff is likely more culturally and socio-economically appropriate for the Mexican context. However, this decision may affect comparability with studies employing different cutoffs to identify antenatal depressive symptoms.

Third, although low income is a recognized indicator of socioeconomic status associated with mental health, this variable was not assessed in the present study due to public reluctance to disclose income, as response to high levels of violence and extortion in Mexico. Instead, educational attainment was used as an alternative indicator of socioeconomic status, which is also widely used in the literature.

Finally, the study focused exclusively on pregnant women affiliated with the IMSS, thereby excluding those without such affiliation. This sampling criterion may limit the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, the sample is both large and diverse, as participants were recruited from 48 IMSS primary care clinics of varying sizes across eight Mexican states in four regions: Western, North, Southeast, and Central, with two states represented per region. This sampling strategy was designed to represent a broader population of pregnant women and enhancing the generalizability of the study’s findings.

Conclusion

This study found that approximately 6.4% (about 1 in 16 women) pregnant women who are treated at the IMSS presents depressive symptoms at their first antenatal visit and has factors associated with a higher probability of this condition, including being under 25 years, experiencing intimate partner violence, and having a history of chronic diseases and depression. These findings highlight the need for routine screening for depression and its risk factors during antenatal care, to detect it in time and provide the necessary psychological care, ensuring comprehensive maternal health services.
