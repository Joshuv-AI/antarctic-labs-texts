Introduction

Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of lung cancers, and around 30% of NSCLC cases are diagnosed at stage III (locally advanced disease) [1], [2]. Standard-of-care treatment for patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC begins with platinum-based chemoradiotherapy (CRT) [3]. For patients without disease progression and without specific epidermal growth factor receptor gene (EGFR) aberrations, CRT is followed by up to 12 months of consolidation treatment with durvalumab, a programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) inhibitor [3], [4]. This recommendation is based on the PACIFIC study (NCT02125461), which demonstrated significant improvements in progression-free survival (PFS; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.52; 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 0.42–0.65; p < 0.001) and overall survival (OS; HR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.53–0.87; p = 0.0025) for patients receiving consolidation durvalumab versus placebo after concurrent CRT (cCRT) [4], [5], [6]. Estimated 5-year PFS rates were 33.1% (95% CI: 28.0–38.2) with durvalumab versus 19.0% (95% CI: 13.6–25.2) with placebo, and estimated 5-year OS rates were 42.9% (95% CI: 38.2–47.4) versus 33.4% (95% CI: 27.3–39.6), respectively [4]. Durvalumab had a manageable safety profile, and patient-reported outcomes were comparable with placebo [5], [6], [7].

Though immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) like durvalumab are generally well tolerated, they are also associated with pneumonitis events [8]. Pneumonitis, or inflammation of the lungs, is the leading cause of treatment discontinuation and treatment-related mortality among patients receiving ICIs [9], [10]. One study estimated that 35% of fatal immune-related adverse events (AEs) among patients who received anti-PD-(L)1 therapies across indications were due to pneumonitis, and another demonstrated that, among patients with lung cancer receiving PD-(L)1-targeted therapy, those who experienced pneumonitis had a 134% increase in risk of death versus those who did not [10], [11]. Such observations are of particular concern for patients receiving durvalumab after cCRT because thoracic CRT can cause radiation pneumonitis (RP), and determining the cause of pneumonitis in patients receiving both CRT and ICIs is clinically challenging [12]. When pneumonitis occurs, guidelines recommend temporary (for grade 2) or permanent (for grade ≥ 3) ICI cessation and provision of corticosteroids; other immunosuppressants may be given if symptoms do not improve within 48–72 h [8], [13].

In PACIFIC, patients receiving durvalumab experienced higher rates of pneumonitis and treatment discontinuation due to pneumonitis (regardless of cause) than patients receiving placebo (33.9% vs 24.8% and 6.3% vs 4.3%, respectively) [5]. Improved knowledge of factors associated with pneumonitis may enable early identification of at-risk patients and corresponding treatment plan modification, but studies to this end have produced inconsistent results. Post-hoc analyses from PACIFIC identified treatment in Asia, stage IIIA (vs IIIB) disease, baseline Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 1 (vs 0), and lack of induction therapy as risk factors for grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis or RP [14]. Conversely, a meta-analysis of 23 real-world studies assessing durvalumab after CRT for stage III NSCLC identified lower age, longer time to durvalumab therapy after CRT, and PD-L1 expression on ≥ 50% of tumor cells as factors associated with lower incidence of any-grade pneumonitis [15]. Elsewhere, factors such as female sex (increased risk), current smoking (decreased risk), obesity (increased risk), and various chemo- and radiotherapy (RT) parameters have been linked to pneumonitis events [12], [16], [17].

PACIFIC-R (NCT03798535) is an ongoing real-world study evaluating the effectiveness and safety of durvalumab in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC enrolled in a durvalumab early access program (EAP). Previous analyses from PACIFIC-R demonstrated a median PFS of 21.7 (95% CI: 19.1–24.5) months and 3-year OS rate of 63.2% (95% CI: 60.3–65.9) [18], [19]. The PACIFIC regimen was well-tolerated, but discontinuations due to pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease in PACIFIC-R (9.5%) were more frequent than in the original PACIFIC study (6.3%) [5], [19]. We report exploratory analyses of RT patterns and factors associated with symptomatic pneumonitis (SP) in PACIFIC-R.

Materials and methods

Study design

PACIFIC-R is an ongoing, international, observational study of adult patients with unresectable, stage III NSCLC (per AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 7th or 8th edition) who received ≥ 1 dose of durvalumab (10 mg/kg intravenously every 2 weeks) within the EAP between September 2017 and December 2018 (earliest start date and latest closure date of country-specific EAP enrollment, respectively). Patients were retrospectively enrolled into PACIFIC-R from 9 European countries, Australia, and Israel within 3 months of exiting the EAP. The designs of PACIFIC-R and the EAP are published elsewhere [19]. The EAP provided early access to durvalumab for patients with unresectable, stage III NSCLC and no progression following platinum-based CRT who had unmet clinical need that their physician believed could not be met with commercially available treatments. PACIFIC-R comprises retrospective chart extractions from established medical records at prespecified intervals over five years from the index date for each patient, defined as the date of first durvalumab receipt within the EAP. This manuscript reports exploratory analyses based on the third PACIFIC-R chart extraction (conducted between September 2021 and November 2021), which was used for the first planned analysis of OS from PACIFIC-R [18].

PACIFIC-R was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines, guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice (GPP), and local legislation and institutional guidelines for non-interventional and/or observational studies. As previously described [18], [19], the protocol and all modifications were approved by relevant ethics committees and regulatory authorities; all patients provided informed consent regarding use of data from their medical records (per local regulations, where applicable). Patients meeting the analysis criteria who died before enrollment were eligible in countries allowing consent waivers or next-of-kin consent.

Objectives

The primary and secondary objectives of PACIFIC-R were described previously [18], [19]. The exploratory analyses reported here evaluated RT patterns and factors associated with development of SP during the durvalumab treatment period (for the collection of AEs, including SP, this was defined as up to 90 days following the last durvalumab infusion or until initiating subsequent therapy, whichever came first). For patients with grade recorded for at least one pneumonitis event, SP was defined as an event of grade ≥ 2 or one that required treatment with corticosteroids; for patients with grade missing for all recorded pneumonitis events, SP was defined as any pneumonitis event requiring treatment with corticosteroids. As PACIFIC-R retrospectively enrolled patients treated within the EAP, no specific adverse event grading system (e.g., National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events) nor management protocol was mandated; available details regarding pneumonitis grade and interventions used to manage pneumonitis were extracted directly from medical records. RT patterns were evaluated in terms of patient characteristics and country of enrollment. RT regimens were defined as 3D-CRT, IMRT, proton, or other. Reasons for durvalumab discontinuation and first subsequent therapy following durvalumab discontinuation were also captured for patients who experienced SP.

Statistical analysis

Hypothesized risk factors for SP included patient characteristics, disease characteristics, medical history, chemotherapy and RT characteristics (Table S1). To facilitate multivariable analysis, which required no missing values for the included data, the exploratory analyses presented here were conducted using the non-missing analysis set (NMAS), defined as all patients from the FAS of PACIFIC-R with no missing data for candidate risk factors for SP. Prior to identifying the NMAS, some candidate risk factors were removed from the analysis for reasons such as high missingness (>25%) in the full analysis set (FAS) or high correlation with another candidate variable, as summarized in Table S2. To identify potential biases in the NMAS, patient characteristics were compared between the NMAS and FAS.

Baseline, clinical, and RT-related characteristics were summarized and descriptively compared between the NMAS and FAS with an emphasis on effect size and clinical relevance of differences. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with development of SP. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were reported. A Cox proportional hazards model was used as a sensitivity analysis to calculate adjusted HRs and 95% CIs accounting for time from the index date to first occurrence of SP. In the absence of SP, patients were censored at the earliest of 90 days after durvalumab discontinuation, subsequent therapy initiation, death, or loss to follow-up. Dosimetric variables (e.g., mean lung RT dose) are right skewed and were therefore log transformed using a log(1 + x) transformation (base e) prior to analysis to reduce skewness in the data and improve model fit. All statistical analyses were carried out in R (v4.3.0).

Results

Patients

Of 1154 patients in the FAS at data cutoff for the third PACIFIC-R chart extraction (November 30, 2021), 268 were eligible for this analysis and included in the NMAS. Median duration of follow-up among patients who were censored at the time of the third chart extraction was 38.6 months (range 13.6–46.7) in the NMAS versus 38.7 months (range 13.6–49.0) in the FAS [18]. Total RT dose and type of RT received were the only patient characteristics that differed in prevalence by > 10% between the NMAS and FAS (Table 1).

In the NMAS, median age (range) was 65 (35–86) years; most patients were aged ≥ 65 years (53.4%), male (60.1%), and current/former smokers (93.7%). In addition, most patients had nonsquamous histology (63.1%) and Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) stage nodal status ≥ N2 (82.1%). Stage IIIA and IIIB/C disease were approximately equally represented among patients in the NMAS (51.1% and 48.9%, respectively), as were ECOG PS 0 and ≥ 1 (50.7% and 49.3%, respectively).

RT patterns and patient characteristics in the NMAS

Most patients in the NMAS received cCRT versus sequential CRT (86.6% vs 13.4%) and intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) versus three-dimensional conformal RT (3D-CRT; 66.8% vs 25.7%; Table 1). Concurrent IMRT was the most common RT regimen (55.2% of patients overall), followed by concurrent 3D-CRT (23.9%) and sequential IMRT (11.6%). RT regimens categorized as 'other' included photon (delivery technique not otherwise specified), rotational, and static beam therapy as well as cases for which RT regimen was unknown; additional technical aspects of these treatments are not available. All patients receiving RT defined as other than IMRT or 3D-CRT (n = 20; 7.5%) received concurrent treatment (Table 2). The total radiation dose was > 60 Gy in 69.0% of patients and the mean (standard deviation [SD]) interval from RT end to durvalumab start was 60.9 (29.8) days. Only 7.1% experienced CRT interruptions.

Compared with patients receiving 3D-CRT, those who received IMRT tended to be older (55.3% vs 46.4% aged ≥ 65 years), were more likely to have nonsquamous histology (65.4% vs 53.6%), had more advanced disease in terms of TNM-T (41.3% vs 24.6% T4), and had a larger mean (SD) tumor size (50.9 [26.0] vs 41.3 [22.0] mm). Patients who received IMRT also had a longer mean (SD) interval from RT end to durvalumab start (64 [32.3] days, vs 51 [20.6] days with 3D-CRT), and a lower proportion of patients receiving IMRT experienced CRT interruptions (3.9% vs 15.9% with 3D-CRT). A higher proportion of patients receiving 3D-CRT had ECOG PS ≥ 1 at EAP entry (60.9% vs 45.8% with IMRT).

Type of RT received by country

The 268 patients in the NMAS were from 10 countries (Fig. 1): France (25.7%), The Netherlands (22.4%), Australia (11.2%), Belgium (9.3%), Israel (9.0%), Norway (7.5%), Italy (6.0%), the United Kingdom (UK; 5.6%), Switzerland (1.9%), and Germany (1.5%). IMRT was the most common RT regimen in eight of the 10 countries (excepting Norway and France); the proportions of patients receiving IMRT in these countries ranged from 56.7% in Australia to 100% in Germany and Switzerland, though observations in Germany and Switzerland are limited by small patient numbers (n = 4 and 5, respectively). In Israel, 95.8% of patients received IMRT and 4.2% received 3D-CRT. Likewise, 93.3% of patients in The Netherlands received IMRT and 6.7% received 3D-CRT. France was the only country in which most patients received 3D-CRT (53.6%). Australia had the second highest rate of 3D-CRT use (40.0%). Interestingly, 60.0% of patients in Norway and 20.0% of patients in the UK received other forms of RT (i.e., photon [delivery technique not otherwise specified], rotational, static beam, or unknown); this number was much lower in other countries (0% to ≤ 4.3%).

Incidence of pneumonitis and factors associated with increased risk

SP, defined as a pneumonitis event with grade ≥ 2 or requiring treatment with corticosteroids, was reported in 52 of 268 patients (19.4%) in the NMAS during the durvalumab treatment period (up to 90 days following the last durvalumab infusion or until initiating subsequent therapy, whichever came first). Overall, 62 patients in the NMAS (23.1%) experienced any AE of pneumonitis during the durvalumab treatment period, with events of maximum grade 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 occurring in 11 (4.1%), 31 (11.6%), 10 (3.7%), 0, and 1 (0.4%) patient(s), respectively; 9 (3.4%) had events of unknown grade. Among patients experiencing pneumonitis of maximum grade 1 or of unknown grade, respectively, 3 (1.1%) and 7 (2.6%) required treatment with corticosteroids for at least 1 pneumonitis event. Multivariable logistic regression identified a higher mean lung RT dose (log transformed; OR [95% CI] 4.73 [1.66–15.07]) and prior chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; OR [95% CI] 2.60 [1.21–5.65]) as factors associated with a higher risk of SP (Fig. 2). Sensitivity analyses based on Cox proportional hazards regression analysis confirmed these findings (Fig. 3).

Reasons for durvalumab discontinuation in patients with symptomatic pneumonitis

Reasons for durvalumab discontinuation were available for all 52 patients in the NMAS who experienced SP (Table 3). Of the 52 patients with SP, 10 were reported to have completed treatment. The reasons for discontinuation for the 42 patients who did not complete treatment were AE (n = 31), followed disease progression (n = 9), death (n = 1), and other reasons (n = 1).

First subsequent therapy after durvalumab discontinuation in patients with symptomatic pneumonitis

In total, 25 of the 52 patients in the NMAS who experienced SP (48.1%) were reported to receive subsequent therapy after durvalumab discontinuation. First subsequent therapies were not mutually exclusive and included chemotherapy (n = 18), immunotherapy (n = 10), anti-angiogenics (n = 2), and targeted therapies (e.g., anti-EGFR therapy; n = 2). No patients in the NMAS who experienced SP restarted durvalumab as their first subsequent therapy.

Discussion

This exploratory analysis from PACIFIC-R evaluated RT patterns and factors associated with SP in a real-world cohort of patients with unresectable, stage III NSCLC receiving durvalumab after CRT. These outcomes expand on the limited data available for real-world RT patterns in NSCLC and conflicting literature on pneumonitis risk factors.

Consistent with the FAS, almost all patients in the NMAS received cCRT and most received IMRT. This may be expected, given that cCRT is associated with survival benefits versus sequential CRT and IMRT is considered superior to 3D-CRT due to its improved capacity to deliver high radiation doses to target sites while sparing other critical structures [20], [21]. However, randomized data on the benefits of IMRT are lacking, and not all patients receive IMRT due to limited availability (in terms of country and center) or patient and disease variables [22], [23]. Notably, IMRT may be preferred in patients with more advanced disease and larger tumors [22], [24], [25]. This idea is supported by the current analysis in which patients receiving IMRT tended to have more advanced disease than those receiving 3D-CRT in terms of mean (SD) tumor size (50.9 [26] vs 41.3 [22] mm3) and the proportion classified as TNM tumor stage T4 (41.3% vs 24.6%). The observation that patients receiving IMRT vs 3D-CRT on average had a longer interval from RT end to durvalumab start (mean [SD] of 64 [32.3] vs 51 [20.6] days) and were more likely to receive sequential CRT (17.3% vs 7.2%) may be linked to their larger tumors, if these patients were deemed less likely to tolerate cCRT and took longer to recover from RT than those with less advanced disease. Finally, varying levels of IMRT and 3D-CRT use were observed between the countries included in this analysis. Previous data on RT patterns for NSCLC in these countries are lacking, limiting further interpretation of these data.

Approximately 19.4% of patients in this analysis experienced SP, which is comparable to the 19.8% incidence of grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis or RP events observed in a post-hoc analysis of PACIFIC [14]. Unfortunately, comparisons between these analyses are limited by disparate definitions for pneumonitis of interest; the PACIFIC-R study could not mandate any particular pneumonitis grading criteria (adverse events reported in PACIFIC were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria version 4.03) and, additionally, the current analysis included ungraded events treated with corticosteroids but the previous analysis did not. The post-hoc PACIFIC analysis also highlighted an increased rate of grade ≥ 2 but not grade ≥ 3 pneumonitis events for patients receiving durvalumab versus placebo after CRT (19.8% vs 14.1% and 4.6% vs 4.7%, respectively) [14]. Likewise, a recent meta-analysis of > 24,500 patients across 185 studies demonstrated a significant association between receipt of durvalumab after CRT versus CRT alone and the occurrence of grade ≥ 2, but not grade ≥ 3, pneumonitis events [26]. Most pneumonitis events in the NMAS from PACIFIC-R were mild or moderate in severity, and previous findings suggest that the rate of grade ≥ 3 events would be comparable in patients receiving CRT alone; however, the lack of a CRT-only comparator arm is a limitation of PACIFIC-R.

Higher mean lung RT doses and prior COPD (but not emphysema) were identified in PACIFIC-R as factors associated with higher risk of SP. This was particularly true for higher mean lung RT doses, with an OR of 4.73 and HR of 3.28. This result is aligned with previous studies of pneumonitis following RT in NSCLC, which highlight total lung RT dose and lung dose-volume metrics as some of the leading parameters predisposing patients to grade ≥ 2 RP [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]. These observations are also consistent with results reported in four previous smaller cohort studies of 36–190 patients with stage III NSCLC receiving durvalumab after definitive CRT [32], [33], [34], [35]. Thus, the current findings reinforce the importance of radiotherapy planning in the management of patients in this setting. Notably, while previous studies suggest that IMRT is associated with lower rates of symptomatic (i.e., grade ≥ 2) and/or severe (i.e., grade ≥ 3) pneumonitis when compared with 3D-CRT [36], [37], [38], [39], radiotherapy type was not associated with differences in SP risk in PACIFIC-R. As dose-volume exposure metrics have been consistently associated with differences in pneumonitis risk [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [36], [37], [38], [39], it may be that dose-volume metrics account for the apparent differences in pneumonitis risk based on radiotherapy type in previous studies. This hypothesis is consistent with the observation that patients in PACIFIC-R had similar mean (SD) lung doses with IMRT versus 3D-CRT (2.7 [0.4] vs 2.8 [0.3] log Gy, respectively). However, such interpretation should be made with caution given the lack of more granular dose-volume metrics in the current analysis, and further investigation is warranted.

COPD is one of the most common comorbidities associated with NSCLC, but reports provide conflicting data regarding the relationship between prior COPD and pneumonitis risk [31], [40], [41]. While the current analysis identified an association between prior COPD and higher risk of SP, some studies have highlighted a protective effect, whereby existing COPD reduces the likelihood or severity of pneumonitis events [31], [40]. These conflicting results may be driven by differences in pneumonitis endpoint (grade ≥ 2 vs grade ≥ 3; presence/absence vs severity), treatment modality/setting, or patient population. Nevertheless, this conflict highlights the need for prospective research designed to evaluate the true relationship between COPD and SP in patients receiving durvalumab after CRT.

This analysis has limitations. First, reasons for choice of CRT regimen (e.g., patient age, frailty, and comorbidities) were not captured. Terminology used to classify RT type was imperfect due to inconsistencies across medical records; it is possible that RT described as ‘rotational’ comprised rotational IMRT, and a separate category for volumetric-modulated arc therapy was not included. In addition, some cases of SP may not have been included, as cases of unknown grade were only considered symptomatic if the patient received corticosteroids. Data were also missing for many variables, which led to a smaller NMAS than the FAS and some variables with high missingness being excluded from the analysis. Missing data are common in international, retrospective studies, such as PACIFIC-R, with data extracted from patient medical records; for an EAP, the level of information recorded in medical records varies by country and may be related to local EAP requirements. The reduced sample size of the NMAS relative to the FAS may partly explain the lack of association between variables associated with risk or severity of pneumonitis in other studies (e.g., CRT regimen [concurrent vs sequential], chemotherapeutic agents received [e.g., taxanes], disease stage, and history of smoking [12], [14], [16], [29], [31], [40]) and SP in PACIFIC-R, given the wide confidence intervals observed. In addition, though PD-L1 expression on ≥ 50% of tumor cells has been linked to pneumonitis events in patients receiving durvalumab after CRT [15], this variable was not included in the current analysis because values were missing for a high proportion of patients. Notably, PD-L1 expression on ≥ 1% of tumor cells was also associated with numeric improvements in PFS and OS in previous PACIFIC-R analyses [18], [19].

Conclusions

This analysis reports RT patterns and risk factors for SP in select patients from PACIFIC-R, and augments the comparatively small knowledge base for these factors among patients receiving consolidation durvalumab following definitive CRT. Most patients received cCRT and IMRT. The type of RT provided differed between countries. Patients who received IMRT (vs 3D-CRT) were more likely to have older age, nonsquamous histology, larger tumor size, and a longer interval from RT end to durvalumab start. Patients receiving 3D-CRT (vs IMRT) were more likely to have an ECOG PS ≥ 1. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses identified higher mean lung RT dose and prior COPD as factors significantly associated with SP. Further analyses of RT patterns and prospective studies of SP risk factors are needed.
