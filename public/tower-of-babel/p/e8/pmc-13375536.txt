Therapeutic inhibition of telomeric DNA damage response rescues hematopoietic dysfunction driven by telomere shortening and aging
Authors: Alessia Oppezzo, Sara Sepe, Giada Cicio, Valeria Cancila, Emanuele Sasso, Anastasia Conti, Eugenia Marinelli, Matteo Cabrini, Rani Pallavi, Nicola Iacomino, Matilde Simoni, Dmitrii Malyshev, Sara Boggio, Alessia di Lillo, Ilaria Rosso, Sara Tavella, Lucrezia A Trastus, Francesca Rossiello, Giulia Roscia, Paolo Cantaloni, Alessandro Manenti, Paola Cavalcante, Pier Giuseppe Pelicci, Raffaella Di Micco, Alfredo Nicosia, Claudio Tripodo, Fabrizio d’Adda di Fagagna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTelomeres progressively shorten and accumulate damage with aging, and this contributes to cellular senescence and hematopoietic dysfunction. We previously showed that telomere dysfunction induces synthesis of telomeric noncoding RNAs required for activation of the telomeric DNA damage response (tDDR), a driver of senescence and inflammation. However, whether the tDDR causally impairs hematopoiesis remained unclear. Here we show in telomerase-deficient Terc−/− mice, which recapitulate telomere-driven hematopoietic dysfunction and aging, that targeting telomeric noncoding RNAs with telomeric antisense oligonucleotides (tASO) suppresses tDDR in hematopoietic organs, reduces senescence and inflammation, alleviates hematopoietic dysfunction, and enhances hematopoietic stem cell fitness and repopulating potential in vivo. Similar observations were recapitulated in aged wild-type mice, and ex vivo treatment with tASO improved the function of human hematopoietic stem cells from aged donors. Taken together, our results identify tDDR as a pathogenic driver of hematopoietic decline and support tASO-mediated tDDR inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy for telomere biology disorders and age-associated hematopoietic aging.Subject terms: Haematological diseases, DNA damage response, Ageing

Main

Telomeres are at the ends of linear chromosomes, composed of TTAGGG DNA repeats and associated proteins. They protect chromosome ends and are essential for genomic stability and normal cellular functions, and thus for maintaining organismal health1. Owing to incomplete DNA replication and nucleolytic degradation, telomeres progressively shorten with each cell division2. When critically short, telomere ends are detected as DNA damage and trigger a telomeric DNA damage response (tDDR), a signaling cascade involving posttranslational protein modifications, such as phosphorylation of histone H2AX at serine 139 (known as γH2AX), which promotes recruitment of DDR factors including phosphorylated KRAB-associated protein 1 (pKAP1) at damaged sites3. Persistent tDDR drives cellular senescence and cell death4,5. The inability of senescent cells to proliferate impairs tissue regeneration, and their secretion of proinflammatory factors, collectively known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, promotes chronic, low-grade inflammation, disrupting the local microenvironment and eventually causing systemic frailty6. Thus, accumulation of senescent cells contributes to tissue dysfunction and is a hallmark of aging.

We previously reported7 that dysfunctional telomeres are transcribed from both strands, leading to local accumulation of telomeric noncoding RNA (tncRNAs) at these sites7. tncRNAs promote recruitment and activation of DDR factors in a sequence- and locus-specific manner by facilitating their liquid–liquid phase separation at damaged telomeres7–11. tncRNA sustain DDR signaling at critically short and dysfunctional telomeres, and their inhibition selectively suppresses tDDR without affecting DDR activation at nontelomeric lesions7. Sequence-specific telomeric antisense oligonucleotides (tASO), complementary to telomeric RNA transcripts, sterically block tncRNA function, disrupting RNA-dependent DDR assemblies and decoupling telomere dysfunction from downstream consequences, in both cells and mice7,12,13.

Telomere shortening and dysfunction strongly affect the hematopoietic system. Telomere erosion in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) drives exhaustion, impairing blood cell production and immune function, leading to bone marrow (BM) failure and hematologic malignancies. Thus, telomere-driven dysfunction is a major contributor to age-related hematopoietic insufficiency and immune decline14,15. Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are a diverse group of diseases caused by germline mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance. The consequent accelerated telomere shortening and dysfunction precipitates age-related pathological conditions15–18. Hematopoietic dysfunction, a hallmark of TBDs, is characterized by impaired HSPC function, decreased blood cell production and altered immune cell populations19. Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) recapitulates the core features of TBDs, with BM failure as its most severe complication20,21. Mutations in TERT and TERC, encoding two core components of the telomerase complex responsible for telomere elongation, are among the most frequently observed genetic alterations in TBDs, including in DC22–26. TBD mutations converge functionally by impairing telomerase activity or telomere integrity21,23. Notably, the pathogenic mechanisms underlying TBDs overlap with those contributing to age-related hematopoietic decline in otherwise healthy individuals. Although telomere shortening and dysfunction have been associated with impaired hematopoiesis, the contribution of persistent tDDR signaling to this decline and the feasibility and safety of targeting it therapeutically within the hematopoietic system have remained unknown.

To investigate this, we adopted the telomerase-deficient Terc−/− mouse model, which lacks the RNA component of telomerase and recapitulates key features of TBDs and of accelerated aging. In this model, progressive telomere shortening leads to phenotypes by the third generation (G3), including hematopoietic abnormalities such as lymphopenia, neutrophilia and impaired HSPC functionality27–31. To determine the causal contribution of tDDR to these phenotypes, we used tASO targeting tncRNAs to inhibit tDDR activation. In G3 Terc−/− mice, tASO systemic treatment significantly reduced tDDR signaling in hematopoietic tissues, attenuated senescence and inflammation, and improved immune composition and HSPC function. Importantly, this approach was well tolerated, with no adverse effects. Notably, tDDR inhibition also improved hematopoietic phenotypes in physiologically aged wild-type (WT) mice and in HSPCs of aged healthy humans, supporting a role for tDDR in normal aging. These results place tDDR as a central driver of hematopoietic decline, underscoring the potential of tASO to reverse key features of TBDs and age-related hematopoietic dysfunction.

Results

tASO effectively inhibits tDDR in the hematopoietic organs of Terc−/− mice

To determine whether tDDR was activated in the hematopoietic organs of Terc−/− mice and could be effectively inhibited by tASO, we treated young (2- to 3-month-old) G3 Terc−/− mice with intraperitoneal injections of tASO targeting either G-rich or C-rich telomeric RNA strands (anti-TeloG or anti-TeloC ASO, respectively) or a control ASO against an unrelated sequence, twice per week for 4 weeks. Two months after tASO treatment, BM cells and splenocytes were collected and analyzed (Fig. 1a). tASO efficiently reached the hematopoietic compartment, as demonstrated by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) on BM cells from WT mice treated with the same dosing regimen analyzed 4 h postadministration (Extended Data Fig. 1a).

γH2AX and pKAP1, two distinct markers of DDR activation, were analyzed by flow cytometry. Cell populations were defined based on the frequency of γH2AXhigh, pKAP1high and γH2AXhighpKAP1high double-positive cells, after validation of antibody performance using 4.5-Gy-irradiated mice as positive controls 1 h postirradiation (Extended Data Fig. 1b–d). Terc−/− control mice exhibited increased DDR activation compared to age-matched WT mice, and such activation was significantly reduced in tASO-treated Terc−/− mice, in BM (Fig. 1b) and splenocytes (Fig. 1c). Here and in subsequent experiments, unless otherwise specified, untreated and control ASO-treated Terc−/− mice, as well as anti-TeloG- or anti-TeloC ASO-treated mice, were pooled into the Terc−/− controls and tASO-treated groups, respectively, as no significant biological or statistical differences were observed within each pair. Western blot analyses overall confirmed a reduction in γH2AX levels in both BM (Extended Data Fig. 1e) and spleen (Extended Data Fig. 1f) extracts from tASO-treated animals compared to control ASO-treated ones. Consistently, quantification of γH2AX foci detected by immunofluorescence confirmed an elevated DNA damage burden in Terc−/− BM cells relative to WT mice, and a significant reduction following tASO treatment (Extended Data Fig. 1g). In addition, to investigate ATM and ATR involvement, we assessed pATM and pCHK1 in BM cells (Extended Data Fig. 1g) and observed that pATM levels were increased in Terc−/− mice and significantly reduced upon tASO treatment, whereas ATR activity appeared to be minimal and unaffected by tASO treatment.

To determine whether tASO specifically modulated DDR at telomeres, we quantified tDDR using immunoFISH, combining immunofluorescence detection of γH2AX with FISH with telomeric probes to identify telomere-dysfunction-induced foci (TIFs). Terc−/− control mice exhibited a significant increase in TIF frequency compared to WT mice, reflecting elevated tDDR (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1h). Notably, tASO treatment markedly reduced TIF numbers in Terc−/− mice, indicating that tASO effectively targeted tDDR. To confirm the specificity of tASO action toward tDDR only, we examined the cellular response to random DNA damage induced by ionizing radiation. TASO-treated mice displayed γH2AX and pKAP1 levels in hematopoietic tissues that were indistinguishable from those of untreated or control ASO-treated mice (Extended Data Fig. 1i,j), consistent with our previous work in cultured cells7 demonstrating tASO specificity for tDDR only.

Finally, to determine whether tASO-mediated suppression of tDDR affected telomere length, we quantified telomeric fluorescence intensity by telomere-FISH. As expected, Terc−/− mice showed a significant reduction in telomeric signal compared to WT controls, consistent with telomere shortening; no differences in telomeric fluorescence intensity were detected between control and tASO-treated Terc−/− mice (Fig. 1e), indicating that tASO inhibits tDDR independently of telomere length modulation. Together, these results demonstrate that tASO selectively attenuates tDDR without impairing global DDR activation or affecting telomere length.

Given the complex cell composition of hematopoietic organs, we tested whether the observed increase in DDR and its inhibition by tASO varied among different cell types. We used multiparametric flow cytometry to analyze composition and quantify DDR activation in different cell subsets in BM and spleen; the flow cytometric strategy is shown in Extended Data Fig. 2a,b. WT, control ASO- and tASO-treated Terc−/− mice did not differ with respect to percentages of total (B220+), pre- (B220+IgM−IgD−), immature (B220+IgM+IgD−) and recirculating (B220+IgM+IgD+) B cells in the BM (Extended Data Fig. 2c), or those of total (B220+CD19+), transitional (B220+CD19+IgM+IgD−) and mature (B220+CD19+IgM+IgD+) B cells in the spleen (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Similarly, no abnormalities were observed in the myeloid cell compartment, with comparable percentages among the three groups of myeloid cells (CD11b+), monocytes (CD11b+Ly-6C+) and granulocytes (CD11b+Ly-6G+) in the BM (Extended Data Fig. 2c), and of myeloid cells (CD11b+), monocytes (CD11b+Ly-6C+), granulocytes (CD11b+Ly-6G+) and dendritic cells (CD11b+CD11c+) in the spleen (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Overall, these results indicate that immune cell population distributions in these settings remained unchanged across conditions. Analysis of DDR in the same cell populations revealed a consistent pattern, with elevated DDR in Terc−/− control mice and DDR reduction in tASO-treated Terc−/− mice. The most affected populations were pre-B cells, immature B cells, and total myeloid cells and monocytes in the BM (Extended Data Fig. 3a), as well as total B, transitional B, mature B and dendritic cells in the spleen (Extended Data Fig. 3b).

tDDR inhibition by tASO treatment leads to reduced senescence burden and diminished inflammation in hematopoietic organs of Terc−/− mice

Persistent tDDR signaling is a key driver of cellular senescence, a process that contributes to aging and functional decline across multiple tissues, including the hematopoietic system4,32,33. p16INK4a, encoded by the Cdkn2A gene, is a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor that plays crucial roles in induction and maintenance of cellular senescence by blocking cell cycle progression and is a widely accepted cellular senescence marker. We therefore tested the impact of tDDR inhibition on cellular senescence by assessing the fraction of Cdkn2A/p16high cells by flow cytometry in the hematopoietic organs of Terc−/− mice. A gating strategy was established in young and aged WT mice and showed the expected increase in Cdkn2A/p16high cells with age (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). We observed a significant increase in the percentage of Cdkn2A/p16high cells in BM cells (Fig. 2a) and splenocytes (Fig. 2b) of Terc−/− controls compared to their WT counterparts. Importantly, tASO-treated Terc−/− mice showed a significant reduction in Cdkn2A/p16high cells in both tissues compared to Terc−/− controls. Analysis of the different cell subsets revealed a general trend across subtypes, with the most significant changes observed in myeloid cells in the BM (Fig. 2c) and B cells in the spleen (Fig. 2d).

As senescent cells secrete proinflammatory factors that contribute to tissue dysfunction, and persistent inflammation can reinforce senescence, creating a detrimental feedback loop34, we analyzed cytokine levels in the plasma and BM fluid of WT, Terc−/− control and tASO-treated Terc−/− mice (Fig. 2e). Among the cytokines examined, IL-3, IL-6, IFNγ and TNFα plasma levels were low among the groups tested, with a trend toward an increase in Terc−/− controls compared to WT mice and a significant reduction in the BM fluid of tASO-treated Terc−/− mice. IL-17A, a proinflammatory cytokine that has been implicated in BM myelopoiesis and age-related inflammation35,36, was instead significantly elevated in the plasma of Terc−/− controls compared to WT mice and significantly reduced upon tASO treatment.

These results indicate that tDDR inhibition by tASO treatment leads to reduced senescence burden and attenuation of some associated proinflammatory cytokines.

tDDR inhibition contrasts progressive immune system dysfunction of older Terc−/− mice

Telomerase-deficient mice recapitulate features of hematopoietic aging, including an altered immune cell composition with lymphoid-to-myeloid skewing27–31. To complement our flow-cytometry-based immunophenotypic analyses, we performed histological analyses of BM and spleen (Extended Data Fig. 5a) in young (5-month-old) Terc−/− mice and age-matched WT counterparts. In the BM of Terc−/− mice (Extended Data Fig. 5b), erythroid colonies showed a significant contraction, whereas segmented myeloid cells expanded slightly, with a more noticeable increase in immature myeloid cell expansion. Terc−/− mice exhibited marked pleiomorphism in megakaryocytes, showing considerable variability in shape and size, a feature suggestive of perturbed megakaryopoietic homeostasis. We observed no significant increase in megakaryocyte clustering, a sign of megakaryocytic hyperplasia, or in the occurrence of erythrophagocytic figures, a feature of ineffective hematopoiesis, in Terc−/− hosts. When these parameters were summed into an overall BM pathology score, a significant difference between WT and Terc−/− mice clearly emerged (Extended Data Fig. 5c). For the parameters analyzed in the spleen (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e), we did not find significant differences between the two groups; this is consistent with the secondary role of the spleen in hematopoiesis relative to the BM and suggests that the observed BM abnormalities may have been early or moderate enough not to require compensatory mechanisms involving the spleen. Taken together, these findings show that histopathological examination can identify aspects of hematopoietic pathology that can be unappreciated by flow-cytometry-based analyses.

Next, to determine whether the observed pathology would develop over time and whether tDDR inhibition by tASO would alleviate age-associated hematopoietic abnormalities, we extended our studies to older Terc−/− mice treated as in Fig. 1a and sacrificed at 12 months of age (Fig. 3a). Quantitative analyses of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained BM and spleen sections (Fig. 3b) revealed more severe pathological alterations in adult Terc−/− mice compared to age-matched WT controls. Specifically, adult Terc−/− mice displayed a significant reduction in erythroid colonies in their BM, accompanied by expansions of both segmented and immature myeloid populations, increased clustering and pleiomorphism of megakaryocytes, indicative of altered platelet turnover, as well as the presence of erythro-hemophagocytosis, suggesting an increase in the fraction of ineffective hematopoiesis. All these alterations were significantly ameliorated by tDDR inhibition (Fig. 3c). Similarly, the spleen of adult Terc−/− mice exhibited structural and functional abnormalities, including alteration of the ratio between white pulp and red pulp, indicating a contraction of lymphoid tissue secondary to increased red pulp hyperplasia, an increase in myeloid and erythroid precursors consistent with extramedullary hematopoiesis increase, megakaryocyte hyperplasia and clustering, and the presence of hemosiderin-laden macrophages, suggestive of increased hemocatheresis (Fig. 3d). tDDR inhibition by tASO treatment effectively reversed both white pulp effacement and red pulp hyperplasia, with a trend toward reduced levels of myeloid/erythroid precursors and hemosiderin-laden macrophages and minimal impact on megakaryocyte alterations. Overall, the total pathological score, calculated as the sum of the above parameters, revealed a clear and pronounced disease phenotype in Terc−/− control mice. tDDR inhibition via tASO treatment effectively reduced the severity of pathology in both BM (Fig. 3e) and spleen (Fig. 3f).

Importantly, when we evaluated the safety of long-term systemic tASO treatment in both young and adult cohorts, tASO-treated Terc−/− mice displayed significantly improved weight trajectories (Extended Data Fig. 6a), indicating no systemic toxicity and overall health improvement. Next, to determine whether these structural and compositional improvements were accompanied by enhanced immune function, we assessed humoral immune responses following antigenic challenge. Young Terc−/− mice treated with tASO were vaccinated with a SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine encoding the viral spike protein 2 months after tASO treatment and sacrificed 3 weeks later, at a time point at which tDDR suppression and senescence burden decrease are detectable but before overt age-associated immune remodeling has occurred (Fig. 4f). Serum IgG antibodies specific for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein were quantified as a functional readout of B cell responsiveness. Although young Terc−/− control mice mounted measurable vaccine-induced IgG responses, tASO-treated animals exhibited significantly higher anti-spike IgG levels compared to controls, indicating an enhanced humoral response upon antigenic challenge (Fig. 4g). These data demonstrate that tDDR inhibition improves immune functional output even at early stages, preceding the restoration of immune cell composition observed in adult mice.

Together, these results demonstrate that tASO treatment induces durable suppression of tDDR in vivo without altering telomere length, and that this translates into functional benefits across the lifespan, enhancing immune responsiveness and preserving hematopoietic tissue homeostasis and immune cell composition during aging in Terc−/− mice.

tDDR inhibition enhances Terc−/− HSPC fitness

Given the widespread impact of telomerase deficiency and tASO treatment, we tested whether these effects might arise from altered stem cell biology by examining the composition and functionality of the HSPC compartment, which is known to be compromised in telomerase-deficiency settings28,29.

Flow cytometric analysis of BM from young mice (using the gating strategy shown in Extended Data Fig. 8a,b) revealed no significant differences in the frequencies of the LIN−SCA1+cKIT+ (LSK) HSPC compartment among WT, Terc−/− controls and tASO-treated Terc−/− mice (Fig. 5a). Deeper characterization of stem and progenitor subsets revealed a modest increase in the long-term HSC (LT-HSC) compartment in tASO-treated mice, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs), without significant changes in downstream progenitor populations (multipotent progenitors (MPP), common lymphoid progenitors, common myeloid progenitors, granulocyte–monocyte progenitors and megakaryocyte–erythroid progenitors) (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 9a). Terc−/− control mice displayed a pronounced increase in DDR activation in the LSK population (Fig. 5c); this was significantly reduced by tASO treatment, restoring DDR levels to those observed in WT mice. Notably, LSK cells from Terc−/− control mice showed higher percentages of Cdkn2A/p16high cells (Fig. 5d), with reductions to WT levels following tASO treatment, consistent with the impact of the treatment in the bulk of the organ (Fig. 2a). Across all HSC and progenitor populations analyzed, tASO treatment consistently reduced DDR activation (Extended Data Fig. 9b) and senescence burden (Extended Data Fig. 9c), supporting the conclusion that inhibition of tDDR broadly improves the cellular state of the hematopoietic stem and progenitor hierarchy.

Disruptions in the balance between quiescence and proliferation can deplete stem cell pools and compromise hematopoiesis37. To investigate HSPC proliferative status, we analyzed the expression of Ki67, a marker of cell cycle progression (using the gating strategy shown in Extended Data Fig. 8b). Cells with low Ki67 levels are typically considered to be in a quiescent state, which is critical for long-term hematopoietic maintenance, whereas high Ki67 expression indicates active proliferation necessary for rapid response during hematopoietic stress or injury38. In Terc−/− control mice, we observed a marked increase in the proportion of Ki67high LSK cells, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in the Ki67low fraction, indicating a shift toward excessive proliferation; this is likely to reflect a compensatory activation of telomere-dysfunctional HSPCs attempting to sustain hematopoietic output under chronic damage, at the expense of increased replicative stress and stem cell fitness (Extended Data Fig. 9d). Notably, tDDR inhibition by tASO treatment restored the balance between quiescent and proliferating cells across the entire LSK compartment, indicating global normalization of HSPC cell cycle dynamics. We next investigated whether this effect was uniformly distributed across distinct stem and progenitor subsets within the LSK population. Subset-specific analysis (Fig. 5e) revealed that tASO treatment restored quiescence to WT levels in LT- and ST-HSCs, whereas no significant changes were observed in the more downstream MPP compartment. This selective effect indicates that tDDR inhibition preferentially stabilizes the most primitive, self-renewing stem cell populations, which are particularly sensitive to chronic tDDR; by contrast, MPP, which are intrinsically more proliferative and less reliant on long-term quiescence, seem largely unaffected. Together, these findings indicate that tASO treatment improves stem cell fitness by reinstating quiescence primarily within the core stem cell pool rather than altering lineage progression.

Given the marked effects of tASO treatment, we next evaluated the functional capacity of HSPCs by performing colony forming unit (CFU) assays, an established method for evaluating the fitness of these cells in culture, using the same BM samples from young mice analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 5a). We observed a significant reduction in colony-forming ability in Terc−/− mice compared to their WT counterparts, despite plating an equal number of BM cells, reflecting impaired HSPC functionality (Fig. 5f). Notably, tDDR inhibition by tASO treatment in vivo enhanced colony formation to levels comparable to those observed in WT mice. As the percentages of HSPCs remained unchanged in both control and tASO-treated Terc−/− mice (Fig. 5a), these results indicate that the impaired HSPC functionality in Terc−/− mice and its improvement upon tASO treatment are driven by changes in the functional capacity of the cells rather than their overall numbers. To assess whether these effects reflected a cell-intrinsic action of tASO on hematopoietic cells, independent of the in vivo microenvironment, we performed additional CFU assays under fully ex vivo conditions. BM cells isolated from Terc−/− mice were cultured in methylcellulose and treated directly with tASO in the absence of stromal support, systemic factors or inflammatory cues (Fig. 5g). Under these conditions, tASO treatment again significantly improved colony-forming capacity, demonstrating that tASO can exert a direct, cell-intrinsic effect on HSPCs.

Next, we extended our analyses to older (12-month-old) Terc−/− mice to determine whether the in vivo efficacy of tASO on HSPC functionality was sustained with age. In these older mice, the frequencies of LSK cells again did not show significant differences between the groups (Fig. 5h); however, CFU assays revealed a decrease in HSPC functionality in aged Terc−/− mice, together with a notable improvement following tASO treatment (Fig. 5i), consistent with the effects observed in younger mice. These results indicate that the beneficial effects of tASO treatment on HSPC function persist as animals age.

Collectively, these results reveal that tASO effectively target the HSPC compartment, inhibiting DDR, reducing cellular senescence and restoring the balance between quiescence and proliferation, thereby improving HSPC fitness.

tDDR inhibition improves in vivo HSPC reconstitution ability of Terc−/− mice

Having observed improvements in HSPC parameters and ex vivo fitness, we next considered whether these cells could compete with healthy stem cells in vivo. Assessment in vivo is critical to determine how HSPCs perform within their native environment, especially under physiological competition. To model this, we used a competitive BM transplant assay to evaluate the functionality of HSPCs based on their ability to repopulate the hematopoietic system in direct competition with WT donor cells. In this assay, two distinct BM cell populations, typically from different genetic backgrounds or treatments, are cotransplanted into a lethally irradiated mouse recipient. The relative contribution of each donor to hematopoiesis over time indicates its fitness, self-renewal capacity and overall performance in the hematopoietic niche39. Telomerase-deficient HSPCs have previously been shown to exhibit reduced repopulating capacity in transplant settings28,29. We therefore tested whether this defect in Terc−/− HSPCs could be improved by tDDR inhibition using tASO treatment (Fig. 6a). For this analysis, we used two distinct BM donor populations that were distinguishable by their isoforms of CD45, a surface marker used to track hematopoietic cell origin: CD45.2 BM donor cells sourced from either WT or untreated, control ASO-treated, anti-TeloG-treated or anti-TeloC-treated Terc−/− mice (the same mice whose HSPC fitness was analyzed in vitro by CFU assay, in Fig. 5f); and competitor CD45.1 WT BM donor cells. All donor mice (CD45.2 and CD45.1) were age-matched (5 months of age) at sacrifice. The BM mixed cell populations were injected in a 1:1 ratio intravenously into lethally irradiated CD45.1 and CD45.2 recipient mice for assessment of the reconstitution ability of the transplanted cells. The percentage of reconstitution (percentage of CD45.2+ cells) was monitored in the peripheral blood cells of the recipient mice over 16 weeks, with assessments every 4 weeks posttransplant (using the gating strategy shown in Extended Data Fig. 10a), culminating in the analysis of LSK reconstitution in the BM at the endpoint (gating strategy in Extended Data Fig. 10b). As expected, we observed a decreased reconstitution ability of BM from control Terc−/− mice, with untreated and control ASO-treated Terc−/− mice showing comparable reconstitution ability and therefore being treated as a single group, compared to their WT counterpart in both peripheral blood (Fig. 6b) and the LSK compartment (Fig. 6c) of recipient mice. Notably, BM from anti-TeloG-treated mice showed an improved reconstitution ability, similar to that of WT mice, in both peripheral blood (Fig. 6b) and LSK cells (Fig. 6c). Consistent with enhanced HSPC fitness, anti-TeloG-treated donor cells contributed robustly to all major hematopoietic lineages following transplantation, including B cells (Fig. 6d), T cells (Fig. 6e) and myeloid cells (Fig. 6f), indicating preserved multilineage differentiation capacity. These results demonstrate that anti-TeloG tASO treatment functionally enhances HSPC competence and competitive repopulating ability in vivo.

We first assessed DDR in the BM by flow cytometry. Consistent with aging-associated hematopoietic stress, aged WT mice exhibited increased frequency of γH2AXhigh, pKAP1high and γH2AXhighpKAP1high cells compared to young controls (Fig. 7b). tASO-treatments induced a trend toward decreased frequencies of these populations, similar to that observed in Terc−/− mice (Fig. 1b). When we examined cellular senescence in total BM by quantifying Cdkn2a/p16 mRNA expression, aged WT mice displayed a robust increase in p16 expression relative to young animals, and tASO treatment led to a consistent and significant reduction in p16 levels, indicating a marked decrease in senescence burden (Fig. 7c). Given the central role of HSPCs in age-related hematopoietic decline, we analyzed DDR activation within this compartment. Although the frequency of LSK cells was comparable across groups (Fig. 7d), tASO treatment induced a clear reduction in DDR markers within aged WT LSK cells (Fig. 7e), demonstrating effective DDR inhibition in HSPCs by tASO in physiologically aged mice.

We next evaluated whether this molecular improvement translated into enhanced functional capacity. The same BM samples from young and aged WT mice (Fig. 7b–e) were plated at equal cell numbers for CFU assays. As expected, BM from aged WT control mice exhibited a pronounced reduction in colony-forming ability compared to that from young WT controls, reflecting age-associated impairment in HSPC functionality (Fig. 7f). Notably, tASO-treated aged WT mice showed a strong and significant improvement in colony formation. As the frequency of LSK cells remained unchanged across aged control and tASO-treated groups (Fig. 7d), these results indicate that the age-related decline in progenitor activity and its rescue upon tASO treatment are driven by changes in functional capacity rather than differences in HSPC abundance, consistent with observations in Terc−/− mice (Fig. 5a,f).

Finally, to determine whether the impact of tASO treatment extended to immune competence, we immunized aged WT mice with an mRNA-based SARS-CoV-2 vaccine following the protocol used for Terc−/− cohorts (Fig. 7a). As expected, aged WT control mice exhibited a marked reduction in spike-specific IgG responses compared to young WT mice, confirming age-associated immune dysfunction (Fig. 7g). Notably, when aged cohorts were compared, tASO-treated aged mice displayed increased spike-specific IgG levels relative to age-matched untreated controls, indicating improved humoral immune responsiveness upon tASO treatment.

Finally, we explored the impact of tDDR inhibition by tASO in the context of human aging. We treated purified human CD34+ HSPCs isolated from the BM of two aged healthy male individuals (aged 75 and 60 years) with tASO ex vivo and monitored its impact in CFU assays (Fig. 7h). Although it was not possible to perform statistical analysis with only two biological replicates, we observed that tASO treatment increased clonogenic potential, with enhanced formation of both erythroid and myeloid colonies compared to control samples, pointing to improved hematopoietic progenitor function in human settings and in normal aging.

Together, these findings demonstrate that tDDR activation in aged WT mice affects their hematopoietic system, indicating that tDDR is relevant not only in telomere-defective but also in normal, physiological aging, and suggesting that molecular, cellular and functional features observed in treated Terc−/− animals, including reduced senescence, attenuated DDR in HSPC and improved HSPC function, occur in normal aging too. Therefore, the beneficial effects of tASO are not restricted to genetic telomerase deficiency but extend to both murine and human physiological aging, supporting the relevance of tDDR targeting in counteracting age-related hematopoietic decline.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether tDDR has a causative role in hematopoietic decline in the context of telomere dysfunction, using late-generation Terc−/− mice. Building on our previous observations that tncRNAs mediate recruitment of DDR factors to dysfunctional telomeres, we tested whether their inhibition alleviated tDDR-driven pathologies. Using sequence-specific tASO in vivo, we demonstrated that targeting tncRNAs effectively suppresses tDDR signaling and improves hematopoietic function without elongating telomeres, decoupling telomere structure from function. These findings are relevant to TBDs16,21,22 and physiological aging, in which telomere dysfunction contributes to hematopoietic decline40, and highlight the therapeutic potential of tDDR inhibition.

Importantly, we observed that tASO selectively attenuated tDDR while preserving canonical genome-wide DDR pathways, consistent with previous results obtained in animal models12. Such selective uncoupling of telomeric and genome-wide DDR, together with the lack of telomere length changes, provides a mechanistic basis for the efficacy and in vivo tolerability of tASO treatment.

The link between telomere shortening, the resulting persistent DDR and the consequent cellular senescence establishment have been well documented4,5,41,42. Consistent with previous findings in other telomerase-deficient mouse models43–45, we showed that G3 Terc−/− mice exhibit increased levels of DDR markers γH2AX and pKAP1 and a higher senescence burden, as indicated by increased frequencies of Cdkn2A/p16high cells, in hematopoietic organs. Notably, tASO reduced both DDR and senescence burden. In addition, as senescent cells release inflammatory cytokines that exacerbate telomere dysfunction through a positive feedback loop46,47, the observed reduction in inflammatory cytokines may further alleviate telomere dysfunction and senescence.

Our study extends previous work27–31 demonstrating that Terc−/− mice exhibit a disrupted hematopoietic homeostasis, with altered cellular composition and decreased stem cell function. By adulthood, Terc−/− mice showed clear signs of hematopoietic dysfunction, with decreased B cell levels, increased myeloid populations and megakaryocytes in both BM and spleen, and a reduction in erythroid progenitors in BM, as well as the presence of extramedullary hematopoiesis in the spleen. Collectively, these changes reflect acceleration of processes that are typical of physiological aging, highlighting the relevance of this model not only to TBDs but also to better understanding of age-related hematopoietic decline. Notably, tASO treatment restored hematopoietic architecture and normalized cell composition in adult mice. Although these findings support a causal role for tDDR in hematopoietic dysfunction, we could not exclude the possibility that additional indirect effects (for example, reduced inflammation) also contributed to the improved phenotypes. Beyond effects on immune cell composition, tASO treatment improved immune function; the enhanced antigen-specific antibody responses following vaccination indicated improved B cell responsiveness, even before overt immune remodeling.

Consistent with their broad effects on multiple cell subsets, tASO treatment improved the functional state of the stem cell compartment by promoting HSPC quiescence and enhancing their colony-forming ability in vitro, as well as improving in vivo reconstitution ability in a competitive BM transplant assay. Analysis of the HSPC compartment indicated that the benefits of tASO treatment affect the most primitive stem cell populations. The selective restoration of quiescence and reduced DDR and senescence in LT-HSCs and ST-HSCs suggests that tDDR inhibition preferentially stabilizes the self-renewing stem cell pool.

In Terc−/− mice, telomere attrition led to HSPC exhaustion and limited hematopoiesis. By inhibiting tDDR, tASO restore HSPC function, preserving the stem cell pool and potentially counteracting the decline in hematopoietic fitness caused by telomere dysfunction21. This is particularly relevant for DC, in which early-onset, progressive BM failure affects up to 80% of patients by the age of 30 years, and for which the only curative treatment currently is allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which carries significant risks21. The protective effects of tASO treatment extended beyond telomerase-deficient models to physiological aging. In aged WT mice, reduced DDR activation and senescence in the BM were accompanied by improved hematopoietic and immune function, despite unchanged HSPC frequencies. These parallel responses support a causal role of tDDR in age-related hematopoietic decline.

Consistent with our previous reports7,12,13, both anti-TeloG and anti-TeloC ASO effectively inhibited tDDR, senescence and inflammation, restoring tissue homeostasis and improving HSPC fitness in vitro. However, only anti-TeloG showed a significant ability to support long-term in vivo reconstitution in competitive BM transplantation assays. A similar pattern was observed in a previous in vivo study in a progeric mouse model12, in which both types of ASO suppressed tDDR, yet only anti-TeloG conferred a robust organismal benefit. In the present study, although anti-TeloG and anti-TeloC displayed comparable efficacy across most molecular readouts, subtle trends toward improved hematopoietic parameters were observed in anti-TeloG-treated mice at late time points (Figs. 3e and 4e and Extended Data Fig. 6d), suggesting that anti-TeloG may confer a slightly more durable benefit under conditions of increased hematopoietic stress and providing a plausible explanation in settings of competitive BM transplant, which represent the most challenging functional assay for HSPCs.

The events that we have characterized in Terc−/− mice mirror those observed in TBDs, DC and physiological aging, including telomere shortening, altered hematopoietic architecture and HSPC exhaustion. These shared features underscore the broader relevance of our findings beyond the Terc−/− model, positioning them within the spectrum of human telomere-driven pathologies and age-related tissue decline. Recently, various strategies have been explored to improve hematopoietic function in these contexts, including telomerase-based therapies and other methods aimed at extending telomere length or restoring telomerase activity48–51 and metabolic and pharmacological interventions to rejuvenate the aging hematopoietic system52–55. However, some of these approaches have limitations. TERT-based therapies may be ineffective in individuals with mutations in telomerase components such as TERC or other genes essential for telomerase function. Pharmacological strategies that broadly target metabolism, senescence or inflammation may lack specificity for telomere-driven dysfunction. A key advantage of tASO-based therapy is its ability to specifically target tDDR, a central and unifying node activated by diverse sources of telomere dysfunction, including genetic mutations, oxidative or metabolic stress, and environmental damage. By blunting this nodal pathway, tASO mitigate multiple downstream consequences of telomere dysfunction, including DNA damage signaling, senescence, inflammation and stem cell exhaustion. This mechanistic focus on tDDR enables broad therapeutic applicability across different etiologies of telomere dysfunction independent of individual genetic defects. Finally, systemic tASO treatment in Terc−/− mice was well tolerated and did not result in detectable adverse effects, supporting its safety in vivo. Notably, our results are in line with those of a recent study25 that supported the role of DDR in the pathogenesis of TBDs and DC, showing that pharmacological inhibition of ATM improves the fitness of TBD cells by allowing bypass of DDR-mediated senescence.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the central role of tDDR in driving hematopoietic dysfunction in the context of telomere shortening. Using tASO as a molecular tool to inhibit tDDR in vivo, we have demonstrated that repression of this pathway is sufficient to alleviate telomere-driven defects in the hematopoietic system, including immune imbalance and stem cell exhaustion. These findings establish tDDR as a causative driver of hematopoietic dysfunction and highlight its relevance across telomere-driven conditions, including rare TBDs, DC and age-related decline, and also suggest that targeted inhibition of tDDR may have therapeutic promise. Although further studies are needed to explore long-term effects and translational potential, our work establishes a foundation for consideration of tDDR as a key target in telomere-related pathologies.

The study had some limitations. Mechanistically, our data support a model in which tDDR inhibition reduces senescence, inflammation and stem cell dysfunction, but the contributions of individual downstream pathways and cellular functional interactions remain to be fully elucidated; this is likely to require single-cell-level analyses. In addition, the cellular basis for the different effects observed between anti-TeloG and anti-TeloC treatments in competitive BM transplantation may require further investigation. Finally, the human primary cell experiments were performed using cells from only two aged male donors; the results would thus benefit from validation in larger and more diverse cohorts.

Methods

Animals and treatments

Experiments involving animals were done in accordance with Italian laws (D.lgs. 26/2014) that enforce Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and the Council of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. Accordingly, the project was authorized by the Italian competent authority (Ministry of Health). Mice were housed in a pathogen-free animal facility and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle, at 20–22 °C and 50–65% air humidity. WT mice on a pure C57BL/6J background and the WT mice for BM competitive transplant experiments (B6.SJL-Ptprca/BoyAiTac) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. Terc+/− mice (B6. Cg-Terctm1Rdp/J stock no: 004132|mTR−/−)27 were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Terc+/− mice were intercrossed to generate first-generation homozygous Terc−/− mice. Second-generation Terc−/− mice were generated by successive breeding of first-generation Terc−/− and then third-generation Terc−/− mice by crosses between second-generation Terc−/− mice. Finally, third-generation Terc−/− mice were used for the experiments. For this study, we used both male and female mice. For experiments in telomerase-deficient mice, 2- to 3-month-old G3 Terc−/− mice were injected intraperitoneally with a control, anti-TeloG or anti-TeloC ASO at a concentration of 15 mg kg−1 twice per week for 4 weeks. The animals were then sacrificed 2 or 9 months after tASO treatment. For assessment of the effects of tASO treatment during physiological aging, aged C57BL/6J WT mice were treated using the same tASO regimen, with injections administered between 15 and 16 months of age, and animals were sacrificed 2 months after the final dose (at 18 months of age). Young WT mice (5 months old) were used as baseline controls. For vaccine response experiments, mice were treated intramuscularly with 1 μg of SARS-CoV-2 spike (Wuhan-Hu-1) synthetic mRNA (see details below) and sacrificed 3 weeks later. Adult C57BL/6J WT mice were injected intraperitoneally with a control, anti-TeloG or anti-TeloC ASO at a concentration of 15 mg kg−1 twice per week for 5 weeks. The mice received 4.5 Gy total body irradiation using a GADGIL X-ray irradiator 24 h after the last injection and were sacrificed 1 h later.

ASO sequences

The locked nucleic-acid-modified oligonucleotides with a fully phosphorothioate backbone were produced by Qiagen as described in ref. 7. Sequences were as follows (5′–3′ orientation): control ASO, ACTGATAGGGAGTGGTAAACT; anti-TeloG ASO, CCCTAACCCTAACCCTAACCC; anti-TeloC ASO, GGGTTAGGGTTAGGGTTAGGG.

BM and spleen processing

BM was harvested from mice by flushing tibias and femurs in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under sterile conditions. BM cells were resuspended in PBS and filtered using 70-µm filters, followed by washing in PBS. RBCs were eliminated using RBC Lysis Buffer (BioLegend, cat. no. 420301), washed again in PBS, and frozen in freezing medium (90% fetal bovine serum, 10% dimethyl sulfoxide). Spleens were smashed on 70-µm filters and rinsed with PBS. The single-cell suspension was then washed once in PBS, and RBC lysis was carried out as for BM. An additional wash in PBS was performed, and cells were frozen in freezing medium (90% fetal bovine serum, 10% dimethyl sulfoxide). The following analyses were performed following thawing of cells in a 37 °C water bath: flow cytometry analysis, CFU assays and BM competitive transplant assays.

Flow cytometry

After thawing, BM and splenocytes were washed once in PBS, and extracellular staining was performed for 1 h at 4 °C using the following antibodies: Pacific Blue anti-mouse Lineage Cocktail (BioLegend, cat. no. 133310); PE anti-mouse CD117 (cKIT) Recombinant Antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 155106); APC anti-mouse Ly-6A/E (SCA1) antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 108112); PE-Cy7 rat anti-mouse CD135 (FLT3) (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 567594); Alexa Fluor 700 rat anti-mouse CD34 (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 560518); Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse/human CD45R/B220 antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 103244); Pacific Blue anti-mouse CD19 antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 115526); Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse IgD antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 405723); APC/Fire 750 anti-mouse IgM antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 406538); APC anti-mouse/human CD11b antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 101212); Brilliant Violet 570 anti-mouse Ly-6C antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 101212); Brilliant Violet 785 anti-mouse Ly-6G antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 127645); Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse CD11c antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 117339); Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD127 (IL-7Ra) antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 135033); APC-Cy7 anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 101327). All the extracellular antibodies were used at a concentration of 1:100 in cell staining buffer (BioLegend, cat. no. 420201). Intracellular protein levels were assessed after cell permeabilization with a True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BioLegend, cat. no. 424401) following manufacturer instructions and using the following antibodies in 1× permeabilization buffer (from the BioLegend kit; cat. no. 424401) at the indicated concentration overnight at 4 °C: histone H2AX [p Ser139] antibody [PerCP] (NovusBio, NB100-384PCP) (1:100); KAP1 [p Ser824] antibody [Alexa Fluor 488] (NovusBio, NBP2-32073AF488) (1:200); Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse/human Ki67 antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 151215) (1:100); and CDKN2A/p16INK4a polyclonal antibody, Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, BS-23797R-A594) (1:200). Analysis was performed using a Cytek Aurora 5 spectral flow cytometer with FlowJo (TreeStar) software.

Immunofluorescence on BM cells

BM cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates containing glass coverslips, allowed to settle at 37 °C for 30–40 min and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Following permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Merck) and blocking with a solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin and 2% gelatin (cold-water fish gelatin), cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C (anti-phospho-histone H2A.X (Ser139) antibody, clone JBW301, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 05-636-I; pATM antibody, Rockland, cat. no. 200-301-400; pChk1 Ser 317 antibody, Cell Signaling, cat. no. 2344S). Samples were then brought to room temperature, washed, and incubated with secondary antibodies for 45 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1 µg ml−1, Merck). Coverslips were mounted in Mowiol (Merck), and images were acquired using a Leica SP5 confocal microscope. Detection parameters were defined using control samples and maintained constant across all specimens.

ImmunoFISH on BM cells

BM cells fixed with 4% PFA were processed as previously described56, with minor modifications. Cells were denatured at 80 °C for 5 min and hybridized with a Cy5-conjugated TeloC PNA probe (F1003, Panagene). After washing, immunofluorescence staining was performed as described above. Images were acquired using a Leica SP5 confocal microscope, with detection parameters set using control samples and kept constant across specimens.

FISH on BM cells

BM cells fixed with 4% PFA were denatured at 80 °C for 5 min and hybridized with Cy5-conjugated TeloC PNA and Cy5-conjugated TeloG PNA probes (F1003 and F1007, Panagene) following the protocol described in ref. 56, without the immunofluorescence step.

Immunofluorescence/FISH signal quantification

Signal quantification and colocalization analysis were performed using Fiji scripts provided by the Advanced Light Microscopy Core Facility (RRID: SCR_026866).

Plasma and BM fluid processing

After BM flushing and centrifugation, the supernatant was kept and analyzed as BM fluid. Blood was recovered into EDTA-coated tubes from the heart postmortem, and plasma was obtained by centrifuging total blood for 15 min at 3,000g and 4 °C and recovering the supernatant.

Multiplex immunoassay

Bio-Plex Pro Mouse Cytokine 23-plex Assay (Bio-Rad, M60009RDPD) was used to measure the concentration of cytokines and chemokines in plasma and BM fluid, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. This magnetic-bead-based antibody detection kit allows simultaneous quantification of the analytes of interest. Each sample was tested following 1:4 dilution and in duplicate. The plate was read on a Bio-Plex 200 system (Bio-Rad), powered by Luminex xMAP technology. The concentration of analyte bound to each bead was proportional to the median fluorescence intensity of the reporter signal and was determined using the standards provided in the kit. Data were expressed in pg ml−1.

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sample preparation

Femur and spleen samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Diapath S.p.A., F0043) at 4 °C overnight and then washed in running water at slow flux for 5 h. Following fixation, the femurs were immediately incubated in Microdec, EDTA-based-1,000 ml (Diapath S.p.A., D0053) for 3 h for decalcification and then washed in running water at slow flux for 2 h. Then, all samples were transferred to 70% ethanol, processed and embedded in paraffin.

Histopathological examination

Paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 4-µm-thick sections and routinely stained with H&E for histopathological analysis. All histopathological evaluations were performed by an experienced pathologist blinded with respect to genotype and treatment. For the assessment of BM pathology, a predefined semiquantitative scoring system was applied, including evaluation of the following morphological parameters: erythroid colony contraction, expansion of segmented myeloid cells, expansion of immature myeloid cells, megakaryocyte clustering, megakaryocyte pleiomorphism, and evidence of erythro- or hemophagocytosis. For spleen pathology, the scoring system included evaluation of white pulp effacement, red pulp hyperplasia, increased myeloid and erythroid precursors, megakaryocyte hyperplasia and clustering, and the presence of hemosiderin-laden macrophages. Each variable was morphologically evaluated and independently scored according to severity (0, normal; 1, slight; 2, moderate; 3, marked) and extent (0, absent; 1, focal; 2, multifocal; 3, diffuse), according to established histopathological criteria. For each variable, a composite score was calculated as the product of severity and extent. An overall pathological score for each sample was then obtained by summing the composite scores across all evaluated parameters, providing an integrated measure of tissue pathology.

IHC staining

Paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 4-µm-thick sections and deparaffinized, rehydrated and unmasked using Novocastra Epitope Retrieval Solutions pH 9 in a thermostatic bath at 98 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were brought to room temperature and washed in PBS. After neutralization of the endogenous peroxidase with 3% H2O2 and Fc blocking with 0.4% casein in PBS (Leica Novocastra), the sections were incubated with primary antibodies. IHC was developed using anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody HRP (Life Technologies, 1:500) or anti-rat IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody HRP (Life Technologies, 1:500) and 3,30-diaminobenzidine (Novocastra) as substrate chromogen. Quantitative analyses were performed by calculating the average percentage of positive cells in five nonoverlapping fields at medium-power magnification (×200) using the Nuclear v.9 and Positive Pixel Count v.9 algorithms (Aperio ImageScope v.12.3.2.8013, Leica Biosystems) or Area Quantification v.2.4.2 and Multiplex IHC v.3.2.3 (HALO, Indica Labs), and the output was expressed as the percentage of positive cells. The following primary antibodies were used for IHC on mouse tissues: rabbit recombinant monoclonal CD41 antibody (Abcam, ab181582; 1:250, pH 9); rabbit recombinant monoclonal IBA1 antibody (Abcam, ab178847; 1:2,000, pH 9); rabbit polyclonal myeloperoxidase antibody (Abcam, ab9535; 1:25, pH 9); rabbit recombinant monoclonal PAX5 antibody (Abcam, ab109443; 1:1,000, pH 9); rat monoclonal anti-Ly76 (TER119) antibody (Abcam, ab91113; 1:50, pH 9) and BD Pharmingen purified rat anti-mouse TER119/erythroid cells (BD Biosciences, 553671; 1:50, pH 9).

mRNA vector generation and lipid-based nanoparticle formulation

SARS-CoV-2 spike (Wuhan-Hu-1) synthetic mRNA was transcribed in vitro using T7 FlashScribe CellScript (C-ASF3507). Uridine was 100% replaced with N1-methylpseudouridine-5′-triphosphate from TriLink (N-1081-100). Capping was performed cotranscriptionally by 3′ OMe CleanCap AGG (TriLink N-7413), as previously described57. mRNA was formulated into lipid-based nanoparticles using an Ignite microfluidic system (Cytiva). SM-102, DSPC, cholesterol and DMG-PEG2000 (Medchem) were used at a ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5, with N/P = 6 and a flow rate ratio of 3:1. The size and polydispersity of lipid-based nanoparticles were assessed by dynamic light scattering, confirming a mean size of 80 nm. Encapsulation efficiency and mRNA concentration were calculated by Ribogreen assay (Invitrogen Q10213).

Serum processing

Whole blood was recovered from the heart postmortem, and serum was obtained by letting blood clot for 1 h at room temperature, followed by centrifuging for 15 min at 3,000g and recovery of the supernatant.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was conducted to measure antigen-specific antibody titers. High-binding 96-well plates (Maxisorp cat. no. 442404, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 1 µg ml−1 of S1 Protein (eEnzyme code SCV2-S1-150P) diluted in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6, Sigma-Aldrich C3041) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (TBS-T; Thermo Fisher cat. no. 28360), wells were blocked with 5% nonfat dried milk powder (PanReac AppliChem, A0830) in wash buffer for 1 h at 37 °C. After three washes with wash buffer, serial dilutions of samples and controls were added, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. Following additional three washes, anti-mouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG1 heavy chain antibody HRP conjugated from Bethyl Laboratories, A90-105P) was applied for 1 h at 37 °C. Detection was performed using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (Sigma-Aldrich, T0440-1L) substrate, and the reaction was stopped with HCl (Fisher Scientific/Fisher Chemical, cat. no. 10080210). Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Device) with SoftMax software installed. Data were analyzed using a cutoff value established from blank wells. For each sample, optical density readings were evaluated across serial dilutions to determine the highest dilution at which the optical density remained above the cutoff. Samples were tested in duplicate.

CFU assay on murine cells

BM cells isolated from mice were counted, and 1 × 105 cells were cultured in methylcellulose-based medium (Stem Cell Technologies, MethoCult M3434) and scored for colonies using a light microscope after 7 days. For in vitro tASO treatment, tASO were added directly to the methylcellulose medium at the time of plating at a final concentration of 10 µM.

CFU analysis on human CD34+ HSPCs

We complied with all ethical regulations for retrieval of biological materials from healthy aged donors. To perform CFU analysis, we extracted CD34+ HSPCs from the BM of two aged healthy male individuals (75 and 60 years old58). BM samples were collected from participants who underwent hip replacement surgery at San Raffaele Hospital, Milan, Italy, after written informed consent had been obtained. We excluded from our analyses any participants with compromised immune function or previous history of cancer or cancer treatment. We also excluded participants that were positive for HBV, HCV or HIV infection. We included only participants that scored as ‘Not Frail’ on the Edmonton Frailty Scale, as previously described58. For the CFU assay, human aged CD34+ HSPCs from the BM of healthy individuals, control ASO, or anti-TeloG or anti-TeloC ASO were administered to the cells at a concentration of 30 µM and added fresh with every splitting of the cells. CFU assay was performed 8 days after the indicated treatments ex vivo, plating 800 cells in methylcellulose-based medium (MethoCult H4434, StemCell Technologies) supplemented with 100 IU ml−1 penicillin and 100 μg ml−1 streptomycin. Two weeks after plating, colonies were counted blind, distinguishing erythroid and myeloid colonies according to morphological criteria. Three technical replicates were performed for each donor, and the mean value was plotted.

Competitive BM transplant and reconstitution analysis

The procedure for competitive BM transplant was adapted from a previously published protocol39. To guarantee the robustness and reliability of our findings in this complex BM competitive transplant experiment, we utilized two independent batches of recipient mice, each transplanted at different time points throughout our study. Briefly, 2- to 3-month-old CD45.1+CD45.2+ recipient mice were bred in-house by crossing CD45.2+ C57Bl/6 mice with congenic CD45.1+ B6.SJL mice and lethally irradiated with two doses of 4.5 Gy (the first dose was given the day before transplantation and the second 2–3 h before transplantation). BM from 5-month-old CD45.1+ competitor mice and 5-month-old CD45.2+ donor mice were harvested and processed under sterile conditions (the detailed protocol is given in the ‘BM and spleen processing’ section). BM cells from CD45.1+ and CD45.2+ mice were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and injected intravenously into the lateral tail veins of the lethally irradiated recipients. Peripheral blood (100 μl) was collected from the lateral tail vein 4, 8, 12 and 16 weeks posttransplant into EDTA-coated tubes and analyzed by flow cytometry. RBCs were eliminated using RBC Lysis Buffer (BioLegend, cat. no. 420301) and washed once in PBS; then, extracellular antibody staining was performed for 30 min at 4 °C using the following antibodies: Brilliant Violet 711 anti-mouse CD45.2 (BioLegend, cat. no. 109847); PerCP anti-mouse CD45.2 (BioLegend, cat. no. 109825); Brilliant Violet 421 anti-mouse CD45.1 (BioLegend, cat. no. 110731); PerCP anti-mouse CD45.1 (BioLegend, cat. no. 110725); Pacific Blue anti-mouse CD19 (BioLegend, cat. no. 115526); APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD19 (BioLegend, cat. no. 115529); PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD3 (BioLegend, cat. no. 100220); APC anti-mouse/human CD11b (BioLegend, cat. no. 101212). All the extracellular antibodies were used at a concentration of 1:100 in Cell Staining Buffer (BioLegend, cat. no. 420201). After staining, cells were washed once in PBS and immediately acquired. For analysis of reconstitution in HSPCs, recipients were sacrificed at 16 weeks posttransplant, and their BM was analyzed by flow cytometry (see the ‘Flow cytometry’ section for the detailed protocol) using a Cytek Aurora 5 spectral flow cytometer with FlowJo (TreeStar) software.

RNA extraction and qPCR with reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted from BM cells with a Maxwell RSC Tissue mRNA Kit (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA (200 ng) was reverse transcribed using a SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A volume corresponding to 20 ng of initial RNA was employed for each real-time PCR reaction using SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche) on a Roche LightCycler 96 detection system. Mouse ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0 (Rplp0) was used as a control transcript for normalization.

The primer sequences (5′–3′ orientation) were as follows: Rplp0 forward, TTCATTGTGGGAGCAGAC; Rplp0 reverse, CAGCAGTTTCTCCAGAGC; Cdkn2A/p16 forward, CCCAACGCCCCGAACT; Cdkn2A/p16 reverse, GCAGAAGAGCTGCTACGTG.

Immunoblot

Aliquots of BM cells and splenocytes were pelleted before freezing and lysed in Laemmli sample buffer (2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 60 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8). Protein concentration was assessed using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 23225). Whole-cell extracts were resolved by SDS–PAGE. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, which was blocked in 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T). Membranes were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, then washed and incubated with a horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were developed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS (Thermo Fisher) and acquired with a Chemidoc Imager (Bio-Rad). The following antibodies and concentration were used: phospho-histone H2A.X (Ser139) (20E3) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling, cat. no. 9718; 1:1,000 in 5% bovine serum albumin in TBS-T) and anti-histone H3 antibody (Abcam, cat. no. 10799; 1:5,000 in 5% milk in TBS-T). For simplicity, we present the corresponding densitometric quantifications; the full blots are provided as raw data.

DNA extraction and relative telomere measurement by qPCR

DNA was extracted by resuspension of total BM cells or splenocytes into 50 mM NaOH and incubation at 96 °C for 30 min. After incubation, 1 M Tris-HCl pH 8.0 was added, and DNA was quantified with a NanoDrop. Relative telomere length was measured by qPCR using SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad, cat. no. 1725200) following an adapted version of a previously published protocol59. Ten nanograms of DNA were used in each reaction, and the following primers were used: actin forward, 5′-CTAAGGCCAACCGTGAAAAG-3′; actin reverse, 5′-ACCAGAGGCATACAGGGACA-3′; telo forward, 5’-CGGTTTGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTT-3’; telo reverse, 5′- GGCTTGCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCT-3′.

Blood analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected from mice via tail vein puncture. A portion of the blood was immediately analyzed with respect to hematological parameters using an automated veterinary hematological analyzer (URIT 3000 Vet Plus), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cytological evaluation, a few microliters of whole blood were smeared onto a coverslip to prepare blood smears. Smears were examined by light microscopy by an experienced pathologist, who performed differential counting of lymphocytes and granulocytes. Cell percentages were calculated based on the differential counts.

Statistical analyses and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. For all experiments, at least two independent cohorts (batches) of mice were treated and analyzed separately to ensure robustness and reproducibility of the findings. No data were excluded from the analyses, except for outliers identified using Grubbs’ test implemented in GraphPad Prism (v.10), applied according to standard statistical criteria. Mice were assigned by simple randomization to the different experimental groups, such that groups were balanced with respect to sex, date of birth and litter origin. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments or outcome assessment, except in the histological analyses shown in Fig. 3, which were performed in a blinded manner.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m. P values were calculated using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, as indicated in the figure legends. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Each data point represents one biological replicate (individual mouse). All measurements were taken from distinct samples; no repeated measurements from the same sample are shown.
