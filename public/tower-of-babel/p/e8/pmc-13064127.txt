Rapid prediction of cerebral edema on CT scan after traumatic brain injury
Authors: Ryan B Felix, Jamie Podell, Kathalyn Urquizo, Yasmin Alamdeen, Devon Beagan, Minahil Cheema, Gunjan Y Parikh, William Teeter, Hegang Chen, Lujie Karen Chen, Roumen Vesselinov, John P Fisher, Shiming Yang, Peter Hu, Neeraj Badjatia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic brain injury (TBI) affects 1.7 million individuals annually in the USA, with cerebral edema (CE) as a critical determinant of outcomes and neurosurgical interventions. Delays in TBI diagnosis and triage in prehospital or resource-limited settings contribute to suboptimal care. This study evaluated the predictive potential of machine learning models using clinical and physiological data to detect CE on initial head CT scans, addressing this gap.MethodsWe conducted a mixed retrospective and prospective study of 1222 suspected TBI patients. Data included clinical characteristics, radiographic scores (Marshall and Rotterdam), and physiological features derived from ECG and photoplethysmography signals collected within 1 hour of admission. Statistical models employed logistic regression and gradient boosting, using Shapley additive explanations for feature importance. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).ResultsThe models demonstrated high predictive accuracy for CE-related radiographic features, including midline shift and cisternal abnormalities (AUROC: 0.79–0.85). Clinical features such as Glasgow Coma Scale components and intubation status, combined with physiological variables like heart rate variability, contributed significantly to predictions. In contrast, predictions for other findings (eg, epidural hematoma) showed lower discriminatory power. Prehospital applicability was highlighted by the reliance on readily available physiological data.ConclusionsMachine learning models effectively predict CE prior to CT scans, offering a rapid decision support tool for triage and neurosurgical prioritization in austere and resource-limited settings. Early identification of CE could improve patient outcomes by optimizing transport and treatment strategies. Future research should focus on multicenter validation and streamlined data collection to enhance generalizability and clinical utility.Level of evidenceLevel III, Prognostic.

There is a critical need for rapid, objective, CT-independent triage support tools that can operate in austere or resource-limited settings.

Background

An estimated 1.7 million traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) are sustained annually in the United States, accounting for a third of all injury-related deaths and an estimated national economic burden of $76.5 billion.1 2 Despite continual advances in care following TBI, both early triage and secondary neurological injury remain major influencers of overall outcome.

Delays in TBI intervention are associated with worse outcomes, especially for patients mistakenly triaged to facilities lacking neurosurgical capability.35 Unfortunately, difficulty exists in the triage and treatment of patients with head injuries as decision-making for severe TBI typically hinges on results from head CT scans.6 7 Prior to the CT, clinicians use multiple parameters, such as the mode of injury paired with clinical exam measures of neurological comatose status and pupillary reactivity, to inform triage decisions. Some groups have attempted to mitigate the neurologic triage problem by bringing the CT scan or other specialized imaging into the prehospital setting as a triage tool; however, these solutions are costly and unrealistic in austere environments.8 9 Other strategies include the creation of neurocognitive examinations10 11 or point-of-care biomarker diagnostics,12 though each of these has its limitations.

As a result, triage and treatment of TBI in the early resuscitation period prove difficult, and decision-making information is rate-limited by neuroimaging results. To date, the radiographic head CT scan remains the “gold standard” in TBI diagnosis, and information obtained in a patient’s first CT serves this purpose in severity classification and early outcome prognostication. In fact, these relevant findings can be extracted from the head CT and decomposed into ubiquitously used radiographic scores.13 14

Both the Marshall and Rotterdam scores are useful tools for categorizing TBI and discussing disease severity and expected prognosis.131517 In practice, these scores are understood as static indicators of a patient’s radiographic state at a single point in time.

Overall, there is a clear clinical demand for rapid decision support tools for neurosurgical resource needs following TBI. Such tools must be readily and rapidly assessable in potentially austere environments18 where difficult TBI triage decisions often occur, such as in remote prehospital or combat settings. As such, we sought to focus on the utilization of rapidly assessable clinical criteria alongside physiological features derived from ECG and photoplethysmography (PPG) ubiquitously available in the prehospital setting from portable monitors.19 20 While not historically used for neurotrauma triage, ECG and PPG are promising physiological signals that may reflect autonomic dysregulation following TBI.21 22 ECG-derived heart rate variability (HRV) captures shifts in parasympathetic and sympathetic tone via modulating the activity of vagal and sympathetic trunk fibers innervating the sinoatrial node and surrounding cardiac tissues, whereas PPG reflects peripheral vascular tone and pulsatile perfusion, which can shift in response to systemic hemodynamic instability, such as that caused by shock or hypovolemia.23 These signals can be obtained rapidly and non-invasively using portable monitors—requiring neither interpretation nor clinical expertise for acquisition. In principle, an untrained bystander could be guided to place a 3-lead ECG or PPG sensor, and physiological features could be autonomously extracted. Given the operational challenges of assessing neurologic function in the prehospital environment, these physiological signals may offer objective input for triage support in settings where imaging and trained personnel are unavailable.

We hypothesized that by utilizing clinical and vital sign data during the acute resuscitation period, machine learning methods could be developed to predict the presence of cerebral edema on the initial head CT scan.

Methods

The TRIPOD-AI and STARD 2015 guidelines were used to ensure proper reporting of methods, results, and discussion (SDC 2). The study protocol was approved by our UMB Human Research Protections and Institutional Review Board (protocol numbers HP-00060944, HP-00087051).

Patient selection

This study used both retrospective and prospective participant identification. For the retrospective phase, patients were eligible if time since injury was ≤6 hours and head abbreviated injury score (AIS) ≥1. Patient exclusion criteria included hospital length of stay < 48 hours, thoracic or abdominal AIS ≥1, and ≤30% of continuous vital signs (ECG/PPG) available from their first hour after arrival at the trauma center. For the prospective phase, in-hospital AIS scores are calculated after the injury and therefore not suitable for prospective enrollment of patients. Thus, the research team performing prospective screening was tasked with enrolling those arriving at the trauma center suspected of suffering a head injury, with a time-since-injury ≤ 6 hours and who did not have definitive thoracic or abdominal injury. Congruently, the prospective phase also excluded those with a hospital length-of-stay < 48 hours or ≤30% of continuous vital signs (ECG/PPG) available from their first hour after arrival to the trauma center. In both phases, patients with thoracic or abdominal injuries were excluded to reduce confounding from extracranial trauma on the ECG/PPG-derived signals and the evolution of intracranial pathology. The study protocol was approved by our institution’s Human Research Protections and Institutional Review Board (protocol numbers HP-00060944, HP-00087051), available on request to the corresponding author. This study was not prospectively registered; there was no patient or public involvement in study design or dissemination.

Clinical data and vital sign collection

Patient clinical characteristics were extracted from their chart, including admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), patient age, sex, intubation status, and mechanism of injury (ie, blunt vs penetrating). For all patients, the initial and follow-up head CTs were reviewed by attending neurocritical care physicians (NB, GP, JP) blinded to the predictor dataset and received both a Marshall score and a Rotterdam score with individual component scores recorded (table 1). The inter-rater reliability of Rotterdam scores has been reported as a Cronbach alpha with a 95% CI of 0.966 (0.949 to 0.979).24 Cerebral edema was defined as the presence of either ≥5 mm of midline shift (MLS) or any cisternal compression/effacement. For ease of outcome prediction, the trichotomous categorical basal cistern component was converted to binary (0=normal, 1=abnormal), and the epidural hematoma categorization was reversed to be consistent with the other outcomes (0=normal, 1=abnormal). Additionally, we captured whether patients required neurosurgical intervention within 48 hours of admission, which included craniotomy/craniectomy, burr holes, and ventriculostomies. Vital signs were recorded from the trauma resuscitation unit bedside monitors (GE Marquette) using a validated reliable framework.25

Feature extraction & dataset preprocessing

Vital sign (vs) preprocessing and feature extraction from waveform data were performed in MATLAB. A set of 406 vs trend and HRV features was extracted in 5-min windows from continuous ECG and PPG recordings of the first hour of resuscitation.

The 5-min windows for each variable were averaged to obtain the final value for each patient. The features were derived from either trend, which was sampled at 2-s intervals, or waveform which represented 240 Hz ECG-derived and PPG-derived variables related to HRV.

Feature calculation

For every 5-min interval within a patient’s first hour of vital signs, a set of HRV-related features was calculated using both ECG and PPG waveforms. These variables included the standard measures of HRV,26 as well as derived statistical variables across the individual’s calculation windows such as means, SD, quartiles, and doses. Using a method described in the literature, transfer learning features for GCS and ISS estimation directly from vital signs were calculated and included in the feature set.27 This feature set has been shown to make inpatient and prehospital predictions for massive transfusion28 29 and secondary neurological decline.24 27 30 These GCS/ISS transfer learning features were included due to their expected importance in describing the clinical exam related to comatose status (GCS) and injury severity (ISS), thus potentially adding value to predictions.

Statistical modeling approach

We developed models that utilized early (≤ 1 hour after admission, pre-CT) clinical and physiological data to make predictions of CE on the initial and follow-up CT, as well as whether the patients received a neurosurgical intervention. The data from both retrospective and prospective datasets were combined to form a final dataset of 1222 patients after exclusion of those with missing outcomes. A hold-out test set of 20% of the data was sequestered using stratified random sampling. The remaining 80% of data was used as the training and validation set. Bootstrapping was used to generate experimental replicates (100 iterations). Preprocessing for statistical modeling was performed in a blinded fashion where the model method was fit on the training and validation set and applied to the testing set, thus avoiding data leakage to the test set. We removed variables missing more than 20% of data, which can occur in variables where excessive motion artifact in the early resuscitative period leads to unreliable sensing. Additionally, we removed variables with low variance, as these are not expected to be good differentiators in predictive modeling. The data were scaled through subtraction of the mean and scaling to unit variance. Missing values were imputed using a K-nearest neighbors strategy considering the nearest (K=5) features from the entire available set.18 31 The total set of 413 features was further reduced in a first round of feature selection by removal of zero coefficient variables using lasso regression, performed independently for all models and outcomes. The first seven features were clinical variables (age, sex, intubation status, blunt vs penetrating injury, GCS motor, GCS verbal, GCS eye), and the remaining 406 were physiologically derived from vital signs at the bedside monitor. Finally, from the remaining features, the top 26 features were selected using a second round of feature selection employing a minimized redundancy maximized relevance method.32 The two-phase feature selection allowed for the reduction of features expected to have no contribution to model performance alongside the consideration of mutual information. Additionally, a total of 129 cerebral edema events and 26 features yield approximately five events per variable, which was expected to be adequately large for suitable predictions.33 34

Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a cross-validated randomized search on the training set (10 folds, 120 iterations) which sampled from uniform distributions of each hyperparameter. The best set of parameters was determined by the maximum validation set area under the receiver operator characteristic curve (AUROC) score. After obtaining the best set of parameters, the models were trained on the consolidated training and validation set (80% of data) and subsequently evaluated on the test set (20% of data). The models used for the prediction of outcome measures were Logistic Regression (LR) with “elasticnet” regularization and Gradient Boosting Tree (XGB)35 classification algorithms. For LR, the hyperparameters tuned were regularization strength and the L1 to L2 ratio. For XGB, the parameters tuned were the maximum depth of the decision trees, the number of trees, the learning weight, and the minimum child weight. All outcomes predicted were binary. The model pipeline and scoring operations were performed in Python using the open-source Scikit-learn shallow-learning library.36 After hyper-parameter tuning, the final models were serialized (joblib format). A lightweight Python script applies the preprocessing steps and then calls predict_proba on clinical and physiological inputs to return an outcome probability. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) were used to visualize variable importance and contribution to model output.37 To account for variability in feature selection across bootstrap iterations, SHAP values were weighted by the square root of their selection incidence.

Results

From a total screening population of 3488 adult patients, a retrospective chart review identified 902 TBI patients who met study criteria between November 2015 and June 2018. Additionally, a prospective cohort of 437 patients directly admitted was enrolled between March 2021 and March 2022 (online supplemental file 1). The mixed retrospective/prospective collection of patients led to inherent differences between the cohorts. This sampling method allowed for the inclusion of participants prospectively suspected of suffering a TBI and subjected to triage. However, changing clinical practices over time (and particularly in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic occurring between cohorts), as well as the lack of definitive AIS scoring prospectively both contributed to differences in cohort demographics and outcomes (table 2). We mapped the clinical time course of patients following admission and tracked the flow of patients from first and second CT (CT1, CT2) to their neurosurgical intervention status (figure 1a). Over half of those receiving neurosurgery had the procedure shortly after their first CT, with a distinct bias towards presence of CE on the first scan yielding a surgical intervention. Most patients had a Rotterdam score of 2 or 3, indicating normal exams or mild injuries (table 2). The cohort incidence of intraventricular or subarachnoid hemorrhage was 38.1%, abnormal basal cisterns was 8.8%, MLS was 7.4%, and epidural hematoma was 6.3%. The time to CT was commonly within the first hour for the first and the sixth hour for the second scans, as is standard practice at our institution (figure 1b). For those patients who received two scans, we mapped the radiographic score changes of each individual patient (figure 2a,b). Irrespective of scoring method, the majority of patients retained the same score from first to follow-up scan. Of those with changing Marshall scores, most patients had higher scores. Additionally, Diffuse Injury Grade V preclusion of any other score can be easily represented visually. For changing Rotterdam scores, nearly equivalent numbers of patients had increasing scores as those with decreasing scores. In contrast, the Rotterdam score contains no preclusive categories; no particular score on the initial scan prevents assignment of a different score on follow-up imaging.

To truly describe changes in the Rotterdam components, we compared the Spearman rank correlations of all first and second CT Rotterdam components (figure 2c). For this analysis, the cisternal component was binarized as normal versus abnormal, rather than including multiclass categorization for normal, compressed, and absent. All components in the first scan were positively correlated with their counterparts in the second scan, indicating that findings were most commonly unchanged between scans. Notably, the presence of MLS on the first scan was strongly positively correlated (r=0.66) with cisternal compression/effacement on the first scan. Both MLS and cisternal abnormality are Rotterdam findings associated with the presence of cerebral edema and thus comprised our definition of CE for this study.

We then performed a study population analysis to determine where Rotterdam components were most changed from first to second scan (figure 2d). Among both those who did and did not receive neurosurgical intervention, little difference between Rotterdam components in the first and follow-up scans was observed. However, there is a stark difference between those who received neurosurgical intervention and those who did not in the Rotterdam components associated with CE, cisternal abnormalities, and MLS. While the nominal fraction of patients with abnormalities in their epidural hematoma (EDH) and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH)/subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) Rotterdam components is greater in the population receiving neurosurgical intervention, the CIs of these scores are either overlapping or nearly so between cohorts.

We then attempted to develop early clinical and combined clinical-physiological models to predict Rotterdam components on the first and second scans. We attempted binary classification predictions of the four Rotterdam components. We consolidated a binary measure of cerebral edema, defined by whether a patient had either abnormal cisterns or MLS. A set of seven clinical features was added to the model (age, sex, admission GCS components, intubation status, and mechanism of injury). The models also used physiological variables collected within the first hour of admission, and predictions were performed using both LR and XGB methods. The models displayed a good ability to discern cerebral edema on the first CT scan, resulting in excellent AUROCs (LR=0.81 (0.81, 0.82), XGB=0.80 (0.79, 0.81) for clinical models and for combined clinical/physiological models (LR=0.79 (0.79, 0.80), XGB=0.79 (0.79, 0.80)) (figure 3a,b). Notably, the addition of vital signs did not improve the model performance.

Discussion

TBI is a complex and heterogeneous condition that poses significant challenges in triage and management. Our study sought to address critical gaps in early decision-making by leveraging clinical and physiological data collected during the early resuscitative period (≤1 hour after arrival to the trauma center) to predict the presence of cerebral edema and its associated neurosurgical needs. The findings underscore both the potential of predictive modeling in TBI care and the necessity for further refinement and validation for translational clinical utility.

Objective and reproducible endpoints are critical for clinical decision support tools, particularly in heterogeneous conditions like TBI, where treatment practices vary by institution. In this context, the Rotterdam components—including MLS, cisternal status, and the presence of intraventricular or subarachnoid hemorrhage—emerge as robust candidates for three reasons. First, they provide granular and clinically relevant insights into ISS that correlate with both prognosis and the need for intervention. Second, their derivation from radiographic features makes them independent of institution-specific treatment practices, enhancing the generalizability of predictive models. Third, these components comprise established radiological scoring systems, offering a familiar framework for clinicians. Nonetheless, our findings also highlight limitations in the Rotterdam score, such as its inability to fully capture subdural hematomas or its non-monotonic handling of EDH, in which EDH absence contributes to a higher Rotterdam score while its presence yields a lower score, unlike the other components where worsening pathology corresponds to higher scores. These limitations warrant further exploration in the context of using the Rotterdam score as an objective endpoint in clinical decision support tools.

Perhaps the most impactful takeaway from our investigation is the demonstration that clinical data collected during the early resuscitative period (≤1 hour after arrival to the trauma center) can effectively predict CE even before CT scans are available. Although our study used data from this early resuscitative period, the same physiological features derived from continuous ECG and PPG monitoring are readily available in prehospital settings. By integrating these features with clinical variables like GCS components, age, and intubation status, our models achieved high AUROCs for CE prediction—driven primarily by the influence of clinical features like GCS. These results are particularly promising for resource-limited and austere settings, where access to advanced imaging is constrained. Early identification of patients at high risk for CE could inform critical decisions, such as prioritizing transport to facilities with neurosurgical capabilities or initiating interventions to mitigate secondary injury. While our models excelled in predicting CE-related components, their performance was less robust for other elements, such as epidural hematoma, indicating room for improvement in capturing the full spectrum of TBI pathophysiology. While the addition of ECG and PPG signals did not significantly improve model performance beyond clinical features such as GCS and age, we interpret our findings as proof of concept for integrating autonomic signals into neurotrauma decision support. Our physiological model performed best when identifying cerebral edema or its associated components on initial CT (figure 4c,d), suggesting that ECG and PPG may partially capture autonomic perturbations related to cerebral edema in the early resuscitative period. However, the lack of added value in the combined model indicates that the relevant information in these signals is both mutually shared and better reflected in clinical indicators like GCS. Future incorporation of physiological signals may benefit from more granular temporal analyses, rigorous extraction of clinically relevant physiological signatures, or utilization of entire vs signals alongside deep learning forecasting techniques.38 Alternatively, portable near-infrared spectroscopy devices have shown promising results for detecting intracranial hematomas39 40 and have been piloted as pre-hospital screening tools during trauma transport. Ultimately, the incorporation of physiological signals into clinical decision-making could support systems where prehospital autonomic monitoring complements or substitutes subjective assessments, particularly when skilled evaluators are unavailable.

Furthermore, our results reveal that neurosurgical interventions (burr holes, ventriculostomy, and craniotomy/craniectomy for hematoma evacuation, etc) are biased toward the presence of CE. This is unsurprising, given that these interventions primarily function to alleviate elevated intracranial pressure, a hallmark feature of CE. Our predictive models demonstrated strong discriminatory power for identifying CE-related components, such as MLS and cisternal compression, on initial and follow-up CT scans. Our findings reinforce the clinical relevance of CE as a central determinant of neurosurgical decision-making during the early, resuscitation phase after injury. This relationship also underscores the importance of improving prehospital triage tools to rapidly identify CE before CT availability, potentially reducing delays in definitive treatment.

Early prediction of cerebral edema, and therefore the bias toward need for neurosurgical intervention, stands to improve prehospital or early ED triage of TBI. Patient outcomes may improve by alleviating the burden of incorrectly triaged patients by reducing both the incidence of secondary hospital transfer and time-to-neurosurgery. Furthermore, additional understanding of trajectories of cerebral edema may lead to a better understanding of the global pathophysiology of severe TBI. Mapping trajectories of radiographic components may aid in better describing the mechanisms and early indicators of contusion progression following TBI.

The machine learning model prediction of abnormal cisterns and >5 mm of MLS performed better than predictions of intraventricular or subarachnoid hemorrhage or the presence of epidural hematomas. MLS and cisternal effacement represent a more severe neurological injury that is characterized by significant mass effect and thus a later stage in contusion progression. On the other hand, the presence of IVH/SAH or EDH can cover a wider range of severities. Their presence does not necessarily indicate that a significant mass effect exists on the CT. As such, our predictive models capture the degree of edema present on CT and are driven by GCS as a clinical predictor. Diagnosis of cerebral edema is important as it is ubiquitously used as a guideline for the need of neurosurgical intervention.

Prehospital care providers could use a prediction of Rotterdam components to inform transport resource and destination decisions such as identifying a need for flight medical evacuations vs ground transport or whether the transport destination should be a Level I or II trauma center. Injured soldiers exiting the theater of war and patients at neurosurgery-incapable hospitals may benefit from earlier identification and evacuation to definitive care.

The utility of continuous vital sign predictions requires further investigation before it can be implemented as a support tool for clinical decision-making. Our study has limitations to be mitigated through future research. We used 1 hour of vital signs in developing our Rotterdam prediction, which in some prehospital settings may prove unrealistic. We have yet to investigate whether less vital sign data can be used without sacrificing the quality of prediction. Furthermore, extracranial factors during the early resuscitation period may influence both the physiological signals used for prediction and the evolution of cerebral edema. ECG and PPG signals are susceptible to systemic perturbations common in trauma, including hypovolemia, thoracic injury, sedation, and ventilation changes. These factors can introduce noise into autonomic measurements, complicating attribution of signal variation to cerebral injury. Simultaneously, systemic insults may influence the development of cerebral edema by altering cerebral perfusion, oxygen delivery, or triggering inflammatory cascades. Future work may benefit from incorporating pragmatic markers of systemic status to better isolate neurologically relevant signal changes in prehospital or resource-limited settings. Additionally, the model results would benefit from comparison to clinical-decision-maker predictions of Rotterdam components in both prehospital and early-ED settings, as this is the context determining whether such a tool can positively support provider decision making. Lastly, while our combined retrospective/prospective study population was diverse, a larger dataset of multicenter data could speak to the broader generalizability of the models created herein.

This study highlights the potential of integrating prehospital clinical and physiological data into predictive models for CE, offering a pathway to improved triage and early intervention in TBI care. By focusing on objective, radiographically derived endpoints like Rotterdam components, we provide a framework for decision support tools that are independent of institution-specific practices. While our findings demonstrate the feasibility of pre-CT CE prediction, further research is needed to refine these models, expand generalizability, and evaluate their impact on clinical decision-making and patient outcomes. Ultimately, bridging the gap between early prediction and actionable triage decisions could transform care for TBI patients, particularly in resource-limited and prehospital environments.

Conclusions

Overall, this early investigation aims to provide a better understanding of radiographic score trajectories in the early resuscitation period following TBI. Our targeted predictions of radiographic scores in the absence of CT information provide new understanding of which clinical variables are most associated with critical CT findings, as well as the limitations of incorporating physiological data into predictive models. More broadly, models such as ours have important clinical value in the triage and treatment of TBI.
