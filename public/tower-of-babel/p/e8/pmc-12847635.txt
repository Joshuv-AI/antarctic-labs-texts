Science-based fluid therapy in the intensive care unit: Professor Rinaldo Bellomo’s legacy
Authors: Simon R Finfer, Naomi E Hammond, John A Myburgh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch into the use of resuscitation and “maintenance” fluids in the intensive care unit was integral to the development of the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group (CTG) and the CTG being recognised as one of the most productive and influential trials groups in the world. Professor Rinaldo Bellomo (Rinaldo) played a central and critical role in this research and in this article; we focus on Rinaldo’s many roles and contributions to the key large clinical trials that changed the critical care research landscape in Australia and New Zealand and clinical practice worldwide. With a proposed recruitment of 7000 participants, the Saline versus Albumin Fluid Evaluation study was the first large pragmatic trial conducted in intensive care units anywhere in the world. Rinaldo brought the concept of the trial to the CTG and was critical to securing funding from multiple sources at a time when Australian National Health and Medical Research Council’s funding limits would cover only a fraction of the costs of such a trial. His personal contacts at CSL Ltd. and the Australian Red Cross Blood Service helped secure the supply of study fluids and made possible the seemingly impossible task of blinding a trial of two fluids that were macroscopically different with one supplied in glass bottles and the other in polyvinyl chloride bags. The successful conduct of the Saline versus Albumin Fluid Evaluation trial, the CTG’s first publication in The New England Journal of Medicine and presentation at the US Food and Drugs Administration, established the CTG on the global stage and served as a template for the Crystalloid versus Hydroxyethyl Starch Study and PlasmaLyte-148® versus Saline Study trials; we can only speculate what fraction of this could have been achieved without Rinaldo.Keywords: Fluid therapy, Resuscitation, Albumin, Saline, Hydroxyethyl starch

Professor Bellomo’s (Rinaldo’s) contribution to the science of fluid administration in the intensive care unit (ICU) and in other acute care setting settings cannot be overstated. The administration of intravenous fluids is ubiquitous in the ICU only being rivalled by the use of oxygen in the treatment of acutely ill patients. Although the history of intravenous fluid to resuscitate hypovolaemic patients can be traced back to the 1832 cholera epidemic in London,1 the choice of which fluid to use, the timing, and amount of fluid to give were based on flawed physiologic principles and familiarity with local practice right up until the end of the 20th century. In this scientific vacuum where opinions were strongly held and data were sparse, somewhat sterile “colloid versus crystalloid” arguments flourished,2 unresolved by small, underpowered clinical trials.3,4 Unbeknownst to us during the early years of the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Clinical Trials Group (CTG), all this was all about change and characteristically Rinaldo would play a pivotal role. Rinaldo’s impact on the science of fluid therapy in the ICU and on the design and conduct of critical care trials can be traced to the Saline versus Albumin Fluid Evaluation (SAFE) study5 and continued through the SAFE traumatic brain injury (SAFE TBI) evaluation,6,7 the Crystalloid versus Hydroxyethyl Starch Study (CHEST),8 and the PlasmaLyte-148® versus Saline Study (PLUS) trials.9

The SAFE study

In 1998, the Cochrane Injuries Group published a meta-analysis of 30 randomised controlled trials which included 1419 participants, an average of less than 50 participants per trial, which concluded that the administration of albumin to critically ill patients increased mortality by 6%.10 As at that time approximately 50,000 patients received albumin in Australian ICUs each year, this suggested albumin administration could be causing 3000 preventable deaths each year. The Cochrane review generated responses ranging from proposing a ban on the clinical use of albumin in critically ill patients11 to questioning the results of the meta-analysis.12 Regulators issued warnings about the use of albumin but left clinicians free to use it if they wished to. Almost immediately, and with his typical fusion of passion and scientific rigour, Rinaldo proposed the CTG conduct a definitive trial to examine the safety of albumin use in Australian and New Zealand ICUs, and thus the SAFE study was conceived.13

Given the Cochrane meta-analysis had suggested an increase in the risk of death, the natural outcome measure for the proposed trial was mortality. The group of CTG officeholders and interested senior clinicians who came together to conceive the trial were largely sceptical about the magnitude of effect proposed by the Cochrane review. Consequently, the study sample size was calculated based on half the reported treatment effect, an absolute mortality difference of 3%. After deciding to exclude cardiac surgery patients due to their very low mortality rate (∼1%), we estimated the baseline mortality for the remaining ICU population at 15% using data from the ANZICS adult patient database. Seeking 90% power, this presented us with the daunting task of conducting a 7000-patient randomised clinical trial, an undertaking that had never previously been attempted in intensive care patients. Further challenges were posed by the limits on National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) funding at that time, the imperative to mitigate clinician bias by blinding the fluids, and the realisation that the CTG did not possess the experience nor infrastructure to attempt such a trial. These hurdles were addressed with Rinaldo playing a pivotal role in funding the trial, securing the supply of albumin and in the supposedly impossible task of blinding the fluids.

The SAFE study “Industrial plant”

The first domino to fall into place was establishing a collaboration with the Institute for International Health, the precursor to The George Institute for Global Health (TGI), which, in 1999, was just being established at Royal North Shore Hospital in Sydney. Principal Director Professor Stephen MacMahon, a cardiovascular researcher with experience of running trials with more than 10,000 participants cemented the relationship by saying “I can give you the industrial plant to run your trial on the condition that you guys run it”. The promise was to provide expertise in the case-report form design, data management, statistics and for the first time, web-based randomisation, data entry, and fluid logistics at a time when commercial web-based systems were not available.

Funding1

A project grant application to the Australian National Health and Medical Research CouncilNHMRC was led by Professor Robyn Norton (co-principal director) and Professor Bruce Neal (cardiologist) at the Institute for International Health, providing the large trials’ track record that the CTG applicants did not possess. The application was successful, but at the time, the NHMRC had a funding limit of a maximum of $250,000 per project per year. Consequently the maximum amount available, namely $608,000, was secured but was clearly inadequate to fund the trial. The public health and financial interests in Australia’s blood supply rested then, and still rest, with Australian governments and with CSL Behring (then CSL Limited) making them the organisations we targeted to provide additional funding to make the trial viable. Rinaldo secured our first meeting with the Victorian Minister of Health and representatives of the Victorian Department of Health and with characteristic passion convinced them of a public health duty to fund the trial. Rinaldo secured a commitment of $150,000, which was critical in convincing other jurisdictions to contribute. In total, we secured additional funding of just under A$1.8M in Australia and were subsequently successful with a Health Research Council of New Zealand Grant yielding NZ $385,739 (Table 1).

Securing the supply of study fluids

The Australian blood supply is owned by the Australian governments, collected from volunteers by the Australian Red Cross Blood Service, and plasma is fractionated by CSL Behring under the National Fractionation Agreement for Australia.14 Consequently, obtaining the albumin needed for the trial required agreements with CSL Ltd. and the Australian Red Cross Blood Service. Fortunately, Rinaldo already had established working relationships with both and secured their support for the trial, specifically that the albumin would be supplied and distributed at their expense. This left the problem of conducting a blinded trial of albumin, a yellow fluid supplied in bottles that froths in the chamber of a standard giving set, with saline, a clear fluid supplied in bags that does not froth.

Blinding the fluids

In Australia, saline is manufactured by Baxter Healthcare at its plant in Toongabbie, New South Wales. While Baxter was prepared to put saline in bottles for the trial, the bottles that would fit on their production line were a different size to the bottles in which CSL distributed albumin. Having either Baxter-bottle albumin or CSL-bottle Baxter’s saline was not possible as both companies had to maintain quality control of the entire manufacturing and packaging process. Consequently, CSL undertook the onerous task of becoming a saline manufacturer for the duration of the trial; this included generating stability data during the trial so that the initial shelf life of 3 months could be extended as the trial progressed. Rinaldo’s personal relationships with CSL were critical to getting this undertaking. The final step once we had a solution to the physical differences in the packaging of the fluids was to mask the difference in their appearance; this was achieved by enclosing the bottles in cardboard cartons with a yellow window and masking the drip and pump chambers of a custom-built green giving set to allow bedside nurses to see the level of the fluid in the bottles and that the fluid was running but not to differentiate the albumin from the saline (Fig. 1).

Recruitment and results

As the only large investigator-initiated trial running in Australian and New Zealand ICUs and with carefully considered simple inclusion and exclusion criteria, the trial recruited the planned 7000 patients in 15 months (Fig. 2). After recruitment was discontinued, we discovered three patients had been randomised twice in error leaving 6997 valid randomisations.

The average treatment effect had no statistically significant difference in the primary outcome of 28-day all-cause mortality, but preplanned subgroup analyses suggested increased mortality in patients with TBI-assigned albumin and reduced mortality in those with sepsis.5,7,15

Impact

The impact of the SAFE study went far beyond confirming the safety of albumin in critically ill patients without TBI. As the first large pragmatic trial conducted in critically ill patients anywhere in the world, it cemented the ANZICS CTG as one of the leading clinical trial groups worldwide. It confirmed that large pragmatic comparative effectiveness trials were possible in critically ill patients and was hailed by editorialist Professor Deborah Cook as heralding a new era in critical care and “not only a landmark trial; but also a milestone for the discipline of critical care medicine”.16 The SAFE study also acted as a template for further fluid trials.8,9

Follow-up of the SAFE TBI subgroup

The finding of increased mortality in patients with TBI assigned albumin was an unexpected observation after a poll of the study investigators and research coordinators at the initial results thought that albumin would be beneficial in those with TBI. Additional data collection and longer-term follow-up of patients with TBI confirmed that the mechanism of increased mortality was increased intracranial pressure (ICP) and that the mortality difference persisted to 2 years after enrolment.7,17 Two theories were advanced to explain the increased cranial pressure: (i) that albumin might leak across the damaged blood–brain barrier causing brain swelling or (ii) that the adverse effect associated with albumin related more directly to the relative hypotonicity of the albumin preparation itself (4% Albumex®).

Through his validated instrumented sheep model based at the Florey Institute, Rinaldo’s team demonstrated that hypotonic albumin was associated with increases in ICP whereas isotonic albumin was not,18 favouring the hypothesis that the hypotonicity of 4% Albumex® was responsible for increasing ICP. This hypothesis is also supported by the findings of the Balanced Solution in Intensive Care Study which reported increased mortality in patients with TBI assigned to receive hypotonic balanced crystalloid solutions.19

The crystalloid versus hydroxyethyl starch study

While in 2007 albumin was the commonest colloid solution used in Australian ICUs, hydroxyethyl starch solutions (HESs) were the predominant colloids used in New Zealand ICUs and in many other countries, particularly in Europe.20 Studies using a high-molecular-weight HES suggested an increased risk of acute renal failure compared to gelatin and crystalloid.21,22 Newer hydroxyethyl starch preparations with lower concentrations (6%), molecular weight (130 kD), and degrees of molar substitution (0.4) were approved for use as intravenous resuscitation fluid in Australia in 2006. Given their increasing popularity in other countries and concerns about toxicity, particularly renal toxicity, the lead investigators of the SAFE study, notably Rinaldo as the world’s leading critical care nephrologist, considered that a similar pragmatic trial should be conducted to determine the safety and efficacy of 6% HES 130/0.4 (Voluven®, Fresenius Kabi) compared to its carrier, 0.9% sodium chloride. The CHEST study, with Rinaldo as a prominent member of the study management committee, was conducted in 32 ICUs in Australia and New Zealand and recruited 7000 participants between 2009 and 2012. The trial reported a nonsignificant increase in 90-day mortality and a significant increase in the use of renal replacement therapy and adverse events in participants who were assigned hydroxyethyl starch. These results and those of a similar trial in patients with septic shock conducted by the Scandinavian Critical Care Trials Group23 resulted in changes in approvals for the licencing and marketing of HESs in multiple medical regulatory authorities. Of these, Rinaldo led correspondence to the European Medicines Agency to emphatically state the case for changes to the licencing of HESs, based on the increased risk of death and acute kidney injury.

CHEST had a worldwide impact on clinical practice and further consolidated the reputation of the ANZICS CTG.

Chloride toxicity, saline versus PlasmaLyte for ICU fluid therapy and the PLUS study

The SAFE and CHEST trials, respectively, compared albumin and hydroxyethyl starch to 0.9% sodium chloride (saline). Saline was the appropriate comparator as it was the carrier fluid for the colloid solutions and it was also the most commonly administered crystalloid used in Australian and New Zealand ICUs at the time.24 However, the choice of saline as a comparative for fluid trials was questioned on the basis of largely observational data suggesting that the physiologically abnormal chloride concentration of saline and subsequent chloride load administered to patients might be toxic, particularly it might increase mortality and worsen renal function in a variety of patient populations.25,26

In a series of studies, Rinaldo’s team explored the physiology and clinical effects of chloride and chloride-rich fluids.27,28 Rinaldo also led clinical trials of restricting the use of chloride-rich fluids in emergency department and ICU patients,[29], [30], [31] a remarkable achievement given the difficulties involved in convincing clinicians to forsake the use of their favourite fluids. These studies supported the hypothesis that patients achieved better outcomes when balanced salt solutions were used rather than saline. This program of work continued with the Saline versus PlasmaLyte for ICU fluid Therapy trial, a double-blind, cluster-randomised, double-crossover trial conducted in four ICUs in New Zealand comparing Plasma-Lyte-148® with saline in four hospitals in New Zealand and led by Professor Paul Young. The primary report of the study, published in JAMA with Rinaldo as the senior author, reported that in a relatively low-risk population, the use of a PlasmaLyte-148® compared to saline did not reduce the risk of acute kidney injury (the primary outcome) but noted that larger trials were needed in higher-risk populations to confirm or refute these findings and to assess the impact on mortality which was numerically lower in patients treated with PlasmaLyte-148®.32 Following the model of SAFE and CHEST, the ANZICS CTG conducted the PLUS trial.9 While the average treatment effect in the PLUS trial did not favour the use of the balanced solution, the secondary analysis suggests the treatment effect may be modified by baseline chloride concentration,33 (See Fig. 3) and both trial-level and patient-level meta-analyses favoured the use of balanced solutions in critically ill patients.34,35

Unfinished business

We have focused on Rinaldo’s contribution to the major fluid trials conducted by the ANZICS CTG. It is impossible within the confines of a single article to do true justice to all of Rinaldo’s interest in research in fluid therapy. Other programs have investigated the effects of hyperoncotic albumin in cardiac surgery[36], [37], [38] and in patients receiving renal replacement therapy.39 Rinaldo’s contribution was also in investigating not only which fluid to use but also how fluids should be used, for example, in the Australasia Resuscitation in Sepsis Evaluation (ARISE) trial,40,41 and the ongoing ARISE FLUIDS trial following on from the ARISE FLUIDS observational study.42

In conclusion

Fluid trials have been central to the evolution of the ANZICS CTG, to evidence-based practice in Australian and New Zealand ICUs and to global recognition of both the CTG and Australian and New Zealand clinical practice as world leading.

Rinaldo was always at the centre of these programs to which he brought his incisive mind, good humour, passion, and commitment. It now falls to others to continue that work which we are confident he would agree is the best way to honour his legacy.
