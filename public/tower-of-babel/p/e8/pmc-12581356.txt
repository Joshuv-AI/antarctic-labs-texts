Odontoid base hypodensity and its role in type II fracture risk and nonunion: a CT study
Authors: Wei-xin Dong, Weihu Ma, Nanjian Xu, Yong Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType II odontoid fractures show high incidence and a notable risk of nonunion. Whether regional variation in trabecular density at the odontoid base contributes to this vulnerability remains unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed cervical CT scans from 136 adults. Standardized oval regions of interest (ROIs) were placed within cancellous bone while avoiding cortex and sclerosis. Mean Hounsfield unit (HU) values were compared across three predefined regions—the odontoid tip, odontoid base, and C2 vertebral body—with age- and sex-stratified analyses using analysis of variance and multiple-comparison adjustment. Two blinded observers performed the measurements, and reproducibility was quantified with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).ResultsHU values were highest at the odontoid tip, lowest at the base, and intermediate at the C2 body. On average, HU declined by ~ 51% from tip to base and then partially increased at the body. This regional pattern persisted across age and sex strata, and older individuals showed lower base HU. All primary regional comparisons remained statistically significant after adjustment.ConclusionsThe odontoid base appears to be a structurally vulnerable zone with reduced trabecular density. This hypodensity may help explain the high incidence and may be associated with nonunion of type II odontoid fractures, particularly in elderly patients; however, biomechanical and clinical studies are required before informing definitive treatment recommendations.

Introduction

Type II odontoid fractures are common in older adults and carry a notable risk of nonunion; factors beyond morphology, alignment, stability, and comorbidity—such as regional trabecular bone quality at the odontoid base—remain underexplored [1, 2]. Opportunistic Hounsfield units (HU) from routine CT provide a practical surrogate for cancellous bone quality, but estimates vary with scanner settings and reconstruction kernels and are seldom phantom-calibrated in routine workflows; therefore our inferences are descriptive rather than causal [3–5]. Prior work indicates structural vulnerability at the odontoid base and regional heterogeneity within C2, with potential age- and sex-related differences; however, standardized ROI protocols isolating the base remain limited [6–8]. Hypothesis and aims. We hypothesized that the odontoid base has lower HU than the tip and the C2 body and that this pattern persists across age and sex. Accordingly, we (i) quantified regional HU using a standardized ROI with blinded reproducibility assessment and (ii) evaluated age-/sex-related trends with multiplicity-adjusted comparisons.

Materials and procedures

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed cervical CT scans from 136 adults (73 males and 63 females; mean age 52.3 years, range 18–90 years) performed between February 2018 and February 2019 at Ningbo No. 6 Hospital. Eligible scans were consecutively identified from the radiology database and limited to adults without prior cervical trauma, surgery, deformity, tumor, or infection, to reduce selection bias; however, this sampling framework also limits generalizability to fracture-prone or osteoporotic populations. Inclusion criteria were: (i) complete cervical CT imaging covering C2; and (ii) no history of cervical spine trauma. Exclusion criteria were: prior cervical surgery, congenital or acquired spinal deformities, and pathological conditions such as tumors or infections. Only the first eligible scan per participant was included to avoid duplication. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo No. 6 Hospital (Approval No. 2022-016(K)). Given the retrospective design, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

CT image acquisition and analysis

Cervical CT examinations were performed using a Philips Brilliance 64-slice scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Amsterdam, Netherlands) with tube voltage 120 kVp, automatic tube current modulation (100–250 mA), collimation 64 × 0.625 mm, and a reconstructed slice thickness of 0.5 mm. Images were exported in DICOM format and imported into Mimics software (version 20.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for three-dimensional reconstruction and quantitative analysis (Fig. 1). For each subject, oval regions of interest (ROIs) were manually placed on axial images of the odontoid process and axis body. ROIs were positioned within cancellous bone to maximize trabecular inclusion while avoiding cortical margins, sclerosis, or vascular channels. To reduce partial-volume effects, ROI areas were standardized to 40–80 mm² and positioned at least 1 mm away from the endosteal cortex. Measurements were independently performed by two blinded observers, and reproducibility was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). HU values were automatically extracted by the software and recorded to two decimal places, representing relative trabecular attenuation. Given the retrospective nature and absence of phantom calibration, HU measurements should be interpreted as semi-quantitative descriptors of bone density rather than absolute volumetric density. The axis was divided into three anatomical zones: (1) the odontoid tip (apical third above the superior articular surface), (2) the odontoid base (subjacent to the articular surface), and (3) the C2 vertebral body (mid-vertebral level) (Fig. 2). HU values were measured across three consecutive axial slices in each region and averaged. All measurements were independently performed by two spine surgeons (≥ 5 years of experience) who were blinded to demographic information and to each other’s results. Each observer repeated measurements on a random subset of 30 subjects after a 2-week interval to assess reproducibility. The mean of repeated values was used for primary analyses, and intra- and inter-observer reliability was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 17.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with range. The normality of distributions was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was assessed with Levene’s test. For sex-based comparisons, independent-samples t-tests were applied. Regional differences in HU (odontoid tip, odontoid base, C2 body) were examined using repeated-measures ANOVA with Region as the within-subject factor and Sex or Age group as between-subject factors. When the sphericity assumption was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. Effect sizes were reported as partial η² for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise contrasts, with 95% confidence intervals provided where applicable. To assess measurement reproducibility, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement model [ICC(2,1) and ICC(2,k)]. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Regional HU distribution

The mean HU values for the odontoid tip, base, and C2 body were 651.4 ± 188.3, 318.4 ± 98.8, and 397.5 ± 93.6, respectively (Table 1; Fig. 3). Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant effect of Region (F(2,252) = 202.3, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.53). Post-hoc comparisons confirmed that the odontoid base had lower HU values than both the tip and the body (all P < 0.001).

Sex-specific analysis

This regional pattern was consistent in both sexes (Table 2). Mean HU values in males were 679.4, 334.0, and 409.6 across the three regions, compared with 618.9, 300.4, and 383.4 in females. Although the main effect of sex reached statistical significance (F(1,252) = 14.3, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.019), the effect size was very small, indicating limited clinical relevance. No significant Region × Sex interaction was detected (F(2,252) = 0.30, P = 0.74, partial η²=0.001).

Age-stratified analysis

Age-stratified analysis demonstrated a progressive decline in HU values with increasing age (Table 3). In participants aged 18–39 years, mean values were 725.1, 360.6, and 455.9 for the three regions; in the 40–59 year group they were 694.2, 343.8, and 414.1; and in the 60–90 year group 589.9, 282.9, and 355.6. The main effect of age group was significant (F(2,252) = 27.6, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.073). The Region × Age group interaction was not significant (F(4,252) = 0.58, P = 0.68) (Table 4).

Discussion

High incidence of type II odontoid fractures

Type II odontoid fractures represent the predominant dens injury and account for a substantial proportion of cervical fractures in older adults [1, 2, 9–11]. This pattern arises at the odontoid–body junction, a transitional zone in morphology and bone quality. Classic histomorphometry has shown that the base of the dens exhibits reduced trabecular volume/connectivity and a thinner cortex compared with the tip and the C2 body, suggesting intrinsic structural vulnerability [6]. In our cohort, CT-based HU mapping demonstrated a pronounced reduction at the odontoid base (mean 318.4 HU), approximately 51% lower than the tip (651.4 HU) and significantly lower than the C2 body (397.5 HU) (Table 1; Fig. 3). These observations are consistent with region-specific imaging reporting HU/BMD heterogeneity within C2 and relatively lower density at the dens–body transition, with additional modulation by age and sex [7, 8]. Finite-element analyses further localize stress concentrations to this junctional region, providing a plausible mechanical context for the clinical predilection of type II fractures [12]. Taken together, our results align with histologic, imaging, and biomechanical evidence that the odontoid base is a relatively hypodense, mechanically disadvantaged zone [6–8, 12]. Because HU estimates depend on scanner/reconstruction parameters and are often obtained without phantom calibration, these findings should be interpreted descriptively rather than causally [3–5].

Factors contributing to nonunion of type II odontoid fractures

Reported nonunion rates for type II odontoid fractures vary widely, particularly with nonoperative management, whereas fixation generally achieves higher fusion rates; this variability likely reflects differences in patient age, fracture pattern, and treatment protocol rather than a single cause [13–15]. Vascular compromise has long been proposed as a contributor; however, classic and contemporary anatomic studies demonstrate an arterial arcade supplying the dens and C2 body, suggesting that ischemia alone is unlikely to account for the consistently higher nonunion observed at the odontoid base [15–17]. Insufficient local bone stock and trabecular quality at the base appear to be more decisive. Histomorphometry and CT/micro-CT studies have shown sparse trabecular connectivity and unfavorable microarchitecture at the base compared with adjacent regions, supporting a “structural weak zone” concept [6, 18]. Because indirect fracture healing relies on callus bridging across disrupted trabeculae, a relative lack of trabecular substrate at the fracture plane could biologically limit union potential [18]. Consistent with these anatomic data, our cohort demonstrated significantly lower HU at the base than at the odontoid tip and C2 body, reinforcing a regionally hypodense transitional zone that may predispose to nonunion. Mechanical factors likely compound this biological disadvantage. Larger fracture footprints (as in type III patterns that extend into the C2 body) are associated with higher fusion, whereas type II fractures present smaller contact areas and greater instability, both of which are unfavorable for healing [14]. Finite-element and biomechanical analyses also localize elevated stresses to the odontoid–body junction under physiological loading, which may further hinder consolidation at this interface [12]. Finally, interposition of soft tissues—most notably the intact transverse ligament—has been documented at the fracture site in some nonunions, providing an additional mechanical barrier to osseous bridging in selected cases [19]. Taken together, these vascular, structural, and mechanical considerations provide a multifactorial explanation for the relatively high nonunion risk observed in type II odontoid fractures, while underscoring that our HU findings are descriptive and should not be interpreted as independently causal.

Aging and prognostic implications

Age-related bone loss further amplifies the vulnerability of the odontoid base. In our cohort, patients aged 60–90 years demonstrated a 32.7% reduction in HU values at the base compared with younger individuals (282.9 vs. 360.6 HU, P < 0.05). This decline reflects trabecular rarefaction, cortical thinning, and microarchitectural deterioration consistent with senile osteoporosis [7, 13, 20]. Histomorphometric analyses have likewise reported that trabecular volume at the odontoid base is markedly reduced compared with the tip and the C2 body, reinforcing its role as a structural weak zone [6]. These age-related structural deficits limit the substrate available for callus formation and may impair fracture healing [18]. Additional vascular and mechanical changes compound this problem in the elderly. The odontoid receives blood from a delicate vascular arcade [15, 16], and age-related atherosclerosis can reduce perfusion. Degenerative cervical changes and instability may also introduce micromotion and abnormal stress at the fracture plane, further hindering union [19]. Consequently, nonunion rates exceed 60% in patients over 65 years when managed nonoperatively [13, 14]. These findings underscore the need for age-specific management strategies. For older patients with type II odontoid fractures and high nonunion risk, early surgical stabilization (anterior screw fixation or posterior fusion) may be preferable to conservative treatment [14]. Adjunctive use of anti-osteoporotic therapies, including bisphosphonates or anabolic agents, has been shown to improve bone mass and may enhance healing potential [20, 21]. Future studies should evaluate combined biomechanical and metabolic interventions tailored to geriatric populations. Anatomical variants. Beyond age-related hypodensity, os odontoideum—a congenital or post-traumatic separation of the dens—can intrinsically compromise C1–C2 stability. Population-based CT series document its non-trivial incidence and anatomo-radiological significance, underscoring the need to distinguish variant-driven instability from metabolic bone vulnerability when interpreting HU maps and planning fixation. Incorporating screening for os odontoideum may refine risk stratification and procedural choice in patients with odontoid-related pathology [22].

Clinical implications

This study indicates that the odontoid base, with its markedly reduced bone density, represents a structural weak point prone to both fracture and nonunion. In clinical decision-making, stability should be evaluated not only by fracture morphology and alignment but also by considering bone quality at the odontoid base. Elderly patients and those with osteoporosis—who face the highest risk of nonunion—may benefit from early surgical stabilization (e.g., anterior odontoid screw fixation or posterior cervical fusion) rather than prolonged conservative management. Adjunctive anti-osteoporotic therapies such as bisphosphonates or anabolic agents may further support bone healing, although direct evidence in odontoid fractures remains limited. Future prospective studies should investigate integrated protocols that combine biomechanical stabilization with metabolic bone interventions to improve outcomes in this high-risk population.

Limitations

This single-center retrospective study used opportunistic CT HU as a surrogate for trabecular bone quality without phantom calibration, which may introduce measurement bias and limits cross-scanner generalizability. Although ROI placement was standardized and reproducibility was quantified with blinded, repeated measurements and ICCs, HU remains an indirect measure and should not be interpreted as absolute bone mineral density. Importantly, participants with acute fractures or known osteoporosis were not included, and scans were drawn from consecutively identified adults undergoing routine cervical CT for non-neoplastic indications; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to fracture-prone or osteoporotic populations. Finally, we did not perform biomechanical testing or analyze longitudinal clinical outcomes (e.g., union/nonunion), so the reported associations should be viewed as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

The odontoid base demonstrates substantially lower trabecular attenuation than the odontoid tip and the C2 body on CT-derived HU mapping. This region therefore appears to be a structurally weak transitional zone that may help explain the clinical predilection for type II odontoid fractures and their risk of nonunion, particularly in older adults. These observations are associative rather than causal, given the retrospective design and absence of phantom calibration or outcome data. In clinical decision-making, fracture assessment may benefit from consideration of local bone quality at the odontoid base in addition to morphology and alignment, while prospective biomechanical and clinical studies are needed to determine how best to integrate such information into treatment algorithms.
