Low Sunscreen Awareness and Misconceptions in Skin of Colour With Acne‐Related Post‐Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation: A Cross‐Sectional Study Highlighting the Need for Targeted Education
Authors: Xiaozhun Hang, Davin Sui Lim, Lisa Byrom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground/ObjectivesPost‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) is a frequent sequela of acne in patients with skin of colour (SOC). While sunscreen is widely recommended for PIH prevention, little is known about real‐world sunscreen practices in this group. This study aimed to evaluate sunscreen use, barriers and factors influencing adherence in SOC patients with acne‐related PIH.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted on 51 SOC patients (FST III–VI) who attended a dermatology clinic in Australia between February and June 2025. An investigator‐developed questionnaire assessed sunscreen use frequency, reapplication, application amount, tinted sunscreen uptake, and perceived barriers. Associations were examined using chi‐square/Fisher's exact tests and logistic regression analyses. Analyses were exploratory.ResultsDaily sunscreen use was inconsistent, with 39.2% applying occasionally (< 3 times/week) and only 33.3% applying daily. Reapplication was inadequate, with 57.5% (23/40) reapplying only when outdoors for prolonged periods. Most patients (79.5%, 31/39) applied less than one finger‐length to the head and neck. Tinted sunscreen uptake was low, though shade match was a key determinant of adherence among users. Few patients recognised sunscreen's role in PIH prevention (19.6%), with professional advice emerging as an important factor associated with correct belief (no advice vs. advice, OR 0.04, p = 0.01).ConclusionsSunscreen use among SOC patients with acne‐related PIH was suboptimal, with inadequate daily use, reapplication, and quantities applied. Clinician‐led education was a key factor in influencing patient beliefs, highlighting the need for targeted counselling. Expanding shade‐diverse, cosmetically elegant, tinted formulations may further improve adherence in SOC populations.Keywords: acne, adherence, post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation, skin of colour, sunscreen

Introduction

Post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) is a common sequela of acne and may occur with or without scarring or persistent inflammation, particularly in skin of colour (SOC) individuals (Fitzpatrick Skin Types III–VI) (Figure 1) [1, 2]. PIH can be long‐lasting. In a multicentre study, over half of the SOC participants had PIH persisting for at least 1 year, and 22.3% reported persistence for 5 years or longer. Many rated PIH as equally or more bothersome than acne itself [3]. This pigmentary aftermath contributes significantly to psychosocial distress even after the resolution of active acne, as facial pigmentation can affect self‐esteem, social confidence and quality of life [4].

Acne develops through interacting pathways including genetic susceptibility, follicular hyperkeratinisation, excess sebum production, hormonal influences, Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) proliferation, inflammation and environmental exposures [5]. Androgens and IGF‐1 enhance sebaceous activity, while innate immune activation, such as NLRP3 inflammasome signalling, amplifies inflammation and tissue injury [6, 7]. These processes promote acne lesion formation.

Acne‐related PIH results from inflammation‐driven melanocyte activation and disruption of the basal layer. Cytokines released during acne inflammation stimulate melanogenesis. When the basal epidermis is damaged, melanin may drop into the dermis, where it is phagocytosed by macrophages to form melanophages that contribute to persistent pigmentation [8]. In SOC, melanocytes are also larger, more numerous, and more reactive, leading to deeper and more prolonged PIH [9]. This increased melanocyte responsiveness explains the disproportionately high PIH burden in SOC.

Current treatment strategies for acne‐related PIH include topical depigmenting agents (hydroquinone, azelaic acid, retinoids and cysteamine) and procedural modalities such as chemical peels, lasers, and radiofrequency microneedling (Table 1) [5, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15]. However, these interventions are often costly, yield variable results, and may paradoxically worsen PIH in darker skin types due to their increased melanocyte reactivity.

In this context, photoprotection is essential as both a preventive and adjunctive measure [16]. Tinted broad‐spectrum sunscreens containing iron oxides can attenuate UVA1 and visible light (VL)‐induced melanogenesis by 93%–98%, improving dyschromia [17]. Consistent use also enhances the efficacy of depigmenting agents. Broad‐spectrum sunscreens with added anti‐inflammatory ingredients have demonstrated reducing post‐laser PIH [12, 13].

Ultraviolet radiation (UVR) and VL have complex effects on immunity, pigmentation, and acne [18]. In pigmentary disorders, they exacerbate PIH through melanocyte stimulation and inflammatory cascades, with stronger impact in SOC individuals (Figure 2) [19]. In SOC, high‐energy visible (HEV) blue light activation of the Opsin‐3 (OPN3) pathway upregulates melanogenic enzymes and stabilises tyrosinase, amplifying pigmentation [20]. UVR may also modulate the skin microbiome, including shifts in C. acnes, potentially worsening acne [5]. While some patients experience transient improvement with sun exposure, up to half report seasonal flares, particularly in summer [21]. UVA1 and VL may reduce inflammation and C. acnes colonisation without increasing cytokines or sebum [22], whereas UVB promotes proinflammatory cytokine expression, keratinocyte proliferation, and sebum production [23]. These photobiological effects may indirectly perpetuate PIH. Inflammation from acne upregulates melanogenesis and can damage the basal epidermis, allowing melanin to descend into the dermis and be phagocytosed into melanophages, causing persistent PIH. In FST III‐VI, HEV blue light (~415 nm) activates OPN3, triggering Ca2+‐dependent signalling that increases MITF activity and melanogenic enzyme expression, further intensifying and prolonging pigmentation. Despite these insights, limited data exist on real‐world sunscreen practices among SOC patients with PIH from acne. This study aims to evaluate sunscreen use and behaviours in SOC individuals with acne‐related PIH attending a dermatology clinic. It also examined whether patients had received sunscreen advice and their awareness of sunscreen's role in preventing pigmentary sequelae.

Materials and Methods

A cross‐sectional survey was conducted at a single‐institution dermatology centre in Brisbane, Australia between 15 February 2025 and 15 June 2025. Participants were recruited consecutively from clinic attendees. The study included 51 adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with FST III to VI who presented with PIH secondary to acne, with or without scarring.

Participants completed a nine‐structured questionnaire that assessed sunscreen use frequency, type, reapplication practices, and perceived barriers to sunscreen use (Data S1). The survey instrument was investigator‐developed and not subjected to formal validation procedures. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics, sunscreen use patterns, and reported barriers. Associations between categorical variables were examined using chi‐square tests. When expected cell counts were small, Fisher's exact test was applied. A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted to identify factors associated with the belief that sunscreen use prevents PIH in acne patients. All analyses were conducted using Stata Version 19 (StataCorp, Texas, USA).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Fifty‐one participants with FST III‐VI and a history of acne with PIH, with or without scarring completed the survey. The mean age was 28.6 years (standard deviation 7.34, 95% CI 26.54–30.67, range 16–48), and 58.8% were female. FST IV was most common (43.1%), followed by FST V (33.3%) (Figure S1).

Sunscreen Usage Patterns

Sunscreen use patterns varied across the cohort (Figure S2). Overall, 21.6% (n = 11) reported never using sunscreen, while 39.2% (n = 20) used it occasionally (< 3 times per week). Daily use was reported by 33.3% (n = 17), and 5.9% (n = 3) applied sunscreen twice daily. None reported using sunscreen more than twice per day.

Reapplication frequency differed among participants. Among sunscreen users (n = 40), 32.5% (n = 13) reported never reapplying, 57.5% (n = 23) reapplied only when outdoors for long periods, and 10.0% (n = 4) reapplied once more. No users reported reapplying every 2–3 h.

For application quantity, responses were available from 39 sunscreen users. Of these, 79.5% (n = 31) applied less than one finger‐length to the head and neck region, 15.4% (n = 6) applied one finger‐length, and 5.1% (n = 2) applied two or more finger‐lengths. Expectedly, non‐users accounted for nearly all missing data (n = 7).

For sunscreen users with available data on application amount, the cross‐tabulation of reapplication frequency by amount showed a non‐significant trend toward higher quantities among participants who reapplied more often (Table S1).

Barriers to Reapplication

Barriers to sunscreen reapplication differed significantly between participants who never reapplied sunscreen and those who did. To capture attitudes toward reapplication more broadly, all participants who reported never reapplying were included, regardless of baseline sunscreen use (n = 20). The most frequently cited reasons were perceiving reapplication as unnecessary for their skin type (45%), greasiness or heaviness (30%), and the belief that reapplication was unnecessary indoors (30%). These barriers were significantly associated with never reapplying sunscreen (p < 0.001, p = 0.002, and p = 0.002, respectively). Less frequent barriers included concern about acne flares (15%) and lack of knowledge regarding the need for reapplication (15%), both showing trend‐level associations (p = 0.06). Forgetting (5%) was not significantly associated. Lack of suitable colour match was not reported as a barrier by any participants (Table S2).

Tinted Sunscreen Use and Influencing Factors

Among the 51 participants, 25.5% (n = 13) reported using tinted sunscreen, with 12 as regular users and one as an occasional user (Figure S3). Within this subgroup, colour match was the most influential factor (92.3%), followed by preference for non‐comedogenic formulations (53.8%). Fewer participants cited SPF rating (23.1%) or cost (15.4%) as key considerations. None of the tinted sunscreen users reported VL protection as a reason for their choice (Figure S4).

Tinted sunscreen use was not significantly associated with FST. However, participants who believed sunscreen reduces PIH and scarring (χ 2 = 9.26, p = 0.01), prevents anti‐aging and fine lines (χ 2 = 11.85, p = 0.003), and lowers skin cancer risk (χ 2 = 7.27, p = 0.03) were significantly more likely to use tinted formulations. Belief in sunburn prevention showed no association (Table S3).

Perceived Sunscreen Benefits and Professional Advice

Most participants associated sunscreen benefits with sunburn prevention (90.2%) and reduced skin cancer risk (70.6%), whereas only 19.6% recognised its role in preventing PIH. Over half (56.9%) were uncertain about sunscreen's role in PIH or scarring, and the majority (72.5%) had not received professional advice regarding sunscreen use (Figure S5). These findings reflect overall sunscreen use without distinguishing between tinted and non‐tinted formulations. Receiving professional advice was significantly associated with higher sunscreen use (χ 2 = 13.58, p = 0.004), with those advised by a dermatologist, general practitioner, or other skin professional more likely to apply sunscreen regularly. However, professional advice was not significantly associated with reapplication frequency (Table S4).

Predictors of Belief That Sunscreen Prevents PIH

To better understand the factors influencing patient perspectives, responses were dichotomised into ‘yes’ versus ‘no/unsure’ to ensure adequate group sizes for analysis.

Chi‐square analyses identified several significant associations. Female participants were more likely than males to believe that sunscreen prevents PIH (χ 2 = 5.96, p = 0.02). Higher sunscreen use (χ 2 = 8.09, p = 0.04), more frequent reapplication (χ 2 = 8.09, p = 0.04), and applying larger amounts (χ 2 = 14.10, p = 0.003) were also associated with correct belief. Tinted sunscreen use (χ 2 = 7.60, p = 0.02) and receiving professional advice (χ 2 = 20.82, p < 0.001) were similarly associated (Table S5).

Univariate logistic regression was performed for each predictor, followed by model selection based on information criteria (Table S6). The final multivariable model included sex, tinted sunscreen use, and professional advice (Table S7). In this model, professional advice emerged as the most important predictor: participants without professional advice had significantly lower odds of believing sunscreen prevents PIH (OR 0.04, 95% CI 0.01–0.28, p = 0.001). Male sex showed lower odds of holding this belief (OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.01–1.62, p = 0.11), and tinted sunscreen use showed higher odds (OR 2.14, 95% CI 0.32–14.51, p = 0.44), although neither association reached statistical significance. The model was statistically significant (LR χ 2[3] = 23.71, p < 0.001) and explained 44.6% of the variance (Pseudo R 2 = 0.446).

Discussion

This study highlights important gaps in sunscreen knowledge and behaviours among patients with SOC and acne‐related PIH. Overall patterns of sunscreen use were suboptimal.

Previous studies consistently reported higher sunscreen use among female sex and younger age. For example, Weig et al. demonstrated that these demographic factors influence sunscreen behaviours in the context of skin cancer prevention [25]. National Australian Bureau of Statistics data similarly show that females are significantly more likely than males to use sunscreen on most days (48.8% vs. 27.0%). They also show that usage declines with increasing age [26]. In contrast, our study found no significant association between sunscreen use and either sex or acne onset group. Male participants were not less likely to use sunscreen than females (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.03–1.32, p = 0.09). Patients with adolescent acne (< 25 years) did not exhibit greater uptake compared to those with adult‐onset or persistent acne (OR 1.28, 95% CI 0.29–5.66, p = 0.74).

Several explanations are possible. First, as this was a dermatology clinic‐based cohort of patients with acne‐related PIH, behaviours may be influenced by disease burden and clinician counselling more than by demographic factors alone. Second, cultural perceptions among SOC patients, particularly the belief that natural melanin provides sufficient protection, may attenuate demographic predictors. This finding is consistent with previous literature indicating that SOC patients often believe their natural melanin affords sufficient photoprotection, thereby reducing the perceived need for sunscreen use [27]. The 2023 Australian Skin and Skin Cancer Centre's position statement notes that routine sunscreen is unnecessary in deeply pigmented skin unless prolonged outdoor exposure is anticipated, reflecting low skin cancer risk and vitamin D requirement [28]. However, our findings emphasise that in patients with pigmentary disorders such as PIH, broad‐spectrum tinted sunscreen remains an essential preventive and therapeutic intervention. Finally, the modest sample size may have limited power to detect demographic effects. Taken together, these findings suggest that educational strategies to improve sunscreen adherence in acne‐related PIH should target all demographic groups.

Awareness of sunscreen's role in preventing PIH was limited. Only 19.6% of patients recognised this benefit, while over half were uncertain. Although sunscreen is a cornerstone of PIH management, 78.4% applied only occasionally, once daily, or twice daily. Reapplication was inadequate. None reapplied every 2–3 h as recommended by the Australasian College of Dermatologists (ACD), and only 5.1% (2/39) applied ≥ 2 finger‐lengths [29]. Thus, no participant met both the frequency and quantity recommendations. Among sunscreen users, 79.5% applied less than one finger‐length to the face and neck, below the recommended two‐finger standard. Reasons for under‐application were not directly explored. However, perceptions that sunscreen was unnecessary for their skin type or for indoor use emerged as key barriers. 29.4% cited greasiness or heaviness as deterrents, in line with prior research highlighting cosmetic elegance as a key driver of adherence [25].

Although tinted formulations provide VL protection, none of the participants cited this as a reason for use. Prior studies indicate that SOC patients are more likely to use sunscreen for melasma than for PIH, reflecting limited awareness of its role in PIH prevention [30]. It is also possible that patients and clinicians may not prioritise sunscreen discussions in the context of acne care, where other issues, such as isotretinoin therapy, scarring, or skin cancer surveillance, often take precedence. While speculative and not directly assessed in our study, this possibility reinforces the importance of integrating sunscreen education within broader acne management. A previous study by Dale et al. demonstrated that patients value addressing acne‐related pigmentary sequelae, highlighting the need to strengthen photoprotection counselling in this setting [4]. An additional unexplored dimension is the potential gap between marketed claims (non‐comedogenic formulations) and patients' lived experiences of product tolerability, which may further undermine trust and adherence, although this remains to be formally studied.

Colour mismatch was reported by 5.9% of participants, exclusively among tinted sunscreen users. For these users, shade match was the main factor guiding product choice. Non‐users of tinted formulations did not cite colour match as a concern. While the small number of tinted sunscreen users limits the strength of conclusions, inadequate shade diversity remains a recognised barrier for SOC patients [14].

Professional advice emerged as a key factor associated with the belief that sunscreen prevents PIH. Participants without professional advice had 96% lower odds of endorsing this belief. This aligns with findings from Weig et al., who showed that dermatologist‐led recommendations increase sunscreen adherence among SOC patients [25]. These findings highlight the pivotal role of healthcare providers in shaping patient beliefs and behaviours, consistent with previous studies showing that dermatology counselling improves sunscreen adherence.

This study has several limitations. First, the questionnaire was investigator‐developed and not formally validated, introducing potential measurement bias. Second, the modest sample size (n = 51) limited the power of regression analyses and increased the risk of model overfitting. The multivariable findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Third, sunscreen behaviours and beliefs were self‐reported and may be affected by recall and social desirability biases, so estimates of use and reapplication may not reflect actual practices. Lastly, while our findings provide novel insights, the single‐centre, Australian clinic setting may limit generalisability. Larger multicentre studies using validated instruments are warranted to substantiate these findings. Despite these limitations, the consistently strong association between professional advice and correct beliefs highlights clinician‐led education as a potentially important and modifiable factor in PIH management. Unlike prior studies that examined sunscreen use in melasma, this study focused on acne‐related PIH, a condition with distinct patient priorities and behavioural influences.

Conclusion

Despite the established role of sunscreen in mitigating UV‐ and VL‐induced PIH, adherence among SOC patients with acne‐related PIH remains inconsistent. Cosmetic concerns, misconceptions about skin type, and lack of professional education are central barriers. These findings reinforce the need for culturally sensitive education addressing common misconceptions. Targeted dermatologist‐delivered education (Table 2), together with greater availability of cosmetically elegant, tinted formulations that provide VL protection, is essential to improve adherence. Future studies should assess whether structured interventions, such as visual demonstrations and tailored recommendations (tinted sunscreens suitable for darker skin tones), can improve both belief and adherence. Longitudinal research linking patient education to clinical outcomes in PIH would further clarify the impact of these interventions. In parallel, industry should be encouraged to expand the availability of cosmetically elegant, tinted formulations that offer broad‐spectrum and VL protection suitable for diverse skin tones. Finally, exploring the discrepancy between marketed sunscreen attributes (such as ‘non‐comedogenic’) and patient‐reported experiences should be investigated, as such gaps may influence patients' trust and adherence.
