Functional Validation of the Melon Fom‐1 Gene, Controlling Resistance to Fusarium oxysporum Races 0 and 2, by CRISPR/Cas9 Mutagenesis
Authors: Balasubramanian Muthusamy, Shahar Nizan, Amalia Bar‐Ziv, Rafael Perl‐Treves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFunctional validation of NLR genes is critical for confirming their specific roles and developing durable disease‐resistant crops. The Fom‐1 gene of Cucumis melo , controlling resistance to races 0 and 2 of F. oxysporum f. sp. melonis (FOM), had been identified by map‐based cloning as MELO3C022146, which encodes a TIR‐NBS‐LRR (TNL) protein. It resides in a head‐to‐head orientation adjacent to another TNL gene, Prv, controlling resistance to papaya ring spot virus (PRSV). In this study we validated the function of Fom‐1 in mediating FOM resistance by applying CRISPR/Cas9 editing to the resistant cultivar, Védrantais. Two gRNAs were designed to target exons 1 and 2, respectively, and mutations were introduced at both target sites, resulting in truncated open reading frames in both alleles. Inoculation assays of T1 plants with FOM races 2 and 0 revealed a breakdown of resistance, manifested by leaf necrosis and wilting, and susceptibility was stably inherited in the T2 generation. This proved that MELO3C022146, the candidate gene for Fom‐1, is responsible for resistance in melon cultivar Védrantais. Future studies will address the molecular functions of this gene as well as possible interactions between Fom‐1 and its neighbour R‐gene, Prv.Keywords: CRISPR/Cas9, Cucumis melo , Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. melonis , melon, NLR gene, resistance gene

Fusarium wilt caused by F. oxysporum f. sp. melonis (FOM) is a widespread, economically important disease of melon ( Cucumis melo ). The soilborne pathogen penetrates the root system and spreads through the xylem (Zvirin et al. 2010 and refs. therein). It persists in infected soils, defeating conventional plant protection regimes. Modern melon cultivars are therefore required to carry genetic resistance against the major FOM races. The Fom‐2 gene confers resistance to FOM0 and FOM1, while Fom‐1 provides resistance to races FOM0 and FOM2 (Risser et al. 1976). These two dominant genes proved durable over decades; they were mapped on the melon genome, and linked DNA markers were developed to assist breeding (Périn et al. 2002; Brotman et al. 2005; Oumouloud et al. 2008; Tezuka et al. 2009). QTLs for resistance to a fourth race, FOM1.2, were mapped as well (Perchepied et al. 2005; Ficcadenti et al. 2002). The Fom‐2 gene was identified on chromosome XI by map‐based cloning (Joobeur et al. 2004), and a candidate gene of the CNL subfamily of NLR genes was identified, sharing only low homology with the Fom‐1 candidate gene (see below). The Fom‐1 locus was located on melon chromosome IX, near the Prv gene, which confers resistance to the papaya ring spot virus (PRSV). The locus was cloned by Brotman et al. (2013) using a map‐based approach, and gene candidates for Fom‐1 (MELO3C022146) and Prv (MELO3C022145) were found. The two adjacent genes form a head‐to‐head oriented gene pair and encode TIR‐NBS‐LRR (TNL) proteins. The expression of the two genes in response to PRSV and FOM2 inoculation was studied by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR), and spatial patterns were analysed by a promoter‐reporter system (Normantovich et al. 2024). The function of Prv in controlling PRSV resistance was validated by knocking out the gene in a PRSV‐resistant genotype, using the CRISPR/Cas9 method, causing loss of resistance (Nizan et al. 2023). In the present study, we applied the same approach to validate the function of the Fom‐1 gene. Genetic transformation of melon, required to introduce the editing machinery to the plant, is technically challenging, with only a few reports of successful genome editing in this species. Validation of R‐gene function paves the way to future molecular studies and biotechnological applications.

To validate Fom‐1 function in controlling resistance to FOM races 0 and 2, we constructed a binary plasmid expressing Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 nuclease under the Solanum lycopersicum Ubiquitin promoter, the NptII marker gene for kanamycin resistance, and two guide RNAs (gRNA, Figure 1a), designed to target two sites in the first and second exons of Fom‐1, respectively (Figure 1b). The gRNAs were cloned following Dahan‐Meir et al. (2018), using the GoldenBraid 2.0 method (Sarrion‐Perdigones et al. 2014) under the Arabidopsis thaliana U6‐26 RNA Pol III promoter (Vazquez‐Vilar et al. 2016). Table S1 shows the oligonucleotides used for cloning. The target sites were selected to contain an appropriate restriction enzyme site, such that a mutation ~3 bp upstream of the PAM site will likely destroy the site and facilitate genotyping of the transgenic plants. Selection of targets and screening against possible off‐targets was carried out using the CRISPOR program (http://crispor.tefor.net, Haeussler et al. 2016), based on the reference melon genome (DHL92 v3.5.1; https://cucurbitgenomics.org).

Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation was applied to cotyledon explants from germinated seeds of the melon cultivar Védrantais, which is resistant to FOM races 0 and 2. Table S2 summarizes the resistance reactions of the melon genotypes used in this study. Figure S1 with the appended text fully describes the transformation and hardening stages of the protocol. Six plants (the T0 generation) were regenerated and hardened, three of which (1, 3 and 6) were independently derived, while the other plants (# 2, 4 and 5) must have resulted from early ramification in vitro. All were tested by PCR for presence of the NptII transgene (Figure 2a). Plants 1, 2, 4 and 5 were NptII and Cas9 positive and Plants 3 and 6 were negative and considered to have escaped kanamycin selection. Plants underwent vegetative multiplication by cuttings, and self‐pollinated to obtain T1 and T2 progeny.

We amplified the region around each gRNA target, resulting in a 335 bp amplicon for gRNA1 (Figure 2b) and 648 bp amplicon for gRNA2 (Figure 2d). When we digested the gRNA1 amplicon with Bsp143I, we could observe, as expected, full digestion of the Védrantais wild‐type sample, as well as samples from escapee Plants 3 and 6 (Figure 2c). The samples from Plants 4 and 5 had both an undigested (mutated) amplicon and a digested one, suggesting a heterozygous state, whereas Plants 1 and 2 did not have a digested allele, suggesting that both alleles have been mutated. As for gRNA2, we observed full TaqI digestion of the wild type (WT) and all six T0 plants (Figure 2e). However, sequencing the amplicons revealed several mutations in Plants 1, 2, 4 and 5. We realized that the TaqI site that we had selected, whose fourth nucleotide overlapped with the PAM motif, has not been affected by any of these mutations and concluded that 6‐bp cutters that digest ~3 bp upstream of the PAM site are preferable for genotyping purposes. We also realized that the situation was dynamic: analysis of leaf samples taken at a later stage suggested that Cas9 nuclease continued to act as the plant developed, and plants that appeared heterozygous lost the WT‐looking allele and became biallelic. Moreover, additional alleles that were not detected in the T0 generation appeared in the T1 progeny (see below), while some alleles found in the T0 samples were no more present in the T1. Full characterization of the alleles was achieved by direct sequencing of the PCR products, or by sequencing plasmids with cloned PCR products.

Table 1 lists, for each target site, the mutations that were stably inherited in the T1 generation. In total, we found two mutated alleles affecting gRNA1, a 5‐bp deletion, and a large, 6381 bp deletion that started from the third nucleotide of exon 1 (477 bp), spanned the entire first intron (5565 bp), and part of exon 2 (656 bp). This deletion became detectable only in the T1 progeny, in individuals that were homozygous for the large deletion, where other alleles did not mask it. Figure 2f shows an example of a T1 progeny with two individuals (nos. 6 and 7) lacking the gRNA1 target region due to the large deletion at the homozygous state, two heterozygous individuals (nos. 4 and 5) having a WT (digested) and a mutated (undigested, small deletion) allele, and the remaining four individuals are homozygous for the undigested allele. The 6381 bp deletion allele could be amplified using primers that resided, respectively, up‐ and downstream of the deleted region (Table S1), showing the precise boundaries of the deletion. Figure S2d shows the amplification of a 900 bp band encompassing the deletion borders.

For gRNA2, we found in the T1 generation three mutations, consisting of a 1 bp insertion, 1 bp deletion and an 8 bp deletion (Table 1).

The large deletion allele is unlikely to produce any protein product, as it lacks the start codon and the first 378 amino acids, out of 757 in the full protein. We analysed the putative translation products of all the smaller insertion and deletion mutations and saw that they encode severely truncated proteins, likely to be inactive (Table 1). Figure S3 shows the alignment of the predicted translation products of the mutated alleles, compared to the WT sequence. To technically check the specificity of mutagenesis, we amplified the region around the most likely off‐target site for gRNA1 and gRNA2 and sequenced it in each of the four mutated T0‐generation plants. We found that the region near the presumed off‐targets has not been mutated and remained identical to that of the WT (Figure S4).

We hypothesized that biallelic mutations in Fom‐1 would compromise resistance to FOM races 0 and 2 of the resistant genotype Védrantais. To evaluate this, T1 progeny were generated by self‐fertilization of the T0 plants and genotyped by PCR amplification of the gRNA1 region. Table S3 summarizes the genotyping data supporting the analyses below, showing that Family T1‐1, derived from T0 plant 1, showed 100% mutated alleles, while Family T1‐2 from T0 plant 2 had ~93% mutated alleles and ~7% WT alleles, respectively. Seedlings were inoculated by pouring FOM race 2 conidial suspensions in each pot. Seedlings of resistant Védrantais (the original genotype used for editing) and susceptible Charéntais‐T were inoculated as a control, and non‐inoculated seedlings of each group served as a negative control. Groups of about 10 seedlings per group (genotype × treatment combination) were tested. Figure 3 shows one out of three independent experiments: the other two, yielding similar results, are shown in Figure S5. We observed that non‐inoculated plants remained green and healthy, as were the inoculated plants of the resistant genotype, Védrantais. Notably, Plant 1 and Plant 2 T1 progenies displayed yellowing, leaf necrosis and eventually wilted, proving that resistance has been broken, and validating the function of the Fom‐1 candidate gene. A small proportion (8%–20%) of non‐symptomatic seedlings was present in some experiments, but this is typical also of the susceptible genotype, Charéntais‐T, where a few plants will escape or resist infection. Similar results were also obtained with T1 seeds from transgenic Plants 4 and 5 (Figure S5). The progeny of the non‐transformed Plants 3 and 6 remained resistant like their parental line, Védrantais (Figure S5E).

Because Fom‐1 confers resistance also to FOM race 0, we repeated the experiment with a race 0 strain and observed necrosis and wilting of the mutant T1 families 1 and 2, confirming the function of our candidate gene also against race 0 of the pathogen. Figure 4 shows the result of one out of three replicated experiments, and Figure S6 shows the two other replicates. Finally, we inoculated representative mutant progeny also with FOM race 1. As expected, the FOM1 resistant line, Charéntais‐Fom2, that carries the Fom‐2 allele, was fully resistant, while Charéntais‐Fom1 and Védrantais, that lack Fom‐2 as well as their Fom‐1‐mutated derivatives, remained highly susceptible to this race (Figure S7; summary of genotypes is provided in Table S2).

We followed the mutations also in the T2 generation. In T2 Family 16, the 5‐bp deletion in the gRNA1 target was fixed (homozygous in all individuals), and in Family 7, the large 6381 bp deletion was fixed. Figure S8 reports the inoculation of these two representative families, in two independently replicated experiments, showing that the mutated T2 progeny plants were susceptible to FOM2. We did not observe any phenotypic difference between the two mutations, the 5 bp deletion or the large 6381 bp deletion. Each of them conferred full susceptibility, because both were expected to act as null mutations.

This study thus proved the function of Fom‐1 (MELO3C022146) in controlling resistance of C. melo towards FOM races 0 and 2. From a technological perspective, genetic transformation of melon is a low‐efficiency, challenging process (Nuñez‐Palenius et al. 2008; García‐Almodóvar et al. 2017), with significant rates of escapes (non‐transgenic shoots) even under stringent selection conditions (Guis et al. 2000). Efficient transformation and regeneration remain prerequisites for CRISPR/Cas9‐based gene editing, although newer delivery systems such as virus‐induced genome editing (VIGE) may help bypass these constraints (Uranga and Daròs 2023). At present, ours is one of a handful of genome‐editing studies in melon (Giordano et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022; Nonaka et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Xin et al. 2022; Leibman et al. 2022; Pechar et al. 2022; Shirazi Parsa et al. 2023). We report high mutagenesis rates, with nearly all transgenic plants carrying mutations in both alleles and in both RNA targets, either in the T0 or later in the T1 generation. Consistent with previous reports (Ishizaki 2016), continued Cas9 activity led to additional mutations and dynamic allele segregation across generations.

Melon has 81 NLR genes (Garcia‐Mas et al. 2012), fewer than most plant species (Jia et al. 2015). In melon, only a few NLRs have been genetically mapped and functionally characterized. The Vat gene, a CNL on chromosome 5, provides resistance to Aphis gossypii and aphid‐transmitted viruses, and was validated by overexpression in an aphid‐susceptible background (Dogimont et al. 2014). A distinct allele of Vat in the WMR‐29 genotype confers strong resistance to powdery mildew (Boissot et al. 2024). Our previous study (Nizan et al. 2023), along with the current work, has added Prv and Fom‐1 to the short list of validated melon NLR genes. Two additional CNL genes, MELO3C015353 and MELO3C015354, have been mapped at the Zym locus and linked to resistance to zucchini yellow mosaic virus (Adler‐Berke et al. 2021) and to powdery mildew race 1 (Ning et al. 2014), awaiting functional validation.

Our high‐resolution mapping (Brotman et al. 2013) suggested that Fom‐1 specifies a distinct resistance trait as compared to its neighbour gene, Prv, which is required for PRSV resistance (Nizan et al. 2023). In a few head‐to‐head gene pairs, the paired genes have been shown to collaborate and deploy a defence response against one or more pathogens, according to the “integrated decoy” model, where one NLR acts as a sensor and the second as an executor (Césari, Kanzaki, et al. 2014; Césari, Bernoux, et al. 2014; Williams et al. 2014; De la Concepcion et al. 2018). Sensor proteins typically carry an additional domain that senses the pathogen effector. In the absence of the pathogen, the sensor inhibits the executor protein, whereas direct recognition of the effector releases such inhibition. The activated executor launches a defence response that, in our case, would block FOM attack or reduce its severity. In agreement with such model, Prv encodes a second, non‐canonical NBS domain that binds the cylindrical inclusion (CI) protein of PRSV (Normantovich et al. 2024), which could support its role as a typical sensor element. The two adjacent NLR genes had similar spatial patterns of expression, around vascular tissues and root tips, which could be interpreted in favour of a collaborative mode of action (Normantovich et al. 2024). Loss of the sensor NLRs, that inhibits the executor in the pathogen's absence, could activate the executor and produce an autoimmune phenotype (Wang et al. 2019), and such a mutation was indeed found for Prv (Nizan et al. 2023).

In the well‐studied examples in Arabidopsis and rice, the executor did not specify a resistant trait of its own. However, mapping per se cannot exclude the possibility that Fom‐1, the putative executor in our gene pair, is also required for PRSV resistance, in accordance with the integrated decoy model. This will need to be proven, in future studies, by mutating Fom‐1 in a PRSV‐resistant genotype. It also remains to be proven, by mutating Prv in a Fusarium‐resistant genotype, whether the Fusarium resistance function of Fom‐1 also requires the Prv gene. Separate editing experiments are needed because no melon cultivar carries both PRSV and FOM2 resistance. In fact, melon breeders have failed to combine in a single plant, by conventional crosses, resistance to both PRSV and to FOM2, despite numerous attempts involving large populations; at best, recombination events caused a necrotic ‘resistance’ phenotype. This persistent difficulty could be explained by the cooperative pair hypothesis, requiring physical interaction between the two partners, based on co‐evolution of the two genes as a haplotype. Trying to ‘force together’ by a rare cross‐over event the Fom‐1 and Prv alleles from unrelated background could have disrupted the fine protein interactions required for their functions, resulting in double susceptibility or necrotic responses. The analysis of sequence variation and evolutionary relationships among different Fom‐1 and Prv alleles across cultivars may provide valuable insight into their possible relationship.

Another interesting challenge would be to look for the Avr factor of Fusarium races FOM0 and FOM2 (either two separate factors, one for each race, or a common one present in both races), recognized by the Fom‐1 gene product. This has been achieved for another melon resistance gene, Fom‐2, specific to FOM races 0 and 1 (Joobeur et al. 2004), where a fungal gene required for recognition by melon Fom‐2 has been identified by genetic complementation of fungal isolates with the Avr candidate gene, resulting in avirulence (Schmidt et al. 2016).
