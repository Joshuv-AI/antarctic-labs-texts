Arrested Cooperative Electron Transfer by Stabilization of a Long-Lived Confined Unpaired Electron
Authors: Cheriehan Hessin, Mokhtar Ben Ghanem, Nolwenn Le Breton, Sylvie Choua, Aurélien Moncomble, Laurence Grimaud, Hervé Vezin, Marine Desage-El Murr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cooperative multielectron transfer is central to electron storage strategies and small-molecule activation and involves a thermodynamically favored second electron transfer occurring after the first electron transfer. This is the trademark of molecular systems with inverted redox potentials, but it is counterintuitive from an energetic point of view, and the nature of the transient intermediates involved has remained elusive. Using a nickel complex with inverted redox potentials, we show that a weakly coordinating anion (WCA), such as BArF 4 – ([B­(C6H3(CF3)2)4]−), can act as a kinetic trap for the second electron to be transferred. Combined electrochemistry, UV–visible spectroscopy, advanced EPR studies, and theoretical calculations support its existence as a stabilized, confined unpaired electron, close to the concept of a solvated electron. This work sheds light on the nature of energized intermediates in cooperative electron transfer and the possibility of influencing that process through counterion effects.

The design of molecular systems able to transfer several electrons is an attractive means to achieve thermodynamically challenging reactions, including the activation of small molecules,such as CO2 and N2, or to design artificial photosynthetic systems that require the accumulation of redox equivalents. , The ability of a chemical substance to transfer electrons is quantified by its redox potential, and successful multielectron transfer relies on the careful tuning of redox potentials in the designed systems. Compounds exhibiting several accessible oxidation states (and thus several redox couples) are able to transfer more than one electron overall and usually do so in a sequential manner, according to the ordering of the redox potentials of the successive couples (E°1 and E°2 for the first and second electron transfer, respectively, Figure ). Due to electrostatic repulsions resulting from charge accumulation, the second electron transfer is usually more difficult than the first one, and in the case of an oxidative process, this negative difference (ΔE° < 0, ΔE°= E°1 – E°2) indicates that the intermediate species D+ arising from single-electron transfer, is stable. The cyclic voltammogram (CV) exhibits two consecutive one-electron waves (Figure , red curve), and redox potentials in this case are said to be normally ordered.

Potential inversion refers to the ability of some compounds to transfer two electrons at the same time in a cooperative manner. In this case, the first electron transfer upon oxidation (or reduction) generates a reactive and unstable intermediate, which is more easily oxidized (or reduced) than the starting compound, thus readily achieving two-electron transfer. The CV of a compound (organic molecule or coordination complex) with inverted redox potentials exhibits a 2-electron wave (Figure , blue curve). The specific reasons behind a thermodynamically favored transfer of the second electron are often case-specific, but factors governing or influencing cooperative transfer include geometric changes, solvent effects, and counterion effects. Among the reported examples of metal complexes with inverted potentials, most are bimetallic complexes, such as iron-based [Fe, Fe] hydrogenase models, − dirhodium, diruthenium, and [Mo, W] complexes. Ligand design is a key element to perform cooperative electron transfer, and successful examples of mononuclear complexes (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Ru) with inverted potentials include engineered (bi)­pyridines − and arenes as ligands.

Inclusion of molecular units with inverted potentials in the design of artificial electron transfer systems is promising and a key step forward lies in the possibility of studying this thermodynamically favored second electron transfer by spectroscopic means.

In solid-state and materials chemistry, the concept of stabilized electrons, referred to as polarons, is well-known and has strong implications in the design of efficient energy transfer or storage devices and photoactive materials. − Their lifetimes can display stability of up to several hours. In solution, stabilized electrons are called solvated electrons and are highly reactive species with very limited lifetimes (in the ns to μs range), compared to the solid state, particularly in organic solvents. Apart from their well-known generation from sodium in liquid ammonia during the Birch reduction, synthetic access to these transient species under milder conditions has been reported by two-photon absorption through a conPET mechanism (consecutive photoinduced electron transfer) , or by using a plasmon source.

Here, we report the interception of the electron involved in the second transfer (E°2) and its stabilization as a confined unpaired electron through interaction with weakly coordinating anions (WCA) such as TfO–, BArF 4 – and BArF 5 – (BArF 4 –: [B­(C6H3(CF3)2)4]−, BArF 5 –: [B­(C6F5)4]−). Using combined CV, EPR, and UV–vis spectroscopies and theoretical calculations, we show that the nature of the WCA strongly influences the stability and electronic environment of the unpaired electron, to the extent that the weakest WCA (BArF 4 –) offers extended delocalization within its structure. This phenomenon, combining three distinctive featuresinverted potentials, WCA, and redox-active ligandsestablishes the possibility to stabilize in solution transient intermediates involved in multielectron transfers by chemical means, beyond electrochemical conditions.

Complex 1 is a stable nickel­(II) complex bearing two redox-active ligands in their radical semiquinone (SQ) form and was reported by Wieghardt and coworkers. Due to the redox-active character of the ligands, this complex could undergo ligand-based or metal-based redox events (Figures a and S1). The full CV of 1 in CH2Cl2 with tetrabutylammonium tetrafluoroborate (TBABF4) as the supporting electrolyte (Figure S2) displays a quasi-reversible 2-electron wave in the oxidation process (1→1 2+ ). The behavior in oxidation could result from compressed or inverted potentials and involve a metal-based oxidation through an ECE mechanism. , The intermediate obtained after the first electron transfer, 1+, is more easily oxidized than 1, and thus undergoes a subsequent electron transfer to give 1 2+ . Following reports by Geiger and coworkers, ,, showing that the stability of an intermediate is affected by the nature of the counterion from the supporting electrolyte, we explored the oxidation of 1 using less coordinating (TfO–) or weakly coordinating (BArF 5 – and BArF 4 –) counteranions. In all three cases, the CVs display two oxidation peaks attributable to two monoelectronic oxidations: 1 into 1 + and 1 + into 1 2+ , respectively (Figures c and S3–S8) with a quasi-reversible second oxidation in the case of TfO– and no reversibility for the second peak with BArF 5 – and BArF 4 –.

These observations are consistent with a possible stabilization of transient intermediate 1 + and, building on these electrochemical studies, we aimed to investigate the electronic structure of intermediate 1 + . To do so, DFT calculations (ωB97XD/SDD/6–311++G­(d, p), full details in Supporting Information) were carried out, and an electron on 1 + stabilized by the BArF 4 – counteranion was chosen as a typical example. First, complex 1 + without its counteranion was computed in both a doublet and quartet state. The first one represents ligand-based oxidation, leading to a structure such as [NiII(SQ)­(BQ)]+ with one unpaired electron, while the second one involves metal-centered oxidation, leading to [NiIII(SQ)2]+ with three unpaired electrons. In this case, the doublet spin state is clearly the most stable with an energy lower by more than 400 kcal/mol compared to the quartet state, which could be due to the difficulty in stabilizing the third unpaired electron.

Then, to check the effect of the BArF 4 – counteranion, we started from the optimized geometries of the doublet and quartet structures, onto which we added a BArF 4 – entity at a chosen distance (defined between the boron atom and the barycenter of the central ring of one of the ligands). The data are reported in Figure . A striking difference is that the doublet and quartet are now close to each other. More interestingly, at the minimum, located around 8 Å, the quartet is lower in energy than the doublet. While the magnitude of this effect (0.7 kcal/mol) is not large, the important result is the strong stabilization of the quartet state, which was unreachable without the counteranion. These results suggest an influence of the counterion on both the electronic nature and the stability of intermediate 1 + .

The electronic structure of intermediate 1 + was then probed by EPR spectroscopy, and the signal was recorded in CH2Cl2 at room temperature after generation either by an in situ coupled electrochemical setup (Figure a,b with TBABArF 4 – as the supporting electrolyte) or by chemical oxidation of 1 by FcY (Fc+ = ferrocenium), a one-electron oxidizing agent incorporating the counterion Y–. In the latter case, with Y– = BF4 –, no signal could be detected, consistent with the direct cooperative 2 e– transfer observed in CV. With Y– = TfO–, a weak signal was detected with g = 2.003 (Figure S12). This value is consistent with a free organic radical localized on a carbon atom and suggests a ligand-based single oxidation, leading to an intermediate [NiII(SQ)­(BQ)]+. The decay of this species was followed and showed low stability (Figure S13). Switching to weakly coordinating anions BArF 5 – and BArF 4 –, a different picture emerges, as in both cases the signal detected has a g value of 1.996, suggesting a different environment for the unpaired electron (Figures c, S14, and S16). The signal is stronger with BArF 4 – than with BArF 5 –, and their respective decays show different kinetics (Figures d, S15, and S17), supporting notable stabilization by BArF 4 – even when increasing the temperature (Figure e,f). Generation of 1 + ·BArF 4 – by electrolysis or chemical oxidation gives similar results (Figures S33 and S34).

This isotropic signal is centered at g iso = 1.996 at room temperature. Kinetic monitoring of the EPR signal revealed a first-order decay at room temperature with a t 1/2 of 3.9 h (Figure d). The EPR spectrum shows a weak anisotropy of the g factor at 100 K due to the progressive localization of the electron between the BArF 4 – counteranion and the nickel complex (Figure S9). This weak rise in anisotropy may be from the nature of the solvent medium and the BArF 4 – stabilization contribution to the electron. Following stability studies over time and temperature (Figures S18–S20), complete evaporation of the solvent led to a solid-state spectrum (Figure S21) with features similar to those of a solid-state polaron, thus hinting toward a stabilized, delocalized unpaired electron.

To get better insight into the electron location and surroundings, pulsed EPR experiments were performed using the 2D HYSCORE sequence at 30 K (Figure ). The echo field sweep recorded at 30 K shows an EPR signal with no g anisotropy compared to those measured at 100 K in CW mode. The g factor measured is 2.001, indicating a value close to the free electron g value. However, the change in the g value compared to that measured at higher temperatures is due to the g-average contribution coming from dipolar broadening. Moreover, compared to the signal measured at room temperature, we observe a drastic increase of the line width from 12 to 25 G. This broadening effect comes from spin–spin interactions between free delocalized electrons via dipolar interaction. The 2D-HYSCORE spectrum shows interaction with the BArF 4 – counterion, especially with 19F, 11B, and 10B atoms (Figures a and S35–S38). In the spectrum, we observe, close to the proton nuclear frequency, a weak signal at the 19F Larmor frequency (13.5 MHz). This signal arises from a pure dipolar interaction between the free electron and the 19F atom. A vertical shift of 0.54 MHz measured along the diagonal gives an e––19F distance of 0.5 nm. The proton ridge is mainly dominated by distant protons from the ligand, with a dipolar interaction of 0.8 MHz yielding an e– −1H distance of 0.46 nm.

2D-HYSCORE spectrum of intermediate 1 + ·BArF 4 – generated by electrolysis, recorded at 30 K with a value of 180 ns and an increment of 24 ns along the t1 and t 2 dimensions. The spectrum was recorded at a maximum magnetic field of 348.5 mT.

Additionally, the 1H ridge exhibits an isotropic hyperfine coupling of 11.2 MHz, which reveals a spin polarization of the free electron wave function on the neighboring ligand or counterion molecules. Interestingly, the distances between the BArF 4 – counterion and the ligand, calculated by DFT, are very close to those measured with HYSCORE experiments, which shows that the electron is in between the ligand and counterion.

In this spectrum, we can also observe the features of both boron isotopes (11B and 10B), with only the nuclear frequency of 11B in the (+,+) quadrant at 4.8 MHz and a weak coupling of 2.6 MHz for 10B at 1.6 MHz. In the (−,+) quadrant, only a 10B feature is observed due to quadrupolar splitting. Similar results were obtained by chemical generation with FcBArF 4 – (Figures S33 and S34), whereas for the TfO– anion, only weak carbon coupling is observed, indicating, in this case, electron localization on the ligand (Figure S36). Combined insights gained from CV, EPR, and DFT suggest that the nature of this intermediate can be described as a [1 2+ ·e–·BArF 4 –] species.

Titration of complex 1 by FcY was followed by UV–vis spectroscopy. When Y– = BF4 –, clean and direct conversion of 1 into doubly oxidized 1 2+ ·2BF4 – with a characteristic band at 498 nm, is observed together with an isosbestic point (Figures a and S41). When Y– = BArF 4 –, titration with up to 1 equiv. of FcBArF 4 induces the diminution of the characteristic band of 1 (879 nm), while two new bands appear at 398 and 700 nm, and an isosbestic point is observed at 736 nm (Figures b, S39, and S42). This new UV–vis signature is clearly distinct from that of independently prepared doubly oxidized 1 2+ ·2BArF 4 – with a band at 514 nm, and thus does not correspond to direct 2e– transfer (Figure S40). Upon addition of a second equivalent, accumulation of unreacted oxidant FcBArF 4 is observed (623 nm), showing that only a 1-electron transfer occurs from 1. The presence of small amounts of 1 and 1 2+ ·2BArF 4 – after the addition of 1 and 2 equiv. of FcBArF 4 in Figure b is attributable to a slight in situ disproportionation of 1 + ·BArF 4 – into 1 and 1 2+ ·2BArF 4 – (Figure S43). These results are consistent with the inversion of potentials in complex 1 in the presence of BF4 – and a normal ordering of potentials of 1 in the presence of BArF 4 –. Worthy of note is the fact that the intermediate 1 + ·BArF 4 – can also be observed by UV–vis under nondeaerated conditions, thus demonstrating its exceptional stability.

In the literature, solvated electrons have been reported in both water − and organic solvents. The possible reactivity of intermediate 1 + ·BArF 4 – with aryl halides was studied using chlorobenzene, bromobenzene, and the more easily reduced p-CF3-bromobenzene, in CH2Cl2. In all cases, the EPR signal remains, while the decay observed is similar to what is observed without any substrate (Figures S22–S27). However, the introduction of ascorbic acid (up to 10 equiv.) led to a faster kinetic decay of intermediate 1 + ·BArF 4 –, thus suggesting possible reactivity (Figures S28–S32). These findings indicate the exceptional stability of this confined electron, and future work will be directed at leveraging external effects to influence and control electron transfer.

In conclusion, we have shown that the interception of a transient oxidation state in a thermodynamically favored 2e– transfer is possible using a weakly coordinating anion. This arrested state displays kinetic stability over several hours and, when analyzed by continuous and pulsed EPR techniques, is shown to have spectroscopic characteristics close to those of a solvated electron. However, while the latter is a highly energetic transient species that is weakly stabilized by a cage of solvent molecules, which are quite small in essence and therefore provide limited stabilization, the species observed in this study displays longer stability due to its highly delocalized nature. Although the ability of perfluorinated boronates as WCAs to stabilize free electrons as polarons is known in the solid state, it was never reported under homogeneous conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of a stabilized polaron observed in solution in the context of a small molecule coordination complex.

Pulsed EPR experiments show a magnetic coupling of the electron with the boron and fluorine atoms from BArF 4 –, which supports a scenario in which the “energized” second electron is intercepted and confined on a surface potential involving the counterion, rather than being stabilized on the redox-active ligand. Considering the striking ability of 3d metal complexes with redox-active ligands to perform activation of small molecules, such as O2, through a 2 e– redox mechanism, this work opens perspectives in the implementation of controlled electron transfer strategies in solution.
