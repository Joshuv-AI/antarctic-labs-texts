When methylene blue fails: a case of methemoglobinemia induced by azoxystrobin and propiconazole managed by exchange transfusion
Authors: Rajat Goyal, Rushabh Bora, Anand Jain, Gopal Krishana Bohra
Published: 2025

Background

Methemoglobinemia is a potentially fatal condition where the oxygen-carrying capacity of hemoglobin in the blood is reduced due to the conversion of one or more of the four iron atoms in hemoglobin from the reduced ferrous [Fe2+] form to the oxidized ferric [Fe3+] form [1]. Under normal physiological conditions, the concentration of methemoglobin in the blood ranges from 0 to 2%. Concentrations between 10% and 20% are generally well-tolerated; however, levels exceeding this threshold are commonly linked to clinical symptoms. Methemoglobin concentrations surpassing 70% can be fatal [2]. In an otherwise healthy individual, cyanosis may become clinically apparent with methemoglobin levels as low as 10% [3]. The characteristic “chocolate brown blood” appearance can be observed at concentrations as low as 15%. It is commonly induced by exposure to exogenous substances such as topical anaesthetics, dapsone, chloroquine and nitric oxide. Dietary nitrates and nitrites, particularly in food and water, are a major cause in some regions [4].

While there are reported cases linking fungicides like Mancozeb [5] and pesticides, such as nitrobenzene-containing compounds like Indoxacarb, along with other chemicals like aluminium phosphide and paraquat, which are commonly implicated in methemoglobinemia [6], the association of methemoglobinemia with Azoxystrobin and Propiconazole has not been described in the current literature.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of methemoglobinemia following ingestion of a fungicide combination containing azoxystrobin and propiconazole. This report aims to describe the clinical presentation and course of toxicity associated with these agents and to underscore the role of advanced therapeutic interventions—particularly exchange transfusion—in managing cases refractory to conventional treatment with methylene blue.

Case presentation

A female in her 20s was brought to medical attention following ingestion of an unknown quantity of a fungicide containing Azoxystrobin 7.1% and Propiconazole 11.9% W/W. She complained of multiple episodes of vomiting and drowsiness after ingestion. There was no history of fever, chest pain, loose stools and seizures. At presentation her oxygen saturation was 80% on room air and was hypotensive and tachypnoeic. On examination patient was not responsive and her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was E1V1M1. General physical examination showed cyanosis of the hands and lips (Fig. 1). Neurological examination revealed plantar extensors with normal reflexes and tone. On respiratory examination bilateral infra-axillary and infra-scapular coarse inspiratory crepitations were heard. She was intubated for airway protection and ventilatory support, and a noradrenaline infusion was initiated for fluid-refractory shock.

Initial laboratory evaluation showed a hemoglobin level of 11.7 g/dL and a markedly elevated total leukocyte count of 19,000/cu mm with neutrophilic predominance (89.9%), suggestive of a possible inflammatory response, likely due to aspiration. Platelet count was within normal limits. Renal function tests were normal. Liver enzymes were largely unremarkable, except for a mildly elevated AST (53 U/L). Initial ABG done revealed pH of 7.216 and PaO₂ of 60 mm Hg on room air. Post intubation ABG done after 30 min with 100% FiO2 revealed pH of 7.187 and PaO₂ of 400.9 mm Hg. A baseline non-contrast CT scan of the brain was normal. Following intubation, despite 100% FiO₂, oxygen saturation remained at 83%. An ABG showed a markedly elevated PaO₂ of 400.9 mmHg, indicating a significant saturation gap. The patient’s blood also exhibited a characteristic chocolate-brown color (Fig. 2.). In the context of a known history of poison ingestion, these findings raised suspicion for dyshemoglobinemias such as methemoglobinemia, carboxyhemoglobinemia, or hydrogen sulfide toxicity. An oxygen saturation gap—defined as a difference greater than 5% between pulse oximetry and calculated arterial oxygen saturation—should be specifically assessed in such cases, as it serves as a critical clue to the presence of abnormal hemoglobin species [5]. Methemoglobin levels were eventually measured and revealed a critically elevated methemoglobin level of 80.7%. Thus, a diagnosis of methemoglobinemia was made. The patient was given an initial 100 mg dose of methylene blue along with intravenous ascorbic acid and shifted to intensive care unit where she was administered repeat boluses of methylene blue over 24 h, reaching a cumulative dose of 7 mg/kg. Concurrent therapy with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and ascorbic acid infusions was also initiated. Serial ABG analyses revealed a reduction in methemoglobin levels to 4%, with corresponding improvement in oxygen saturation and mental status (GCS improved to E3VTM5). However, approximately 12 h later, her methemoglobin levels began rising again, accompanied by declining SpO₂ and a drop in GCS to E1VTM1. A repeat NCCT brain revealed diffuse cerebral edema, for which anti-edema measures were initiated.

With methemoglobin levels again increasing to 73.2%, high-volume red blood cell exchange transfusion was done (Fig. 3.). Discussion

In this report, we present a case of methemoglobinemia secondary to ingestion of a fungicide—azoxystrobin and propiconazole, an uncommon but important aetiology, particularly in agricultural communities.

Oxidation of iron component of hemoglobin from its ferrous (Fe²⁺) to ferric (Fe³⁺) state, results in the formation of MetHb. This alteration impairs the oxygen-carrying capacity of hemoglobin and shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the left, reducing oxygen delivery and increasing the risk of tissue hypoxia [7]. MetHb is produced at a low rate (~ 3% per day) under oxidative stress, but cellular reductive systems typically keep its level below 1% [7]. The primary enzymatic system responsible for reducing MetHb is the cytochrome b5-MetHb reductase pathway, where cytochrome b5 reductase uses nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) as a cofactor to convert MetHb back to functional hemoglobin [7–9]. A secondary mechanism, involving nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)-MetHb reductase, utilizes NADPH as a cofactor [1, 7]. The production of NADPH relies on glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD). While this pathway normally contributes to a smaller portion (around 5%) of MetHb reduction, it can be pharmacologically enhanced with external cofactors such as methylene blue, significantly increasing its reducing capacity [1, 7]. Additionally, other cellular mechanisms, including the action of intracellular glutathione and ascorbic acid, help reduce oxidant compounds, thereby minimizing MetHb production [10].

The literature on the association between fungicides and methemoglobinemia remains limited. Copper sulfate, a fungicide widely used in agriculture, has been implicated in several reports of severe toxicological effects, including methemoglobinemia [11–14]. Additionally, compounds like transfluthrin have been shown to induce oxidative stress and methemoglobinemia, particularly in individuals with underlying vulnerabilities such as glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency [15]. While there are case reports describing the association between pesticide exposure and methemoglobinemia [6], the relationship between commonly used agricultural fungicides such as azoxystrobin and propiconazole and methemoglobinemia remains largely unestablished. Ferdous et al. reported one such case of methemoglobinemia following fungicide ingestion [5], in which the patient responded favourably to methylene blue therapy. In contrast, our case was notable for its refractoriness to methylene blue, necessitating escalation to alternative interventions.

Intractable methemoglobinemia is a rare but serious condition in which methemoglobin levels remain persistently elevated or are unresponsive to standard therapies such as methylene blue. The etiologies can be broadly classified into congenital and acquired categories. Congenital forms are primarily due to genetic defects in the enzymes responsible for reducing methemoglobin to hemoglobin, most notably autosomal recessive variants in the CYB5R3 gene, which encodes cytochrome b5 reductase, and autosomal dominant mutations in globin genes leading to hemoglobin M disease. These hereditary conditions often result in chronic or intractable methemoglobinemia, as the underlying enzymatic defect cannot be corrected by standard antidotal therapy [2, 16]. G6PD deficiency can also cause or exacerbate intractable methemoglobinemia by impairing both the natural and therapeutic reduction of methemoglobin, particularly in the presence of oxidative stress, and by limiting the effectiveness and safety of methylene blue therapy [17]. In our case, however, G6PD levels were assessed and found to be within the normal range, effectively ruling out G6PD deficiency as a contributing factor.

Toxin-induced methemoglobinemia can result from exposure to a wide range of drugs and chemicals with oxidative properties. Persistent exposure to toxins and drugs can lead to intractable methemoglobinemia. While it is commonly linked to known oxidizing agents, there are also reports of its occurrence in less typical scenarios, such as overdoses of acetaminophen, metoclopramide, and cases of aluminum phosphide poisoning [18]. In our case, however, other potential causes were effectively ruled out based on a clear and reliable clinical history, supporting the likelihood of a direct association with the ingested fungicide.

Management of methemoglobinemia should be based on the clinical severity of the condition, with MetHb levels serving as a supplementary indicator for initiating treatment. Intravenous methylene blue remains the first-line therapy and is typically administered to symptomatic patients or asymptomatic individuals with MetHb concentrations exceeding 25% [5, 7, 19]. It functions by accepting electrons from NADPH and converting into leukomethylene blue, which then reduces ferric iron (Fe³⁺) in hemoglobin back to its functional ferrous form (Fe²⁺) within red blood cells. The recommended initial dose is 1–2 mg/kg (equivalent to 0.2 mL/kg of a 1% solution), administered intravenously over 3 to 5 min. If MetHb levels remain high after 30–60 min, a second dose of 1 mg/kg may be given. Typically, MB leads to a marked decrease in MetHb within an hour. However, repeated dosing may lead to toxicity, particularly if the total dosage exceeds 7 mg/kg, potentially worsening methemoglobinemia [7, 19]. Ascorbic acid is considered the preferred alternative when methylene blue is unavailable and in cases of methemoglobinemia associated with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency [19]. The role of N-acetylcysteine (NAC) in managing methemoglobinemia remains uncertain. While in vitro studies suggest that NAC may function as a cofactor to promote methemoglobin reduction by enhancing intracellular glutathione levels, clinical evidence is limited. It has been proposed as a potential therapy in patients with G6PD deficiency or in cases of acetaminophen-induced methemoglobinemia [20].

In refractory cases of methemoglobinemia, when standard therapies are ineffective or contraindicated, alternative interventions such as exchange transfusion, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and plasmapheresis have been reported in the literature [19, 21, 22]. Although several case reports have described the use of hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) in methemoglobinemia [23, 24], there are currently no established guidelines endorsing its routine use. HBOT is not widely accessible, and standardized dosing or treatment protocols have yet to be defined. Moreover, its clinical efficacy remains unproven [6]. A systematic review reported that therapeutic whole blood exchange (TWBE) achieved a survival rate of 81.6% in patients who were unresponsive to methylene blue therapy [25]. However, the optimal volume for exchange and the appropriate number of sessions have not been conclusively determined [6].

In our case, treatment was initiated with methylene blue, with the maximum cumulative dose administered within the first 24 h. Adjunctive therapy with ascorbic acid and N-acetylcysteine was also given. Initially, methylene blue led to a rapid drop in MetHb levels from 80 to 4% with clinical improvement. However, levels rose again on Day 2, prompting consideration of refractory methemoglobinemia. An exchange transfusion was performed on Day 3 due to worsening clinical status and rising MetHb levels. Despite aggressive management, including multi-modal therapy and exchange transfusion, the patient’s condition deteriorated, and she ultimately succumbed to the illness.

This case underscores the importance of early recognition and timely escalation in managing methemoglobinemia, particularly in patients presenting with unexplained cyanosis or hypoxia in agricultural settings where fungicide exposure is likely. To our knowledge, this is one of the few reports documenting methemoglobinemia secondary to azoxystrobin and propiconazole ingestion, with methylene blue failure necessitating exchange transfusion. Given the limited literature on such presentations, our case adds valuable insight into the spectrum of fungicide-induced toxicities and the potential need for advanced interventions beyond standard therapies.

This case report is limited by its nature as a single-patient observation, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. While it provides insight into a rare cause of methemoglobinemia and its atypical clinical course, causality between the fungicide ingestion and refractory methemoglobinemia cannot be definitively established due to the absence of confirmatory toxicological assays or serum levels of azoxystrobin and propiconazole. Furthermore, due to the fatal outcome, histopathological examination or post-mortem analysis was not performed, which could have provided mechanistic insights into the systemic effects of fungicide toxicity.

Conclusion

This case adds to the limited literature on fungicide-associated methemoglobinemia, particularly involving agents like azoxystrobin and propiconazole. It highlights that while methylene blue remains the first-line treatment, refractory cases can deteriorate rapidly. Early consideration of advanced interventions—such as exchange transfusion—may be lifesaving, especially when first-line therapies are ineffective. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for recurrence and escalate care promptly when needed. Agricultural pesticide ingestion remains a common mode of poisoning among agricultural workers, particularly affecting the younger population. In a country like India, which is heavily dependent on agriculture, the effective management of insecticide and pesticide poisoning is crucial to reducing morbidity and mortality. Future research is warranted to better characterize the toxicodynamic profiles of azoxystrobin and propiconazole and to establish clearer treatment protocols for fungicide-induced methemoglobinemia.
