Nickel-catalyzed ring-opening difunctionalization of methylenecyclobutanes via strain-release-enabled C-C activation
Authors: Tingting Wang, Haina Liu, Meihong Luo, Wen-Wen Zhao, Chao Ma, Mingshuai Zhang, Jian-Biao Liu, Hongyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe efficient construction of molecular complexity from simple building blocks is a significant goal in catalysis, and the controlled ring-opening functionalization of strained carbocycles provides a powerful strategy to achieve it. However, selective catalytic strategies for ring-opening difunctionalization of methylenecyclobutanes (MCBs) remain elusive. Here, we report a nickel-catalyzed method that enables the 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization and 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionalization of MCBs via selective C–C bond cleavage. This protocol exploits the strain energy and dual reactivity of MCBs to deliver nonadjacent C(sp3)/C(sp2) frameworks in good to excellent yields under mild conditions. A broad substrate scope, scalability to the gram scale, and versatile downstream transformations demonstrate the synthetic utility of this approach. Mechanistic studies, including control, isotope-labeling experiments, and DFT calculations, reveal a distinct cooperative pathway involving strain-driven β-carbon elimination, offering new opportunities for catalyst-controlled activation of MCBs.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Homogeneous catalysis

Introduction

The efficient assembly of molecular complexity from simple building blocks is a significant goal in catalysis1–3, with profound implications for synthetic chemistry, drug discovery, and materials science. In this context, catalytic multicomponent reactions that form multiple carbon–carbon bonds in a single step under mild conditions are desirable for generating molecular complexity because they enhance atom economy, reduce waste, and streamline synthetic design4,5. Among these, the difunctionalization of unsaturated hydrocarbons has become a key strategy6–8, enabling the selective addition of two different functional groups to access a variety of C(sp3)- and C(sp2)-hybridized frameworks commonly found in bioactive molecules and functional materials9–11, thus making the synthesis of functional molecules easier.

A breakthrough in this field has been the 1,2-difunctionalization of terminal alkenes and alkynes (Fig. 1A)12–15, which is made possible by their readily available nature, and has advanced significantly through transition-metal catalysis (Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Pd, etc.)16–22, photocatalysis23–25, electrocatalysis26, and synergistic techniques27. However, these approaches typically produce either two C(sp3)– or two C(sp2)– hybridized carbon centers, thereby limiting access to molecules with differently hybridized carbon groups. The ability to simultaneously build both C(sp³) and C(sp2) centers in one step remains a key challenge with broad implications for molecular design and increasing complexity. In this regard, terminal allenes stand out as especially useful substrates, offering a solution to this challenge due to their orthogonal π-systems and distinct electronic properties (Fig. 1B)28,29. Their regio- and chemoselective difunctionalization enables the concurrent formation of C(sp3)/C(sp2) centers, providing a more straightforward route to densely functionalized, three-dimensional structures that are difficult to access using traditional alkene or alkyne methods. This reactivity has greatly expanded the synthetic toolkit for creating complex, sophisticated molecules. Inspired by recent advances, efforts have shifted toward strain-activated olefin surrogates such as methylenecyclopropanes (MCPs) (Fig. 1C)30–32, which replace the π-bond of alkenes with a highly strained three-membered ring. These MCPs exhibit allene-like reactivity in transition-metal-catalyzed 1,3-difunctionalization, allowing the simultaneous formation of two nonadjacent C(sp3)/C(sp2) frameworks via strain-release pathways33–36, which have made significant progress over the past years.

Similar to MCPs, methylenecyclobutanes (MCBs) are versatile organic reagents in chemical synthesis, offering a built-in reservoir of strain energy (strain energy of cyclobutane: 26.5 kcal/mol, cyclopropane: 27.5 kcal/mol) and dual reactivity through their C=C π-system and cyclobutane σ-system37–39. This duality enables transformations via C=C bond difunctionalization or strain-driven C–C bond cleavage, thus significantly enriching the chemical space of MCBs. To date, extensive studies on the applications of methylenecyclobutanes (MCBs) have been reported, with approximately 379 publications and 333 patents documented in SciFinder®. Nevertheless, progress has predominantly focused on 1,2-difunctionalization across the C=C bond40–43 and ring-expansion transformations44–47, whereas ring-opening strategies that enable 1,4-difunctionalization of cyclobutanes through selective C–C bond cleavage remain largely underexplored48, thereby limiting their utility in multicomponent reactions for molecular complexity. Given the high ring strain and electronically unbiased nature of the C=C bonds in methylenecyclobutanes (MCBs), achieving regioselective migratory insertion across these alkenes is both a fundamental challenge and a critical prerequisite for efficient ring-opening functionalization. Recently, Koh and Shi developed a state-of-the-art method for the directing-group-free difunctionalization of unactivated alkenes, and mechanistic insights have shown that bulky Ni–NHC complexes enable the selective difunctionalization of unactivated alkenes by promoting branch-selective migratory insertion of arylmetal species across terminal C=C bonds49–51. Inspired by this advance, we hypothesized that Ni–NHC catalysis could overcome inherent steric and electronic biases in MCBs, and the branch-selective insertion into the exocyclic methylene group of MCBs would induce strain-driven β-carbon elimination, allowing for direct C–C bond cleavage and subsequent 1,4-difunctionalization (Fig. 1D). This process would produce nonadjacent sp2 and one sp3 carbon centers, which are important building blocks for furnishing many value-added molecules52,53. Herein, we report a Ni–NHC–catalyzed multicomponent strategy that enables efficient 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization and 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionalization of MCBs in this paper.

Results and discussion

Identification of the catalytic conditions

Initially, we systematically optimized the reaction conditions of the 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization of MCBs via strain-release-enabled ring-opening, selecting 1a, 2a, and 3a as the model substrates. As shown in Table 1, the desired ring-opening product 4a was obtained in 76% yield when the reaction was conducted in cyclohexane using Ni(cod)2 as the catalyst and nitrogen-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) L6 as the ligand. Replacing L6 with other phosphine or nitrogen-based ligands failed to promote the transformation, highlighting the unique property of L6 in this multicomponent cross-coupling reaction. Further modification of the NHC ligand through steric tuning of the aryl substituents did not enhance the reaction efficiency (L5, 36% yield; L7, 34% yield). Likewise, variations of the imidazole backbone aimed at adjusting both electronic and steric properties failed to improve the yield of 4a (L8, 34% yield; L9, 27% yield; L10, 47% yield), indicating that an appropriate balance of steric and electronic effects is crucial for this transformation and reinforcing L6 as the optimal choice. Solvent screening revealed that low-polarity solvents were more suitable for the reaction, with toluene and PhCF3 yielding comparable yields of 66% and 73%, respectively. Variations in reaction temperature and substrate concentration were also explored, but no further improvements in yield were observed (see Supplementary Information for details). Notably, reducing the catalyst loading to 1 mol% Ni(cod)2 still afforded the desired product 4a in 56% yield (see Supplementary Information for details).

Substrate scope

With the optimized conditions established, we next evaluated the substrate scope to assess the generality of the approach for the 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization of MCBs (Fig. 2). We began by examining the electrophilic coupling partners (ArOTf) bearing various substituents to explore electronic and steric effects. Para-substituted aryl triflates with alkyl, trifluoromethoxy, fluoro, or methoxy groups produced the desired products in good yields (45–83%, 4a–4i). Similarly, meta-substituted analogues with comparable functionalities were well tolerated, giving products in 64–81% yield (4j–4o). Next, we explored the substrate scope of nucleophiles with a range of alkylmagnesium chlorides, all of which reacted smoothly under standard conditions to give the ring-opening products in 75–84% yield (4p–4w). In contrast, arylmagnesium halides did not produce the desired products; instead, only Kumada-type cross-coupling products54 between the aryl nucleophiles and ArOTf were detected by GC-MS analysis, highlighting the difficulty of achieving selective ring-opening in the presence of aryl nucleophiles. We also investigated the structural diversity of MCB substrates. Substrates bearing quaternary carbon centers were generally well tolerated, giving moderate to good yields (4x–4ah, 40–81%), indicating minimal steric interference. However, those containing tertiary carbon centers gave the corresponding products 4ai and 4aj in lower yields (34% and 37%) despite their high conversions, which can be attributed to the competitive β-hydride elimination to furnish the 1,4-dienes55. Additionally, the structure of 4ah was confirmed unambiguously by single-crystal X-ray analysis.

To further extend the utility of the method, we investigated the 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionalization of MCBs (Fig. 3). Following systematic optimization (see Supplementary Information), the optimal conditions employed ligand L5 and sodium tert-pentoxide in THF, with aryl bromides as the electrophiles and sodium isopropoxide as the hydrogen source. In contrast, the use of aryl triflates, chlorides, or aryl iodides as electrophilic coupling partners resulted in diminished yields compared to the aryl bromides (see Supplementary Information, Table S6). Moreover, GC-MS analysis revealed the formation of 1,2-hydrocarbofunctionalization byproducts under these conditions, indicating that the nature of the anion plays a crucial role in governing the β-C elimination process. Under the optimal conditions, the target product 7a was obtained in 88% yield. A variety of aryl bromides bearing alkyl, ether, amino, trifluoromethyl, halogen, and fused-ring substituents were well tolerated, affording products 7b–7r in 40–85% yield. Notably, heteroaryl bromides could also undergo 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionalization, delivering 7s–7u in 30–63% yield. Furthermore, a highly conjugated substrate, 1,1,2,2-tetrakis(4-bromophenyl)ethene, could be employed to furnish tetraphenylethylene derivative 7 v in 37% yield, potentially enabling access to new fluorescent scaffolds56,57. Finally, a broad range of MCBs was subjected to 1,4-hydrocarbonation under the optimized conditions. As shown in Fig. 3 (7w–7ag), MCBs with quaternary carbon centers furnished products in up to 95% yield. Remarkably, tertiary-substituted MCBs were also well tolerated, affording 7ah–7am in 64–86% yield.

Applications of the method

To underscore the versatility and practical value of this method, gram-scale reactions and diverse downstream transformations were carried out. As shown in Fig. 4A, both 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionalization and 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization of MCBs proceeded efficiently on a gram-scale under standard conditions, delivering the desired products in high yields (7a, 86%; 4a, 59%). The operational simplicity of the protocol was further demonstrated by employing the air-stable Ni(0) precatalyst Ni(4-tBustb)358,59, which enabled the 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization of 1a to furnish 4a in a reduced yield of 52% (Fig. 4B), likely due to the steric bulk of Ni(4-tBustb)3. Collectively, these results underscore the scalability and robustness of the method. Importantly, selective hydrogenation and oxidation of the sterically hindered C=C bond were achieved under mild conditions (Fig. 4C), showcasing excellent functional group tolerance and enabling access to structurally diverse alkanes 8 (79% yield) and 9 (51% yield). Moreover, the cobalt/photoredox-catalyzed difunctionalization of 7a delivered ketone 11 in 73% yield (Fig. 4D)60, further demonstrating the synthetic utility of the products. Notably, the bisphenol derivative, as an important monomer for polycarbonate resins, was synthesized in 42% yield over two steps via difunctionalization of 12 followed by demethylation (Fig. 4E), underscoring the method’s potential for constructing functional materials.

Mechanistic evaluation

Mechanistic insights were elucidated through control experiments, isotope-labeling studies (Fig. 5), and DFT calculations (Fig. 6). Complete reaction blockage without benzylmagnesium chloride (3a) and the absence of Heck-type byproducts when 2a was omitted confirm the crucial roles of both reagents in enabling C–C single-bond cleavage of MCBs (Fig. 5A). 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionation of 1a with sodium deuterated isopropoxide produced the fully deuterated product (>99% D, confirmed by 1H-NMR analysis), clearly indicating the alkoxide as the hydride source61. Substituting sodium isopropoxide with sodium 1-phenylethan-1-olate produced 7a (38% yield) along with acetophenone (detected by GC-MS analysis), providing further evidence for this hydride transfer pathway (Fig. 5B). Additionally, a significant kinetic isotope effect (KIE = 3) suggests that hydride transfer is likely the rate-determining step. Furthermore, the radical-trapping experiments for the 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization using one equivalent of TEMPO or BHT afforded 4a in 62% yield and 66% yield, respectively, thereby excluding the involvement of a radical pathway (Fig. 5C). In addition, Ni(IPr)2 was evaluated as a catalyst for the dicarbofunctionalization of 1a, delivering 4a in a 43% yield (Fig. 5D), which supports the participation of an active Ni–NHC catalytic species.

Computed free-energy profiles for the Ni-catalyzed reaction leading to products P1 and P2.

To gain deeper insight into the reaction mechanism, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed (see Supplementary Information for computational details). Substrates 1a’, 2b, and 3a were selected as model systems. The computed free-energy profiles for the formation of products P1 and P2 are summarized in Fig. 6, while an alternative pathway leading to P1 is depicted in Fig. S14. Initial coordination of PhOTf to the active Ni0(NHC) species is exergonic, affording intermediate int1 (ΔG = −21.0 kcal/mol), which undergoes irreversible oxidative addition via TS1 to generate the Ni(II) species int2. Subsequent coordination of alkene 1a’ to int2 leads to the formation of four-coordinate Ni–MCB complexes int3 and int3’, corresponding to the precursors of TS2 and TS2’, respectively. Notably, formation of both int3 and int3’ is endergonic, indicating that alkene coordination is thermodynamically uphill. Consequently, the free-energy barriers for olefin insertion are referenced to int2. From int2, 1,2-insertion via TS2 is favored over the competing 2,1-insertion pathway (TS2’), and the 1,2-insertion step exhibits the highest activation free energy of the catalytic cycle, identifying it as the rate-determining step.

Following olefin insertion, the resulting alkyl-Ni(II) intermediate int4 represents a key branching point of the reaction manifold. As shown in Fig. 6, int4 preferentially undergoes transmetalation with Grignard reagent 3a to generate alkyl-Ni(II)-benzyl species int5. Subsequent isomerization affords int6, in which the alkyl and benzyl groups adopt a trans relationship. From int6, β-carbon elimination proceeds via TS3, involving C–C σ-bond cleavage accompanied by π-bond formation, to give intermediate int7. Further isomerization leads to int8, which undergoes C–C reductive elimination via TS4 to furnish product P1. Alternatively, int5 can directly undergo reductive elimination through TS3’ to form product P2. This direct reductive elimination pathway is kinetically less favorable, with an activation barrier of 17.8 kcal/mol relative to int5, and also thermodynamically disfavored compared to P1 formation, consistent with the experimental observations.

In addition to the pathway shown in Figs. 6 and S14 illustrates an alternative pathway, in which β-carbon elimination precedes transmetalation. In this scenario, int4 first undergoes β-C elimination, followed by transmetalation, isomerization, and reductive elimination to ultimately afford product P1. Importantly, the activation barriers associated with the elementary steps following int4 along both pathways are substantially lower than those of the olefin insertion step. Therefore, while the pathway in Fig. 6 is kinetically preferred, the competing pathway shown in Fig. S14 cannot be excluded and may operate in parallel after the rate-determining olefin insertion step.

In summary, we have developed a broad nickel–NHC-catalyzed strategy for the selective ring-opening 1,4-difunctionalization of methylenecyclobutanes, including hydrocarbonation and dicarbonation. This catalyst-controlled approach allows efficient access to molecules with nonadjacent C(sp3) and C(sp2) centers through strain-release pathways under mild conditions. The method’s broad functional group tolerance, scalability, and diverse post-functionalization options demonstrate its practicality for building structurally complex molecules. Detailed mechanistic studies and DFT calculations reveal the potential pathway, expanding the synthetic toolkit for challenging C–C bond cleavage and opening new avenues for designing complex molecular architectures in synthetic chemistry and drug discovery. Moreover, ongoing work in our lab on diverse 1,4-difunctionalization of MCBs will be reported in due course.

Methods

General procedure for the 1,4-dicarbofunctionalization of MCBs

In a N2-filled glove box, an oven-dried 4 mL vial equipped with a stir bar was charged with Ni(cod)2 (10 mol%), L6 (10 mol%), and cyclohexane (1.0 mL). Grignard reagent 3 (0.3 mmol, 3.0 equiv.) was slowly added to the system subsequently. The reaction mixture was allowed to stir at room temperature for 0.5 h. Methylenecyclobutene 1 (0.1 mmol 1.0 equiv.) and aryl triflate 2 (0.2 mmol 2.0 equiv.) were then added to the system. The vial was sealed, and the reaction mixture was allowed to stir at 40 °C for 24 h. After cooling to ambient temperature, the crude mixture was quenched with aqueous NH4Cl. The mixture was diluted with ethyl acetate (5 mL), washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4, and purified by flash column chromatography to afford the desired products 4.

General procedure for the 1,4-hydrocarbofunctionalization of MCBs

In a N2-filled glove box, an oven-dried 4 mL vial equipped with a stir bar was charged with Ni(cod)2 (10 mol%), L5 (10 mol%), t-AmONa (25 mol%), and THF (1.0 mL). The reaction mixture was allowed to stir at room temperature for 10 min. Aryl bromide 5 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 equiv.), cyclobutene 1 (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), and sodium isopropoxide (0.3 mmol, 3.0 equiv.) were then added to the system. The vial was sealed, and the reaction mixture was allowed to stir at 40 °C for 12 h. After cooling to ambient temperature, the crude mixture was quenched with aqueous NH4Cl. The mixture was diluted with ethyl acetate (5 mL), washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4, and purified by flash column chromatography to afford the desired products 7.
