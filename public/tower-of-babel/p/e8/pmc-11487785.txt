Characteristics and health insurance profile of brought-in-dead children in a rural tertiary hospital
Authors: Adaugo Chizoma Owobu, Chiedozie Ike, Michael Ogbogu, IfeanyiChukwu Chukwu, Frank Onyeke, Henrietta Ugbeni, Matthew Apeleokha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the regular occurrence of brought-in-dead (BID) phenomenon, little is known about their prevalence, pattern and prehospital contributors in rural settings in Nigeria. In addition, the impact of health insurance in this context has not been described in our locale. Our study aimed at identifying the characteristics and access to health insurance of paediatric BID cases.DesignA cross-sectional, retrospective study in the paediatric emergency department of a rural tertiary hospital.MethodsData were obtained from the medical records of BID cases from January 2019 to December 2023. Their sociodemographic details, the nature and duration of preceding illnesses/complaints, prior treatment, referral and health insurance types were obtained.ResultsSixty-seven (1.38%) of the 4872 admissions were BID. Their ages ranged between 12 hours and 14 years with a male:female ratio of 1.1:1. Forty-four (69.84%) were under 5s. Acute encephalitis syndrome, gastroenteritis and sepsis/severe malaria were the most prevalent presumed causes of death. Prior to arrival, 30.56% had obtained treatment outside the hospital setting (medicine vendors, herbal treatment, home nurse and religious centres). Almost half of the referrals were informal referrals by family and neighbours. None (0%) presented health insurance records nor was brought in by an ambulance.ConclusionInfectious diseases were the most common presumed causes of death and under 5s bore the greatest burden of BID. The healthcare choices were poor, but the informal sociocultural support system was strong. There was a dearth of health insurance enrolment, a poor patient referral and transfer system, and poor documentation of BID cases. We identify the need for concerted health education, improved patient referral and transfer, well-coordinated death registration and appropriate health insurance schemes in rural communities.Keywords: Child Health, Health Policy, Health services research, Mortality, Low and Middle Income Countries

Introduction

A brought-in-dead (BID) patient (also called dead on arrival) is one who, on arrival at the hospital, was found by a physician to be already clinically deceased (determined by unconsciousness and the absence of cardiac activity, blood pressure and respiration) with or without cardiopulmonary resuscitation attempt.1 2 In general, BID statistics are reflective of prehospital variables such as national policies, sociocultural, environmental and disease-related factors.1

They contribute about 2.2–7.4% of total presentations in the paediatric emergency department (ED) in Nigeria.2,4 Over 80% of deaths in children in the country are infection-related, mostly preventable and caused by malaria, pneumonia, gastroenteritis, meningitis and sepsis.2,7 Parental poverty, long distance from a health facility, inefficient primary health centres, high cost of healthcare services, unavailability of emergency medical/ambulance services, bad roads and dearth of child health specialists in the rural areas are perpetuating factors of BID in children especially in the low- and medium-income countries (LMICs) including Nigeria.1,7

Health insurance, which promotes universal health coverage, is also grossly suboptimal in Nigeria.5 Current statistics indicate that only about 5% of Nigerian citizens are covered by the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), comprising majorly of government employees.8 Expectedly, of the 5% NHIS coverage, the locals in rural communities are hardly represented. At the State level, the Edo Health Insurance Scheme (EdoHIS) only provides health insurance for 0.9% of the populace.9 10 Apart from the government insurance schemes, some private organisations have a retainership arrangement with private or government hospitals by which healthcare costs are subsidised for their employees. However, such organisations are mostly located in big cities and towns.

This means that in most rural areas in the country, health services are procured on out-of-pocket basis by the locals and therefore, health decisions are grossly determined by the financial status of the sick person. Furthermore, these communities are often more severely affected by the dearth of skilled medical professionals (who prefer to live in cities), poorly maintained or non-existent health facilities and unmotorable access roads. Electricity and potable water are mostly unavailable as well. In addition, illiteracy and adherence to cultural practices are other factors that characterise rural communities in Nigeria. These factors jointly contribute to the outcome of illnesses in these communities.

The current study was conducted in Irrua Specialist Teaching hospital which is located in a rural community in Edo State Nigeria. Most of the locals are farmers or petty traders, and experience multidimensional poverty which affects 72% of people in rural areas compared with 42% in urban areas in Nigeria.11 This study therefore sought to evaluate the pattern and sociodemographic peculiarities of paediatric BID in a rural setting in Nigeria. It also evaluated their unique patient referral system as well as the access of the locals to health insurance schemes12 and the impact of the latter on their health-seeking behaviour.

Methods

Study location

Irrua Specialist Teaching Hospital (ISTH) Edo State is a 375-bed tertiary hospital run by the Federal Government of Nigeria. ISTH is not the main federal teaching hospital in the State and is situated in a rural community, Irrua, which is difficult to reach due to poor connecting roads to the State capital and other parts of the country. The community and her environs lack basic infrastructure and amenities including equipped primary health centres, potable water, steady electricity supply and good schools. Common to most rural communities in the country, there is a dearth of factories and industries in Irrua, thus confining most of her inhabitants to subsistence farming and petty trading.

ISTH is a healthcare provider under the NHIS but does not enrol patients/clients into the scheme. Enrolment is done at the State capital in Benin City which is about 100 km away from Irrua, and has a dilapidated and almost impassible connecting road. The distance, bad road and official processes hinder the enrolment of local community members thereby preventing them from benefiting from the scheme. New employees of ISTH and other government institutions similarly need to go to Benin City for enrolment. Sometimes, the new employees may get enrolled locally if their parent institution makes special arrangements for enrolment in the institution by bringing NHIS staff from the headquarters. This mainly happens following a recent employment exercise, where large numbers of new intake have been employed in the institution. However, the special arrangement is only for staff and does not accommodate the locals.

As there are no government-run emergency ambulance services in the country, sick people are brought to the hospital from home by self effort, that is, by self, relatives or private ambulances. However, private ambulance services in the study locale are very rare, expensive and frequently lack standard patient-transfer equipment. A few private hospitals in the locale transfer patients to ISTH by ambulance but this service is only available to patients who were managed in their hospital. Therefore, critically ill patients in the community are brought to the hospital by means affordable to their relatives that is, mostly by motorcycles, which is the most common means of transportation among the locals. Where a sick person becomes critically ill in the deep hours of the night, presentation to the hospital may be delayed until morning due to unavailability of transportation at night.

This study was carried out in the children emergency room (CHER) of ISTH, which is a 15-bed ward responsible for receiving paediatric medical and surgical emergencies. It is the major entry point for acutely ill children in the hospital, offering 24 hours services inclusive of weekends. It is manned by nurses, resident doctors and consultants. Children up to 18 years of age receive care in CHER. Thus, BID cases including newborns are often first rushed to CHER for resuscitation. Inpatient and outpatient registers and individual patient medical records are kept in CHER where patients’ details are documented. The BID details include their biodata, the history of the illness, treatment obtained prior to arrival, findings on examination in CHER and details of the resuscitation carried out in CHER. Health insurance details are recorded where available. Referral notes, results of investigations done prior to arrival and any other document relating to the illness are attached to their medical records.

Study population and design

The study population comprised of the BID cases that presented to CHER from January 2019 to December 2023. Total sampling method was used and cases with incomplete documentation were also included. It was a descriptive, cross-sectional, retrospective study.

Data collection and analysis

The data used in the study were extracted from the CHER BID medical records. There was no interaction between the researchers and the relatives of the BID children. The data obtained comprised of their biodata, health insurance records, details of the preceding illness and treatment, nature of injuries (for trauma cases), time and date of arrival and referral information. Symptoms were classified according to systems of the body, and the presumed causes of death were determined according to basic paediatrics standards.13 To ensure reliable data, a standard diagnostic/reference form, that is, the 2022 WHO verbal autopsy form was used for the broad classifications.14 The presence of fever in the history was regarded as indicative of an infectious illness and sepsis was presumed for febrile illnesses with multisystem involvement. Three researchers determined the diagnoses/presumed causes of death independently and were blinded to the diagnoses of the others. Thereafter, for each BID case, the diagnosis common to two or all three of the researchers was selected as the final diagnosis. Only the researchers with a background specialisation in paediatrics were involved in arriving at the diagnoses/presumed causes of death

Data cleaning and analysis were done using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) V.27. Continuous variables were summarised using median and IQR, while categorical variables were expressed in frequencies and percentages, and their relationships determined by regression analysis and χ2 test. For all statistical tests, the level of significance was set at 0.05 and p values were reported.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Of the 4872 children admitted into CHER in the study duration, 67 (1.38%) were BID. Thirty-four (53.13%) of the 64 entries were male, while 30 (46.88%) were female, giving a male:female ratio of 1.1:1.

Health insurance and referral details

None (0.0%) of the 67 cases in the study presented health insurance records. All (100.0%) of the cases who had obtained prior treatment had procured treatment by out-of-pocket payment, either by self or by relatives/well-wishers. Majority (59.26%) were brought to the ED by the parent(s), while in 40.74% of cases, relatives accompanied parent(s). None (0.0%) was brought by an ambulance. Data regarding the mode of referral were available in 36 cases, and of these, 17 (47.22%) had been informally referred by friends/extended family, 11 (30.56%) were verbally referred by a health facility, while 8 (22.22%) had written referral notes from health facilities.

Treatment choices of the study population

Majority (76.47%) had obtained prior treatment from a health facility, while 30.56% had patronised alternative sources of treatment comprising of Patent and Proprietary Medicine Vendors (PPMVs), herbal treatment, home treatment by a nurse and treatment in religious centres.

Duration of preceding illnesses

Of the 45 cases with relevant data, 14 (31.11%) had been ill for less than 24 hours, 7 (15.56%) between 1 and 3 days, 20 (44.44%) between 4 and 7 days, 2 (4.44%) between 8 and 14 days, and 2 (4.44%) for greater than 2 weeks. As shown in table 2, among the 40 cases with complete entries, there was a significant relationship between the nature of disease and the duration of the illness before death (R2=0.66–0.70; p=0.001, 95% CI=0.001–0.003). However, there was no significant correlation between the duration of the preceding illness and treatment choices.

Presumed causes of death

Due to missing entries, presumed causes of death were determined in 54 of the BID cases. Majority (29, 53.7%) were of infectious nature, followed by neonatal deaths (8, 14.8%) and trauma (7, 13.0%). Poisoning, aspiration and sudden infant death were the least prevalent. Table 3 shows the breakdown of the diagnoses. Of the infectious causes, acute encephalitis syndrome (AES) (meningitis/encephalitis/cerebral malaria) was the most prevalent. Pattern of presentation

The pattern of presentation with respect to months of the year is grouped in quarters. Most of the children were brought during the first and third quarters of the year, 22 (37.93%) and 18 (31.03%), respectively. Similarly, most of the infection-related deaths presented during the first (30.43%) and third quarters (43.49%) of the year. Overall, there was no statistically significant relationship between the nature of illness and the death month or season of the year; χ2=24.02, p=0.15 and χ2=10.66, p=0.72, respectively.

Discussion

The prevalence of BID in this study was 1.38%, which is lower than the prevalence from other parts of Nigeria, ranging from 2.2% to 7.4%.2,7 The comparatively low rate observed in this study may reflect the low patronage of the tertiary hospital services by the financially disadvantaged locals. The BID rate in this study may not be truly reflective of the child mortality rates in the community. This is because in the setting where majority of the locals live below the international poverty line,15 and in the presence a perceived lack of obligation to document deaths, many families in rural communities bury their children at home (ie, in their family land) when the death occurs outside the hospital setting. In addition, some bereaved families may not desire the further emotional stress from the questions and due processes in the hospital, especially soon after losing their child and may therefore stay away from the hospital. These factors may also have contributed to the low BID rates.

Furthermore, in Nigeria, registration of deaths which would require all deaths to be certified by a medical doctor, and therefore, all BID cases to be brought to the hospital, is weakly implemented. Thus, families may bury their dead relatives without a death certificate or autopsy. The main drive for obtaining a death certificate by relatives of the deceased is often for official and legal processes including claims to property and funds of the deceased or in potentially medicolegal cases. In such instances, patients who die outside the hospital setting are brought to the hospital to be certified dead and for the issuance of a death certificate. However, as children often lack personal possessions of value, in instances where there is little likelihood of future medicolegal issues, it is common for them to be buried silently by their families in rural communities. Therefore, although comparatively low, the prevalence of about one BID every month in the study location is significant.

Over half of the BID patients in this study were under 5s, in keeping with the high under-5 mortality indices in the country. The predominance of under 5s among BID patients is common in Nigeria.2,6 The reasons for this include their proneness to accidents, ineptitude with self protection, vulnerability to infections and dependence on other persons for healthcare. Nigeria has a high under-5 mortality rate of 117 per 1000 live births,16 and WHO reports that half of the global under-5 deaths occurred in five countries including Nigeria.17

Our study further reveals a high burden of neonatal deaths in the locale which is in tandem with the high national neonatal mortality rate of 34.9 deaths per 1000 live births.18 In this study, all the neonatal deaths occurred within 1 week of illness and half within 3 days. A plausible explanation for this is that neonates have little physiological reserve and therefore deteriorate very rapidly. In addition, the signs of illness in neonates are often subtle, causing delays in identification and intervention. Ironically, the most common causes of neonatal deaths are readily preventable and treatable. The high neonatal mortality rate in Nigeria emphasises the vital importance of standard newborn follow-up after birth, as well as efficient neonatal-care facilities in rural areas and in the country in general. Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest neonatal mortality rate in the world, accounting for 43% of the global newborn deaths.19 Like under-5 mortality, neonatal mortality is a critical indicator of the strength of the healthcare system of a country because children who die within the first 28 days after birth suffer from conditions and diseases associated with the lack of quality care at birth, immediately after birth or in the first days of life.19

The high BID rates among under-5s and neonates in this study suggest crucial delays in seeking healthcare among mothers of under-5 children in the surrounding communities. The reasons for these delays are multifaceted and common to other LMICs. For instance, Getasew et al in Northwest Ethiopia20 as well as Ekyaruhanga et al in Uganda21 reported that rural residence, seeking healthcare at government hospitals, the poorest households, self-medication, delayed referrals and using traditional medicine before seeking healthcare were significantly associated with a delay in seeking healthcare for under-5 children. The locals in the current study location have a high prevalence of all the factors noted by these previous studies in LMICs,20 21 thereby demonstrating the intricate interaction and cumulative effect of social, economic and political factors on the health and survival of under-5 children in the poorest and most underserved homes in the country, who often live in rural areas.

On the other hand, our study revealed a robust family support system in the community. In about 40% of cases, the parent(s) of the BID child was accompanied by a family member to the ED, a reflection of the availability of psychosocial support for parents in critical conditions. This culture, which is often compromised by the inter-city migration found in towns and cities, still finds prominent expression in rural communities where the indigenes are resident in their hometown among members of their immediate and extended families. This informal yet strong social support system can be harnessed for designing health-oriented programmes and policies.

Further buttressing the strength of the social network system in the locale was the referral system observed in our study, where almost half of the referrals were informal verbal referrals by family relatives/ friends. This illustrates the potential value of the culture of cohesiveness in the community as a fulcrum for integrating the sustainable developmental goals in the community as well as other invaluable programmes including health insurance. Overall, however, our study reports a poor referral system. Less than half of the previous hospitals had issued written referral notes to the patients. This may be due to the existing poor interaction between the levels of health institutions, including the absence of reverse-feedback for referred cases. In general, the patient referral system between hospitals in Nigeria is poor.

The access to health insurance opportunities in our study was very poor, with none of the families presenting any health insurance record. In Nigeria, the NHIS7 is yet to be integrated into all strata of the country and currently caters for public servants majorly, which latter get enrolled into the NHIS on employment. However, subsistence farming and petty trade are the most prevalent occupations in the study locale and have no coordinated or statutory provision for enrolment into the NHIS or alternative health insurance schemes. This creates a gap.

Although community-based health insurance programme (CBHI) is a component of the NHIS, its uptake has been very poor from the inception of NHIS in 2005 until date.22 23 The reasons for the poor uptake include ignorance of the CBHI, poor implementation in rural areas, non-involvement of scheme members in the setting up and management of programmes, poor trust/confidence in the scheme and inequity in provision.22 23 Furthermore, the distance from the site of enrolment and the poor access road are additional factors in the current study location. This buttresses the value of exploiting the unique rural social network identified in this study community as a fulcrum for the CBHI and other future schemes intended for the locals. In general, the coverage of the entire NHIS (i.e., the different schemes put together) in the country is grossly inadequate at 5%.

The dearth of health insurance schemes has several untoward ripple effects including delays in seeking standard healthcare which was prominent in our study. For instance, significantly more of the non-traumatic deaths were caused by diseases which had lasted for days to weeks, giving ample time for adequate intervention. Furthermore, about one-third of the patients had not presented at any hospital prior to their death. Conversely, substandard sources of treatment thrived even in critical conditions, the most prevalent of which was PPMV. Similar findings of high patronage of alternative treatment choices among BID children were documented in Ekiti State.3

The overall health-seeking choices observed in our study was inappropriate and can be explained in context of the prevailing financial and economic conditions in Nigeria. With the decreasing per capita income,24 increasing rates of inflation and high poverty levels,9 citizens with poor earning abilities (prominent among rural dwellers) are often the most adversely affected by the economic hardship. Poverty is itself a harbinger of several dysfunctional outcomes including severe disease and death. Latunji and colleagues in Southwest Nigeria reported that members of the poorest quartile are six times more likely to have inappropriate health-seeking behaviour than the richest quartile.25

Infectious diseases were the most common presumed causes of death, contributing over half of the BID cases in our study, similar to Ndu et al2 who reported a rate of 61% in Enugu. Similarly, high rates of infectious diseases were reported in other parts of Nigeria and Asia among paediatric BID cases.1 14 Infectious diseases are prominent in the tropics due to the high prevalence of biological and environmental factors that foster the proliferation and infectivity of vectors and pathogens.16 The causes of death in our study were congruent with the leading causes of under-5 deaths globally which are preterm birth complications/neonatal causes, pneumonia, congenital anomalies, diarrhoea and malaria.26 27

The current study documented a seasonal trend of the presumed causes of death. There was an increased incidence of diarrhoea/gastroenteritis in the first quarter of the year, while malaria/severe malaria deaths were more prevalent in the third quarter. A plausible explanation is that diarrhoeal diseases are mostly caused by viruses which are more prevalent in the dry season. Furthermore, poor access to water in the rural community during the dry season fosters poor hygiene and consequently, outbreaks of diarrhoeal diseases. On the other hand, the rainy season promotes breeding of mosquitoes, leading to increased incidence of malaria. However, this seasonal trend was not statistically significant.

The non-infectious deaths in our study occurred from road traffic and home accidents, drowning, electrocution, poisoning, aspiration and sudden infant death. Like deaths from infectious diseases, these deaths are mostly preventable. Deaths from trauma in children are often pointers to poor adult supervision and child neglect.28 Beginning in early childhood, curiosity and exploration of the environment are healthy developmental milestones.29 Hence, it is the responsibility of adults (parents and caregivers) to provide adequate and continuous protection for children under their care. Continuous health education is imperative to provide parents with the requisite knowledge for successful parenting. In addition, legislative policies regarding protective equipment for children in public places are vital.

Conclusion

Most of the BID cases in the study were preventable deaths arising mostly from infectious diseases, neonatal morbidities and trauma, and most of the deaths occurred in under 5s. There was a seasonal trend in the nature of infectious diseases. Furthermore, although the community upheld a strong sociocultural support system, their healthcare choices were poor. We noted a dearth of health insurance opportunities, a weak referral and patient transfer system and poor documentation of BID cases.

Recommendation

We recommend active and better implementation of registration of deaths in the country and that BID data should be included in the national vital statistics. In turn, this will promote standard documentation of BID cases in all health institutions across the country. We also recommend robust community health insurance schemes for rural communities. Continuous medical education of the public and healthcare providers is required to foster early recognition, timely diagnosis and treatment, prompt referral, optimal ambulance services and to coordinate beneficial interaction across all tiers of health institutions.

Policies and programmes designed for rural communities should be integrated into their existing sociocultural network for the sustainability and longevity of such programmes.

Limitation

We note several missing entries in the medical records of BID cases in this study. Missing entries, which are common limitations of retrospective studies, were further accentuated by the absence of a national policy on registration of BID cases in the country. In addition, neither histopathologic nor verbal autopsy was done, and so, our study relied on the pattern of illness prior to demise for the diagnoses. Thus, incomplete and imprecise documentations could not be verified. However, to minimise the impact of this limitation on the accuracy of the diagnoses, independent reviews of the charts were done by three researchers before arriving at the final diagnosis for each individual case.

Line of future research

Future BID studies should incorporate verbal autopsy for more detailed evaluation of the causes of death. In addition, the seasonality trend of severe forms of malaria in a holoendemic region, as observed in this study, should be further investigated through large epidemiological studies. The results of such studies may guide targeted interventions for severe malaria in under 5s and thereby optimise the impact of available scarce resources.
