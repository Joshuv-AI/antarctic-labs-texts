The fiscal sustainability of Burkina Faso’s free health care policy for maternal and child health: an analysis using the fiscal space diamond
Authors: Alain Siri, Patrick Gueswendé Ilboudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn April 2016, Burkina Faso implemented a free maternal and child health care program. Program monitoring after the policy’s introduction showed increased stock-outs of essential drugs and supplies. The program’s outcomes could differ from those expected if financing and resources were insufficient.ObjectiveThis study investigated the program’s fiscal sustainability by examining ways to finance its implementation effectively.MethodsA slightly modified four-dimension definition of fiscal space was used to examine whether Burkina Faso had sufficient financial capacity to support its free health care program. Secondary data from various sources covering the years 1970–2018, 2008–2018, and 2009–2021, were used to estimate the optimal tax rate, analyze the efficiency of health care service delivery, and examine official development assistance, respectively. 2008–2018, 2009–2021, and 1970–2018 were used to estimate the optimal tax rate, analyze official development assistance, and the efficiency of the delivery of health care services, respectively. Furthermore, the fiscal burden of the free care program was evaluated through a desk review.ResultsUnder scenario 1, the free health care program will consume 14.60% and 16.00% of the MoH budget in 2018 and 2023, respectively, equaling 0.40% and 0.50% of the overall GDP in those years. The burden of the program will be substantially lower under scenarios 2 and 3, ranging from 11.70% to 14.60% and 9.70% to 14.60% of the MoH’s budget, respectively. Furthermore, the estimated optimal tax rate for expanding the fiscal space was 24.00%, in contrast to the current tax rate of 17.30%. This indicates that a slight increase in total tax revenues would be sufficient to fund the program, as the 6.70% margin for increasing tax revenues (24.00%-17.30%) will suffice to cover the policy, whose burden was estimated at 2.50% of the total tax revenues. The findings also reported an average technical efficiency score of 0.73 from 2008 to 2018, suggesting there may be room for additional savings.ConclusionsTo efficiently meet the policy’s financial requirements, it is imperative to strengthen domestic resource mobilization through taxation, reduce health care inefficiency, and improve the coordination of joint financing arrangements. These must go together with a strong commitment from the government to pursue program implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14427-z.

Introduction

After implementing three significant health care provision changes to improve maternal and child health between 1996 and 2015 (a user fee policy from 1996 to 2006; a national subsidy policy for maternity care from 2006 to 2015, which included an 80% reduction in fees at health centers, district hospitals, and referral hospitals; and a results-based financing scheme from 2006 to 2015), Burkina Faso introduced a free health care policy for maternal and child health in April 2016. Reducing or eliminating user fees aims to improve access to care, advancing the goal of universal health coverage. This, in turn, should increase the use of institutional health care services and improve health outcomes for vulnerable populations like women, children, and the poorest segment.

The literature shows that many nations have begun establishing universal free health care services across their territories. Instead of abolishing user fees for everyone, some nations have concentrated on particular services or diseases, such as HIV, TB, malaria, renal failure, and caesarean sections, or particular population segments, such as the older adults and poor [1]. Burkina Faso implemented its free health care policy for select populations, notably women and children under five. At launch, the amount of funding needed to implement the free health care policy from 2006 to 2015 was expected to be FCFA 31 billion (Franc de la Communauté Financière de l’Afrique de l’Ouest), or USD 53 million each year [2]. This total fund was intended to pay for free maternity care, including caesarean sections, normal deliveries, and other emergency obstetric and newborn care services. However, the available funds were just FCFA 18 billion, or USD 31 million, leaving a 42% funding gap.

Moreover, abrupt increase in stock-outs of essential drugs and supplies were reported through the program’s monitoring following the implementation of the free health care policy. For example, only 24% of the essential drugs were replenished in 2016 compared to 89% in 2015 [1]. Research indicates a correlation between revenue loss due to the removal of user fees at the facility level and subsequent drug and supply shortages, negatively affecting health care service delivery [3–5]. The outcomes of the free health care program could differ from those expected if funding and resources are insufficient, possibly leading to unanticipated consequences. In light of the challenges associated with its implementation, it is crucial to comprehend whether Burkina Faso’s free health care program is sustainable. This study examines the fiscal sustainability or channels through which resources could be leveraged to finance the policy’s pursuit, which is essential to increasing effects. It complements earlier research that examined the effects of Burkina Faso’s free health care policy on maternal and child care [6, 7].

Methods

Study settings

Burkina Faso is a low-income, landlocked West African country with a total population of 20,505,155 inhabitants as of 2021 [8]. Women of reproductive age comprise 17.43% of the total population, while children comprise 24.10% [9]. As indicated by the estimated 39.4‰ crude birth rate and the synthetic fertility rate of 5.4 children per woman, the target populations for the free health care policy for maternal and child care appear to be expanding [10]. Education and health care remain limited because of the population’s low economic power. The maternal and child mortality rates are high, at 371 per 100,000 live births and 61 per 1,000 infants under five [11]. The free health care policy for maternal and child care, which is currently in vigor, aims to make health care more accessible by eliminating user fees at the point of care. This policy is in effect in all public health facilities (primary health centers, district, regional, and national hospitals) as well as some confessional accredited facilities for childhood diseases, pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum care, as well as screening and treatment of precancerous cervical lesions and breast examination. All direct in-facility costs, including hospitalization, medicine and consumables, and medical acts, such as consultations, postoperative care, lab exams, and referral transportation costs, are waived under the policy [12]. A standard operating procedure ensures management and outlines how it will be implemented. Internal Ministry of Health reports show increased service use since the policy’s implementation [13]. However, several implementation-related challenges have been raised, including insufficient monitoring and evaluation and various irregularities in patient care in certain health facilities. Furthermore, implementing the free health care policy for maternal and child care has coincided with increased terrorist attacks in several health districts. Consequently, security spending now accounts for a larger portion of the state budget. For example, Table 1 shows that military spending as a percentage of total government spending nearly doubled from 2016 to 2022, increasing from 5.40% to 9.20% of the state budget. In the meantime, government spending on health decreased from 10.00% in 2017 to 8.40% in 2022 after peaking at 11.00% in 2016 with the introduction of the free health care policy for mother and child health, and 11.60% in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, although the tax-to-GDP ratio increased between 2015 and 2022, the budget deficit worsened with public debt rising steadily. Table 1 also shows a general decrease in official development assistance from 2015 to 2022, despite the noted peak in 2020. The funding shifts in favor of defense could jeopardize the previously demonstrated benefits of the free mother and child health care policy [6, 7], highlighting the significance of studying the policy’s long-term viability. In this context, it becomes especially crucial to examine the financial space available for the government to raise funds or reallocate savings in order to support the free health care policy for mother and child health.

Understanding the concepts of fiscal space and fiscal sustainability

The fiscal sustainability of the free health care program was analyzed using the fiscal space diamond. Fiscal space is “the capacity of a government to provide additional budgetary resources for a desired purpose without any prejudice to the sustainability of its financial position” [14]. Fiscal space analysis examined whether sufficient financial resources for free health care services are available for public expenses. Fiscal space covers a wide range of aspects. Under macroeconomic stability and debt sustainability, it refers to raising taxes, reducing spending, and mobilizing resources [14]. Fiscal space was also appreciated according to the market’s perception of sovereign risk, government debt sustainability, balance sheet composition, and external and private sector debt [15]. These definitions link fiscal space to fiscal sustainability, which is the capacity of the government to finance its desired expenditure plans, settle any debt, and preserve its solvency, at least over the long term.

This study used a slightly modified R Roy, A Heuty and E Letouze [16] four-dimension definition of fiscal space to assess whether Burkina Faso had adequate financial capacity to support its free health care program. J Mathonnat, L Petitfour and Y Tapsoba [17] and M Audibert, J Mathonnat and S Belem [1] also employed R Roy, A Heuty and E Letouze [16] four-dimension definition of fiscal space in their research. The four dimensions considered included mobilization of domestic revenues (1), efficient resource use/spending (2), mobilization of official development assistance (3), and deficit financing through monetary financing, i.e. the creation of central bank money (4), as shown in Fig. 1. Compared to earlier research that merely used expert and trend analyses to project additional resources that could be mobilized through each dimension of the budgetary space [1, 17], the current study introduces innovation. Indeed, econometrics techniques were factored into objective additional resources that could be mobilized throughout two of the four dimensions of the fiscal space, including mobilization of domestic revenues and efficient resource use/spending. However, the analysis of monetary financing is of only marginal significance in this study because the Central Bank of West African States (also the Central Bank of Burkina Faso) prohibited, since 2002, money issuance as a strategy to pay government initiatives [18]. In consequence, the present fiscal sustainability analysis focused on estimating additional resources that could be raised through the first three fiscal space dimensions.

Study design and data sources

The study combined quantitative techniques with a desk analysis of financial and programmatic documents to answer the research question. The fiscal burden of the maternal and child free health care policy, which was analyzed under various scenarios, used data from the Ministry of Health [19]. The data included projected target populations and unit costs per target group for the years 2018 to 2023. The optimal tax rate that should allow the mobilization of additional domestic resources was estimated through the application of a tax rate model developed by WG Scully [20]. This optimal tax rate was calculated using the West African Economic and Monetary Union Central Bank’s historical tax pressure rate and annual real GDP data, which covered the years 1970 through 2018 [21]. The use of the West African Economic and Monetary Union Central Bank’s data provides a robust regional macro-fiscal framework to anchor the tax pressure and possibilities of comparisons with regional tax pressure objectives. The analysis of official development assistance as a financing channel covered the period from 2009 to 2021 using data from The World Bank’s World Development Indicators [22]. The data used to investigate the efficiency of health service provision by districts were retrospectively collected from the National Health Information System (NHIS), for which data reliability has been acknowledged [23–25]. Input and output data used for the latter analysis covered the period from 2008 to 2018. The analysis of the burden of the free health care policy for maternal and child health, optimal tax rate, and official development assistance used complete retrospective data from national and international sources. The analysis of the efficiency of health service provision used NHIS-based data from 40 of the country’s 70 health districts. The excluded districts were either newly created (11 health districts) or had incomplete data.

Statistical analysis

Fiscal burden of the free health care program

According to the Ministry of Health, the annual costs of the free health care program are obtained by summing the costs of providing four harmonized groups of services including (1) childbirth and obstetric care; (2) care for children under five; (3) prenatal care; (4) and screening for precancerous lesions (Ministry of Health, 2019). Each group of services’ annual cost was computed by multiplying the unit cost by the predicted demand. Thus, to estimate the yearly cost of childbirth and obstetric care, the average unit cost of childbirth and obstetric care is multiplied by the projected numbers of childbirth and obstetric procedures. The annual cost of care for children under five is calculated by multiplying the average unit cost of the service group by the projected demand. Similarly, the annual costs of prenatal care and screening for precancerous lesions are calculated by multiplying the average unit costs of the two benefit groups, respectively, by the projected number of cares during pregnancy and screening for precancerous lesions among women aged 25 to 55 years.

Based on the abovementioned methods, the total cost of the free health care program was projected based on three broad scenarios. The first scenario considers historical cost patterns between 2016 and 2018, followed by an increase in service delivery efficiency, from 58.50% in 2019 to 90.00% in 2025 (scenario 1). The second scenario predicts that starting in 2019, increased contraceptive use will lead to a decrease in expected deliveries. It also assumes that, starting in 2021, the deployment of more community-based health workers will lead to a decline in consultations, resulting in a gradual 20.00% and 35.00% reduction in benefits for children under five (scenario 2). Finally, the third scenario predicts that health facilities will, in addition to the anticipated decline in deliveries and consultations for children under five, universally adopt the rates, i.e. tariffs of the homogenous groupings of services approved in 2018 (scenario 3).

Estimation of the optimal tax rate

A model of determining the optimal tax rate was estimated to forecast fiscal revenues that could be mobilized to finance the free health care program in the short and medium terms. The model in question is a version of the one developed by WG Scully [20]. This approach aims to identify the ideal tax rate that will not impede the country’s economic growth. It aimed to project the mobilization of additional domestic resources to fund the free health care policy. This approach was chosen over the qualitative projection of tax revenues, which relies on the analyst’s knowledge of both ongoing tax reforms and the development of the economy. Analyzing the efficiency of the provision of health services

A second model was estimated to assess the efficiency of health care input utilization across the health system. The model evaluated the percentage of health care resources that can be saved to pay for health care services when the available resources are used as efficiently as possible. A data envelopment analysis evaluated the health district’s technical efficiency in producing services [28, 29]. An output-oriented estimation was applied to determine the optimal proportion of outputs that can be generated under current input constraints, assuming that organizations evolve in variable returns to scale (variable returns to scale model). The variable returns to scale hypothesis assume that each entity can operate at different scales of production and that the relationships between inputs and outputs are not proportional. In other words, a proportional input increase will not necessarily lead to a proportional output increase. This assumption is realistic because it recognizes that entities may exhibit economies of scale or diseconomies of scale depending on their size, technology, or other factors. The inputs selected for the analysis included health workforce density, defined as the number of health workers (doctors, nurses, midwives, and other health professionals) and the number of health facilities (primary health care centers and district hospitals) in each sanitary district. The outcomes included the total number of assisted deliveries, severe malaria cases in children under five, and maternal deaths due to obstetric complications. Analyzing official development assistance as a strategy for funding the free health care program

The potential for financing the free health care policy for maternal and child care through official development assistance was appreciated by examining trends in official development assistance. The suitability of financing the free health care policy for maternal and child care through aid was evaluated according to the general trend in official development assistance in percentage of the current government expenditure and the gross national income (GNI) over ten years.

Results

Fiscal burden of the free health care program

Table 1 reports the fiscal burden of Burkina Faso’s maternal and child free health care program from 2018 to 2023, based on the three scenarios using national forecasts. The baseline scenario, i.e. scenario 1, shows that the free health care policy will consume 14.60% and 16.00% of the MoH budget in 2018 and 2023 (Table 2, L3), respectively, equaling 0.40% and 0.50% of the overall GDP in those years (Table 2, L2). Including the MoH’s current expenditures, the free health care policy would represent 18.70% to 20.90% of the MoH’s budget over 2018–2021 (Table 2, L4). Moreover, implementing the free health care policy will necessitate between 2.30% and 2.50% of total tax revenues (Table 2, L8) or between 2.30% and 2.70% of total current expenditures from 2018 to 2023 (Table 2, L10).

Discussion

This work is unique in thoroughly investigating the various avenues for sustaining the free health policy for maternal and child care that has been in effect in Burkina Faso since 2016. This study sheds light on resource mobilization to finance the policy and channels for increased impacts. Most previous studies on user fee removal policies focused on the effects of such policy reforms without adequately addressing the challenges in funding the pursuit of these policies in the face of their positive effects. The current investigation sheds light on some important insights worthy of discussion.

According to the analysis, the government of Burkina Faso could easily fund its free health care policy for maternal and child health by encouraging joint financing arrangements that increase tax revenue collection and enhance aid disbursement. According to the analysis of the funding projections under the different scenarios, the fiscal cost of the free health care policy for maternal and child health will account for 14.60% to 16.00% of the MoH total budget from 2016 to 2023, or 2.30% to 2.50% of the total tax revenues for the country during the period under consideration. Given that the free health care policy’s cost burden appears to be manageable compared to the total government budget, funding it will require a financial trade-off to the MoH’s advantage.

Moreover, according to our estimations, the country’s real GDP increased by 6.40% throughout the study period. This forecast puts real GDP growth slightly above the International Monetary Fund’s (IMF) predicted 6.00% increase over the same period. However, our estimate was lower than the real GDP increase of 7.70% predicted by the nation’s National Plan for Economic and Social Development [27]. According to several researches, increases in real GDP support the expansion of the fiscal space [14, 32, 33]. However, further fiscal space expansion through good macroeconomic conditions seems improbable given that the nation’s macroeconomic conditions over the analyzed period appeared steady. Additionally, our research found that the optimal tax rate is 24.00%, as opposed to the current tax rate of 17.30% [26]. The free health care policy for mother and child care, anticipated to cost between 0.40% and 0.50% of GDP between 2018 and 2023, will be more than adequately funded by the possible rise in tax collections of 6.70% (24.00% − 17.30%).

Moreover, the IMF predicted that the tax burden would gradually rise from 17.3% of GDP in 2018 to 18.70% in 2020 and 19.30% in 2023 under the terms of the Extended Credit Facility from Burkina Faso [26]. The average tax burden is anticipated to be 19.30% in 2018 under the nation’s national plan for economic and social development [27], with rates of 20.40% in 2019 and 21.00% in 2020. According to the government’s trend prediction of tax revenues, tax revenues are anticipated to rise steadily from 17.60% of GDP in 2019 to 19.60% in 2023. Again, if any increase in tax revenues under these three possibilities mentioned above or that from the present study comes to reality, the additional funds that would be generated would be sufficient to cover the cost of the free health care program, which has been projected to range between 0.20% and 0.50% of GDP as shown on Table 2, L2 Scenario 1, 2, 3).

The findings of this study also revealed possibilities for increasing the fiscal space if health districts can improve their technical efficiency in health care delivery. The analysis also demonstrated that initiatives to increase the efficiency of health care delivery in public facilities might contribute to freeing up more financial resources for this program and expand the fiscal space. The technical efficiency analysis found that districts that provide health care services waste substantial resources, with 70.00% to 87.00% of districts being inefficient. Between 2008 and 2018, districts may have boosted their outputs by 27.00%. Accordingly, more districts should have been able to create more products with the resources at their disposal. It bolsters the case for improved tariff harmonization for covered services and supports the argument that if rates for covered services were better harmonized between districts and levels of care, inefficiencies would be reduced.

On the other hand, monetary financing and additional government borrowing to fund the user fee removal policy were risky strategies. For instance, the analysis showed that official development assistance had decreased steadily. Finally, the free health care policy for maternal care and children under the age of five covers approximately 25.50% of the country’s population at a cost of no more than 16.00% of the MoH budget and 2.40% of tax revenues (Table 2). Although the current analysis suggests that the user fee removal policy is sustainable, additional steps could be taken to reduce its fiscal burden and thus increase its sustainability. Changing the content of the care bundle and the type of service covered could be a viable option. Another option would be to revise the eligibility criteria, such as conditioning eligibility on income and poverty levels to further limit eligible people to those who cannot afford the services.

Limitations and strengths

This study has limitations. Although Burkina Faso’s National Health Information System (NHIS) has often been acknowledged as appropriate [34–36], it does not offer a wide variety of variables specific to assessing the efficiency of health service delivery at the primary healthcare level. The analysis only took into account the number of ambulances, the density of health workers, and the number of health facilities per district. Moreover, the analysis relied heavily on secondary data on which the evaluators had limited control over their completeness. This might have had some impact on the analysis’s conclusions as well. However, this study has a significant strength. The present study employed a data-driven quantitative approach to fiscal space analysis rather than using qualitative approaches to assess fiscal space. In contrast, most prior analyses of budgetary space available for financing health policies conducted by P Heller [14], J Mathonnat, L Petitfour and Y Tapsoba [17] and M Audibert, J Mathonnat and S Belem [1] were based on simple projections of fiscal variables. There are more uncertainties with simple projections because they depend on how the analyst views the fiscal and economic policies and shocks. Instead, the econometric methodology used to estimate the ideal tax pressure and technical efficiency analysis in health care delivery minimizes the impact that the assessors’ opinions, knowledge of the country’s policies, and perception of economic shocks might have on the analysis.

Conclusions

This study provides insights on ways for more significant impacts and resource mobilization to finance the maternal and child-free health care program in Burkina Faso. The findings suggest that sustainable program financing is within reach range. Improved taxation and technical efficiency are two possible avenues for effectively funding the pursuit of the maternal and child-free health care program. Despite these potential avenues, there will still be difficulties because the government must make fiscal concessions and firmly commit to implementing the program. Therefore, to effectively meet the policy’s financial requirements, it is imperative to strengthen domestic resource mobilization through taxation, reduce health care inefficiency, and improve the coordination of joint financing arrangements as more technical and financial partners become interested in assisting the country.
