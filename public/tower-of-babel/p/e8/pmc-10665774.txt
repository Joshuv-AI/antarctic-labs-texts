Pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents for HER2‐altered advanced non‐small cell lung cancer: A retrospective real‐world study
Authors: Yaning Yang, Guangjian Yang, Weihua Li, Xuezhi Hao, Shuyang Zhang, Xin Ai, Siyu Lei, Haiyan Xu, Yan Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough some targeted therapies have been shown to be effective in treating HER2‐altered non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the survival demands have not yet been met due to the high cost and limited availability. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness and safety of pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents, including apatinib, anlotinib, and bevacizumab, in previously treated patients with HER2‐altered advanced NSCLC.MethodsIn this retrospective real‐world study, patients with HER2‐altered NSCLC who received pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents as a second‐ or later‐line treatment between November 2015 and January 2022 were reviewed. The objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), progression‐free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and safety profiles of patients were analyzed.ResultsA total of 107 patients were included in the analysis, of which 59 patients (55.1%) had received at least two lines of prior chemotherapy or tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Most of them (87.9%) were identified as harboring HER2 exon 20 insertions. At the data cutoff date (May 13, 2022), the ORR, DCR, median PFS, and median OS were 19.6% (21/107), 94.4% (101/107), 7.13 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 6.26–8.01), and 19.50 months (95% CI: 12.83–26.17), respectively. There was no difference in the PFS between patients receiving apatinib or anlotinib/bevacizumab (median PFS, 7.13 vs. 6.27 months, hazard ratio [HR] = 1.49, 95% CI: 0.87–2.54, p = 0.15). The most frequent grade 3 or higher treatment‐related adverse events was diarrhea (17.6%), followed by hypertension (11.0%) and nausea (3.3%). No treatment‐related death occurred.ConclusionIn this study, pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents demonstrated promising efficacy and were tolerable in HER2‐altered NSCLC patients.Keywords: antiangiogenic agents, HER2 alteration, non‐small cell lung cancer, pyrotinib, real‐world study

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide. According to statistics from 2022, only around 20% of patients lived over 5 years. 1 With the advances in targeted therapy, novel molecularly targeted treatments, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), have made a significant impact on standard treatments for non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with corresponding genetic alterations. 2 In the era of genetic testing, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2, also known as ERBB2) is another oncogenic alteration associated with lung cancer that has been identified. It is estimated that 1.4%–6.7% of NSCLC cases exhibit HER2 mutations or primary HER2 amplification. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In addition, HER2 mutations are more prevalent in females and nonsmokers. 3 , 4

The effectiveness of anti‐HER2 agents, including TKIs, anti‐HER2 antibodies, and anti‐HER2 antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs) in patients with HER2‐altered NSCLC has been evaluated in numerous studies. 8 Contrary to breast or gastric cancer, these agents showed limited benefit for NSCLC patients with HER2‐alterations. The TKIs targeting HER2 such as afatinib, dacomitinib, neratinib, and poziotinib only achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of less than 30% and progression‐free survival (PFS) of 3–5.5 months for HER2‐altered NSCLC, which was insufficient for patients in such need. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Moreover, the addition of trastuzumab to chemotherapy failed to improve the outcomes of such patients. 15 Anti‐HER2 ADCs, such as ado‐trastuzumab emtansine (T‐DM1) and trastuzumab deruxtecan (DS‐8201), have recently presented promising efficacy on HER2‐altered NSCLC, with ORRs of 44%–55%. 16 , 17 Nevertheless, due to the high cost and limited availability in China mainland, the application is still limited. Therefore, there is a need to explore more anti‐HER2 therapeutic approaches.

Antiangiogenic agents such as apatinib, anlotinib, and bevacizumab inhibit angiogenesis and suppress tumor growth by targeting the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathway. 18 The upregulation of EGFR signaling leads to increased VEGF production, which in turn leads to resistance to EGFR‐TKIs. 18 The ARTEMIS, NEJ‐026, and RELAY studies have demonstrated the benefit of combining an EGFR TKI with a VEGF inhibitor in NSCLC. 19 , 20 , 21 Additionally, the VEGF pathway has also been reported to be crucial to the HER2 pathway, 6 and, in another study, the combination of antiangiogenic regimens was found to enhance the antitumor effects of HER2 inhibitors. 22

Pyrotinib is an irreversible oral pan‐HER TKI targeting HER1, HER2, and HER4. In HER2‐altered NSCLC, pyrotinib monotherapy achieved an ORR of 30% and a PFS of 6.4 months, 23 whereas the addition of apatinib, an antiangiogenic agent, improved the ORR to 45.5%, with a PFS of 6.8 months. 24 However, the combination of pyrotinib and antiangiogenic regimens has yet to be proven in the real world. Thus, we conducted this retrospective real‐world study to investigate the effectiveness and safety of pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic regimens on HER2‐alterd NSCLC patients. We present the following article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist.

METHODS

Study design and patients

In this retrospective study, advanced NSCLC patients harboring HER2 alterations who received pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents as second‐ or later‐line treatment from November 30, 2015 to January 31, 2022 at the National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences were included. Eligible patients were 18 years of age or older, with histologically or cytologically confirmed advanced NSCLC, and with HER2 mutations or HER2 amplification. HER2 alterations were detected by next‐generation sequencing (NGS) testing in institutional laboratories or qualified genetic testing companies, based on the Illumina sequencing system. The specimens included tissue or circulating tumor DNA from plasma.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection and response assessment

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected from electronic medical records, including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), smoking status, brain metastasis status, HER2 alteration subtype, and pyrotinib‐based therapeutic regimens (including treatment line, antiangiogenic agent name, and dosage). Computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were performed to evaluate the tumor response according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever came first. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. ORR was defined as the proportion of patients who had a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), and disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the rate of patients with a CR, PR or stable disease (SD).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were mainly used in this study. Kaplan‐Meier analysis was used to estimate PFS and OS, and stratified log‐rank tests were used to compare survival data between groups. Corresponding hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated using the Cox proportional regression model. Two‐sided p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. R version 4.1.3 was used to plot survival curves, forest plots, and swimmer plots. Data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software version 23.0 (SPSS, IBM Corporation).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

From November 30, 2015 to January 31, 2022, 107 patients with HER2‐altered advanced NSCLC who received pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents as second‐ or later‐line treatment were included in this study (Figure S1). NGS testing was conducted in all patients, and tumor tissues were used as specimens in the majority of them (n = 98, 91.6%). A total of 94 (87.9%) patients were identified as harboring HER2 exon 20 insertions. Among them, A775_G776insYVMA was the most common variant (n = 67, 62.6%), followed by P780_Y781insGSP (n = 11, 10.3%), and G776delinsVC (n = 11, 10.3%). In addition, seven patients (6.5%) were identified as harboring missense mutations and gene amplification was confirmed in six patients (5.6%) (Figure 1). A total of 60 patients had comutation genetics, the most common being TP53 (40/60, 76%), followed by RB1 (6/60, 10%), and KMT2A mutation (6/60, 10%).

The proportion and detailed molecular landscape of non‐small cell lung cancer patients with distinct HER2‐activating alterations.

In the study, 27.1% of the patients were over 60 years old (n = 29), 47.9% were male (n = 47), and 73.8% were nonsmokers (n = 79). A total of 16 patients (15.0%) presented with brain metastasis at baseline. The antiangiogenic agent was apatinib in 90 patients (84.1%), and anlotinib or bevacizumab in 17 patients (15.9%). Nearly half of the patients received pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents as second‐line treatment (n = 48, 44.9%) (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

By the date of the data cutoff (May 13, 2022), 82 patients had experienced PFS events. There were 21 patients who had a PR, 80 patients who had SD, and six patients experienced disease progression within 1 month of treatment initiation. The ORR, DCR, median PFS, and median OS of patients were 19.6% (21/107), 94.4% (101/107), 7.13 months (95% CI: 6.26–8.01), and 19.50 months (95% CI: 12.83–26.17), respectively (Figure 2a).

Kaplan–Meier curves of progression‐free survival (PFS) in all patients (a). PFS outcomes among subgroups with apatinib or other antiangiogenetic agents (b).

A subgroup analysis revealed that patients with ECOG PS of 2–3 had shorter PFS than those with 0–1 (median PFS 3.47 vs. 7.87 months, HR = 5.15, 95%CI: 2.64–10.05) (Figure 3). As for other baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, smoking history, NGS specimen, and whether patients had taken other TKIs previously, no significant differences in PFS were found.

Subgroup analysis of progression‐free survival.

No significant difference in PFS was found between patients treated with pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents as second‐line and as third‐ or later‐line therapy (median PFS 8.47 months vs. 6.77 months, HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.75–1.81, p = 0.49). In the second‐line setting, the ORR and DCR were 14.6% (7/48) and 97.9% (47/48), while in the third‐ or later‐line setting, the ORR and DCR were 23.7% (14/59) and 91.5% (54/59), respectively. Nevertheless, the OS was prolonged in the second‐line group (median OS: 26.33 months vs. 15.90 months, HR = 1.88, 95% CI: 0.99–3.57, p = 0.05).

The PFS was similar in patients who received apatinib as an antiangiogenic agent and in those who received anlotinib or bevacizumab (median PFS: 7.13 vs. 6.27 months, HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 0.87–2.54, p = 0.15) (Figure 2b). For patients with brain metastases, the ORR, DCR, median PFS, and median OS were 6.3% (1/16), 100% (16/16), 10.43 months (95% CI: 6.41–14.46), and 17.93 months (95% CI: 7.17–28.70), respectively (Figure S2). As for the various types of alterations, the median PFS of patients with insertion, missense mutation and amplification were 7.03 months (95% CI: 5.87–8.20), 17.53 months (95%CI: NR), and 6.77 months (95% CI: 6.26–8.01) (Figure S3). There was no difference in PFS between A775_G776insYVMA and other HER2 alteration subtypes, with median PFS of 7.47 (95% CI: 6.13–8.80) months and 7.03 (95% CI: 4.92–9.15) months (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.57–1.41, p = 0.63) (Figure S4).

Among 107 patients who received pyrotinib and antiangiogenic agents, 80 cases had comutation record. TP53 (n = 40, 50.0%) aberration and RB1 mutation (n = 6, 7.5%) were also the most frequent comutations observed in this study. The ORR was 90.2% (37/41) and 100% (39/39). There was no difference in PFS of patients with TP53 and RB1 mutation and patients without (median PFS 7.03 vs. 8.03 months, HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 0.80–2.32, p = 0.26).

Safety profile

Adverse events (AEs) were recorded in 91 of 107 patients. As summarized in Table 2, the majority of treatment‐related adverse events (TRAEs) were grade 1 or 2, with the most common being weakness (75.8%), diarrhea (74.7%), hypertension (63.7%), nausea (62.6%), and oral mucositis (47.3%). A total of 16 patients (17.6%) reported at least one grade 3 TRAE. The most common grade 3 or higher TRAEs were diarrhea (17.6%) and hypertension (11.0%). Only one patient experienced grade 4 TRAE, which was due to increased alanine transaminase (ALT). A total of 35 patients (32.7%) experienced pyrotinib dose reductions (to 320 mg once daily) or dose interruptions due to TRAEs. Antiangiogenic agents were discontinued in 10 patients (9.3%) due to TRAEs.

DISCUSSION

Our study provided real‐world evidence regarding the effectiveness of pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents in the second‐ or later‐line setting for advanced NSCLC with HER2 alterations. Additionally, we identified 12 types of genetic alterations of HER2, with exon 20 insertions being the most prevalent variant, in which the predominant was A775_G776insYVMA, followed by G776delinsVC and P780_Y781insGSP. TP53 aberrations were the most common comutation (40.4%), which is consistent with previous studies. 25 , 26

In this retrospective cohort study, the median PFS was 7.13 months (95% CI: 6.26–8.01), with an ORR of 19.6% and a DCR of 94.4%. HER2‐targeted agents, such as afatinib, dacomitinib, neratinib, and poziotinib, have not shown favorable antitumor activities in HER2‐altered NSCLC patients. In the NICHE trial, afatinib was administered to NSCLC patients with HER2 mutations, and the median PFS was 15.9 weeks (95% CI: 6.0–35.4) and the median OS was 56.0 weeks (95% CI: 16.3–not estimable). 11 Another study revealed that among heavily pretreated NSCLC patients with HER2 mutations treated with afatinib, the median time to treatment failure was 2.9 months, and the ORR was 19%. 12 In a phase II clinical trial, Kris et al. gave oral dacomitinib to patients with HER2‐mutant or amplified lung cancers, and the outcome was unfavorable since the ORR was only 12% and the median PFS was 3 months. 14 In addition, neratinib only obtained a median PFS of 5.5 months in HER2 mutant lung cancer, 13 and poziotinib, a novel irreversible pan‐HER inhibitor, achieved a median PFS of 5.6 months and an ORR of 42%. 27

Recently, anti‐HER2 ADCs showed promising efficacy in HER2‐altered NSCLC. In a phase II basket study of ado‐trastuzumab emtansine, the ORR was 44% and the PFS was 5 months. 17 The DESTINY‐Lung01 trial administered trastuzumab deruxtecan to patients carrying HER2 alterations. An ORR of 24.5% (95% CI: 13.3%–38.9%) was demonstrated with a median PFS of 5.4 (95% CI: 2.8–7.0) months in HER2‐overexpressing patients, and an ORR of 61.9% (95% CI: 45.6%–76.4%) and a median PFS of 14.0 months (95% CI: 6.4–14.0 months) in HER2‐mutated patients. 16 Based on these results, trastuzumab deruxtecan was approved for use in pretreated advanced NSCLC patients with HER2 mutations by Food and Drug Administration on August 11, 2022. However, there is still a limited application due to the high cost and limited availability in China mainland.

Previous studies have demonstrated the potential efficacy of pyrotinib in patients with HER2‐altered lung cancer. In a single‐arm, phase II study of pyrotinib monotherapy in patients with HER2‐mutated advanced NSCLC, patients whose disease had progressed after first‐line treatment had a median PFS of 6.9 (95% CI: 5.5–8.3) months, median OS of 14.4 (95% CI: 12.3–21.3) months and an ORR of 30%. 23 Another single‐arm, phase II study with pyrotinib combined with apatinib as the second‐ or later‐line setting in patients with HER2‐altered NSCLC yielded a median PFS of 6.8 (95% CI: 5.3–9.77) months and an ORR of 45.5%. 24 In our study, the PFS of patients who received pyrotinib in combination with antiangiogenic agents was 7.13 months (95% CI: 6.26–8.01). Exon 20 insertion was the most common type in HER2 alterations and the PFS was 7.03 months (95% CI: 5.87–8.20), which was similar to previous studies. The ORR in our study was inferior to the above studies, possibly due to the complexity and higher heterogeneity in real‐world settings. In this study, there were 13.1% patients with ECOG PS of 2–3, and our studies revealed ECOG PS of 2–3 had worse outcomes than those with 0–1. PFS did not differ statistically between different treatment lines or antiangiogenic agents. Nevertheless, the OS was prolonged in patients who received pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents as the second‐line treatment (median OS, 26.33 months vs. 15.90 months, HR = 1.88, 95% CI: 0.99–3.57, p = 0.05), which indicates that early use of pyrotinib in conjunction with antiangiogenic agents may result in better survival outcomes.

In terms of safety, the majority of TRAEs were grades 1 or 2, with no new TRAEs reported. 23 , 24 The most frequently reported TRAE in this study was weakness (75.8%), diarrhea (74.7%), hypertension (63.7%), nausea (62.6%), oral mucositis (47.3%), with grade 1–2 predominately. The most common reported grade 3 TRAE was diarrhea (17.6%), and only one patient experienced grade 4 ALT increase, which was similar to a previous study. 23 There were only 7.7% of patients who developed proteinuria after taking apatinib, which may due to the low‐dose of apatinib in this study (at a daily dose of 250 mg). All TRAEs were tolerable and manageable through dose reduction or interruption.

This study had several limitations. First, due to its retrospective nature, it inevitably contains selection bias. Furthermore, the majority of our study patients were given apatinib as the antiangiogenic agent, which might have biased the results of analyzing the effectiveness of different antiangiogenic agents on outcomes. Moreover, the sample size of patients with HER2 missense mutations and primary HER2 amplification was limited, and the effectiveness of pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic regimens in such patients should be further investigated.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated promising efficacy and tolerable safety profile of pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic regimens for pretreated patients with advanced HER2‐altered NSCLC in the real‐world setting. Pyrotinib plus antiangiogenic agents might be a potential treatment strategy for HER2‐mutated or amplified NSCLC patients, which should be evaluated in future clinical trials.
