Fragility of Observed Associations in Selection of Empirical Antifungal Therapy for Critically Ill Non-Neutropenic Patients [Letter]
Authors: Saba Manzoor, Daniele Roberto Giacobbe, Ronald Chow
Published: 2026

Dear editor

In a recent multicenter observational study, Albakri et al evaluated empirical antifungal therapy (EAFT) in 324 critically ill, non-neutropenic ICU patients.1 The authors report a statistically significant association between antifungal selection and in-hospital mortality on unadjusted analysis (p = 0.028), with lower mortality in patients receiving fluconazole (63.2% vs. 76.8% for caspofungin, 75.0% for liposomal amphotericin B, and 84.8%for anidulafungin), along with differences in clinical improvement, hepatotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity apparently favoring fluconazole. Importantly, as the authors acknowledge, the mortality association did not persist after multivariable adjustment, underscoring the complexity of interpreting observational data in this setting. Conversely, no multivariable analyses were conducted for adjusting associations of empirical antifungal selection with clinical improvement, hepatotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity.

Fragility analysis is a complementary methodological tool that evaluates the stability of statistically significant results. The FI estimates the minimum number of outcome reclassifications required to render a significant result nonsignificant, providing an intuitive measure of how sensitive an observed association is to potential misclassification or bias.2 In observational studies of critically ill patients, where confirmed infection rates are low and subgroup sizes are small, results may be especially fragile. We therefore applied fragility analysis to the key significant findings reported by Albakri et al regarding the favorable association of fluconazole selection with endpoints assessed only in univariable comparisons (clinical improvement, hepatotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity), to better assess the generalization potential of their conclusions.

We extracted raw event counts and sample sizes for each statistically significant comparison from the published tables of Albakri et al1 We constructed 2×2 contingency tables of events and non-events for each antifungal agent pair. The FI was calculated by iteratively simulating changes from non-events to events in the comparator arm until a nonsignificant result was obtained using Fisher’s exact test, with type I error set at 0.05. Comparisons that did not achieve statistical significance in the original study were not amenable to fragility analysis.

Our analysis yielded an FI of 6 for clinical improvement (fluconazole vs. anidulafungin), 3 for hepatotoxicity (fluconazole vs. anidulafungin), and 2 for both nephrotoxicity comparisons (fluconazole vs. caspofungin; fluconazole vs. anidulafungin). In practical terms, even the most robust finding, clinical improvement with an FI of 6, could be overturned by reclassifying the outcome of just six patients. The safety outcomes were more fragile still: the hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity differences could be reversed by changing the outcome status of 3 and 2 patients, respectively.2

There are multiple complementary interpretations of these fragile findings. From an epidemiological perspective, low FI values reflect the challenges of detecting between-agent differences in a population where confirmed fungal infection occurred in only 7.41% of patients and subgroup sizes were small, with the fluconazole arm comprising just 38 individuals. Even modest imbalances in baseline severity, plausible in a retrospective non-randomized design, could produce associations that dissolve with minimal adjustment, as seen for mortality. Furthermore, sparse event data and random variation may also limit the extrapolability of findings regardless of cohort size.3

The apparent advantages of fluconazole across clinical improvement, hepatotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity likely reflect confounding by indication. Fluconazole was preferentially prescribed to patients with lower illness severity and fewer organ failures, a pattern well-documented in observational antifungal studies.4 Echinocandins and liposomal amphotericin B, reserved for sicker patients, may thus carry inflated apparent toxicity rates and worse outcome profiles independent of pharmacological mechanism.1 The 2024 American Thoracic Society Clinical Practice Guideline now conditionally recommends against routine EAFT in non-neutropenic, non-transplant ICU patients, and the fragility of all observed between-agent differences reinforces this recommendation.5

At a health systems level, antifungal stewardship in the ICU remains underdeveloped relative to antibacterial stewardship, despite growing concern about resistance, drug toxicity, and cost.3 If between-agent outcome differences across efficacy and safety endpoints are statistically fragile and driven primarily by confounding, positioning one empirical agent over another on this basis risks perpetuating unjustified treatment hierarchies. Furthermore, it should be mentioned that universal empirical antifungal treatment has also been debated. The 2024 American Thoracic Society Clinical Practice Guideline now conditionally recommends against routine EAFT in non-neutropenic, non-transplant ICU patients.5

This analysis has limitations. The FI depends on the quality of the underlying data and shares the limitations noted by Albakri et al, including retrospective design, single-country sampling, and potential unmeasured confounding. It is calculated using Fisher’s exact test, which may differ from the original analysis, and applies only to binary outcomes, limiting comparison with adjusted analyses.6 The FI should be interpreted alongside effect estimates, confidence intervals, and the broader evidence base, including the 2024 ATS guideline.5

Taken together, our analysis may complement the findings reported by Albakri et al by quantifying the generalization instability of their key comparisons. While their study makes an important descriptive contribution by characterizing real-world antifungal prescribing in an underrepresented geographic and clinical setting, prospective studies with adequate power and tailored study designs with proper adjustments remain necessary to clarify whether antifungal selection could influence outcomes, or whether the observed differences just reflect underlying patient severity and confounding by indication.
