The neglected role of partner treatment in congenital syphilis control in Brazil: nationwide evidence from 2007 to 2023
Authors: Mariana D. Fernandes, Rodrigo C. Menezes, Isadora B.C. Almeida, Catarina D. Fernandes, Isabella B.B. Ferreira, Bruno B. Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCongenital syphilis (CS) remains a significant public health issue in Brazil despite universal access to testing and treatment. Untreated partners can enable maternal reinfection, yet its national coverage and determinants are unclear. This study aimed to quantify the coverage of adequate partner treatment among CS cases and to identify sociodemographic and care-related factors associated with it.MethodsRepeated cross-sectional study of all CS notifications in Brazil's Notifiable Diseases Information System (SINAN), 2007–2023. Records with missing information on partner treatment status were excluded from main analysis. The outcome was documented adequate partner treatment in SINAN. Multivariable logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% CIs.FindingsFrom 2007 to 2023, 297,062 CS cases were reported. CS notification rates increased from 1·9 to 9·6 per 1000 live births in the period. Coverage of adequate partner treatment rose modestly from 20% (n = 809) in 2007 to 27·7% (n = 4864) in 2023. Median maternal age was 23 years (IQR 20–28). Most cases occurred among Mixed (126,434; 57·9%), White (54,465; 24·9%), and Black women (20,534; 9·4%). Higher maternal education (aOR 2·41, 95% CI 2·35–2·48) and receipt of prenatal care (aOR 2·64, 95% CI 2·56–2·73) were associated with partner treatment, whereas odds were lower among Black (aOR 0·85, 95% CI 0·82–0·89) and Mixed women (aOR 0·81, 95% CI 0·79–0·84) and in the North region.InterpretationDocumented adequate partner treatment remained low and inequitable across race, education, and region amid rising CS rates. Because cases missing partner-treatment status had poorer care engagement, real estimates are likely worse. Strengthening partner management within antenatal care, ensuring benzathine penicillin availability and improving surveillance capture of partner-treatment indicators are essential to advance EMTCT goals in Brazil.FundingThe work was supported by the Intramural Research Program of the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation, Brazil (B.B.A.) and CNPq.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, SciELO, PAHO/WHO websites, and Brazilian Ministry of Health publications (Portuguese and English) for studies and reports on congenital syphilis (CS), partner management, surveillance definitions, and prenatal strategies in Brazil and the Americas (Jan 2000 to Nov 2025). Search terms included “congenital syphilis Brazil,” “partner treatment”, “prenatal care”, “penicillin shortage”, and “EMTCT syphilis”.

Across the Americas, syphilis incidence has increased since 2020. Estimates from WHO and PAHO indicate approximately 3·36 million new adult infections in 2022, reflecting a substantial CS burden and underscoring the need to strengthen testing and treatment during pregnancy. Under the current EMTCT (Elimination of Mother-to-Child Transmission) validation framework, WHO requires a sustained CS incidence of ≤0·5 cases per 1000 live births, along with service coverage and human-rights criteria.

In Brazil, MoH surveillance bulletins document persistently high CS incidence and periodic adjustments to case definitions and reporting practices. A key methodological change occurred in 2017, when partner treatment was removed as a criterion for CS notification. Structural barriers have further aggravated this scenario. Benzathine penicillin shortages between 2013 and 2017 were temporally associated with increased CS incidence, and subsequent evaluations revealed declining completeness of core SINAN variables, particularly the partner-treatment field, after the 2017 revision.

Despite these observations, no previous study has comprehensively quantified, at the national level, the coverage of adequate partner treatment among confirmed CS cases or examined how sociodemographic inequities and surveillance gaps jointly influence this indicator over time.

Added value of this study

Using 17 years of nationwide surveillance, this study provides the first comprehensive quantification of partner treatment coverage among congenital syphilis (CS) cases in Brazil. It documents how infrequently partners of mothers of infants with CS are recorded as adequately treated, characterizes geographic and temporal heterogeneity, and identifies key sociodemographic and care-related determinants of partner treatment. Potential bias from missing partner-treatment information was also assessed to ensure robustness.

Nationally, fewer than one in four CS notifications documented adequate partner treatment. Coverage was substantially higher among women who received prenatal care and increased progressively with maternal education, whereas odds were markedly lower among Black and Mixed women and in the North region.

Implications of all the available evidence

The convergence of a rising or sustained syphilis burden in the Americas, persistently low and unequal partner treatment in Brazil, and surveillance definitions that no longer capture partner management underscores the need for renewed policy attention. Ensuring uninterrupted access to benzathine penicillin, integrating partner management as a tracked indicator within EMTCT monitoring frameworks, and reinstating or strengthening mandatory reporting fields for partner treatment in SINAN are critical steps. Expanding the Pré-natal do Parceiro model with equity targets could further reduce maternal reinfection and accelerate progress toward EMTCT goals. These findings also call for implementation research to test outreach strategies for partner engagement, evaluate post-2017 data-quality interventions.

Introduction

Syphilis, caused by Treponema pallidum, remains a persistent global health problem more than a century after the advent of effective treatment.1 When acquired during pregnancy, the infection can be vertically transmitted to the fetus, causing congenital syphilis (CS), a preventable condition associated with spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, preterm birth, congenital malformations, and irreversible neurological damage.2 In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated 700,000 congenital syphilis cases and 390,000 adverse birth outcomes globally.3

The burden of CS is disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries. Roughly three quarters of CS burden occurs in the WHO African and Eastern Mediterranean regions, highlighting persistent gaps in access to antenatal screening and treatment in resource-limited settings.4,5 In the Americas, incidence is the highest globally, reaching 6·5 cases per 1000 live births.6 To address this, the WHO launched the initiative for the elimination of mother-to-child transmission (EMTCT) of HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B.7 For syphilis, countries must sustain a CS incidence of ≤0·5 cases per 1000 live births for at least one year to receive elimination validation. In alignment with EMTCT framework, Brazil has established national validation processes for the elimination of vertical transmission, including certification mechanisms and recognition of good practices at the municipal and state levels.8 However, the country remains far from this target, with the national rate of 7·7 per 1000 live births in 2021, despite public nationwide screening and treatment.9

One of the main challenges in CS elimination is the inadequate treatment of sexual partners of infected pregnant women.10,11 Although timely maternal treatment with benzathine penicillin is highly effective in preventing vertical transmission, its protective effect can be nullified when sexual partners remain untreated, leading to maternal reinfection during pregnancy.12 Evidence from antenatal syphilis control programmes suggests that interventions incorporating partner notification and treatment reduce maternal reinfection and are associated with lower rates of adverse pregnancy outcomes and congenital syphilis.13 In Brazil, national guidelines recommend universal partner testing and treatment during prenatal care.14 However, implementation remains inconsistent, with documented barriers such as limited partner engagement in prenatal services, stigma, difficulties in contact tracing, and structural constraints within primary health care.15

In Brazil, most antenatal syphilis screening, diagnosis, and treatment are delivered through primary health care (PHC). Therefore, partner management is both a behavioral challenge and a health-system performance indicator, dependent on timely testing, availability of benzathine penicillin, continuity of prenatal follow-up, and the capacity of PHC teams to engage partners and document care.16 Heterogeneity in PHC coverage and quality across regions and municipalities may translate into unequal opportunities for partner treatment, reflecting existing racial, socioeconomic, and regional inequities in congenital syphilis burden.17 Furthermore, in 2017, the national case definition for CS was revised, and the variable indicating concomitant partner treatment, previously required to classify maternal treatment as adequate, became optional. This change likely increased missingness in partner treatment reporting and reduced the sensitivity of routine surveillance to detect failures in partner management over time.18

Understanding and quantifying the determinants of partner treatment is essential for informing EMTCT processes, guide strengthening of PHC services, and designing effective public health interventions. Yet, no prior national-level analysis has quantified coverage and inequities in partner treatment. Therefore, this study uses 17 years of national surveillance data to (i) describe spatial and temporal trends in CS incidence across Brazil, and (ii) identify sociodemographic, clinical, and regional factors associated with adequate partner treatment.

Methods

Ethics statement

Publicly available, de-identified data from the Brazilian Ministry of Health were analyzed. These datasets had been pre-processed by the Ministry to remove duplicate entries, verify consistency, and ensure data integrity. In accordance with Resolution No. 674/2022 of the Brazilian National Health Council, secondary analyses of anonymized public data do not require individual informed consent or approval by a research ethics committee.

Study design

This is a nationwide observational, surveillance-based repeated cross-sectional study that used CS notifications from the Notifiable Diseases Information System (Sistema de Informação de Agravos de Notificação, SINAN) and live-birth data from the Live Births Information System (Sistema de Informação sobre Nascidos Vivos; SINASC) from January 2007 to December, 2023, according to data availability. The study comprised two analytical components: an ecological approach of annual congenital syphilis reported rates per 1000 live births by state/region, and repeated cross-sectional (individual-level) analyses of congenital syphilis notifications. All CS cases reported during this period were eligible for inclusion. Records with missing information on partner treatment status were excluded.

Data source

CS notifications data were acquired from SINAN, a nationwide compulsory notification system that compiles epidemiological surveillance data to support public health planning at all government levels.19 Congenital syphilis has been included in Brazil's list of compulsory notifiable diseases since 1986. As with other routine surveillance systems, data quality may vary by variable, place, and time. Completeness is generally higher for demographic fields and lower for care-process variables. Data are routinely checked by municipal and state surveillance teams before consolidation at the national level.

Per Brazilian Ministry of Health guidelines, CS is confirmed when one or more of the following are present: newborns, stillbirths, or abortions from women with untreated or inadequately treated syphilis; microbiological evidence of T. pallidum in fetal or neonatal specimens (nasal secretion, skin lesion, biopsy, or autopsy); or children under 13 years with compatible clinical, cerebrospinal fluid, or radiological findings plus a reactive non-treponemal test. Laboratory criteria include infant non-treponemal titers at least two dilutions higher than maternal titers in paired samples at delivery; a two-dilution increase in titers during follow-up of exposed children; persistence of reactive titers beyond six months in adequately treated neonates; or reactive treponemal tests after 18 months of age in children without prior diagnosis. CS is further classified as early/recent when manifestations occurred within the first two years of life, reflecting active infection acquired in utero, and late when they were detected after two years of age, representing chronic or progressive disease if not treated. Further details and data-dictionary resources are available at: https://portalsinan.saude.gov.br/sifilis-congenita.

Live birth data were obtained from SINASC, which records information from the Declaration of Live Birth, a legal document completed by the healthcare professional attending the birth.20 SINASC is Brazil's official national Live Birth Information System, implemented to collect counts and characteristics of live births across the entire country. It covers more than 90% of births nationwide and includes maternal sociodemographic characteristics, pregnancy history, and newborn outcomes. These data served as denominators for notification rate calculations. Official details in SINASC are available at: https://opendatasus.saude.gov.br/dataset/sistema-de-informacao-sobre-nascidos-vivos-sinasc.

Variable definitions

The primary outcome was the report of adequate partner treatment, recorded in SINAN as a binary variable. It is recorded in SINAN by local surveillance teams during case investigation, based on clinical records and reported information at the time of notification. As defined by the Brazilian Ministry of Health, it refers to administration to the sexual partner of the same therapeutic regimen given to the pregnant woman (benzathine penicillin G in the appropriate dose for syphilis stage), initiated concurrently with or shortly after maternal treatment, and completed before delivery.21

Exposure variables were grouped into three domains. Maternal sociodemographic characteristics included age, race/skin color (classified according to the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics [IBGE]), region and federative unit of residence, and education level. Pregnancy care and diagnosis variables comprised receipt of prenatal care and timing of maternal syphilis diagnosis (prenatal, delivery, or postpartum). Infant characteristics included sex, final case classification of congenital syphilis, and clinical outcome (live birth, stillbirth, or neonatal death). Detailed variable definitions were reported in Supplementary Table S1. Annual notification rate coefficients were calculated using CS notifications from SINAN and the number of live births from SINASC per year and maternal place of residence.

Statistical analysis

Temporal trends and spatial distribution of congenital syphilis were assessed descriptively. Annual notification rates were calculated as the number of reported congenital syphilis cases per 1000 live births, using live-birth counts from SINASC as denominators and classifying cases by year of diagnosis and maternal place of residence. Notification rates were summarized graphically over time and mapped by state and region to describe geographic heterogeneity. To assess temporal trends in congenital syphilis rates, a log-linear Prais–Winsten regression was fitted with first-order autocorrelation correction. Results were presented as annual percentage change (APC) with 95% confidence intervals derived from model-based standard errors. Given the 2017 revision of surveillance criteria, trend models were fitted for the full period (2007–2023) and as stratified periods (2007–2016 and 2017–2023).

Maternal age was summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Formal normality testing was not emphasized given potential skewness and the limited value of normality testing in very large sample sizes. Categorical variables were reported as absolute counts and percentages and compared using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Category-specific post-hoc comparisons were reported with Bonferroni adjustment within each variable. Records with missing data on partner treatment status were excluded from the primary analysis. To evaluate potential selection bias, sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of included versus excluded cases were compared. For covariates, no imputation was utilized given the surveillance nature of the data and the potential for non-random missingness. Categories coded in SINAN as “ignored/unknown” or with missing values were retained as an explicit level rather than treated as missing to avoid substantial loss of sample size and because missingness is plausibly informative in routine surveillance data.

Associations between maternal and care characteristics and adequate partner treatment were assessed using binary logistic regression. Covariates were selected a priori assuming a causal structure based on prior literature. Factors included maternal sociodemographic characteristics (age, race, and education) and geographic context (macro-region as a proxy for health-system access and performance) influence engagement with prenatal care and opportunities for partner testing and treatment. Prenatal care was treated as a care-process exposure reflecting access to and continuity of antenatal services. As secondary analysis, to explore regional heterogeneity, additional multivariable models were fitted separately for each Brazilian macro-region using the same set of covariates, excluding region. Model performance was summarized using McFadden's pseudo-R2 and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Given the large sample size, interpretation emphasized the magnitude and direction of associations and their programmatic relevance. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0·05.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4·1·2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with the compareGroups (version 4·5·1) package. The map was generated using QGIS (version 3·24·3), using shapefiles publicly available by IBGE. State-level average congenital syphilis annual CS notification rates were classified using equal-interval categorization.

Role of the funding source

Funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, writing of the report or decision to submit.

Results

Between 2007 and 2023, 96,743,330 live births occurred and 297,062 CS cases were reported in Brazil. Of these, 78,734 (26·5%) records were excluded due to missing information on partner treatment, resulting in a final sample of 218,328 cases (Fig. 1A). Compared with included cases, excluded cases had higher proportions of missing or “ignored” sociodemographic and clinical data, lower documented prenatal care coverage, and less often a diagnosis during prenatal care. Considering all eligible participants, national reported CS notification rate increased from 1·95 cases per 1000 live births in 2007 to 9·64 per 1000 live births in 2023. The overall trend showed a sustained increase over the study period, with an annual percentage change (APC) of 10·7% per year (95% CI 6·7–14·8; p < 0·001) and a high first-order autoregressive [AR(1)] coefficient (ρ = 0·91). With 2017 as the breakpoint, annual CS notification rate increased rapidly before 2017 (APC: 17·9% per year; corresponding slope on the log scale β = 0·164, p < 0·001) and continued to increase thereafter but at a markedly slower rate (post-2017 APC: 2·3% per year), with strong evidence of a change in slope after 2017 (Δβ for slope change = −0·142, p < 0·001). Annual notification rate varied year-to-year, including a decline in 2020 and a recovery thereafter. The overall pattern was consistent across macro-regions, although levels differed, with the Southeast and Northeast persistently presenting the highest values and the Central-West the lowest (Fig. 1B). At state level, the highest cumulative rates were observed in Rio de Janeiro and Sergipe, whereas Rondônia had the lowest rates (Fig. 1C).

Partner treatment remained consistently low throughout the study period (Fig. 1D). Nationally, the proportion of cases with documented partner treatment remained below 20% (n = 809/4036) through 2017, increased to approximately 30% (n = 5941/19,023) in 2018–2019, declined in 2020, and remained around 25–30% from 2021 to 2023 (n = 4864/17,542; 27·7). Macro-regional patterns were heterogeneous, with the Central-West showing the highest levels in 2018–2019 and the North persistently showing the lowest partner treatment rates across most years (Fig. 1D). Of note, given that excluded records disproportionately reflected weaker engagement with prenatal care and poorer data completeness, partner treatment coverage estimated is likely overestimated relative to the full set of notified cases.

Regarding CS cases with available partner treatment status, more than half occurred in children born to mothers who self-identified as Mixed (57·9%; n = 126,434), followed by White (24·9%; n = 54,465) and Black (9·4%; n = 20,534). Indigenous and Asian women accounted for smaller portions of the sample (0·4%; n = 757 and 0·4%; n = 765, respectively) (Table 1). Prenatal care was reported for 82·4% (n = 180,009) of cases. The diagnosis of maternal syphilis occurred during prenatal care in 56·0% (n = 122,366) of cases, at the time of delivery in 33·3% (n = 72,798), and in the postpartum period in 7·2% (n = 15,757). Discussion

In this nationwide analysis of 17 years of congenital syphilis surveillance data, fewer than one in four cases had documentation of adequate partner treatment, despite universal access to testing and treatment in Brazil. This directly undermines the EMTCT targets set by the WHO of ≤0·5 cases per 1000 live births, and highlights a persistent gap in the congenital syphilis prevention cascade. To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide analysis in Brazil to quantify documented partner treatment and its inequities across sociodemographic and geographic strata. Because untreated partners can sustain exposure and lead to maternal reinfection during pregnancy, inadequate partner management remains a plausible mechanism through which transmission chains persist and preventable adverse outcomes continue to occur.10,11

The distribution of infant outcomes among notified cases reinforces the clinical severity and preventability of congenital syphilis. Even in a setting of universal access to screening and treatment, fetal loss and neonatal deaths remain present among reported cases, underscoring persistent failures pointing to failures in timely diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up along the continuum of antenatal and perinatal care. Although infant outcomes were presented descriptively and not analyzed as consequences of partner treatment in this study, they provide critical context for the urgency of strengthening partner management as part of EMTCT implementation.

Prenatal care emerged as the strongest predictor of partner treatment, with approximately threefold higher odds compared with women without prenatal follow-up. Given the very large sample size, statistically significant associations are expected even for modest effects. Therefore, public health prioritization should be guided primarily by effect size, consistency across strata, and programmatic relevance. This finding reinforces antenatal care as the main operational entry point for partner management and aligns with Brazilian guidelines recommending early initiation of prenatal care (by the 12th gestational week), a minimum of six consultations, repeated syphilis testing across pregnancy, and timely benzathine penicillin treatment.22

However, national evaluations consistently document gaps in prenatal care performance, including late initiation, insufficient visits, incomplete delivery of recommended procedures, and limited capacity for partner engagement, often compounded by workforce turnover, high service demand, and time constraints in primary health care that reduce opportunities for counseling, follow-up, and documentation.23,24 Within this context, the “Pré-natal do parceiro” policy, designed to integrate male partners into prenatal visits for counseling, vaccination, testing and linkage to care, represents a critical but underutilized strategy.25 Barriers such as limited awareness, inflexible clinic hours, stigma around sexually transmitted infections, gender norms that discourage preventive health-seeking among men, and fragmented service organization likely constrain its potential and should be central to efforts to strengthen partner management.11,26

Marked inequities were evident by race and maternal education. Women identifying as Black or mixed-race and those with lower educational attainment had consistently lower odds of partner treatment, reflecting broader structural barriers that shape access to, and continuity and quality of, prenatal care in Brazil. These disadvantages are not independent, as Black and Mixed-race populations in Brazil are disproportionately affected by lower educational opportunities,27 longer travel distances to health facilities, uneven distribution of healthcare resources, and historical underinvestment in their communities.27, 28, 29, 30 Beyond differential access, racism acts as an upstream determinant of health that can also influence the quality of care received, including diagnostic delays and inadequate follow-up, factors that plausibly undermine partner engagement and treatment documentation.

Educational attainment may also operate through complementary pathways. At the individual level, it can be a proxy for health literacy, autonomy, and communication capacity, which can facilitate navigating services and negotiating partner participation in treatment.31,32 At the service level, lower education may also mark populations less consistently reached by PHC outreach and retention strategies, where competing socioeconomic demands and discontinuity of care reduce both delivery and recording of partner management, leaving exposed precisely those whose management is most critical.33

Geographic differences further highlight system-level determinants in partner management. Women residing in the Central-West and South regions had higher odds of partner treatment compared to those in the North, where access barriers, service capacity constraints, and weaker PHC capillarity in remote territories can limit timely diagnosis, treatment, and continuity of care.16,34,35 Persistent regional disparities in resource allocation and service readiness, including uneven PHC coverage and the concentration of health professionals and specialized services in larger urban centers, may reduce opportunities for partner testing and treatment in underserved areas.36,37 Similar access-related challenges have been reported across Latin America, where populations in rural or marginalized settings face structural barriers to timely antenatal services.38,39

Service readiness is also uneven. Recent evidence found substantial gaps in primary-care readiness to deliver benzathine penicillin: in the Southeast and Northeast, only 22·7% and 40·8% of municipalities, respectively, reported administering benzathine penicillin in more than half of their primary care teams, which likely constrains timely treatment access.36 In the same analysis, greater availability of benzathine penicillin administration in primary care was linked to lower congenital syphilis incidence and was interpreted as consistent with reduced vertical transmission, reinforcing the programmatic importance of on-site treatment capacity. Beyond healthcare infrastructure, greater population mobility in the Brazilian’ South, including migration flows and tourism, may facilitate transmission, while in the Southeast, higher notification rate has been partly attributed to stronger surveillance systems and improved diagnostic capacity, leading to more complete detection rather than a true excess in transmission.16,22,23,35,40

Temporal trends in congenital syphilis notification rate must be interpreted considering major changes to Brazil's surveillance system and epidemiological dynamics. The reported annual notification rate increased sharply during 2007–2016, and no clear monotonic trend was observed from 2017 onwards, despite marked year-to-year fluctuations. This early rise likely reflects a combination of persistent gaps in prenatal care within PHC and an expanding capacity for detection and notification, due to wider availability of diagnostic testing and strengthened reporting routines. Over the study period, improvements in SINAN coverage and surveillance performance, supported by decentralization of syphilis diagnosis and management to primary care and by enhanced notification routines, may have increased case ascertainment and reporting completeness over time. Therefore, part of the observed rise in reported CS notifications, particularly in earlier years of the series, may reflect improved detection and reporting rather than a true increase in transmission alone.

National analyses of syphilis trends in Brazil have also shown a high, and in some settings increasing, conversion of maternal infection into congenital outcomes, attributed to failures in timely application of diagnostic and treatment algorithms for pregnant women and newborns and to structural constraints in health-service infrastructure.41 Evaluations of SINAN-Syphilis indicate that overall completeness of reporting fields improved over time, although care-process variables that are central to the prevention cascade, such as partner treatment and maternal education, remain among the least complete fields.42

The 2017 peak coincided with a substantial revision of the national case definition.18 Historically, one of the defining criteria for CS notification in Brazil was the absence or inadequacy of treatment of the pregnant woman's sexual partner. Under this definition, any live birth, stillbirth, or spontaneous abortion to a woman with syphilis during pregnancy, in which the partner was untreated or the partner's treatment status was unknown, was reportable as CS.43 In 2017, aiming to harmonize surveillance criteria with Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) and WHO recommendations, the Brazilian Ministry of Health updated the case definition. The revised criteria focused on the mother's clinical-epidemiological history and the child's clinical, laboratory, and radiological findings, thereby excluding partner treatment status from the epidemiological case definition.44,45 National strategies accompanied this shift, including expanded distribution of diagnostic and treatment supplies, development of information dissemination tools, and targeted research investments.46 Consequently, temporal patterns in partner treatment and related indicators should be interpreted primarily as reflecting shifts in surveillance capture and data completeness, rather than direct evidence of changes in underlying partner-management performance.

Although standardization improved cross-country comparability, it also removed an important accountability mechanism for partner management within Brazil's surveillance system. Studies indicate that this change reduced data completeness and increased underreporting of partner treatment, weakening partner tracing efforts and, consequently, syphilis control.42,47 This revision may have reduced programmatic visibility and prioritization of this intervention, with possible negative repercussions for control measures. Of note, WHO and PAHO continue to emphasize that partner testing and treatment are central to preventing maternal reinfection and interrupting transmission.44,45

Superimposed on these surveillance changes, recurrent shortages of benzathine penicillin may have disrupted timely therapy for pregnant women and their partners during part of the series.48 More recently, the COVID-19 pandemic caused substantial interruptions in antenatal care and sexually transmitted infection services, plausibly contributing to the sharp decline in reported cases in 2020 through reduced detection rather than a true reduction in transmission.49 Therefore, the post-2021 increase in reported notification rates should be interpreted cautiously as a mixed signal of service restoration and ongoing transmission risk.

The assessment of selection bias indicates that missing partner treatment data were not randomly distributed and concentrated among cases with weaker engagement in prenatal care, later or undocumented maternal syphilis diagnosis, and poorer recorded outcomes. Because these characteristics are consistently associated with access barriers and weaker service performance, missingness is likely related to the outcome itself. The most plausible direction of bias introduced by excluding these records is an overestimation of partner treatment coverage and an attenuation of social and health-system inequities. In this context, the observed prevalence of adequate partner treatment (23·1%) should be interpreted as an upper-bound estimate, and the true magnitude of gaps in partner management is likely larger, particularly among socially vulnerable populations. The removal of partner treatment status from the national congenital syphilis case definition in 2017 may have further amplified this bias by reducing incentives for complete reporting, thereby diminishing the visibility of programmatic failures in partner management.

In addition to missingness in the outcome field, item nonresponse in key covariates, particularly maternal education, was substantial. To minimize loss of information and avoid listwise deletion, “ignored/unknown” categories were retained in regression models. The fact that “ignored/unknown” categories were themselves associated with documented partner treatment, often heterogeneously by region, supports that item nonresponse in SINAN is informative and likely reflects variation in service documentation and care processes rather than missing at random.

This study has some limitations. First, the analysis relied on routinely collected surveillance data introduces the possibility of information bias due to underreporting, recording errors, misclassification, and variable completeness across place and time. The partner treatment field in SINAN may not fully capture the Ministry of Health's definition of adequacy (regimen, timing, and completion before delivery). Also, treating “ignored/unknown” covariate values as explicit categories reduced listwise deletion but does not eliminate bias from non-random missingness, therefore estimates for these categories should be interpreted as documentation/process indicators.

Second, exclusion of more than one quarter of reported cases because of missing partner-treatment status likely produced selection bias, as shown in the analysis, overestimating partner-treatment coverage and underestimating disparities. Third, the observational design precludes causal inference and residual confounding from unmeasured social and health system factors may influence may influence both documentation and the probability of partner treatment. Finally, because adequate partner treatment was not rare, odds ratios may overstate the corresponding risk ratios and should be interpreted as measures of association rather than causal effects on probability. Moreover, findings are limited to notified congenital syphilis cases, which may not generalize to all pregnancies affected by syphilis, especially in contexts with incomplete case detection.

Despite these limitations, this study leverages 17 years of surveillance data to provide the most comprehensive assessment to date of documented partner treatment among congenital syphilis notifications. Although the analysis did not evaluate specific interventions, the patterns observed identify actionable targets for policy and practice. At the municipal level, municipal health secretariats and Family Health Strategy teams could strengthen the “Pré-natal do parceiro” strategy through targeted outreach, flexible service hours, and integration of partner management into antenatal protocols; Community Health Agents could support active partner follow-up, particularly in remote and riverine settings where access barriers are greatest. At the state level, health secretariats can strengthen supervision, training, and audit-and-feedback cycles, and ensure coordinated referral pathways for high-risk pregnancies and congenital syphilis case management. At the federal level, the Ministry of Health is central to ensuring uninterrupted benzathine penicillin supply, strengthening partner-management indicators within routine monitoring, and considering mechanisms to improve completeness of partner-treatment fields, including integration into EMTCT dashboards to support accountability.

Adequate partner treatment was recorded in fewer than one in four cases and was more likely among women with prenatal care, higher education, and residence in the Central-West and South, and less likely among Black and Mixed-race women. Strengthening partner management, particularly through approaches that integrate prenatal care, address structural barriers, and improve data completeness, should be a priority for research and policy agendas. Addressing these challenges will be essential to reduce maternal reinfection, interrupt transmission, and move closer to the elimination of congenital syphilis in Brazil.
