Plasma proteomic and metabolomic profiling reveals molecular features relevant to neoadjuvant therapy response in locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Chu Xiao, Ziqin Deng, Guochao Zhang, Zhenlin Yang, Long Zhang, Hongfei Yin, Tao Fan, Di Wang, Yixiao Liu, Wenpeng Cai, Yu Ji, Jia Li, Tianle Liao, Chunxiang Li, Jie He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoadjuvant therapy (NAT) followed by radical surgery is the standard treatment for locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (LA-ESCC), yet many patients fail to achieve pathologic complete response (pCR). Predictive biomarkers and biological mechanisms underlying NAT resistance remain elusive. We aimed to address this challenge through longitudinal multi-omics profiling of patient plasma during NAT.MethodsWe performed longitudinal plasma proteomic and metabolomic analyses in a discovery cohort of patients with LA-ESCC (n = 40) receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy (nCT) or neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) to reveal molecular features relevant to treatment response. Predictive biomarkers for pCR were assessed in plasma samples from an independent validation cohort (n = 93) by ELISA. Mechanistic studies were conducted through in vitro cell co-culture, in vivo mouse experiments, and analysis of ESCC tissue samples from another validation cohort (n = 33).ResultsMulti-omics analysis revealed distinct molecular profiles between treatment responders and non-responders. Metabolomic profiling revealed dynamic treatment-associated changes in amino acid and lipid metabolism during NAT, and proteomic profiling highlighted baseline differences related to neutrophil activity and inflammatory responses. DEFA1 was validated as an unfavorable predictive biomarker for achieving pCR after nICT. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that DEFA1 fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment by promoting myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) differentiation and impairing CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity.ConclusionsThis study delineates the longitudinal plasma multi-omic landscape associated with NAT response in LA-ESCC and identifies DEFA1 as a predictive biomarker and potential therapeutic target for overcoming immunotherapy resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02733-3.

Background

Esophageal cancer imposes a substantial global disease burden as the seventh most common malignancy, with rising incidence and mortality rates in recent years. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the predominant histological subtype, accounting for approximately 90% of all cases [1]. Esophagectomy remains a cornerstone of treatment for locally advanced ESCC (LA-ESCC), but it faces significant challenges in achieving radical resection and delivering long-term benefits. Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has effectively improved tumor downstaging and the R0 resection rate, and has shown overall survival (OS) benefit in some trials [2, 3]. Currently, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by surgery is the standard of care for LA-ESCC [4]. Meanwhile, emerging evidence from studies such as the phase III ESCORT-NEO trial suggests that neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) incorporating anti-PD-(L)1 blockade may offer superior clinical outcomes [5–7].

Although advances in NAT hold promise for better patient outcomes, the 5-year survival rate for ESCC remains poor, at only 20–25% [8]. Notably, pathologic response is a major indicator for evaluating NAT response and guiding subsequent therapeutic strategies. Pathologic complete response (pCR) and major pathologic response (MPR) are commonly employed as pathological response endpoints in clinical trials [9–11], although their association with long-term survival remains variable across studies in ESCC. Compared with MPR, which reflects substantial but incomplete tumor regression, pCR represents the absence of residual viable tumor and thus a more stringent response phenotype. For patients with a suboptimal response to NAT, there remains an unmet clinical need for reliable predictive biomarkers and actionable therapeutic targets. Therefore, elucidating the molecular profiles and underlying mechanisms of differential response to NAT is essential for advancing therapeutic efficacy and enabling personalized treatment.

The advent of high-throughput sequencing has further accelerated the discovery of molecular biomarkers and resistance mechanisms [12, 13]. Extensive research has been conducted to identify determinants of NAT response, focusing on driver mutations [14], tumor microenvironment (TME) [15, 16], and host factors such as systemic inflammation [17, 18]. In LA-ESCC, single-cell and spatial omics studies showed that incomplete response to nICT was consistently linked to an immunosuppressive TME at baseline, especially exhausted CD8+ T cells, which contribute to pCR prediction [19, 20]. However, the clinical feasibility of such tissue-derived biomarkers is limited by the accessibility of pretreatment tumor tissues, coupled with significant intratumoral heterogeneity [21]. This has spurred growing interest in liquid biopsies, using blood, secretions, or excretions, as a minimally invasive alternative for biomarker discovery and mechanistic studies [22–24].

Currently, circulating tumor DNA clearance and cell-free DNA detection have shown promise for predicting NAT response in several solid tumors, including LA-ESCC, but remain highly dependent on detection precision [17, 25, 26]. Notably, plasma proteomics and metabolomics directly characterize the abundance and composition of circulating proteins and metabolites, providing potential approaches for biomarker and therapeutic target discovery [23, 24]. As complementary molecular layers, proteomics captures the functional effectors and regulators of biological processes, whereas metabolomics reflects downstream metabolic consequences and the physiological status of the host [27]. Their integrated interpretation may provide additional information on disease diagnosis and treatment-response characteristics [28–30], particularly through longitudinal profiling that captures dynamic molecular changes during disease progression and therapy, thereby facilitating treatment monitoring and the identification of response-associated features [29, 31]. Nonetheless, the utility of plasma proteomics and metabolomics for NAT-response prediction remains unclear.

Here, we performed longitudinal plasma metabolomic and proteomic profiling in a discovery cohort of 40 patients with LA-ESCC undergoing NAT, including nCT and nICT, to delineate circulating signatures associated with treatment outcome. Metabolomic analysis revealed metabolic remodeling linked to pCR, primarily involving amino acid-related pathways. Concurrently, untargeted proteomics together with Olink-based targeted profiling of immuno-oncology-related proteins identified circulating protein markers associated with therapeutic response. Among these, DEFA1 and galectin-3 emerged as predictive biomarkers of pathological response to nICT and nCT, respectively, and were independently validated in an external cohort of 93 patients. Moreover, preclinical studies in cell and animal models, complemented by tissue analyses from an additional validation cohort of 33 patients, demonstrated that DEFA1 contributes to an immunosuppressive TME, which supports the potential of DEFA1 as a therapeutic target to overcome resistance to nICT in ESCC.

Methods

Participants and specimens

Patients with LA-ESCC in the discovery cohort were enrolled in a phase II clinical trial in which 64 participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either nICT (socazolimab + TP) or nCT (placebo + TP). All patients received four cycles of treatment followed by surgery. Clinical staging was performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC, 8th edition). Each 21-day treatment cycle consisted of intravenous (IV) administration of socazolimab or placebo at 5 mg/kg on day 1, IV nab-paclitaxel at 125 mg/m² on days 1 and 8, and IV cisplatin at 75 mg/m² on day 1. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT was performed before and after NAT, and the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), defined as the highest voxel value of 18F-FDG uptake within the region of interest (ROI), was measured. Pathologic response was evaluated by experienced pathologists using post-NAT surgical specimens. pCR was defined as no residual viable tumor cells in either the primary tumor or resected lymph nodes, whereas MPR was defined as ≤ 10% residual viable tumor cells in the primary tumor. Of the 64 randomized patients, 40 with qualified longitudinal plasma samples and complete pathological response data were included in the discovery cohort; the remaining patients were excluded due to failure in sample quality control or missing paired/longitudinal specimens. For validation, two independent cohorts of LA-ESCC patients were recruited from the Department of Thoracic Surgery, National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The plasma biomarker validation cohorts comprised 66 patients receiving nICT and 27 patients receiving nCT. Pre-treatment plasma samples were collected from all participants, and pathological response was assessed using the Mandard tumor regression grade (TRG) system on surgical specimens. TRG 1 corresponds to pCR, TRG 1–2 generally corresponds to MPR, and TRG 3–5 indicates lesser or absent treatment response. An additional tissue-validation cohort included surgically resected tumor samples and corresponding pathological response data from 33 LA-ESCC patients who underwent nICT between 2019 and 2021. Fresh surgical specimens were fixed in formalin, processed, embedded in paraffin, and assembled into donor blocks. Regions of interest were identified on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections, and tissue cores were arrayed into a recipient paraffin block, which was serially sectioned for subsequent analyses.

Sample collection and multi-omics profiling

In the discovery cohort, plasma samples were collected at baseline (T0), after each treatment cycle (T1-T3), and before surgery (T4). A total of 194 qualified longitudinal plasma samples were subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiling to characterize treatment-related metabolic changes. In addition, paired baseline and post-treatment plasma samples (T0/T4; n = 80) were analyzed using the Olink® Target 96 Immuno-Oncology panel and untargeted LC-MS/MS proteomic profiling.

Plasma sample preparation

Peripheral blood samples were collected in 5 mL vacutainer tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and centrifuged for 15 min at 1500×g, 4 °C. The supernatant was stored at -80 °C until analysis.

LC-MS/MS proteomics

Plasma aliquots were thawed on ice, prepared, and analyzed in a randomized order. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000×g at 4℃ for 10 min to remove cellular debris, and the supernatant was transferred to a clean centrifuge tube. The top high-abundance proteins were removed (A36369, ThermoFisher Scientific), and protein concentration was measured by the BCA kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were subjected to enzymatic digestion. Dithiothreitol was added to a final concentration of 5 mM, followed by reduction at 56 °C for 30 min. Iodoacetamide was subsequently introduced at 11 mM for alkylation, with samples incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The alkylated samples were ultrafiltered and buffer-exchanged using urea solution. Trypsin was added for digestion overnight. Peptide fragments were collected by centrifugation with a recovery step using ultrapure water.

The peptides were dissolved in mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile in water) and were separated with an EASY-nLC 1200 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (ThermoFisher Scientific) using gradient mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in 90% acetonitrile), with a constant flow rate of 500 nL/min. The separated peptides were ionized by a nanospray Flex™ (NSI) ion source and analyzed by an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). The ion source voltage was 2.3 kV. The full MS scan resolution was set to 60,000 with a scan range of 400–1200 m/z. The MS/MS scan resolution was set to 30,000 with a fixed first mass of 110 m/z. The data acquisition employed a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set at 75% with an intensity threshold of 1E4 ions/s and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. The raw MS/MS data were processed by Proteome Discoverer (v2.4.1.15) and searched against the UniProt human SwissProt database (20,422 entries) concatenated with a reverse decoy database. The false discovery rates (FDRs) for protein, peptide, and peptide-spectrum match identifications were all set to 1%. Protein abundance was determined by label‑free quantification (LFQ) based on precursor ion intensities.

Olink target 96 platform

The targeted proteomic assay of plasma samples was performed using the Olink® Target Immuno-Oncology panel (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) technology was described by Assarsson et al. [32]. In brief, pairs of oligonucleotide-labeled antibody probes bound to target proteins. When two probes were close in proximity, the oligonucleotides hybridized in a pair-wise manner. Upon dual binding in proximity, the paired oligonucleotides hybridize and are extended by DNA polymerase to form a unique amplifiable sequence, which is subsequently quantified by real-time PCR (Signature Q100, LC-Bio Technology CO., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Internal controls, including the Incubation Control, the Extension Control, and the Detection Control, were spiked into every sample for quality control (QC). The raw cycle threshold (Ct) values obtained from the qPCR were sequentially adjusted using the Extension Control, the Inter-plate Controls (IPCs), and a pre-determined correction factor to generate the Normalized Protein Expression (NPX) values. NPX is a relative quantification unit on a log2 scale, logarithmically related to protein concentration. All assay validation data (detection limits, intra- and inter-assay precision data, etc.) were available in the Validation data-immuno-oncology document on the manufacturer’s website.

LC-MS/MS metabolomics

200 µL plasma aliquots were thawed at 4℃ and mixed with 400 µL cold methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v/v). The mixture was sonicated and centrifuged, and the supernatant was dried. Samples were redissolved in 100 µL acetonitrile/water solvent (1:1, v/v) before centrifugation and loading.

Plasma untargeted metabolomics was performed with ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC, 1290 Infinity LC, Agilent Technologies) coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight (AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer). For sample separation, mobile phase A contained 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in water, and mobile phase B contained acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was implemented as follows: 0–0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5–7 min, linear gradient from 95% to 65% B; 7–8 min, linear gradient from 65% to 40% B; 8–9 min, 40% B; 9–9.1 min, linear gradient returning to 95% B; 9.1–12 min, 95% B. MS data were acquired in both positive and negative ESI modes. All samples were maintained in the autosampler. Samples were analyzed in randomized order, and QC samples were interspersed within the sample queue. Following separation, mass spectrometry detection was performed in electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative ion modes: Ion Source Gas1 (Gas1) was set to 60 psi, Ion Source Gas2 (Gas2) as 60 psi, curtain gas (CUR) as 30 psi, ion source temperature was 600 °C, and IonSpray Voltage Floating (ISVF) was set to 5.0 kV ± 0.5 kV. The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) scan ranges were set to 60-1000 for primary scans and 25-1000 for secondary scans. The accumulation times were 0.20 s/spectrum for primary MS and 0.05 s/spectrum for secondary MS. Secondary mass spectra were acquired via data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode.

The raw MS data were converted to .mzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert (v3), followed by peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction performed with the XCMS software. Peak picking parameters were set as follows: centWave m/z = 10 ppm, peakwidth = c(10, 60), prefilter = c(10, 100). Peak grouping parameters were set as follows: bw = 5, mzwid = 0.025, minfrac = 0.5. The annotation of isotopes and adducts was performed with the Collection of Algorithms of Metabolite pRofile Annotation (CAMERA). Among the extracted ion features, only those detected in > 50% of all samples were retained for downstream analysis.

QC samples were prepared using mixed plasma samples. Four consecutive QC samples were detected before the LC-MS analysis. One QC sample was inserted into the detection sequence after every ten samples. Monitoring the total ion chromatogram (TIC) of QC samples in both positive and negative ESI modes showed QC samples having stable retention time and signal intensity throughout the run, so no additional QC-based signal drift correction was applied. The identification of metabolites was performed by comparing with an in-house database (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology) established with available authentic standards based on accurate m/z value (< 10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra. Metabolite identification reached MSI level 1 or 2. There were 636 metabolites under positive ESI and 532 metabolites under negative ESI.

Longitudinal analysis of plasma metabolites and proteins

Longitudinal changes in plasma metabolites and proteins were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with time point, pathological response status (pCR vs. non-pCR), and their interaction specified as fixed effects, and patient identity included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements: Abundance ∼ Time + pCR + Time × pCR + (1|PatientID). Time was modeled as a categorical variable with five levels (T0–T4) in metabolomics analysis or two levels (T0 and T4) in proteomics analysis. Patients from nICT or nCT groups were separately analysed. For each feature, the statistical significance of differential temporal change between pCR and non-pCR patients was determined from the Time × pCR interaction term using analysis of variance (ANOVA) of the fitted mixed-effects model. P values for the Time × pCR interaction were obtained using Type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite’s approximation. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was additionally applied across all tested features. The linear mixed-effects model was fitted using the R packages lme4 (v 2.0.1) and lmerTest (v 3.2.1).

Cell lines

Tumor cell lines MC38 (mouse colon carcinoma) and B16F10 (mouse melanoma) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The mouse ESCC cell line mEC25 [33] was kindly provided by Dr. Fu from Shenzhen University Health Science Center. All cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, KeyGEN) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. To generate ovalbumin (OVA)-overexpressing MC38 and B16F10 cells, full-length OVA cDNA (NM_205152.3) was cloned into the lentiviral vector Ubi-MCS-SV40-blasticidin (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). Lentiviral particles were then produced by co-transfecting this vector with Lenti-Pro Packaging Mix (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) into 293T cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocols. MC38 and B16F10 cells at 30% confluency were transduced with the resulting lentiviral particles and subsequently selected with 20 µg/mL blasticidin (Beyotime) for two weeks. All cell lines were routinely monitored for mycoplasma contamination and tested negative using the Mycolor One-Step Mycoplasma Detector (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mouse experiments

C57BL/6J mice (female, 6 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River (Beijing, China). C57BL/6 OT-1 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions maintained on 12-hour light/dark cycles, at 20–25 °C and 30–70% humidity. For tumor induction, mice were subcutaneously injected in the abdominal flank with 1 × 106 MC38 cells, 5 × 105 B16F10 cells, or 1 × 106 mEC25 cells. When the tumors became palpable, mice were randomized and assigned to different groups for treatment. Mice received intratumoral injections of recombinant mouse DEFA1 (rmDEFA1, 5 mg/kg; CSB-EP006652MO, CUSABIO) every other day for a total of five doses, with or without intraperitoneal administration of anti-PD-1 antibody (10 mg/kg; BioXcell) twice weekly for two weeks. For Ly6G depletion experiments, mice were additionally treated with intraperitoneal injections of anti-Ly6G antibody (10 mg/kg; Selleck) every three days, and the depletion efficiency of Ly6G⁺ cells was confirmed by flow cytometry. The length (L) and width (W) of tumors were measured with digital calipers. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: (L × W × W)/2. Group sizes (n) are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Isolation of mouse bone marrow cells and MDSC induction

Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femur and tibia of normal C57BL/6J mice under sterile conditions. Cell density was adjusted to 2 × 10⁶ cells/mL in complete medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL GM-CSF (PeproTech) and 20 ng/mL IL-6 (PeproTech) to induce bone marrow-derived MDSC (BM-MDSC) differentiation. Every 48 h, 50% of the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium supplemented with GM-CSF and IL-6. After 5 days of culture, cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry. To quantify total MDSCs, cells were stained with anti-CD11b-FITC and anti-Gr1-PerCP-Cy5.5. To distinguish MDSC subsets, cells were stained with anti-Ly6C-PE and anti-Ly6G-APC to distinguish monocytic-MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and polymorphonuclear-MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs). To evaluate the effect of rmDEFA1 on MDSC differentiation, bone marrow cells from the same isolation were divided into three treatment groups, and PBS, 10 ng/mL rmDEFA1, or 100 ng/mL rmDEFA1 was added to the medium, respectively. All other culture conditions and flow cytometry analyses remained identical to those described above.

CD8+ T cell mix-culture and co-culture assays

CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of OT-1 mice using the EasySep™ Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Stemcell) following the manufacturer’s protocol. To detect the inhibitory effects of BM-MDSCs on CD8+ T cell activation with or without antigen-specific stimulation, the purified CD8+ T cells were cultured together with BM-MDSCs at a ratio of 1:5 for 12 h in complete medium containing 1 µg/mL SIINFEKL (OVA257− 264, Sigma) peptide or not, and Brefeldin A (1:1000) was added to block cytokine secretion. Mixed cells were washed and resuspended in staining buffer, followed by staining with anti-CD8-BV421 and anti-TNF-α-BV785. For the CD8+ T cell killing assay, activated OT-1 CD8+ T cells pretreated with OVA257− 264 were mixed-cultured with BM-MDSCs and target cells (MC38-OVA, B16F10-OVA) in a round-bottom 96-well plate (Corning). Target cells alone were seeded in parallel at the same density to evaluate spontaneous death. Following mix-culture for 12 h, mixed cells were stained with anti-CD45-eFluor™506 for 30 min on ice, then stained with Annexin V-Alexa Fluor® 647 and propidium iodide before flow cytometric analysis. Tumor cells were gated as CD45-negative cells.

The Transwell co-culture assay was used to evaluate CD8+ T cell proliferation. 2 × 106 activated CD8+ T cells labeled with CFSE were seeded in the upper chambers, and BM-MDSCs pretreated with rmDEFA1 were inoculated in the lower chambers of the 24-well, 0.4 μm pore size transwell plate (Corning) for incubation of 24–72 h. The proliferation of CD8+ T cells was detected by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometric analysis of mouse tumor-infiltrating and splenic immune cells

Fresh mouse tumors and spleens were collected after mouse euthanasia. Tumors were dissociated to generate single-cell suspensions by using the Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi) and the gentleMACS Dissociator according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Spleens were directly ground, and the single-cell suspension was obtained by filtration through 70-µm cell strainers. Red blood cells were removed by using ACK Lysis Buffer (Beyotime). Fc receptors were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (eBioscience) at 4 °C for 15 min. For surface staining, cells were stained with antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by washing and staining with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience) at 4 °C for 30 min. Before intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized by the Fixation and Permeabilization Buffer kit (eBioscience) according to the manual. The following antibodies were used in flow cytometry: anti-mouse CD45 (30-F11, eFluor™506, eBioscience), anti-mouse CD3 (500A2, Alexa Fluor®700, Biolegend), anti-mouse CD8a (53 − 6.7, BV421, eBioscience), anti-mouse PD-1 (J43, PE, eBioscience), anti-mouse IFN-γ (XMG1.2, PE-Cyanine7, eBioscience), anti-mouse TNF-α (MP6-XT22, BV785, eBioscience), anti-mouse CD11b (M1/70, FITC, eBioscience), anti-mouse CD11c (N418, Super Bright™436, eBioscience), anti-mouse Gr1 (RB6-8C5, PerCP-Cyanine5.5, eBioscience), anti-mouse Ly6C (HK1.4, PE, eBioscience), anti-mouse Ly6G (1A8-Ly6g, APC, eBioscience), and anti-mouse MHC class II (NIMR-4, PE, eBioscience). Flow cytometry data were acquired on a Cytek NL-CLC3000 flow cytometer (CYTEK Biosciences) and analyzed by FlowJo software (v10.0.7).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

The formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue microarray (TMA) of patients’ surgical tumor samples from the tissue-validation cohort was used to assess the presence and density of DEFA1+ cells, the densities of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, and MPO+ neutrophils, respectively. The TMA sections underwent dewaxing, hydration, and antigen retrieval. The endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited by 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol, and then sections were washed and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma). After incubation with different primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, each section was incubated with the corresponding secondary biotinylated antibodies for 30 min at 37 °C and subjected to streptavidin-biotin complex (SABC) incubation and DAB staining. Finally, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted. Sections were scanned by a digital section scanner (Leica, Aperio, USA) and analyzed by ImageJ (v.2.14.0/1.54f). Briefly, the staining intensity (I) was graded as 0 (negative), 1 (low), 2 (moderate), and 3 (high), and the histoscore (H-score) was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positively stained area (A) by the intensity (I): H-score = A × I [34]. The maximum score was 300. For tumor-infiltrating immune cell quantification, the number of positive cells was counted in each high-magnification field (40×), and the average counts for 10 random fields were used as the cell density for the sample. The following primary antibodies were employed for IHC: DEFA1 (18057, Proteintech), CD8 (GB12068, Servicebio), and MPO (ab208670, Abcam).

For mIHC, FFPE sections underwent dewaxing, hydration, and antigen retrieval, and the experimental operations included sequential incubation with primary antibodies, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, and tyramide signal amplification (TSA) when staining for each target antigen. Sections were microwave heat-treated after each TSA operation. After all staining rounds, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. The mIHC staining targeting CD11b (AFRM0075, Opal 650), DEFA1 (18057-1-AP, Opal 570), and MPO (22225-1-AP, Opal 520) was performed using Opal 7-color Manual IHC kit (NEL801001KT, PerkinElmer) to detect the colocalization of DEFA1+ cells, CD11b+ cells, and MPO+ neutrophils in tumor tissues. The mIHC staining targeting CD66b (ab197678, Opal 520), CD14 (ET1610-85, Opal 690), HLA-DR (ET1610-66, Opal 570), CD11b (ab133357, Opal 650), and pan-cytokeratin (panCK) (PA125, Opal 620) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sections were scanned using the Mantra System (PerkinElmer, USA) and quantitatively analyzed by the multispectral imaging analysis and inForm image analysis software (Version 2.4).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The peripheral blood samples from patients in the biomarker-validation cohort were collected, and plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 2500×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The plasma supernatant was stored at -80 °C. The concentrations of plasma DEFA1 (CSB-E14155h, CUSABIO), galectin-3 (EK1126, MULTI SCIENCES), and FAP (CSB-EL008424HU, CUSABIO) were quantified using ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical and bioinformatic analyses were performed using R (v4.1.1) and GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2). Unless otherwise specified, all tests were two-sided. For high-dimensional differential analyses, nominal statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P values were also calculated and reported in the corresponding supplementary tables. For untargeted metabolomics, metabolites with more than 50% missing values across all samples were excluded. The remaining missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm (k = 10). Data were log10-transformed and normalized before downstream analyses. Prior to multivariable modeling, the data were mean-centered and scaled to unit variance using the scale function in R. Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the R package ropls (v1.42.0). Model robustness was assessed by 7-fold cross-validation and permutation testing (n = 200). Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were used to rank metabolite contributions. Univariable differential metabolite analysis was performed using Welch’s t-test. For untargeted proteomics, proteins with more than 10% missing values were removed. The remaining missing values were imputed using the KNN algorithm (k = 10). Data were log2-transformed and mean-normalized before analysis. Differential protein abundance analysis was performed using the limma package (v3.54.2) with empirical Bayes moderation. For the Olink® Target Immuno-Oncology panel, differential protein expression analysis was performed using Welch’s t-test. For high-dimensional analyses, nominal P values were used to identify candidate features, while BH-adjusted FDR values were additionally calculated and reported in the supplementary tables.

Univariable logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between individual clinical variables and pCR in the discovery cohort. For multivariable analysis, Firth’s bias-reduced logistic regression was applied to assess associations between protein markers and NAT response in the validation cohort. Protein variables were standardized to Z-scores before inclusion in the multivariable models. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC). The R packages caret (v6.0.94), logistf (v1.26.1), and pROC (v1.18.5) were used.

Functional enrichment analysis of plasma proteins was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v4.12.0) and Metascape 3.5, with the background set as all proteins identified in the untargeted proteomics experiment. KEGG pathway analysis of metabolites was performed using MetaboAnalyst (v6.0). For other comparisons, Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with thresholds denoted as P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), and P < 0.001 (***); P ≥ 0.05 was considered not significant (ns).

Results

Clinical characteristics and treatment response of the study cohorts

Two independent cohorts were used for validation, including a plasma cohort of 93 patients and a tissue cohort of 33 patients (Tables 2 and 3; Supplementary Tables 2–4).

In the discovery cohort, 36 of 40 patients (90.0%) achieved >50% tumor regression, and 27 (67.5%) achieved MPR. The pCR rate was 36.4% in the nICT group and 33.3% in the nCT group, while the MPR rate was 72.7% and 61.1%, respectively (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 1A). MPR was significantly associated with improved 3-year overall survival, whereas the association for pCR did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Fig. 1B). PET/CT showed marked metabolic regression in many patients who achieved pCR, suggesting that SUVmax reduction alone may have limited accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). Among baseline clinical variables, only advanced clinical stage was associated with lower odds of pCR in univariable analysis (Fig. 1F).

In the nICT group, this separation remained evident at T4, but not at intermediate time points (Fig. 2D). In the nICT group, baseline differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) identified from OPLS-DA were enriched in pantothenate/CoA biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and α-linolenic acid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Coenzyme A (CoA) is an essential cofactor known to enhance anti-tumor immunity by modulating T cell activity [36, 37]. Pantothenic acid (vitamin B5), a precursor of CoA synthesis, was relatively elevated in pCR patients (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Furthermore, univariable DAM analysis yielded consistent results with pathway enrichment findings, again highlighting amino acid- and lipid-related pathways (Fig. 2E-F; Supplementary Table 5). Among these metabolites, aspartate and γ-aminobutyric acid were lower, whereas kynurenic acid were higher, in pCR patients (Supplementary Fig. 2C); these metabolites have recently been implicated in the regulation of cancer immunity [38, 39]. During treatment (T1-T3), differences between pCR and non-pCR patients were mainly enriched in inositol phosphate, fatty acid, and amino acid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 2D-F; Supplementary Tables 6–8). At T4, analyses showed persistently lower amino acid-related metabolites in pCR patients than in non-pCR patients (Supplementary Fig. 2G-H; Supplementary Table 9). Intersection of the T0 and T4 DAMs identified metabolites consistently associated with response in nICT, most of which were phospholipids that remained higher in non-pCR patients at both time points (Supplementary Fig. 2I-K).

Baseline differential pathways in the nCT group based on OPLS-DA also involved amino acid and lipid metabolism, along with glutathione metabolism and the citrate cycle (Supplementary Fig. 2L). Univariable DAM analysis similarly revealed increased enrichment in α-linolenic acid metabolism and decreased enrichment in D-amino acid metabolism in pCR patients, consistent with the nICT group (Fig. 2G–H; Supplementary Table 10). Unlike nICT, longitudinal metabolic changes in nCT were less consistent and less pronounced at T4 (Supplementary Fig. 2M; Supplementary Tables 11–14).

Longitudinal clustering revealed distinct response-associated metabolic trajectories across patients. In nICT-pCR patients, increasing metabolite clusters were enriched in vitamin B6 and arachidonic acid metabolism, pathways previously linked to immune checkpoint regulation and tumor microenvironment reprogramming [40–42], whereas decreasing clusters were mainly related to amino acid metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, and the citrate cycle (Fig. 2I-L). In nCT-pCR patients, taurine/hypotaurine metabolism increased over time, while fatty acid- and amino acid-related metabolites decreased (Fig. 2M-P).

To further evaluate patient-level longitudinal changes in response-associated metabolites, we visualized representative metabolites and tested their significance using linear mixed-effects models. These included increasing pyridoxamine 5-phosphate and decreasing DL-serine in pCR versus non-pCR patients in the nICT group, as well as increasing taurine and decreasing α-linolenic acid in pCR versus non-pCR patients in the nCT group (Supplementary Fig. 2N-O). Overall, pathological response to NAT was associated with dynamic plasma metabolic reprogramming, predominantly involving amino acid- and lipid-related pathways.

Plasma proteomics reveals distinct features associated with pCR in ESCC patients receiving NAT

We next examined plasma protein signatures associated with pCR at T0 and T4. After depletion of high-abundance proteins, 873 plasma proteins were retained for analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Across all baseline samples, few differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were detected between pCR and non-pCR patients without stratification by treatment regimen (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

In the nICT cohort, 46 baseline DAPs were identified between pCR and non-pCR patients (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table 15). These proteins were enriched in leukocyte migration, acute inflammatory response, and response to virus (Fig. 3B). Among these, proteins elevated in pCR were linked to wound healing and actin cytoskeleton organization, whereas those reduced in pCR were associated with acute inflammatory response, neutrophil activation, and phagocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 3D–E). Given the importance of systemic immune status in immunotherapy response [43], we focused on DAPs related to leukocyte migration and neutrophil activation. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis showed functional connectivity among SELPL, CD34, and CD47 (Fig. 3C).

In the nCT cohort, 32 baseline DAPs were identified between pCR and non-pCR patients (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Fig. 3F; Supplementary Table 16). Consistent with the nICT group, DAPs were enriched in leukocyte migration and neutrophil chemotaxis. In particular, proteins related to myeloid leukocyte migration and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction were reduced in pCR patients, including SERPINE1, TSP1, PLF4, CCL5, and CXCL7, whereas galectin-3 was increased (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. 3G-H). PPI analysis supported functional interactions among these proteins (Fig. 3F), which have been implicated in neutrophil regulation [44–47].

Integration of DAPs identified at T0 and T4 revealed distinct response-associated proteomic patterns across time points (Supplementary Tables 15–18). Hierarchical clustering of the 114 combined DAPs identified eight major protein clusters (Fig. 3G), which were functionally annotated by enrichment analysis (Fig. 3H; Supplementary Fig. 3I; Supplementary Table 19). In the nCT group, proteins reduced in pCR patients were mainly captured in Cluster 3, enriched for chemokine-related processes, platelet activation, TGF-β receptor signaling, and wound response. In the nICT group, baseline proteins reduced in pCR patients were grouped into Clusters 5 and 8, enriched in cell adhesion and acute inflammatory response (Fig. 3H). Proteins in Cluster 2 suggested reduced leukocyte activation in pCR patients from the nCT group at baseline and post-NAT. In contrast, Cluster 7 in the nICT group was characterized by increased abundance of proteins involved in cytoskeleton organization and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion in baseline samples from pCR patients (Supplementary Fig. 3I). Longitudinal analysis using linear mixed-effects models identified proteins showing significantly different temporal changes between pCR and non-pCR patients based on the Time × pCR interaction P value (Fig. 3I; Supplementary Fig. 3J). Proteins showing significant temporal differences in the nCT group were mainly enriched in pathways related to cell migration and antimicrobial immune response (Fig. 3J), whereas no pathways were significantly enriched among the corresponding proteins in the nICT group. Together, these findings indicate that response-associated plasma proteomic differences were most evident at baseline, particularly in pathways related to neutrophil activation and inflammatory response.

Unsupervised clustering revealed specific IO protein profiles at baseline and after treatment (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. 4C). At baseline, pCR samples preferentially clustered in groups with higher IO protein levels, whereas this pattern was not evident after treatment.

Differential protein analysis indicated that most IO proteins had relatively high abundance (log₂FC > 0) in pCR patients compared with non-pCR patients across both NAT regimens, although only a subset reached statistical significance (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Tables 21–22). At baseline, MMP7 was consistently elevated in both treatment groups (Fig. 4C). Specifically, in the nICT group, IFN-γ and KIR3DL1, key markers related to cytotoxic lymphocyte and natural killer cell function, showed a trend toward elevation in pCR patients (Fig. 4D). Predictive plasma proteins for pathological response in ESCC under NAT

To identify plasma proteins predictive of pathological response, we integrated baseline untargeted proteomics with IO protein profiles and applied ensemble feature selection (EFS) [48] to the combined baseline protein matrix and then evaluated selected candidates using univariable logistic regression. In the nICT cohort, EFS identified nine candidate proteins, among which DEFA1, FAP, and TANGO2 showed the strongest discriminative patterns (Fig. 5A-C). DEFA1 and FAP were prioritized for further study as measurable circulating proteins. FAP is expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts and has been linked to immunosuppressive remodeling of the TME. However, whether its plasma level correlates with response to NAT remains unknown [49, 50]. In contrast, DEFA1, primarily derived from neutrophils, is involved in antimicrobial defense and antigen-presenting cell activation, though its role in cancer immunity is unclear [51]. DEFA1 was higher in non-pCR patients at both time points, whereas FAP was higher in pCR patients only at baseline (Fig. 5D). However, neither DEFA1 nor FAP showed significant Time × pCR interactions between pCR and non-pCR patients based on the linear mixed-effects model (Fig. 5E).

We then evaluated these candidates in an independent plasma validation cohort. In this cohort, no available clinical variable showed a significant association with pCR (Supplementary Fig. 5A). ELISA was used to measure baseline DEFA1 and FAP levels in nICT-treated patients. Baseline DEFA1 was lower in patients achieving pCR and increased with poorer pathological regression (Fig. 5F-G). By contrast, baseline FAP was higher in pCR patients but did not yield robust predictive performance (Fig. 5H; Supplementary Fig. 5B). A univariable logistic regression model based on DEFA1 showed moderate predictive accuracy for pCR (AUC = 0.706, Fig. 5I), while FAP performed poorly (Supplementary Fig. 5C).

In the nCT cohort, galectin-3 and FAM3C emerged as candidate predictive biomarkers (Fig. 5J-L). Galectin-3, a secreted inflammatory protein detectable in blood, has been implicated in cancer immune regulation and chemoresistance across multiple cancer types, and has been proposed as a therapeutic target [52–54]. Both untargeted proteomics and ELISA validation confirmed higher baseline galectin-3 levels in pCR patients in the nCT group (Fig. 5M-O). In addition, galectin-3 showed significantly different treatment-related changes between pCR and non-pCR patients (Fig. 5P) and strong predictive performance for pCR in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.857; Fig. 5Q).

We next tested whether DEFA1 and galectin-3 predicted pathological response independently of baseline clinical variables using multivariable logistic regression. In the discovery cohort, both markers showed only borderline associations after multivariable adjustment (Supplementary Fig. 5D). In the validation cohort, models were adjusted for age, gender, and lymph node metastasis status, while clinical T stage was excluded because its distribution was highly imbalanced, with most patients classified as T3 stage. To evaluate pathological regression using Firth logistic regression, TRG categories were dichotomized into favorable versus unfavorable response groups. In the validation cohort, lower baseline DEFA1 level remained independently associated with pCR (P = 0.024) and favorable pathological regression (TRG1/2 vs. TRG3/4, P = 0.0033) in patients receiving nICT (Fig. 5R; Supplementary Fig. 5E). In contrast, galectin-3 independently predicted pCR (P = 0.023) and favorable pathological regression (TRG1/2/3 vs. TRG4/5, P = 0.012) in patients treated with nCT (Fig. 5S; Supplementary Fig. 5F). None of the included clinical covariates were significant, suggesting that these markers provided information not captured by the included baseline clinical variables.

DEFA1 facilitates MDSC expansion and inhibits CD8+ T cell function

Given its association with poor pathological response to nICT, we next investigated whether DEFA1 contributes to immunosuppressive remodeling of the TME. To this end, we first employed murine subcutaneous tumor models generated from B16F10 (melanoma) and MC38 (colorectal cancer) cell lines, both of which are well-established systems for mechanistic exploration of tumor immunity, and we assessed the effects of recombinant mouse DEFA1 (rmDEFA1) in vivo (Fig. 6A). rmDEFA1 treatment significantly increased tumor growth (Fig. 6B–C). IHC revealed a marked reduction in CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in rmDEFA1-treated tumors (Fig. 6D-G). Flow cytometry further showed reduced IFN-γ expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 6H–J). In tumor-draining lymph nodes, rmDEFA1-treated mice exhibited reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration accompanied by elevated PD-1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6A-D), as well as fewer CD11c⁺MHC-II⁺ dendritic cells (Supplementary Fig. 6E-F). Notably, rmDEFA1 treatment was associated with increased intratumoral infiltration of MDSCs, including both CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clow PMN-MDSCs and CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi M-MDSCs (Fig. 6K-M), consistent with MDSC expansion and migration under high tumor burden (Supplementary Fig. 6G). Additionally, rmDEFA1-treated mice developed splenomegaly and showed elevated levels of splenic MDSCs, particularly PMN-MDSCs (Fig. 6N-P; Supplementary Fig. 6H). We further examined whether elevated DEFA1 promotes MDSC differentiation and expansion in vitro. rmDEFA1 enhanced the differentiation of CD11b⁺Gr1⁺ BM-MDSCs from murine bone marrow precursors (Supplementary Fig. 6I), and increased the frequency of both Ly6ClowLy6G⁺ and Ly6C⁺Ly6G⁺ subsets at higher concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 6J). Co-culture assays further showed that rmDEFA1 enhanced MDSC-mediated suppression of CD8⁺ T cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 6K–M). We next co-cultured OT-1 CD8⁺ T cells with BM-MDSCs to assess whether DEFA1 further enhanced MDSC-mediated inhibition of CD8⁺ T cells. In the absence of OVA257−264 peptide stimulation, OT-1 CD8⁺ T cells showed minimal activation, and the low-level TNF-α response detected under co-culture conditions was further reduced by rmDEFA1 (Supplementary Fig. 6N-O). Under OVA257−264 peptide stimulation, rmDEFA1 further enhanced BM-MDSC-mediated suppression of OT-1 CD8⁺ T cell activation (Fig. 6Q–R). To further evaluate the tumor-killing ability of CD8+ T cells, we co-cultured MC38-OVA or B16F10-OVA cells with BM-MDSCs and OT-1 CD8⁺ T cells in the presence or absence of rmDEFA1 (Fig. 6S). MDSCs suppressed CD8⁺ T cell-induced tumor cell apoptosis, and this effect was further enhanced by rmDEFA1 (Fig. 6T–V; Supplementary Fig. 6P-Q). Further validation in a mouse ESCC model revealed that intratumoral rmDEFA1 impaired the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 6W-X). Ly6G⁺ cell depletion abolished the tumor-promoting effect of rmDEFA1 and restored responsiveness to anti-PD-1 blockade (Supplementary Fig. 6R–T). By contrast, Ly6G+ cell depletion alone had only a marginal effect in the absence of rmDEFA1, indicating that DEFA1 amplifies Ly6G⁺ cell-mediated tumor promotion. Together, these findings indicate that DEFA1 promotes immunosuppression and resistance to immunotherapy by expanding MDSCs, particularly PMN-MDSCs, and impairing CD8⁺ T cell responses in the TME.

To extend these findings to human ESCC, we first examined the cellular localization of DEFA1 in tumor tissues. mIHC showed that DEFA1 was primarily localized in CD11b⁺ myeloid cells (Fig. 7A). Some CD11b⁺MPO⁺ cells also expressed DEFA1, supporting tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells as a major source of DEFA1 in the TME (Supplementary Fig. 7A).

We next evaluated the association between DEFA1⁺ cell density and immune composition in a tissue-validation cohort (n = 33). IHC was used to quantify CD8⁺, DEFA1⁺, and MPO⁺ cells (Fig. 7B). DEFA1⁺ cell density correlated positively with MPO⁺ cell density (Fig. 7C). In contrast, tumors with high DEFA1⁺ cell density showed reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (Fig. 7D). mIHC was then used to quantify PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs as defined in the Methods (Fig. 7E). High-DEFA1 tumors showed increased PMN-MDSC infiltration, accompanied by higher CD66b⁺ cell abundance (Fig. 7F-G; Supplementary Fig. 7B). M-MDSCs also tended to be increased in high-DEFA1 tumors (Fig. 7H). CD8⁺ T cell abundance was inversely associated with MDSC density (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Together, these findings support an association between DEFA1 and an MDSC-enriched, CD8⁺ T cell-poor immune microenvironment in human ESCC.

Discussion

Despite the potential of NAT to improve patient outcomes and enable organ preservation in esophageal cancer [55], treatment responses remain highly heterogeneous, and conventional clinicopathological features and biomarkers such as PD-L1 have shown limited predictive value for pathological response. Tissue-based omics profiling has linked specific genomic alterations and an immunosuppressive TME to treatment resistance in ESCC [4, 19, 56, 57], but the clinical utility of such approaches is constrained by the limited accessibility of biopsy samples. In this context, liquid biopsy-based omics profiling offers a minimally invasive alternative [22, 58]. In this study, we performed integrated plasma proteomic and metabolomic profiling to identify molecular features associated with response to nCT and nICT in patients with LA-ESCC.

Longitudinal plasma metabolomic profiling revealed that persistent downregulation of multiple amino acid metabolism pathways during NAT distinguished pCR patients in both nCT and nICT subgroups, highlighting the important roles of amino acid metabolism in tumor immunity [59]. Regimen-specific alterations were also evident: nICT responders showed decreased levels of several phospholipid species and enrichment of pantothenate/CoA and vitamin B6 metabolism, whereas nCT responders demonstrated enhanced taurine metabolism. These alterations were implicated in immune regulation and TME remodeling [37, 40–42], illustrating the capacity of plasma metabolomics to capture systemic metabolic remodeling associated with treatment response, and warranting further investigation into whether dietary or pharmacological modulation of relevant metabolic pathways could enhance NAT efficacy.

Untargeted proteomic analysis revealed that baseline differences in leukocyte migration and neutrophil activation pathways were associated with pathological response to NAT. This is consistent with previous single-cell data showing downregulation of neutrophil-related programs in nICT-responsive ESCC [56]. Olink-based targeted proteomics identified elevated baseline levels of multiple IO proteins such as IFN-γ, CD4, CD40, CD244, and PDCD1 in treatment responders, highlighting the value of targeted panels for detecting low-abundance proteins not readily captured by discovery proteomics. Notably, our analyses showed that differences in plasma proteomic profiles between responders and non-responders were more evident at baseline than after treatment. This likely reflects pre-existing heterogeneity in systemic immune and inflammatory states that influences treatment sensitivity. Such heterogeneity may be partially obscured by the broad host- and tumor-level remodeling induced by NAT. Importantly, the attenuation of post-treatment separation does not imply that post-treatment samples are uninformative, as selected proteins retained response-associated differences and longitudinal metabolomics continued to identify discriminatory features during therapy.

Among the baseline proteins associated with nICT response, we identified DEFA1, a neutrophil-derived antimicrobial peptide whose role in cancer immunotherapy resistance remains unclear [51]. We found that elevated plasma DEFA1 was associated with failure to achieve pCR following nICT, and mechanistic experiments revealed that DEFA1 promotes an immunosuppressive TME by expanding MDSCs and suppressing CD8⁺ T cells. These findings align with previous reports linking nICT resistance in ESCC to increased neutrophil infiltration and myeloid immunosuppression [60, 61], and are consistent with the established role of MDSCs and tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) in forming the myeloid suppressive axis within the TME [62, 63]. Although DEFA1 is not yet established as a therapeutic target, our findings provide a rationale for further investigation of DEFA1-related pathways as potential strategies to enhance immunotherapy sensitivity.

The proteomic and metabolomic datasets provided complementary insights into the molecular basis of NAT response. Proteomic analysis identified upregulation of neutrophil-derived DEFA1 in non-responders, which was mechanistically linked to MDSC expansion and CD8⁺ T cell impairment. In parallel, metabolomics captured metabolic alterations consistent with a similar immunosuppressive context. For example, dysregulated lipid and amino acid metabolism in non-responders is consistent with the metabolic programs used by neutrophils and MDSCs to support their immunosuppressive functions [64–66], and alterations in CoA and vitamin B6 metabolism may influence the anti-tumor activity of T cells [37, 67]. Together, these omics layers provide an integrated view of the molecular alterations underlying NAT response in ESCC.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with modest sample sizes in both the discovery and validation cohorts, which may limit statistical power. The discovery cohort was derived from a clinical trial, which helped mitigate potential confounding, and multivariable Firth’s bias-reduced logistic regression was used to adjust for major available covariates in biomarker assessment. However, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out, particularly given the possibility of baseline imbalances across response subgroups. Second, the study population consisted predominantly of middle-aged to older East Asian men, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic, gender, and age groups. Third, all omics analyses were performed in human ESCC, so extrapolation to other cancer types should be made with caution. Finally, in line with the scope of this study, DEFA1 was investigated here primarily as a response-associated biomarker and mechanistic mediator of nICT resistance. Its prognostic relevance for long-term survival and its therapeutic tractability remain to be established in larger independent cohorts with mature follow-up.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identifies plasma molecular features associated with pathological response to NAT in ESCC and highlights DEFA1 as a candidate biomarker and mediator of resistance to nICT. Our findings suggest that baseline systemic immune and inflammatory states and on-treatment metabolic remodeling jointly shape therapeutic response, supporting plasma multi-omics as a minimally invasive approach for response stratification and mechanistic insights in ESCC.
