Integrating the sustainable development goals into post-mining land use selection
Authors: Gareth B. Simpson, Kim Ferguson, Neeltje Slingerland, Graham P. W. Jewitt, Alexey V. Alekseenko, Zoe Z. Simpson, Jaclyn Ennis-John, Raina Hattingh, Kelly Stock
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Active mines regularly commit to post-mining land uses (PMLUs) several decades before their planned closure, setting closure outcomes and commitments in regulatory instruments during the initial, pre-mining phases. Considering the mounting global challenges of water, energy, food, and livelihood security, as well as climate change, many of these PMLUs may be considered sub-optimal for a future context. Whether new land uses are being defined or existing land uses are being refined, options for PMLUs should be selected using various planning lenses. In this paper, three of these lenses are considered to demonstrate how post-mining landscapes could contribute to addressing complex global challenges through effective mine closure transitions. These lenses are: (i) safe, stable, and non-polluting; (ii) suitable, practicable, and aligned with land capability and local/regional needs, supported by a comprehensive knowledge base; and (iii) integration of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the water-energy-food (WEF) nexus. This approach is presented as a proposed conceptual framework, demonstrating how the SDGs can be utilised as a lens for selecting PMLUs, and which of these PMLUs are aligned with addressing water, energy, food, and/or livelihood security, as well as climate change mitigation. Selected case studies are highlighted, after which regulatory considerations and policy recommendations are made.

Introduction

Mining yielded the fuel and materials that enabled the Industrial Revolution and sustains new economies. It is both an enabler of energy supply and energy-intensive in its own right. Yet the processes and the use of many products emanating from mining—principally carbon-based fuels—have contributed towards another revolution: global warming and the associated climate crisis.

Mining remains essential to our everyday life; it has been said that “if you can’t grow it, you have to mine it”. Our reliance on mined materials is endless, from gravel for roads to phosphates for fertilisers, copper for telecommunication, and iron for infrastructural development. Like other modern mobile devices, iPhones contain at least 30 different chemical elements, ranging from common to rare-earth metals1 . This reliance is predicted to increase as we generate more lithium batteries, electric cars, solar panels, and wind and hydro turbines. Furthermore, mining operations often serve as the economic backbone of communities, particularly in remote or developing regions. Operational mining activities generate stable incomes and create business opportunities—including primary, secondary, and tertiary industries—thus enhancing local, regional, and national prosperity2.

However, mining is a temporary land use; dedicated efforts are required to manage and mitigate the potential environmental impacts, social challenges, and economic vacuum that can follow in its wake. Planning post-mining land uses (PMLUs) has emerged as a key best practice for mitigating the negative environmental and social impacts of mining.

Several national and international commitments, by governments and multinational companies (including mining companies), have been made regarding the transition to a low-carbon economy. These commitments include the Just Transition3,4 , which is a framework developed to equitably secure the livelihoods and rights of workers and their communities during this transition. It consists of a dual commitment to human well-being (concerning income, education and health) and sustainability (vis-à-vis, e.g., decarbonisation, resource efficiency, and ecosystem restoration)5. The Just Transition aligns with the seventeen Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 20156. These goals provide an ambitious, aspirational roadmap to eradicate poverty, reduce inequality, and protect the planet against climate change by 20307.

While mining houses and consultants produce countless optimistic, glossy reports on sustainability, critics question whether sustainable mining is an oxymoron. Mining occurs in urban, rural, and remote areas, as well as in both developed and developing nations. Marginalised communities abound in former mining regions, particularly in the least developed countries8. Mine closure, including rehabilitation and repurposing, has not generally yielded an overall positive narrative. The thousands of ownerless, abandoned, and unrehabilitated mines globally also speak to a loss of livelihoods and economic activity—there are reportedly some 80,000 abandoned mines in Australia alone9.

We argue that when mines close, their PMLUs should be aligned with relevant SDGs. In so doing, mine closure and asset transition could contribute to, inter alia, water, energy, food, ecosystem, and livelihood security. To achieve this, we recommend that mine closure policies and supporting mine closure legislation incorporate the SDGs as a complementary lens for selecting PMLUs, in tandem with the prevailing safe, stable, and non-polluting paradigm and the knowledge base associated with the mine. We recommend this for both mine sites with authorised baseline PMLUs and greenfield developments proposing PMLUs.

Global sustainability challenges and opportunities

In their seminal work, Meadows et al. warned almost half a century ago, “If the present growth trends in world population, industrialisation, pollution, food production, and resource depletion continue unchanged, the limits to growth on this planet will be reached sometime within the next one hundred years”10. Some three decades later they stated that “the human economy is exceeding important limits now and that this overshoot will intensify greatly over the coming decades”11.

In 2011, before the release of the SDGs (when the Millennium Goals were the aspirational sustainability targets), the water-energy-food (WEF) nexus was proposed as a framework for addressing the synergies and trade-offs of resource security12. This focus was due to the expected growth in demand for food, water, and energy by 2030 ( 35 percent, 40 percent, and 50 percent, respectively)13. Predictions like these were driven by an increasing global population, urbanisation, and the addition of three billion more middle-class people by 203014. There was (and is) also the dire need to enhance the livelihoods of the ‘bottom billion’, the largely undernourished portion of the global population without access to electricity, clean water, and safe sanitation.

When the SDGs were agreed in 2015, the target horizon was 2030. By 2023, only 15 percent of the SDGs were on track due to challenges such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the worsening climate crisis, and the war in Ukraine7. The 2023 Sustainable Development Report stated categorically, “At the midpoint of the 2030 Agenda, all of the SDGs are seriously off track”15. The world is facing a conundrum: how to achieve energy security amid a climate crisis in a manner that upholds distributive, recognitional, and procedural justice5 – all of this while planetary boundaries are being exceeded16. Simultaneously, land degradation and sustainable land management have been termed ‘wicked problems’17. Van Vuuren et al. state that if no new policies related to water, energy, food, biodiversity, and climate change are adopted, food production and energy generation could increase by 60% between 2015 and 205018. This increased food production would need to occur on the same (or less) amount of land, using water more efficiently, while reducing emissions; it is, therefore, a profound challenge. Further, the Paris Climate Agreement is far from being achieved.

Mine closure challenges and opportunities

Mining interfaces with the SDGs in that it is a core user of water, energy, and land. It also provides decent work for millions through primary, secondary, or tertiary employment. Endl et al. state that “there is scarce knowledge about the mining sector’s contribution to the UN Sustainable Development Goals”19. They note that innovations within the sector yield synergies, trade-offs, and imbalances between individual SDGs. They also note that innovations in the sector have not contributed to progress on SDG 5 (gender equality). In several jurisdictions, mining is often engaged in a competition for land, with agricultural land often being lost to facilitate mining20. To this end, mining can threaten water and food security (related to SDGs 6 and 2, respectively). The former is often due to the potential for acid mine drainage at many sites.

Few mining companies globally have achieved the milestone of obtaining a closure certificate21, and the industry does not yet fully understand what ‘good closure’ looks like or who defines it22. A separate study found that approximately 75 percent of the 1,000 worldwide mine closures between 1981 and 2009 were sudden and premature24, i.e., they did not reach resource depletion.

It has been argued that mining is generally not on the correct sustainability path at this stage, and is at risk of not meeting societal expectations regarding climate change25. Mining disasters and rising safety and environmental concerns have put a strain on mining projects in general19. It has been established that if carbon pricing is applied to the mining industry to support climate action, the impacts on the sector would be significant—revenues would diminish, and in some cases, it would render mining unprofitable26.

There is potential for exploring the beneficial use of mining land post-closure to promote sustainable initiatives. In a recent study using the visual interpretation of Sentinel-2 images for 2019, researchers identified that globally, over 100,000 km2 of land is used for mining, including artisanal and small-scale mining23—approximately the same surface area as South Korea or Iceland. Not only do these sites yield much-coveted land for alternative or substitutive industries, but they typically have many other value-adding assets, e.g., access roads, parking, rail loops, high-voltage power supply and distribution, water supplies, infrastructure, engineered platforms/terraces, and a skilled workforce who can be reskilled, up-skilled, side-skilled, or redeployed—these could feed into various SDGs, e.g., SDG 8. It has been argued that ‘closure’ is the incorrect term to apply to mining assets approaching the end of their economic life. Instead, the process should be viewed as succession planning—a transition of land from one purpose or custodian to the next27. Any negative impacts on the land or water must be addressed in the transition process, e.g., contaminated soil or acid mine drainage.

The last of these methods defines fifteen Transitional Post-Mining Landscape Profiles (TPMLP) based on four questions. These questions ask (i) whether the site is still active, (ii) whether the site has a private entity responsible for its environmental liability, (iii) whether environmental control and remediation fieldwork has been implemented for the site, and (iv) whether the fieldwork has been implemented for revitalisation or the new land use. These questions allow the site to be categorised as either abandoned, non-active, or active33. These leading questions indicate the degree of closure planning, who is responsible for the closure, the status of the baseline studies, and the extent to which these align with the final land use. The EIT ReviRIS Project proposed six topics for the multi-criteria analysis—economics, environmental, technical issues, social, regional development, and geoethics33—which are evaluated in an Initial Decision Matrix (IDM). Geoethics encompasses the needs of local populations, natural potential, the knowledge base, health and safety, and the interaction between these factors and economic activities.

CRC TiME notes that the roadmap to mine repurposing in many developed economies is time-consuming and potentially hindered by numerous technical, economic, and regulatory hurdles34. One challenge with CRC TiME’s proposed mine repurposing decision tree is that the requirement to rehabilitate a site seems to preclude repurposing. This perception is understandable, as in some jurisdictions, any new use of a site can only begin once a responsible entity has fully completed the rehabilitation process. Rehabilitation and repurposing are, however, not mutually exclusive. It is also important to note that there may be various potential concurrent repurposing options at a mine site, given that a specific site may have diverse assets and land uses, such as final voids, waste dumps, infrastructure, and rehabilitated opencast areas. Different final land uses may be applicable to the various domains on a site.

When considering ecological restoration and the recovery of mine sites, the highest level of possible ecological rehabilitation should be sought35. In Queensland, Australia, selecting and justifying appropriate and viable PMLUs is a statutory obligation within the regulated Progressive Rehabilitation and Closure Plans (PRCP)36. The Office of the Queensland Mine Rehabilitation Commissioner lists potential PMLUs for residual material-filled voids, including native ecosystems, grazing, cropping, renewable energy (solar, wind farms, pumped hydro-energy storage (PHES), and hydrogen), phytomining, protected horticulture, intensive livestock, regenerative cropping, manufacturing, and tourism36. They also list PMLU options for water-filled voids, including aquaculture, ‘smart’ water supply systems, irrigation, mine-water trading, aquatic ecosystems, and the production of algae, seaweed, biomass, or microalgae. Despite significant research, many of these potential uses have not been proven to be viable options.

The Australian Land Use and Management (ALUM) Classification provides a nationally consistent method for collecting and presenting land use information for various users across Australia37. Pershke and Elliot38 propose an approach to PMLU selection based on a strategic land use planning process, which includes context analysis, opportunity analysis, opportunity evaluation, strategic plan development, and implementation. They note that this approach can bridge the gap between traditional mine closure planning and regional socio-economic development planning.

Simpson39 presented a framework where a nexus within the SDGs serves as a lens for selecting PMLUs. Within this framework, a land–water-energy-food-jobs nexus was employed to inform the reconsideration of the baseline PMLU39. The first step was a review of the obligations to confirm, inter alia, the baseline PMLU, which included the knowledge base and land capability, applicable legislation and authorisations, and regional and local strategic development plans and priorities. This review generated a list of potential alternative PMLUs aligned with SDGs 2, 6, 7, 9, 12 and 13. Following a review of the closure vision, principles, and SMART closure objectives and relinquishment criteria, these PMLU options underwent a sustainability assessment (social, environmental, economic, and technical), after which a risk/opportunity process was undertaken. After identifying latent risks, the preferred closure alternative was selected based on a set of evaluation criteria.

Alignment of PMLUs with the SDGs

Some may view ‘sustainable mining’ as an oxymoron. However, an innovative approach to mine closure—including responsible rehabilitation and land or asset repurposing—has the potential to contribute to humanity’s goal of meeting its present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. The influence of mining can be more sustainable if site-specific, substitutive economies are developed during the mine’s operational and closure phases. If the PMLUs are aligned with the SDGs, this is a potential indicator of sustainability, particularly if they contribute to, inter alia, water, energy, food, ecosystem, and livelihood security. To achieve this, extensive stakeholder participation, mitigation of environmental liabilities, and the involvement of third parties with appropriate expertise are among the fundamental considerations.

Integrating the SDGs into PMLU selection is also aligned with the International Council on Mining and Metals’ performance expectations, which seek to define good practice ESG requirements for the mining and metals industry29. These expectations encompass integrating sustainable development into corporate strategy and decision-making processes, as well as biodiversity conservation, social performance, and water stewardship.

While various alternative PMLUs can be aligned to the SDGs, Gagen states that innovative approaches or new technologies are not necessarily needed. Rather, a commitment to integrating progressive closure into operations will make the most significant difference in managing environmental risks and realising the opportunities to contribute to a nature-positive future42. It is also acknowledged that there is a strong need for integrated mine closure that combines environmental and social aspects22.

Literature study of PMLU case studies per SDG

The following section provides case studies of how the SDGs can be integrated into mining operations, processes and, ultimately, closed mine sites. The case studies are categorised according to relevant SDGs, noting that several straddle multiple SDGs, as demonstrated in Table 1.

SDG 1 – No poverty

The first SDG is to end poverty in all its forms everywhere. In 2020, the number of people living in extreme poverty (living on less than USD 2.15 per day) increased to 724 million. Those living in extreme poverty struggle to fulfil the most basic needs, i.e., health, education, access to water and sanitation6.

The establishment of mines, particularly in developing countries, supports job creation, which in turn raises living standards and alleviates poverty. Many mines are located in the world’s least developed countries, and invest in both the local and regional economies, and international trade. The level of services generally improves when mining moves to an area. The exit of mines from an area or region can have devastating socio-economic impacts on the local and even regional economy.

With the imminent closure of the Kromdraai section of the Khwezela Colliery in Mpumalanga Province, South Africa, an agri-industrial hub is being planned. It will include several greenhouses and employ up to fifty people45. In the Limpopo Province of South Africa, the Mogalakwena Incubator was created to develop sustainable farming projects in areas where the mine operated46. In the four years preceding January 2018, a total of 650 adults were trained, and five farming enterprises were initiated. A combination of greenhouse tunnels, drip irrigation, and other efficient irrigation methods were developed. These are two small but valuable examples of how mines can seek to invest in an area during and following mining.

SDG 2 – Zero hunger

The aspirational SDG 2 is to end hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture. In 2022, approximately 9.2 percent of the global population was facing chronic hunger, equivalent to about 735 million people. This was 122 million more than in 20196. Feeding the worldwide population in an equitable manner is an imposing task. With the burgeoning world population, the challenge is to produce more food on less land, with less water.

Mines often have significant land holdings and valuable services, e.g., roads, electricity, and water supply. A significant opportunity exists for mines to utilise their land, particularly after closure, for agricultural activities. This substitutionary land use also provides potential benefits for the mining company, such as the ongoing maintenance of land, e.g., erosion and weed management by the farmer(s).

In the Hunter Valley in Australia, a mine was required to demonstrate successful long-term rehabilitation by producing an equivalent agricultural production yield for three consecutive years. The yields were to be compared to production prior to the mine’s establishment. This test found that the post-mining crop yields were above average for three consecutive years compared to the pre-mining yields47. Diamond Coast Aquaculture (DCA) operates a 400-hectare land-based marine aquaculture enterprise north of the town of Kleinzee, on South Africa’s west coast, on the site of a former diamond mine. DCA produces about 100 tons of abalone and 200 tons of seaweed per annum48,49. Plans were in place to increase their production capacity to 1,000 tons of abalone, 2,000 tons of finfish, 5,000 tons of seaweed, and 300 tons of oysters, sea urchins and/or sea cucumbers. DCA also planned to construct a small wind energy facility with up to 28 wind turbines on overburden mine dumps to the east of the existing aquaculture farm. Aquaculture as a PMLU has also been commercially developed in Collie, Western Australia, and in West Virginia in the United States50. Marron (large crustaceans) were bred in Collie.

Following the cessation of mining at the ‘Big Hole’ in Kimberly, South Africa, a series of drainage tunnels were created around the 60 m underground pit to reduce seepage into the pit. In 1996, these tunnels were identified as having optimal temperature and humidity conditions for growing oyster mushrooms commercially51. A group of entrepreneurs established an oyster mushroom-growing business after an 18-month trial period.

The goal of the New Vaal Irrigation Project in Mpumalanga Province, South Africa, is to transform post-mining land so that it is suitable for agricultural use. A total of 1,200 hectares has been dedicated to this initiative, with key targets being local job creation, food production, and environmental stewardship52.

SDG 3 – Good health and well-being

SDG 3 is to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all. Much progress has been made in improving people’s health in recent years. Out of 200 countries or areas, 146 have already met or are on track to meet the SDG target on under-5 mortality. Effective HIV treatment has cut global AIDS-related deaths by 52 per cent since 2010, and at least one neglected tropical disease has been eliminated in 47 countries6. AngloGold Ashanti turned their site-based malaria control program in Ghana into a non-profit, non-governmental organisation (AGAMal) after the program resulted in reducing malaria cases in the mine’s hospital by 50% in just 13 months. AGAMal became the first private company to become a Principal Recipient of the Global Fund in 201053. Funding outside of AngloGold Ashanti provides resilience for the continuation of this service post-closure. Many mining companies develop clinics and support hospitals in the communities surrounding their mining operations, with these being handed over to local municipalities after mining. This benefits mine workers, their families, and the general population.

In Northumberlandia, a 19-hectare community art park provides four miles of footpaths on and around the landform and a café and visitor centre. The park’s centrepiece is ‘The Lady of the North’, a human landform sculpture of a reclining lady, which is over 30 m in height and spans about 1,600 m. The landform was created as part of the restoration of the adjacent Shotton surface coal mine54.

SDG 4 – Quality education

This aspirational goal is to ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all. Progress towards quality education was already slower than required before the pandemic, but COVID-19 had negative impacts on education, causing learning losses in 80 per cent of the 104 countries studied6.

The Eden Project, in Cornwall, England, is one of the most prominent PMLU examples globally and offers learning programs from preschool to undergraduate degree levels and beyond. The site in Cornwall is one of the world’s largest rainforests in captivity, with over 20 acres of outdoor gardens and a wild outer estate55. Several new Eden Projects are being developed. One example is in Anglesea, Australia, a planned eco-tourism attraction on the site of a former coal mine that has been projected to create more than 1,300 new jobs and generate AU$350 m for the regional economy in its first ten years of operation. It is projected to attract approximately 750,000 visitors per annum55.

SDG 5 – Gender equality

SDG 5 seeks to achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls. On average, women in the labour market still earn 23 per cent less than men globally. Women also spend about three times as many hours in unpaid domestic and care work as men6. At the Vryheid Coronation Colliery, South Africa, an old coking coal site was converted into a food processing plant for exporting organically grown beans to Europe and North America50. The site, along with the power supply and machinery, was donated to a joint venture that included emerging farmers and the local communities. About 130 women were employed at the plant in 2009. Many of the projects listed under other SDG sections also have the potential to help address gender equality, and there is a growing call for gender equity in mine closure processes56.

SDG 6 – Clean water and sanitation

SDG 6 aims to ensure the availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all. In 2022, 2.2 billion people still lacked safely managed drinking water, including 703 million without a basic water service; 3.5 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation, including 1.5 billion without basic sanitation services; and two billion lacked basic handwashing facilities, including 653 million with no handwashing facilities at all 6.

Acid mine drainage (AMD) and the failure of tailings dams are risks that the general public often associates with mining. Water stewardship and compliance with the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM) are, therefore, core responsibilities of modern mining companies. Access to clean drinking water and safe, hygienic sanitation are crucial aspirational indicators of this SDG.

The eMalahleni water treatment plant is located in the Witbank Coalfields in the Mpumalanga Province of South Africa. This region hosts several coal mining operations, with most current and historic operations being in underground mines and some open-cast voids. Many of these mines have significant potential for AMD, generally with high sulphate levels. Dewatering is required for active mines, and water management is necessary for historic and closed mines. The eMalahleni water treatment plant treats water from a number of operations. Treated water is provided to the eMalahleni Local Municipality. Additionally, the gypsum-based solids produced as a byproduct of the water treatment process are made into bricks, and 66 homes have been built from gypsum waste for employees57.

The flooding of coal mine voids can often limit the acid generation potential of the exposed carbonaceous material since it excludes the presence of oxygen. However, this depends on various factors, including the site’s water and salt balance. In Germany, almost all the lignite mines in southern Lusatia were closed, ultimately forming an extensive lake system that supports irrigation, flood mitigation systems, and recreation58.

SDGs 7 – Affordable and clean energy

SDG 7 aims to ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all. From 2015 to 2021, the proportion of the global population with access to electricity increased from 87 per cent to 91 per cent. In 2021, developing countries installed a record-breaking 268 watts per capita of renewable energy-generating capacity. And yet, in 2021, there were still 675 million people around the world with no access to electricity6.

Energy-related projects that have been developed on former mine sites include the Klettwitz wind farm on a former opencast coal mine in Brandenburg, Germany; the Black Law wind farm on an abandoned coal mine in Forth, Scotland; a geothermal plant in the abandoned shafts of a coal mine in Heerlen, Holland; a photovoltaic plant on a former lignite waste site in Espenhain, Germany; methane plants in former coal mines in the UK, such as a 5.4 megawatts plant in South Yorkshire; and biomass on 10 000 hectares of former coal-mined land in Ruhr, Germany50 . The world’s largest floating solar power plant in China was commissioned at a former coal mine. This 70 MW project covers more than 63 hectares of flooded area and can provide electricity for 21,000 homes59.

The German energy company RWE is investigating the possibility of developing wind power on former opencast coal mines much earlier than has been previously possible. The soils of an opencast mine are often unstable for many years after their closure, needing several years to settle before they can be developed. Moreover, with modern wind turbines weighing as much as 6,500 tonnes, it can take up to fifteen years before rehabilitated opencast mines can be built. However, on the initiative of RWE, experts from the Ruhr University Bochum and engineering firm Jörss-Blunck-Ordemann are investigating the possibility of accelerating the timeframe to allow wind farms to be built on closed opencast mines much earlier60.

The Ti Tree Bioenergy in Queensland, Australia, is a waste disposal facility that utilises bioreactor technology to stabilise waste while capturing methane and converting it into electricity61. The facility utilises a 36 million cubic metre void left from an open-cut coal mine (Fig. 2) and has a generation capacity of 3 MW. For the Just Transition to be effected, significant energy storage will need to be built into electricity grid systems. This is because the baseload energy being provided by coal-fired power stations is systematically being replaced by variable energy sources such as wind and solar energy. To this end, many mines have realised the opportunity to develop energy generation and batteries on their sites. Mines with final voids or pit lakes have the potential to develop pumped-hydro storage systems or bioreactor landfills. Several pumped storage systems are in various stages of development.

For example, the Kidston project in far north Queensland is Australia’s first pumped hydro project to use abandoned gold mining surface pits in a reservoir configuration. Solar and wind power generation are integrated into the pumped hydro system, which will provide 250 MW of storage over eight hours. Construction is expected to be completed this year62.

SDG 8 – Decent work and economic growth

The goal of this SDG is to promote sustained, inclusive, and sustainable economic growth, as well as productive employment and decent work for all6. The document ‘Opportunities for Australian industry’ outlines an analysis that estimates that expenditure on mine closure and rehabilitation activities at 240 Australian mines expected to close by 2040 could exceed $4 billion annually67.

Successful post-mining land uses that contribute to economic growth include golf courses, shopping centres, mountain biking parks, ski slopes, lakes, swimming facilities, and tourist attractions. For example, the Bluewater Shopping Centre is a 97-ha shopping centre located about 29 km from London’s centre in Kent, in an old chalk quarry50. The centre, which has 330 stores, was opened in 1999, employs 7000 people, and serves 27 million visitors annually.

In terms of recreation, a former Lake Macquarie (Australia) coal mine site will be repurposed into a multi-million-dollar racetrack for car enthusiasts68. Another example of recreational land use is the old Midland Coal Company’s Atkinson processing plant in Illinois, which was transformed into an 87-hectare ATV/dirt bike park and camping ground50.

In 2001, after operating for almost 100 years, Teck’s Sullivan mine in British Columbia, Canada, was turned into a tourism and recreation destination29. A portion of the Teck-owned land was handed over to the city to expand the local ski hill and recreational resorts. Rehabilitation after mine closure included tree replanting, water collection and water treatment system enhancements. Part of the mine site was also turned into Western Canada’s largest solar farm, called SunMine, which began operating in 2015. This is an excellent example of a site with integrated PMLUs.

In Romania, the old Slănic salt mine in Prahova county is now open for visitors, featuring a microclimate with natural air-conditioning and constant temperature and atmospheric pressure throughout the year69. A site in the UK features the world’s deepest hotel, with beds 1,375 feet below the earth’s surface70. Other successful PMLUs include a disused copper mine in Valpelline (Italy) being used for aging cheese71, a wine cellar72, a rock climbing centre73, a swimming centre74, and a surf park75 . Commercial diver training occurs at the Blue Rock Quarry in Gordon’s Bay, South Africa76.

A twelve-metre-long swimming pool is located in front of the coke oven battery in the middle of the former Zollverein Coking Plant. Where coal was once baked into coke, two overseas containers have been welded together to hold the water. During winter, the Zollverein Coking Plant becomes an ice rink77.

SDG 9 – Industry, innovation, and infrastructure

The goal of SDG 9 is to build resilient infrastructure, promote inclusive and sustainable industrialisation, and foster innovation6.

In Australia’s Northern Territory, Equatorial Launch Australia has lodged plans for the Arnhem Space Centre. If approved, it would occur on partially rehabilitated land previously mined by the Gulkula Mining Company, a corporate arm of the Gumatj traditional owners of Arnhem Land78. The same Traditional Owner group has signed a Memorandum of Understanding with Allied Green Ammonia to build a production hub on the Gove Peninsula79.

The Municipal Stadium of Braga, in Portugal, is a football stadium carved out of rock at the site of a former quarry50. It only consists of two lateral stands that are connected to each other by steel cables. The stadium has a capacity of over 30,000 spectators. China is home to several luxury hotels, including the Banyan Tree Nanjing Garden Expo luxury hotel80 and the InterContinental Shanghai Wonderland81.

In terms of innovation, the Boulby Underground Laboratory is the UK’s deep underground science laboratory, located 1.1 km below ground in Boulby Mine, a working polyhalite and salt mine in the North East of England82. At another site, deep underground, within the Stawell Gold Mine, progress has been made on the development of the Stawell Underground Physics Laboratory. This facility will become a centre of dark matter research in Australia83. A former gold mine in Lead, South Dakota, is home to the Sanford Underground Research Facility, an underground physics lab where scientists also study dark matter and subatomic particles84. A former limestone mine in Pennsylvania has stored 200 acres of mainly physical data for clients, including the federal government, since the early 1950s85.

SDG 10 – Reduced inequalities

This SDG aims to reduce inequality within and among countries. Inequality threatens long-term social and economic development. The incomes of the poorest 40 per cent of the population had been growing faster than the national average in most countries, but emerging yet inconclusive evidence suggests that COVID-19 may have dented this positive trend6.

All mines close, yet the impact of mine closures on communities is rarely a priority when mines open. The issue of how towns/cities/regions effectively manage a mine’s closure can be treated as an afterthought86. Research has estimated that a complete global exit of coal, followed by mine closures, could disrupt the lives of at least 33.5 million people87. Managing the resulting socio-economic transition—without significant disruptions and negative impacts—is extremely complex and politically sensitive. The effective management of mining transitions requires long-term planning and extensive stakeholder engagement88. This engagement should include minority and disadvantaged subgroups, given their crucial rolen community social dynamics89.

SDG 11 – Sustainable cities and consumption

This aspirational goal is to make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable. Half of the world’s population lives in cities, and this is projected to reach 70 per cent by 20506 . Mining sites can be used to promote responsible consumption and production once they have reached the end of their life. This is especially true of mines and quarries that are within or on the fringes of cities and large towns. The primary focus of these former mining areas is to make them safe, stable, and non-polluting. This has been an inter-generational challenge for cities such as Newcastle in New South Wales, Australia. Much of the city is built on mined land.

In terms of consumption, the Woodlawn Bioreactor is one of the largest purpose-built bioreactor landfill projects in the world50. It is located in an old copper, lead and zinc open-cut mine 21 km south of Sydney. The bioreactor currently manages around 20% of Sydney’s putrescible waste and generates enough clean energy from the methane gas emissions to power 30,000 homes. Liquids are added to the solid waste to facilitate the degradation of bacteria in the material, generating gas as a byproduct that is captured and extracted to recover energy in the bioreactor plant90. The bioreactor has associated facilities, including a wind farm, solar farm, and aquaculture and horticulture, using the heat from the energy production to produce 3600 kg of fish annually.

In response to the pressure for land and housing due to rapidly growing cities in Utah around Salt Lake City, Rio Tinto reclaimed the legacy evaporation pond area and created a separate business unit, Kennecott Land, to develop the land into a sustainable community called Daybreak. All the homes are certified by the US Green Building Council for their sustainability, and energy and water efficiency91.

SDG 12 – Responsible consumption and production

The goal of this SDG is to ensure sustainable consumption and production patterns. If the global population reaches 9.8 billion by 2050, the equivalent of almost three planets will be required to provide the natural resources needed to sustain current lifestyles6. Many post-mining land uses can result in disturbed land being repurposed into food production areas. Examples include the Teck Resources Highland Valley Copper operations in Canada, which have converted the Trojan tailings pond into a self-sustaining fish habitat92. Another example is the Salmon Gold collaboration between Regeneration and Mejuri. Salmon Gold works with miners, communities, conservationists, government agencies, restoration experts, manufacturers, and brands to reclaim salmon habitat from legacy placer gold mining disturbance, extracting gold from legacy waste piles to fund the effort. The gold is sold through jewellers such as Mejuri, who launched their ‘responsible gold’ Salmon Gold Capsule Collection in June 202493.

SDG 13 – Climate action

The impacts of climate change include changing weather patterns, rising sea levels, and more extreme weather events. If left unchecked, climate change will undo much of the development made over the past decades. It could also provoke mass migrations that could lead to instability and wars. Between 2010 and 2020, highly vulnerable regions—home to approximately 3.3 to 3.6 billion people—experienced 15 times higher human mortality rates from floods, droughts and storms compared to regions with very low vulnerability6.

The Mine Closure Operations Spekboom Project at Rietspruit Colliery, South Africa, aims to improve soil health, prevent erosion, and promote water retention by planting Spekboom on both unmined and rehabilitated land. This trial explores Spekboom’s carbon sequestration potential and adaptation to the highveld region while fostering community engagement through conservation and eco-tourism opportunities52.

SDG 14 – Life below water

SDG 14 aims to conserve and sustainably use the oceans, seas, and marine resources for sustainable development6. Rehabilitated mine sites can positively impact water quality and life below water. This can be in the form of restored aquatic or marine ecosystems or in the development of aquaculture facilities.

A former uranium opencast mining site of Puy de l’Age in France was remediated between 1991 and 1996 and today hosts a leisure centre with a fishing lake29. Fish were introduced into the impounded opencast mine workings, and the lake profile was adapted for spawning. The remainder of the site has been revegetated as grassland and is now home to diverse fauna and flora.

In Buenos Aires, Argentina, the cultivation of pejerrey (a species of Neotropical silverside, an euryhaline fish) within the Paso de Piedra mine pit lake in the form of established aquaculture activities within flooded open pits has proven feasible94.

Cluff Lake was fully decommissioned in 2013. In May last year, the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) revoked the uranium mine licence held by Orano Canada for the Cluff Lake Project, clearing the way for Orano to transfer the site to the Province of Saskatchewan95. The site includes a lake and is safe for hunting, fishing, drinking water, and gathering berries96.

SDG 15 – Life on land

This SDG seeks to protect, restore and promote the sustainable use of terrestrial ecosystems, sustainably manage forests, combat desertification, and halt land degradation and biodiversity loss6. Many mines have the restoration of native vegetation as the target PMLU.

On the eastern coast of South Africa, in the KwaZulu-Natal Province, Richards Bay Minerals (RBM) has extracted heavy mineral sands from the coastal dunes since 197650. RBM has concurrently restored the coastal dune forest while mining during this period. More than twenty years of ecological research and monitoring have indicated that the natural vegetation restoration program is successful97.

The National Reclamation Award-winning Peanut Mine in Colorado is a case study of a successful PMLU98. The abandoned mine contained coal refuse and silver mill waste, posing risks of spontaneous combustion and acid generation. Critical goals attained in the reclamation project included restoring an unused, impacted portion of land into a valuable public open space. Further, sediment transport from the site was reduced to acceptable levels, resulting in an aesthetically acceptable and stable landform.

At the Felicíssimo limestone mine in São Paulo, Brazil, the reclamation of waste dumps is returning the mining areas to the Ipanema Forest99. This includes the reclamation of the crushing area and the construction of a fauna and flora observatory using the crushing facilities.

The Svea Nord coal mine in Norway, the nation’s last coal mine, is being restored as a vast national park after a century of operational life100. Mining ceased in 2016, and an area of 2,914 km2, which this mine was part of, is being returned to nature.

Bamburi Quarry in Mombasa, Kenya, involved a rehabilitation project that transformed an area around a cement factory into grassland, wetlands and rainforest50. The Butchart Gardens (also called the Sunken Garden) are 22 ha of botanical gardens located in an old limestone quarry near Victoria, British Columbia, in Canada50. The gardens are an internationally known public attraction that has been open to the public since 1904. It has been designated a National Historic Site of Canada and receives more than a million visitors annually.

The former PT Newmont Minahasa Raya (PTNMR) mine area is being restored29. The PTNMR site was one of the first large-scale mines in Indonesia to close, and reforestation started with 155,814 trees and fruit crops planted on 200 ha of reclaimed land. Over time, it evolved to include working with the Ministry of Forestry to designate 221 hectares of the forest as a botanical garden. In 2014, PTNMR earned the botanical garden designation for the former mine site. PTNMR finally delivered 443 ha of revegetated land to the Government of Indonesia, and today, the area is a thriving forest of mahogany, teak, nyatoh and sengon trees. Both the reclaimed forest and botanical garden have the potential to create favourable economic and environmental conditions for local inhabitants. In addition, the site’s botanical garden designation ensures the protection of habitats for hundreds of species of plants, birds, insects, and other animals. A former mine site in Australia was used as a possum finishing centre101.

SDG 16 – Peace, justice and strong institutions

SDG 16 aims to promote peaceful and inclusive societies for sustainable development, provide access to justice for all and build effective, accountable and inclusive institutions at all levels6. To this end, several mining sites have been developed into heritage sites50. At one arctic site, cultural heritage features, such as pre-1946 buildings, structures, and mining traces, have been preserved, while the rest of the landscape has been restored to a near-natural state. This site was interesting because the focus was to restore geodiversity in an environment where biotic processes are slow102.

A study in China—where large-scale mine closures resulting from resource depletion have taken place—has indicated that implementing national mine parks has improved the sustainability of local communities103. The sustainability scores of the economic and environmental sectors have increased post-mining; however, it is important to note that the score for the social sector decreased, predominantly due to the movement away from the area after the mine closure.

Elsewhere in China, the Tangshan Quarry Park is an abandoned quarry that has been transitioned into a biodiversity environment, yielding an attractive public space104. Several projects in the quarries of Jinyun combine aspects of landscape planning, interior design, artistic installations, and social planning with an economic revitalisation of the rural area. In this way, a ruined and exploited landscape can be converted into a space that is beneficial for the surrounding communities.

In the Colombian city of Zipaquirá, more than 650 feet underground, a salt mine has been converted into a cathedral with a 50-foot-high glowing cross105.

SDG 17 – Partnerships for the goals

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development is universal and calls for action by all developed and developing countries to ensure no one is left behind. It requires partnerships between governments, the private sector, and civil society6.

One report stated that repurposing defunct mining land for use by the renewable energy, agriculture, manufacturing and conservation sectors could create more than 13,000 jobs in the Hunter Valley, New South Wales, Australia106. Research has shown that the best transitions are equitable, just, and well-planned. The Hunter Valley has experienced a transition since BHP closed its Newcastle steelworks in 1999. While mistakes were made, many lessons can be applied to the next transition for the region. The collaborative approach between unions and management was fundamental to the closure of the steelworks. This ensured workers had support for redundancy, retraining, financial planning, and finding alternative employment. A current Hunter Valley alliance between unions and environmental groups is pushing for the same level of collaborative planning in the coal transition107. Further, the Newcastle steelworks closure illustrates how mining closure may be the last step in a decades-long process of decline. In such a scenario, there is time to reskill workers, explore economic diversification opportunities and build collaboration and trust between the main actors involved108.

There are opportunities for strategic partnerships, whereby mining, government and, for example, energy companies partner to reduce land conflicts and work towards Net Zero goals. Purtill109 emphasises that utility-scale solar arrays, which will be imperative to contribute to decarbonising the resources sector (and contribute to national Net Zero efforts), will consume vast swathes of land. He argues that rehabilitated mine lands present a challenging yet unique opportunity to host utility-scale solar installations. This can reduce the number of land conflicts between the agricultural, conservation, and renewable energy sectors. Further, Purtill notes five reasons for reassessing the role of rehabilitated lands for utility-scale solar energy generation: increasing demand for renewable energy, the global decarbonisation agenda, transformation in regional mining economies, as a progressive rehabilitation and post-mining land use, and a solution to reduce land use conflict. In an Australian context, he notes that the Renewable Energy Zones (REZ) identified by the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO) as the best locations to develop such projects, particularly the thermal and metallurgical coal mines located in or near these REZs. In Queensland, for example, 80 percent of disturbed mined land relates to coal, and bringing renewables in as a potential PMLU may contribute to the long-term sustainability of these regional economies. Developing renewables on already disturbed land may also help reduce the conflict in some regional Australian communities resistant to renewables being developed on previously pristine landscapes.

Conceptual framework

Typical goals in mine closure are to achieve safe, stable, and non-polluting mine sites. Goals for PMLUs include suitability, practicability, and alignment with land capability and local or regional needs. Society also increasingly expects mining land to be transferred to new, productive uses, which typically requires a decade-long planning horizon. The closure and repurposing of mines must, therefore, be incorporated into the mine planning as early as possible—ideally before initiating active mining operations. Holcombe et al.115 note that communities must play a role in developing the closure goals, vision, success criteria and PMLU selection; engagement can support both maximising the benefits of the mining operation and minimising the impacts of closure. Early engagement can also support the development of effective benefit-sharing mechanisms that can be implemented throughout the lifecycle of a project, particularly for benefit-sharing projects that aim to leave a lasting legacy, rather than focusing solely on short-term wins.

Incorporation of SDGs into PMLU selection

A conceptual framework for selecting PMLUs that incorporates the SDGs as a complementary lens is presented in Fig. 4. Building on a previous framework116, a step has been added to determine the Transitional Post-Mining Land Use Characterisation33.Fig. 4A proposed framework for selecting PMLUs with the SDGs as a lens (from Simpson, et al.116 , Simpson39 and Amaro, et al.33).

A proposed framework for selecting PMLUs with the SDGs as a lens (from Simpson, et al.116 , Simpson39 and Amaro, et al.33).

The overarching requirement is that a post-mining site—be it active, non-active, or abandoned—must be safe (for humans and fauna), stable (erosionally and geotechnically), and non-polluting (geochemically). In this framework, the knowledge base criteria typically included in a PMLU selection process, such as commitments, regulations and approvals, opportunities, risks/threats, land capability, and environmental baseline studies, serve as a secondary lens for identifying the baseline and alternative PMLUs. The knowledge base helps establish the site’s regional, social, economic, regulatory, and environmental context. Undertaking a Knowledge Maturity Assessment (KMA) for each relevant technical discipline is highly beneficial for evaluating the knowledge base, readiness for closure, and any key gaps that need to be addressed to facilitate successful closure studies and execution.

The tertiary lens for selecting PMLUs in this framework is the SDGs. The regional and local socio-economic context, land suitability assessment, and site characteristics can guide the selection of the SDGs to which the site could be aligned. Are the regional or local needs related to water, energy or food security, housing, biodiversity, or developing alternative economic opportunities for the mining community?40 Does the site have pits or shafts that are conducive to pumped storage or gravity-batteries? Is the site’s water quality such that it could contribute to water supply, storage or aquaculture? Is the site, or portions thereof, conducive to agriculture, be it grazing or cultivated crops? Could the wind potential and geotechnical conditions on site make the site suitable for the development of wind power energy generation, potentially utilising the existing electrical distribution infrastructure on site?

Once potential PMLU options have been chosen per domain utilising these three lenses, the baseline and potential alternative PMLUs should be subject to a multi-criteria analysis (MCA). The MCA will involve developing a decision matrix, with key criteria or topics to be assessed. Weightings must be selected for these criteria. The decision matrix will be significantly aided if each option has undergone a Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT) assessment. A key aid in this MCA process is the spatial representation of the knowledge base, per domain. This includes soil, hydrological and hydrogeological, contaminated land, spatial (including LiDAR), ecological data, site assets (e.g. infrastructure condition assessments), and the final landform. This spatial representation of data per domain has been successfully utilised in stakeholder engagement, including partnerships and co-design with Indigenous people groups. How data is presented to local stakeholders is key, and information should be tailored to the specific context of the community. Incorporating inclusive and accessible language helps to build a bridge between technical decision-making and community understanding and input. The preferred investment alternative is usually selected from the highest-ranked alternative and the minimum viable closure cost option that emanates from the MCA.

The preferred PMLU or PMLUs—for multiple PMLUs could be selected for a mining site, e.g. per domain on site28—should be assessed by undertaking a technical feasibility study, regulatory assessment, and developing a business plan. This phase may also involve developing designs and conducting a pilot study. After this assessment, it could be found that the preferred PMLU is not feasible, which would require the process to be restarted. The type of land zoning and the tenure may either hinder or assist in the repurposing of a mine site28. If the preferred PMLU or PMLUs are feasible, investment and regulatory approvals would be necessary before these alternatives are executed.

Mining is a temporary land use, and there must, therefore, be a subsequent land use, for better or for worse. The subsequent land use must be site-specific, place-based, informed by stakeholder engagement/co-design, and ultimately relevant to the site’s environmental, social, and economic context. This conceptual framework supports the early and targeted identification of those values. It minimises the risk of ‘blank slate’ PMLU discussions that can result in stalemates or unachievable outcomes (often with elevated stakeholder expectations). Implementing this framework should also leave space for external stakeholder engagement throughout the process to challenge the framework’s identified values and potential PMLUs. Engagement/co-design would also ideally inform the first phase of identifying values, potentially via the knowledge base’s socio-economic assessments and stakeholder engagement if those aspects include engagement on PMLU options.

The proposed framework has limitations—it serves as a guide to the process, rather than a recipe. George Box noted that all models are wrong, but some are useful117. No single nexus method fits all scenarios; rather, the approach must be tailored to each unique situation40. Other tools can be considered in parallel with the proposed methodology30,33,118,119. The Transitional Post-Mining Land Use Characterisation will influence the process, as will relevant regulatory requirements.

Stakeholder engagement/co-design

As detailed above, stakeholder engagement is a critical component of the knowledge base associated with a site’s closure planning. Co-designing PMLUs with key stakeholders/rights holders is therefore imperative—after all, SDG 17 calls for partnerships in contributing to these aspirational goals. Exclusion of representative stakeholder viewpoints presents the risk of a preconceived outcome. Stakeholder/rights holder representative groups should involve Indigenous Peoples, farmers, energy companies, and local water authorities to ensure that synergies and trade-offs associated with any development are adequately incorporated. Engaging with stakeholders requires the consideration of fairness in the responsibilities placed on those involved. Questions should be asked about the resourcing of local organisations to facilitate their engagement/partnership in the process. Fairness in this sense may manifest as funding, training, resources, or long-term support to enable communities to engage. Mining companies may develop benefit-sharing programs or community investment programs that can be aligned with supporting local leadership to take a greater role in decision-making and input into operations, ultimately leading to closure. Parsons and Mottee120 identify six forms of equity when it comes to social outcomes. One relates to democratic (decision-making) equity and asks practitioners to consider whether those impacted more significantly are “those most or least able to represent themselves” in the decision-making process.

A critical component of stakeholder/rights holder engagement/co-design relates to the meaningful involvement of Indigenous peoples (with appropriate remuneration). Inclusive planning and design have led to the emergence of an Indigenous peoples-led collaborative, social innovation121. Keenan and Holcombe28 note the deficiency in good practice and the dearth of data on meaningful engagement with Indigenous Peoples in this regard. For many Indigenous communities—often the inheritors of post-mining landscapes—mine closure intersects with larger questions of Indigenous land rights, self-determination, and social and economic reclamation115. Rodon et al.115 note that local communities are increasingly demanding participation in mine closure planning and objective-setting. They want local knowledge and values to be incorporated into mine rehabilitation and reclamation activities. Kemp et al122 propose that rehabilitation should only proceed with the approval of Indigenous people before a mining company is permitted to relinquish its lease.

Fiscal and planning considerations

Perlatti and Gagen41 explain that one of the core hurdles for companies in achieving effective mine closure is the cost involved in the process. Progressive rehabilitation during a site’s operations somewhat mitigates this, but many companies’ financial provisioning liabilities have increased significantly in recent years. Repurposing offers a compelling opportunity to generate post-mining value by attracting investment through the use of new and innovative technologies and solutions41.

To this end, Keenan and Holcombe28 state that there are very few examples of industry-led and/or funded repurposing initiatives relative to the number of mines in post-production. Regulatory considerations

Establishing socio-economic transitions in post-mining regions will remain challenging without state intervention in terms of policy development and financial support28. This, together with uncertainty related to the costs and criteria associated with closure and relinquishment, has resulted in thousands of mines being in an idle and perpetual state of ‘care and maintenance’124. Creating enabling regulatory pathways will require a concerted effort. With few mine closure success stories compared to the negative mining legacies in Australia, the literature suggests that mine rehabilitation and closure regulations may be inadequate125.

Challenges presented by state regulation

Two key challenges persist in the regulatory context: the need to broaden the existing regulatory focus from rehabilitation as the primary outcome to include alternative PMLUs, preferably aligned with the SDGs; and the promotion of flexible regulatory parameters to permit and encourage amendments to baseline PMLUs.

Mine closure legislation and regulatory guidelines often fall short of encouraging an operator to look beyond, or in addition to, the traditional mine closure outcome of rehabilitation, which is typically an ecological outcome. Returning the site to a natural environment (wilderness, native vegetation, or grazing lands) is commonly the first option proposed. Regulators and site operators often view it as a PLMU that can readily be monitored, providing a quantifiable pathway to formal relinquishment. Heyes et al.126 make this observation and state that the current regulatory frameworks for mine closure focus primarily on the protection of environmental values and the minimisation of liabilities that may fall to governments and host communities. While environmental concerns are critical, they do not necessarily address the socio-economic challenges associated with mine closures. A study by Vivoda et al.127 of the social transition aspects of mine closure in three Australian states (New South Wales, Queensland and Western Australia) identified that there was little regulation of these social issues at the time of closure; instead, there was a heavy reliance on front-end environmental and social impact assessments. The authors concluded that what communities and regulators contend with at the early stages of project approval is very different from what they will need to contend with at closure.

Once the ‘approved’ PMLU is set, changes to agreed PMLUs are not a straightforward regulatory exercise in many jurisdictions worldwide. Indeed, there is generally no enabling environment for alternative PMLUs in most jurisdictions globally. Operators are generally reluctant to amend their approved PMLU in mine closure documentation, despite the local benefits for the community and the economy, as well as the opportunity to align with the SDGs. They hesitate to engage with regulators about proposed changes that go beyond the original approvals and deviate from standard practice, due to the regulatory challenges of amending an approved document and the associated delays and costs. A more straightforward solution is often sought.

A recent case in New South Wales, Australia, involving the Black Rock Motor Resort, which is to be developed on the former Rhondda Colliery, took a developer seven years to obtain the relinquishment of the mine site, despite having a development approval for their proposed alternative PMLU68. For the proposed Black Rock Motor Resort regulatory processes, there was no overlap between the Development Authorisation (DA) and closure processes, except for the Subsidence Advisory process.

Queensland’s mine closure regulatory requirements are similar, with an emphasis on returning the land to its pre-mining land use. The Environmental Protection Act 1994 (Qld), amended in 2019 to introduce new requirements for progressive rehabilitation and closure plans (PRCPs), requires that all areas disturbed within the relevant mining tenure be rehabilitated to a post-mining land use, unless they are defined as a non-use management area (NUMA)129. PMLU examples in guidelines include native ecosystems, habitat, ecosystem services, and grazing. However, in this guidance, examples extend beyond ecological benefits and include options such as landfill, water storage, and infrastructure, including renewable energy sources.

In anticipation of the closure of numerous mines in the coming decades, the New South Wales state parliament launched an inquiry into beneficial and productive post-mining land use130. The recommendations included a call for the government to undertake a comprehensive review of the post-mining regulatory framework and processes, including relevant provisions of the Mining Act 1992 (NSW), the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 (NSW), and the Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 (NSW), as well as associated regulations and policies. There are precedents for the streamlining of regulations within New South Wales. To support a Master Plan to reactivate land adjacent to the Port Kembla Steelworks, the government announced the establishment of a whole-of-government working group to coordinate decision-making across government departments130,131. The Port Kembla Land Transformation Project’s goal is to transform approximately 200 hectares of underutilised land, which could support up to 30,000 jobs132. Elsewhere in New South Wales, a significant residential and retail development occurred on Pasminco’s former lead and zinc smelter at Cockle Creek in Boolaroo133. The Pasminco smelter closed in 2003, leaving heavy metal contamination after over 100 years of operation. The successful remediation and redevelopment of this site involved managing a myriad of issues, including threatened flora and fauna, major soil and groundwater contamination, mining leases, mine subsidence, and archaeology134.

Conclusion

Globally, mine closures have not generally yielded a positive overall narrative. The thousands of ownerless, abandoned, and unrehabilitated mines worldwide have resulted in the loss of livelihoods and, too often, unacceptable environmental and social impacts.

The primary responsibility of mining companies is to make their sites safe, stable, and non-polluting. However, there is the potential for substitutive economies to be developed on these sites. These opportunities could be led and implemented by third parties. While these developments on former mine sites could yield socio-economic benefits, they also have the potential to reduce the mining company’s liabilities post-closure, as the new proponent may assume responsibilities for the site’s maintenance, such as access, erosion, and weed control.

There are barriers to repurposing a mine, the foremost of which is that supportive government policy generally does not exist to facilitate multiple or non-mining uses of mining leases. State regulation is often rigid and consists of complex processes in discrete regulatory silos. These potential regulatory hurdles can create timing and financial issues, frequently preventing mining companies from further assessing repurposing as a valid PMLU option.

Effective socio-economic transitions in post-mining regions will continue to be a challenge unless state intervention in policy development and financial support exists. State regulation needs to be harmonised across regulatory streams and allow for overlap between the mine closure/relinquishment process and the future use/development process. Alignment of PMLUs to the SDGs could support the desired integration for the beneficial transition of mine sites.

To this end, a framework has been proposed for incorporating the SDGs as a lens in PMLU assessments, complementing the existing knowledge base. It would be advantageous to integrate other approaches, tools, and methodologies into the decision-making process. Co-design of PMLUs will enable the best outcomes—ideally involving all key stakeholders/rights holders, including local communities (especially Indigenous peoples), the government, research organisations, and the private sector. Inclusive governance frameworks would best guide co-design to ensure all voices are heard, not just the loudest or most powerful in the room.

The selection of case studies has limitations. The aim of this article is to illustrate how the selection of post-mining land uses can be strategically aligned with one or more of the seventeen SDGs. The initial pool of SDG-relevant PMLU examples was compiled through expert consultations among the co-authors and their professional networks, as well as through a database search. However, this initial list reflected some regional and thematic biases based on contributors’ backgrounds and areas of experience. The synthesis of the results and the identification of countries that are significant to the global mining sector but not included in the study provide a guide for the inclusion of additional case studies in future work.

With the global focus on energy transition, the large-scale rollout of renewable energy projects on repurposed mines could yield an ironic conclusion to this industry’s narrative, particularly for coal mines. However, this is also true, directly or indirectly, of many other mineral and metal operations. The transmission lines that entered the mine site to enable fossil-fuel-based energy generation could be repurposed to convey renewable energy in the opposite direction, generating a 180-degree energy transition. But this needs to be scaled if it is to be significant. It will require a concerted global effort.
