Age-dependent obesity paradox in acute myocardial infarction prognosis: a cohort study of body mass index and recurrent myocardial infarction
Authors: Lingling Zhang, Zhican Liu, Jianping Zeng, Mingxin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a known cardiovascular risk factor, but the “obesity paradox” has been observed in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), where obesity may be linked to better survival outcomes. The relationship between body mass index (BMI) and recurrent myocardial infarction, particularly with age-specific effects, remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 4023 AMI patients from a tertiary medical center (2015–2023). Patients were stratified by age: ≤60 years (n = 1277) and >60 years (n = 2746). Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between BMI and recurrent myocardial infarction, adjusting for demographics, biomarkers [N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT)], imaging parameters [left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)], comorbidities, and treatment regimens. Curve-fitting models were also applied. The median follow-up time was 35 months (Q1–Q3 25–58).ResultsIn the ≤60 years group, higher BMI was associated with a significantly lower risk of recurrent myocardial infarction [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) = 0.965, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.936–0.994, P = 0.018]. In contrast, the >60 years group showed a trend toward higher risk (unadjusted HR = 1.032, 95% CI 1.012–1.053, P = 0.001), which lost statistical significance after adjustment (adjusted HR = 1.015, 95% CI 0.994–1.037, P = 0.151). Curve fitting revealed a negative linear correlation in the ≤60 years group and a positive relationship in the >60 years group.ConclusionsThis study presents the first evidence of an age-dependent obesity paradox in AMI. In patients aged ≤60 years, higher BMI reduced recurrent myocardial infarction risk, whereas in those aged >60 years, the protective effect disappeared and reversed, indicating potential harm. These findings highlight the need for age-stratified secondary prevention strategies for AMI. Summary of Principal Study Outcomes. Subject terms: Risk factors, Epidemiology

Introduction

Obesity is an established independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, and its global burden has been confirmed by several multinational cohort studies [1, 2]. Interestingly, in the context of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), epidemiological research has revealed a pathophysiological paradox: although obesity significantly increases the risk of initial cardiovascular events, patients with obesity who are diagnosed with AMI tend to have lower mortality and better prognostic outcomes. This phenomenon is referred to as the “obesity paradox” [3–5]. The potential mechanisms behind this paradox may involve several biological pathways. Individuals with obesity often have higher levels of adiponectin and greater metabolic adaptability, which may help reduce inflammation and improve myocardial repair. Furthermore, patients with obesity are more likely to receive intensive, guideline-directed treatments, such as early revascularization and high-dose statin therapy, which could partly explain their improved survival outcomes [6, 7].

While the existence of the obesity paradox in AMI prognosis has been widely reported [5, 8], the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and recurrent cardiovascular events remains controversial, particularly with regard to age-related differences.

Age is an important independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and may modify the relationship between BMI and cardiovascular events [9]. A study on cardiogenic shock showed that BMI had significant age-related heterogeneity: low BMI increased 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients, while it had no significant effect on younger individuals [10]. Another study found that a high BMI was associated with improved survival in elderly patients with renal cell carcinoma, but showed no significant effect in younger patients [11]. These findings underscore the importance of age stratification in analyzing the relationship between BMI and prognosis. Therefore, understanding how BMI influences recurrent myocardial infarction risk at different ages, particularly in relation to the obesity paradox, is essential for optimizing individualized care in AMI patients.

Building on our previous work, we found that mild obesity was associated with a reduced risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in male patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [12]. This study extends our previous research in two key areas: (1) it is the first to systematically assess the age-specific effects of BMI on recurrent myocardial infarction, and (2) it uses a larger sample size (n = 4023 vs. n = 664) to develop an age-stratified risk prediction model. Through multivariable Cox regression analysis, we aim to: (1) identify the age-dependent nature of the obesity paradox in recurrent events, and (2) develop individualized risk management pathways based on age. These findings will provide important evidence to help refine secondary prevention strategies for AMI.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included 4023 patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) treated at the cardiovascular center of a tertiary hospital between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) a diagnosis of AMI based on established criteria [13]; (2) age ≥18 years; (3) non-pregnant status. The exclusion criteria were: (1) active malignancy; (2) life expectancy <6 months due to non-cardiovascular conditions; (3) missing key laboratory or imaging data; (4) severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m²). A total of 4023 patients meeting these criteria were included and divided into two age groups: ≤60 years (n = 1277) and >60 years (n = 2746) (Fig. 1).

Data collection and quality control

Data were collected using a standardized case report form (CRF) and extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records (EPIC, v2023) by cardiovascular specialists who received professional training.

The data included: (1) demographic characteristics; (2) laboratory results [high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), etc.]; (3) cardiac imaging parameters [left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) measured by transthoracic echocardiography]; (4) comorbidities and treatment information. To ensure data quality, a three-tier verification system was implemented: (a) primary verification: performed by data managers who cross-checked the electronic data against the original medical records; (b) secondary verification: conducted by senior cardiovascular physicians who blindly reviewed 20% of random samples; (c) logical verification: automated validation using Python 3.9 (Pandas 1.5.3 library) for range checks and logical relationships between variables.

Endpoint determination and follow-up protocol

A standardized endpoint determination process was followed. The primary endpoint of this study was recurrent myocardial infarction (defined according to the 2023 European Society of Cardiology [ESC] Acute Coronary Syndrome Guidelines [13]). Secondary endpoints included all-cause mortality and heart failure readmissions (defined according to the 2021 ESC Heart Failure Guidelines [14]). The endpoints were determined by an independent committee of five senior cardiologists who were not involved in the study. The follow-up protocol was hybrid, with 38% of follow-ups conducted in outpatient clinics and 62% by telephone. The median follow-up period was 35 months (Q1–Q3 25–58), with the last follow-up on September 15, 2024, and a loss-to-follow-up rate of less than 5%.

Ethical standards and data management

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangtan Central Hospital (Approval No. 2023-02-001) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Guidelines for Ethical Review of Health Research Involving Humans. Because this was a retrospective observational study, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent. All data were stored on encrypted hospital servers to ensure data security and traceability.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as means ± standard deviation and compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians (interquartile range) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1, with a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05.

Stratified Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze the relationship between BMI and recurrent myocardial infarction, with stratification by age groups. Multivariable-adjusted models were developed: (A) unadjusted model; (B) demographic-adjusted model (sex, age); (C) fully adjusted model, incorporating N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), Killip class, hemoglobin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), comorbidities [hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), etc.], and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT; dual antiplatelet therapy, high-dose statins, etc.). Curve fitting models were used to explore the relationship between BMI and recurrent myocardial infarction risk.

Primary effect of BMI on recurrent myocardial infarction

Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed an age-dependent effect of BMI on the risk of recurrent myocardial infarction (Table 2). In the ≤60 years group, an increase in BMI by 1 kg/m² was significantly associated with a 3.5% reduction in the risk of recurrent myocardial infarction (fully adjusted hazard ratio [HR] = 0.965, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.936–0.994, P = 0.018).

BMI and secondary outcomes in AMI patients

Cox regression models adjusted for age and sex showed no significant association between BMI and all-cause mortality (≤60 years group: HR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.89–1.02, P = 0.191; >60 years group: HR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.96–1.01, P = 0.220), and the overall analysis remained neutral (HR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–1.00, P = 0.118). Similarly, BMI was not significantly associated with heart failure readmission risk (≤60 years group: HR = 1.00, 95% CI 0.93–1.09, P = 0.927; >60 years group: HR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.94–1.03, P = 0.521), and the overall analysis remained neutral (HR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.95–1.03, P = 0.597) (Table 3).

AMI Type and Treatment Approach: Among patients undergoing surgical treatment, the younger group showed a significant protective effect of BMI (HR = 0.953, 95% CI 0.923–0.984, P = 0.003), while no significant association was found in the older group (HR = 1.020, 95% CI 0.994–1.046, P = 0.130). Notably, in patients with a Killip class ≥2, BMI exhibited a significant protective effect in the younger group (HR = 0.958, 95% CI 0.926–0.992, P = 0.015), whereas the older group showed a trend toward increased risk (HR = 1.033, 95% CI 1.010–1.057, P = 0.005).

Comorbidities and Biomarkers: In patients without atrial fibrillation, BMI showed a significant protective effect in the younger group (HR = 0.969, 95% CI 0.941–0.997, P = 0.033), while in the older group, BMI was associated with an increased risk (HR = 1.039, 95% CI 1.018–1.061, P < 0.001). In patients with NT-proBNP ≤900 pg/mL, BMI was significantly protective in the younger group (HR = 0.961, 95% CI 0.929–0.995, P = 0.025), whereas in the older group, BMI was associated with increased risk (HR = 1.050, 95% CI 1.018–1.082, P = 0.002).

Medication Treatment: In patients using angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, BMI showed a significant protective effect in the younger group (HR = 0.946, 95% CI 0.914–0.979, P = 0.002), while in the older group, BMI was associated with increased risk (HR = 1.036, 95% CI 1.011–1.061, P = 0.005). In patients on statin therapy, BMI showed a significant protective effect in the younger group (HR = 0.965, 95% CI 0.938–0.994, P = 0.017), whereas in the older group, BMI was associated with increased risk (HR = 1.031, 95% CI 1.011–1.052, P = 0.002) (details provided in Table 4).

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically evaluate the effect of body mass index (BMI) on the risk of recurrent myocardial infarction in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), revealing an age-dependent bidirectional relationship. Our main finding indicates that, in patients aged ≤60 years, an increase in BMI is significantly associated with a reduced risk of recurrent myocardial infarction. In contrast, in patients aged >60 years, an increase in BMI tends to be associated with an increased risk of recurrent myocardial infarction, although this association did not reach statistical significance after multivariable adjustment (Graphical abstract). Our results provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying the obesity paradox and suggest that clinical practice should incorporate stratified management based on age and BMI to optimize individualized treatment strategies.

Mechanisms of the obesity paradox

Although the mechanisms underlying the obesity paradox remain unclear, previous studies suggest that individuals with overweight or mild obesity may have greater metabolic reserves, enabling them to better withstand acute cardiovascular events [15, 16]. However, this does not appear to apply to patients with severe obesity. Our study further supports this hypothesis, particularly in patients aged ≤60 years, where an increase in BMI correlates with a lower risk of recurrent myocardial infarction. This trend does not hold for patients with severe obesity. The protective effect observed in these patients may be linked to metabolic adaptations caused by obesity and lower levels of chronic inflammation [17], which could enhance recovery after acute coronary syndromes [16, 18]. Additionally, individuals with obesity are often administered more intensive treatment, which may contribute to improved short-term prognosis [6, 7].

However, as age increases, the protective effect of obesity seems to diminish and may even transform into a risk factor. This shift is closely associated with the higher comorbidity burden observed in older patients. Comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension can increase the risk of cardiovascular events [19, 20]. Our findings suggest that in patients aged >60 years, BMI increases are linked to worsening comorbidities, particularly hypertension and diabetes, which may contribute to the higher risk of recurrent myocardial infarction.

Expansion of existing evidence

Our study is consistent with previous literature, emphasizing the unique nature of the obesity paradox in AMI patients [3–5, 8, 12, 16, 18]. Through multivariable-adjusted analysis, we have demonstrated for the first time a significant age-dependent effect of BMI in AMI patients. In patients aged ≤60 years, BMI increases may be associated with changes in metabolic factors such as adiponectin, which is believed to promote myocardial repair by regulating metabolic responses [21]. However, in older patients, some studies suggest that the effect of adiponectin is not linear but instead follows a U-shaped curve in relation to cardiovascular events, which differs from the response observed in patients aged >60 years [22]. Other research has suggested that interventions targeting adiponectin biosynthesis could protect the heart from ischemia-reperfusion injury [23]. Nonetheless, some studies question the existence of the obesity paradox in all cardiovascular disease patients, proposing that it may not apply universally across all conditions [24]. With the advancement of artificial intelligence, future research into BMI, adiponectin, and other metabolic markers in relation to cardiovascular prognosis is expected to become more refined [25].

Regional differences in obesity cut-points

Regional differences in BMI distribution and obesity cut-points may also help to interpret our findings. In our Chinese AMI cohort, the mean BMI was in the low-to-normal range (around 20 kg/m²), whereas large US population data show that more than one third of adults meet criteria for obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), indicating a substantially higher BMI burden in Western populations [26]. In addition, a WHO expert consultation has highlighted that Asian populations develop cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI levels than European populations and has proposed lower BMI cut-points for overweight and obesity in Asian populations [27]. Taken together, these differences highlight the importance of considering region-specific BMI distributions and cut-points when interpreting the obesity paradox across diverse populations.

Clinical translational value

Based on the significant age-dependent effects observed in this study, we propose a new management strategy to optimize weight management in AMI patients:

Patients aged ≤60 years: For patients with a BMI between 25 and 35 kg/m², we suggest moderately relaxing weight management targets, especially for those with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50% or those undergoing PCI. This approach aims to avoid excessive weight loss that may lead to muscle wasting.

Patients aged >60 years: BMI should be strictly maintained below 28 kg/m², with particular attention to high-risk subgroups, such as females, patients with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and those with LDL levels ≥3.4 mmol/L.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it may be subject to selection and information biases, particularly concerning BMI measurements and baseline characteristics. Second, since the data were collected from a single center, external validity must be confirmed through multi-center cohort studies. Additionally, due to the observational design, we cannot establish a causal relationship between BMI and recurrent myocardial infarction.

Third, because this registry was based on routinely collected clinical data, we did not have systematic information on body composition (e.g., body fat distribution), indices of muscle mass and muscle strength, or cardiorespiratory fitness and habitual physical activity. Cardiorespiratory fitness and physical activity have been shown to be powerful, independent predictors of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality and other major outcomes in both general and high-risk populations [28–33]. In addition, recent reviews on obesity and the obesity paradox have emphasized that the interaction between adiposity and cardiorespiratory fitness may be crucial for interpreting apparently paradoxical associations between BMI and prognosis [34–37]. Therefore, BMI in our study cannot distinguish between adiposity and lean mass or between fit and unfit phenotypes, and residual confounding by these unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

Although adjusting for a large number of variables in Model C might reduce statistical power, it is crucial to control for key confounders such as NT-proBNP, hsTnT, LVEF, and comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes). These variables are clinically relevant and have been shown to significantly influence the prognosis of AMI patients. Reducing the number of variables could introduce omitted variable bias, potentially distorting the true relationship between BMI and recurrent myocardial infarction.

Lastly, due to the limitations of the retrospective database, we set the upper BMI limit at 35 kg/m², excluding patients with severe obesity. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the obesity paradox persists in this group. Future studies should not only incorporate more accurate body composition measurements, such as bioelectrical impedance analysis and cardiac MRI, but also objective or questionnaire-based assessments of muscle mass, muscle strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, and physical activity, in order to more comprehensively evaluate the interplay between adiposity, fitness, and prognosis in AMI patients.

Conclusion

This study is the first to systematically examine the age-dependent nature of the obesity paradox following AMI. Our findings reveal a bidirectional effect: an increase in BMI significantly reduces the risk of recurrent myocardial infarction in ≤60 years patients, but this protective effect diminishes and may even become harmful in >60 years patients. These results challenge the “one-size-fits-all” approach to weight management and provide strong evidence for personalized secondary prevention strategies based on age, comorbidities, and treatment responses. This discovery has the potential to influence clinical guidelines and contribute to the development of cardiovascular risk management in precision medicine.
