Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms arise from neural crest cells, thus they can originate from different sites in the body. They commonly arise in the gastrointestinal tract; in the lungs and the pancreas [1]. Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) are categorized as poorly differentiated carcinomas in the current 4th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification list. Regardless of the origin site, cells from neuroendocrine neoplasms tend to express synaptophysin and chromogranin A [1]. Head and Neck SCNEC locations are rare and development in salivary glands is even rarer [2,3]. Small cell carcinoma accounted for 1.8 % of major salivary gland malignancies [3]. Treatment may include surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or some combination of these modalities. SCNEC carries a poor prognosis with the majority of patients developing distant metastases [4].

We herein describe an extremely uncommon example of cervical SCNEC arising in the parotid gland, diagnosed at an advanced metastatic stage.

Methods

The work has been reported in line with The SCARE criteria [5].

Presentation of case

A man, aged 76 years old, non-smoking, non-alcoholic, without history of cancer and/or cervical irradiation, consulted for a left side-cervical swelling that had evolved for four months. It had rapidly increased in size. It was associated with a significant weight loss (not quantified).

At cervical examination: left side-cervical mass measuring 10 cm located in the parotid space and II, III, IV and V lymph node groups. It was indurated, fixed, non-tender. Nasal cavities and nasopharynx examination was normal as well as laryngopharyngeal endoscopic examination. The patient had not facial palsy. The rest of ENT examination was normal. Bronchial endoscopy was normal.

The patient underwent panendoscopy under general anesthesia including nasopharynx examination, laryngoscopy, hypopharyngoscopy, examination of the oral cavity, palpation of palatine tonsils and the base of the tongue. Panendoscopy did not show any tumor.

Given the mass size, the local aggressiveness and the secondary hepatic lesions, we opted for a tumor biopsy. Pathological examination concluded to a small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma showing small round to oval tumor cells, arranged in cords and nests containing chromatin with low abundance cytoplasm, multiple mitoses. On immunohistochemical examination CD56, CK, Synaptophysin and chromogranineA markers were positive. CD45 was negative.

The multidisciplinary consultation meeting decided on chemotherapy based on cisplatin and Etoposide (etoposide 100 mg/m2/day IV on days 1–3 and cisplatin 80 mg/m2 IV on the first day every 3 weeks). Despite the speed of treatment, the evolution was unfavorable with fulminant increase in tumor size. Death occurred after 2 cycles of chemotherapy (less than one month from the treatment start).

Discussion

Extrapulmonary SCNEC represent 2.5–5 % of all SCNEC [1,2], of which head and neck cases account for 10–16 %. SCNEC of the head and neck are characterized by a male predominance with a peak incidence from the fifth to seventh decades of life, and occur frequently in men with a smoking background [6]. The larynx is the most commonly affected site of head and neck, followed by the salivary glands and the sinonasal region [7,8]. Small cell carcinoma account for 1.8 % of major salivary gland malignancies among which 1.7 % of primary parotid gland carcinomas. SCNEC is histologically regarded as a malignant tumor similar to small cell carcinoma occurring in the lungs [6,9]. Tumors are characterized by composed of sheets and nests of round to spindle-shaped cells with dense nuclei, fine granular nuclear chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli and scant cytoplasm. Cell necrosis and mitotic activity are commonly observed. Most of SCNEC tumor cells are immunoreactive for cytokeratins and epithelial membrane antigen, as well as the neuroendocrine markers, neuron-specific enolase, synaptophysin, chromogranin A, CD56 and CD57 [10]. Although some differences may exist in the clinical behavior of tumors arising from different subsites, generally, SCNEC of the head and neck have a tendency for aggressive local invasion and a strong propensity for regional and distant metastasis both [8,11]. Approximately 20 % to 27 % of patients have distant metastatic disease the time of diagnosis such as our patient [12,13]. There are no randomized control trials to support the treatment guidelines [1]. Data were predominately extrapolated from treatment of hepatobiliary tract and pancreas SCNEC. Our patient presented with a large leftsided neck mass and was found to have stage IV disease with liver involvement. As per National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendations, a palliative chemotherapy is recommended with platinum-based regimens in combination with etoposide: etoposide 100 mg/m2/day IV on days 1–3 and cisplatin 80 mg/m2 IV on the first day every 3–4 weeks) [1,14]. But, it is to be noted that these guidelines were based on two small retrospective studies including 21 and 19 patients, respectively, for cisplatin15 and carboplatin16 regimens in combination with etoposide. The overall prognosis of head and neck SCNEC is poor [8,11]. The majority of authors founded that these tumors were rapidly progressive and aggressive [13] as was the case of our patient. Median survival of 20.3 months had been reported in a National Cancer Database (NCDB) study [15].

A SEER database review [16] founded that head and neck SCNEC survival varied significantly based on site and metastatic status, with oropharyngeal tumors having the poorest prognosis. A small case study of 14 patients with SCNEC of the head and neck noted that this subset of patients had better survival rates than SCNEC of the lung [2].

Conclusion

The current study presents an extremley rare case of a cervical advanced stage SCNEC whose starting point is probably the parotid gland. The diagnosis of cervical SCNEC must be present in clinician mind when faced with a man in the seventh decade of life who presents a rapidly expanding cervical mass. The correct diagnosis is formed through the combination of morphological and immunohistochemical evaluations. Due to the absence of randomized trials regarding head and neck SCNEC, optimal treatment has not been defined. Recent advancements in immunotherapy and checkpoint inhibitors may represent an opportunity to improve outcomes in patients.
