The mediating effect of social networks and depressive symptoms on the relationship between older adults’ participation in volunteer activities and life satisfaction: a cross-sectional study involving Chinese older adults
Authors: Yinliang Ge, Wenjun Jiang, Mo Xue, Yanxia Lyu, Lixin Hong, Kan Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVolunteer activities have been widely acknowledged as a meaningful form of social participation for older adults, contributing to their subjective well-being. However, the psychological and interpersonal mechanisms linking volunteering to life satisfaction remain insufficiently explored. In particular, how social networks and depressive symptoms jointly mediate this relationship in the Chinese context is yet to be clarified.PurposeThis study investigates the association between older adults’ participation in volunteer activities and their life satisfaction, with a focus on the sequential mediating roles of social networks and depressive symptoms.MethodsData were drawn from the 2020 wave of the Chinese Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), involving 9,828 respondents aged 60 and older. Volunteer participation was measured through self-reported engagement in community service activities. Social networks were assessed using the six-item Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS-6), while depressive symptoms were evaluated by the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). Life satisfaction was measured with a single-item indicator. A serial mediation model was tested using structural equation modeling with bootstrapping procedures (5,000 samples) to estimate indirect effects.ResultsParticipation in volunteer activities was positively associated with life satisfaction among older adults. The relationship was partially mediated by social networks and depressive symptoms in sequence. Specifically, volunteering predicted stronger social networks (a₁ = 0.121, p < .001), which in turn were associated with lower depressive symptoms (a₃ = − 0.135, p < .001), subsequently enhancing life satisfaction (b₂ = − 0.094, p < .001). The total indirect effect through both mediators was significant (a₁ × a₃ × b₂ = 0.002, 95% CI [0.001, 0.004]). The direct effect remained significant (c′ = 0.095, 95% CI [0.060, 0.130]), indicating a partial mediation pattern.ConclusionThis study reveals that social networks and depressive symptoms jointly mediate the relationship between volunteer participation and life satisfaction among older adults in China. These findings provide empirical support for integrating social and psychological mechanisms into models of active ageing. Promoting volunteer engagement may enhance both social connectedness and mental health, ultimately improving quality of life in later life.

Introduction

As the global population ages, promoting the well-being of older adults has become a pressing priority for researchers and policymakers [17]. In China, the rapidly growing elderly population is increasingly at risk of social isolation, psychological distress, and declining life satisfaction [20]. Among various interventions, participation in volunteer activities has emerged as a promising strategy to promote active aging, enhance psychological resilience, and foster social connectedness [6]. However, the psychosocial mechanisms through which volunteering enhances life satisfaction remain underexplored, particularly in non-Western contexts [43]. Existing studies predominantly focus on Western societies, where individualism, formal volunteering institutions, and distinct role expectations prevail [37]. In collectivist societies such as China, where familial obligations and community interdependence shape social roles, the psychosocial implications of volunteering may operate differently [49].

To better understand these mechanisms, this study developed and empirically tested a serial mediation model linking volunteer participation to life satisfaction through social networks and depressive symptoms, drawing on three theoretical frameworks: Social Integration Theory, Stress-Buffering Theory, and Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [46]. In contrast to previous studies that applied these theories in parallel, this study integrates them into a unified structural-emotional-motivational framework [15].

In this model, Social Integration Theory provides the structural foundation by highlighting how volunteering fosters external social connections and network embeddedness, thereby enhancing older adults’ access to interpersonal resources [36]. Stress-Buffering Theory functions as the emotional mechanism, explaining how these strengthened social ties offer emotional support that reduces depressive symptoms and promotes psychological resilience [15]. Self-Determination Theory contributes the motivational basis by positing that volunteering satisfies core psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—through meaningful, self-directed activities [50]. Together, these theories correspond to distinct but interconnected layers of psychosocial resources: external structure (social connections), relational support (emotional buffering), and internal motivation (psychological need fulfillment) [39]. Framing the model along these structural, emotional, and motivational dimensions—aligned respectively with contextual, interpersonal, and individual levels—enhances conceptual clarity and provides a coherent explanation of how volunteer participation contributes to life satisfaction by sequentially shaping social integration, emotional well-being, and motivational fulfillment [11].

Volunteer participation and life satisfaction: a motivational pathway

Volunteer participation has been increasingly recognized as a meaningful form of social engagement that promotes well-being in later life [35]. For older adults, volunteering provides an avenue to remain socially connected, cognitively stimulated, and emotionally fulfilled, especially in the context of retirement and shrinking familial or occupational roles [1]. Numerous studies have documented a positive association between volunteering and life satisfaction among older populations across diverse cultural settings [41]. This relationship can be well explained through the lens of Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which posits that human flourishing depends on the fulfillment of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness [13]. Volunteering allows older adults to engage in freely chosen, socially valued activities (autonomy), demonstrate skills and receive recognition (competence), and maintain meaningful interpersonal relationships (relatedness) [15]. Empirical research supports this pathway, demonstrating that volunteering enhances psychological well-being, self-esteem, and a sense of purpose in older adulthood [10]. While the direct effects are sometimes modest in magnitude, they are both theoretically grounded and statistically significant, particularly when volunteer roles are perceived as intrinsically rewarding [19]. In the Chinese context, where traditional family caregiving roles are gradually declining and public discourse increasingly promotes “productive aging,” volunteer participation offers a socially endorsed and personally meaningful means of remaining active [54]. National initiatives such as the “Silver Volunteer Action” further legitimize this role and provide institutional support. Therefore, within the structural-emotional-motivational framework adopted in this study, the direct path from volunteer participation to life satisfaction (Path c′) is conceptualized as a motivational mechanism, reflecting the psychological enrichment derived from voluntary engagement (Fig. 1) [23. The mediating role of social networks: a structural pathway

Beyond its direct motivational effects, volunteer participation may enhance life satisfaction by improving older adults’ structural social resources, particularly their social networks [15]. Volunteering provides regular opportunities for interpersonal interaction. It fosters new social ties and strengthens existing ones. This helps mitigate social isolation, a significant risk factor for poor mental and physical health in later life [27]. This pathway aligns with the tenets of Social Integration Theory, which posits that engagement in structured social roles strengthens individuals’ embeddedness in community networks and promotes access to both emotional and instrumental support [46]. Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that older adults with broader and more supportive social networks report higher levels of life satisfaction, greater sense of belonging, and reduced loneliness [28]. Volunteering can be particularly important in this regard, as it expands relational circles beyond kinship ties and offers older adults socially legitimate roles in community life [37]. In collectivist societies such as China, where family structure and face-to-face sociality remain central, the role of social networks in shaping subjective well-being is especially salient [24]. Moreover, government-endorsed community-based volunteer programs, such as “neighborhood mutual-aid teams,” provide culturally appropriate platforms for elderly engagement and relationship-building [47]. Accordingly, this study conceptualizes the path from volunteer participation to life satisfaction via social networks (Path a1 × b1) as a structural mechanism, whereby voluntary engagement fosters social connectedness that, in turn, enhances subjective well-being [34]. This mediation pathway not only reflects the instrumental value of volunteering in maintaining older adults’ relational capital but also highlights the importance of structurally embedded support systems in promoting healthy aging (Fig. 2) [45].

The mediating role of depressive symptoms: an emotional pathway

In addition to enhancing social connectedness, volunteer participation may influence life satisfaction by mitigating negative emotional states, particularly depressive symptoms [15]. Depression is one of the most prevalent mental health challenges in older adulthood, often triggered by bereavement, declining physical health, reduced economic participation, and social withdrawal [4]. Prior studies have consistently shown that depressive symptoms are negatively associated with life satisfaction, making depression a significant emotional barrier to well-being in later life [39]. Participation in volunteer activities may help buffer depressive symptoms by providing structure, meaningful routine, social recognition, and emotional uplift [1]. However, these psychological benefits may not occur in isolation, but may instead be influenced by the quality and extent of one’s social relationships [37].

This connection is well explained by Stress-Buffering Theory, which posits that supportive social ties protect individuals from the adverse effects of stress and reduce emotional vulnerability [51]. Volunteering enhances opportunities for emotional expression and reciprocity, allowing older adults to experience validation, empathy, and companionship [41]. These interactions reduce feelings of loneliness, worthlessness, and despair [35]. In the Chinese sociocultural context—where emotional restraint and stigma surrounding mental illness may limit help-seeking behaviors—volunteering offers an informal, socially sanctioned channel for emotional support [29]. Moreover, involvement in collectivist-oriented volunteer teams (e.g., community patrols, mutual-aid programs) may provide older adults with emotional meaning and perceived usefulness, both of which are critical in combating depressive ideation [22].

Therefore, this study proposes that depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between volunteer participation and life satisfaction (Path a2 × b2), reflecting an emotional pathway within the structural-emotional-motivational framework [34]. While this mechanism has been alluded to in previous research, empirical testing of its mediating role—especially alongside social networks—remains limited [10]. By incorporating depressive symptoms as a distinct but interrelated mediator, this study seeks to elucidate the emotional complexity underlying the volunteering-well-being linkage.

Importantly, while depressive symptoms and life satisfaction are statistically correlated, they are distinct constructs, both theoretically and functionally [55]. Depressive symptoms primarily capture short-term negative emotional states, such as sadness, hopelessness, and anhedonia, whereas life satisfaction reflects a broader, cognitively-driven evaluation of one’s overall quality of life. The two variables may share reciprocal influence but are not redundant. In aging research, depressive symptoms are often treated as proximal emotional outcomes that mediate the relationship between social or behavioral factors and distal well-being indicators [55]. Thus, modeling depressive symptoms as an intermediary process is theoretically grounded and consistent with established psychosocial frameworks (Fig. 3) [5].

A serial mediation mechanism

While social networks and depressive symptoms have been widely studied as independent mediators in the relationship between volunteer participation and life satisfaction, growing evidence suggests that these factors may also function in an interconnected and sequential manner [15]. In particular, social networks may serve as a precursor to emotional well-being, reducing the risk of depressive symptoms, which in turn enhances life satisfaction [5]. This perspective reflects a more nuanced understanding of how social engagement may contribute to well-being through cascading psychosocial pathways [26].

The logic of this sequential pathway is grounded in the integration of Social Integration Theory and Stress-Buffering Theory [15]. Volunteering expands social networks by fostering interpersonal contact and role reinforcement, while these networks provide emotional support and a sense of belonging that buffers against psychological distress. In other words, the expansion of social ties through volunteer activity may not only directly contribute to life satisfaction but also indirectly enhance emotional stability, thereby forming a structural-to-emotional chain of influence.

Empirical studies lend support to this chained mechanism. For instance, Gyasi et al. and Kim & Moen found that social connectedness reduces the risk of depressive symptoms, and depression is in turn a reliable predictor of life satisfaction [16]. Yet few studies have explicitly tested this serial mediation effect within a comprehensive model that includes both simple and chained indirect paths [10]. In the context of Chinese older adults, where interpersonal harmony and indirect emotional regulation are culturally emphasized, the role of social networks in supporting emotional resilience may be particularly critical [8].

Therefore, in addition to examining the separate mediating effects of social networks and depressive symptoms, this study proposes a serial mediation pathway (Path a1 × a3 × b2), whereby volunteer participation enhances social networks, which then reduce depressive symptoms and ultimately lead to improved life satisfaction [34]. This chained mechanism complements the direct and individual mediation paths and contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted ways through which volunteerism promotes well-being in older adulthood [45].

Although this study is based on cross-sectional data, the proposed directional pathway from volunteer participation to life satisfaction is theoretically justified [1]. Life course theory suggests that behavioral engagement such as volunteering initiates role acquisition and identity construction, which precedes changes in psychological well-being across time [40]. Furthermore, intervention studies have demonstrated that introducing volunteer programs among older adults leads to subsequent improvements in social integration and reductions in depressive symptoms [19, 35]. These findings collectively support a conceptual logic in which volunteering acts as a modifiable antecedent that triggers downstream psychosocial changes, rather than merely reflecting prior well-being [1]. Therefore, while causality cannot be definitively established due to data limitations, the theoretical directionality of the mediation model is grounded in both longitudinal reasoning and intervention evidence (Fig. 4) [18].

Research objectives and hypotheses

While prior studies have addressed individual mediators of the volunteering-well-being relationship, few have integrated motivational, structural, and emotional mechanisms into a cohesive framework [15]. Moreover, most research has been conducted in Western countries, which limits their generalizability [37]. Considering the unique role of family structure, interdependence, and community ties in Chinese society, investigating this model in a Chinese context is both timely and necessary [32]. Given the potential for reciprocal associations among well-being, emotional states, and social engagement, supplementary models exploring reverse pathways are also warranted [5].

By integrating these theories, we propose that structural engagement through volunteering facilitates emotional regulation, which collectively satisfy core psychological needs, establishing a sequential mechanism from role integration to emotional buffering and motivational fulfillment [46].

Using data from the 2020 Chinese Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), this study applies a serial mediation model to examines the mechanisms by which volunteer participation influences life satisfaction, mediated sequentially by social networks and depressive symptoms, within a unified structural–emotional–motivational framework.

The following hypotheses are proposed:

H1: Volunteer participation is positively associated with life satisfaction (Path c′ ).

H2: Social networks mediate the relationship between volunteer participation and life satisfaction (Path a1 × b1).

H3: Depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between volunteer participation and life satisfaction (Path a2 × b2).

H4: Social networks and depressive symptoms jointly mediate the relationship in a serial pathway (Path a1 × a3 × b2).

Methods

Participants

This study used data from the 2020 wave of the Chinese Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), a nationally representative survey conducted by the National Survey Research Center at Renmin University of China. The CLASS employed a multi-stage stratified probability sampling strategy to collect information from individuals aged 60 and above across 28 provincial-level administrative units in China. The survey covered demographic characteristics, health status, social participation, psychological well-being, and family relationships among older adults. It has strong national representativeness.

A total of 11,398 respondents were initially included in the 2020 CLASS dataset. After excluding cases with missing information on core variables—such as surviving children count (n = 326), self-rated health condition (n = 16), life satisfaction (n = 81), Depressive Symptoms (n = 414), and other key control variables (n = 733)—a final sample of 9,828 older adults was retained for cross-sectional analysis (see Fig. 5 for the sample selection flowchart).

Respondents were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 60 or older, and (2) completed valid responses for all primary study variables. Participants with severe cognitive impairment, institutionalized status, or extensive missing data were excluded to ensure the integrity of multivariate analysis.

The CLASS 2020 survey received ethical approval from the Academic Research Ethics Committee of Renmin University of China. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The current study was conducted using de-identified public-use data, and all procedures complied with relevant ethical standards for secondary data analysis.

Measures

The CLASS survey employed a face-to-face interview approach using a standardized structured questionnaire administered by trained interviewers. The questionnaire included validated instruments such as the Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS-6) for social networks and a 9-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depressive Symptoms Scale (CES-D) for depressive symptoms. Life satisfaction was assessed using a single-item question validated in prior Chinese gerontological studies. All questionnaires were pilot-tested prior to administration to ensure reliability and cultural relevance.

Life satisfaction

The dependent variable in this study is the life satisfaction of older adults, which is a crucial indicator of older adults’ subjective attitude towards their quality of life. The study uses the corresponding question from the CLASS questionnaire, “Overall, are you satisfied with your current life?” to measure this indicator. The responses “Very dissatisfied, Somewhat dissatisfied, Neutral, Somewhat satisfied, Very satisfied” are assigned values of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, respectively [7]. Although life satisfaction is a multifaceted construct, prior research has demonstrated that single-item global assessments can yield comparable validity to multi-item scales, especially in large-scale aging surveys [9, 21]. The single-item format is particularly suitable for older respondents and reduces survey fatigue while capturing core evaluative judgment [2].

Volunteer service

The independent variable in this study is a binary variable indicating whether older adults participate in volunteer activities [57].The questionnaire lists eight types of volunteer activities, including community safety patrols, caring for other older adults and children, environmental protection, mediating neighborhood disputes, companionship and conversation, participation in professional technical volunteer services such as free clinics and cultural and technological promotion, caring for the education of the next generation beyond their own grandchildren, and other specified volunteer activities. Due to the highly skewed distribution of responses—with over 85% of respondents indicating no involvement—a binary indicator was created to distinguish between participation (1 = yes, any volunteering) and non-participation (0 = no). This dichotomization approach is widely adopted in volunteerism literature [29] and emphasizes the behavioral threshold of engagement rather than its intensity [44].

Mediating variables

The mediating variables in this study include social networks and Depressive Symptoms status of older adults. The social network scale developed by Lubben [31] has been widely recognized for measuring social networks. The CLASS questionnaire also uses this scale to measure social networks of older adults, mainly including family networks and friend networks, each measured with three questions. The indicators for the family network section include: (a) “How many family members/relatives can you meet or contact at least once a month?” (b) “With how many family members/relatives can you confidently discuss your private matters?” (c) “When you need it, how many family members/relatives can provide you with help?” The indicators for the friend network section include: (a) “How many friends can you meet or contact at least once a month?” (b) “With how many friends can you confidently discuss your private matters?” (c) “When you need it, how many friends can provide you with help?” The study assigns scores of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 to the answers “none”, “1”, “2”, “3–4”, “5–8”, “9 and above” respectively for the six questions [56],and sums up the scores to construct a measure of older adults’ social networks, with a range of 0–30. The higher the score, the stronger the social network of older adults. In this study, Cronbach’s α is 0.746.

This study assesses the depressive symptoms of older adults with chronic diseases using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depressive Symptoms Scale (CES-D) provided in the questionnaire. It is important to note that depressive symptoms measured through self-report scales do not constitute a clinical diagnosis of depression. The CES-D scale captures the severity of depressive symptomatology rather than establishing psychiatric diagnoses. This scale consists of 9 items, including six negative affect questions covering feelings of loneliness, sadness, lack of appetite, poor sleep, feeling unimportant, and feeling idle over the past week, as well as three positive affect questions about feeling happy, feeling life is going well, and finding enjoyment in life. The 9 items are scored on a three-point scale, with negative affect questions assigned values of 0, 1, and 2 for “never,” “sometimes,” and “often,” respectively. Positive affect questions use reverse scoring. The scores for the 9 items are then summed to create a composite index of depressive symptoms among older adults, with a range from 0 to 18 [25], A higher score indicates a higher level of Depressive Symptoms. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is 0.864.

To enhance conceptual clarity and ensure consistency in the mediation model, all path coefficients are explicitly defined as follows. In the serial mediation model, a₁ denotes the path from volunteer participation to social networks, a₃ represents the path from social networks to depressive symptoms, and b₂ captures the association between depressive symptoms and life satisfaction. The direct effect of volunteer participation on life satisfaction is denoted by c′. Additionally, a₂ denotes the path from volunteer participation to depressive symptoms, and b₁ reflects the path from social networks to life satisfaction. Based on these definitions, the model examines one serial mediation pathway (a₁ × a₃ × b₂) and two parallel mediation paths (a₁ × b₁, a₂ × b₂) through which the total indirect effect of volunteer participation on life satisfaction is estimated.

Covariates

The covariates in this study primarily include: gender (0 = female, 1 = male), marital status (0 = widowed/divorced/single, 1 = married with spouse), living situation (0 = not living alone, 1 = living alone), self-rated health status (0 = unhealthy, including somewhat unhealthy and very unhealthy; 1 = average; 2 = healthy, including somewhat healthy and very healthy), employment status (0 = not engaged in income-generating work, 1 = engaged in income-generating work), and number of living children.

Statistical analyses

The study first conducted descriptive analysis to determine the distribution of characteristics among the participants, calculating frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and mean values and standard deviations for continuous variables. Subsequently, the study employed multiple linear regression to analyze the factors affecting the life satisfaction of older adults. Before testing for mediating effects, the study performed Harman’s single-factor exploratory factor analysis on all items to check for any serious common method bias issues. The study also conducted partial correlation analysis on the items to eliminate the influence between mediating variables and other possible variables, thereby more accurately assessing the mediating effects. The study used the bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method and Hayes’ SPSS macro (Model 6), controlling for sex, marital status, living conditions, self-rated health status, work status, and the number of surviving children. By drawing 5000 samples, the study estimated the 95% confidence interval for the mediating effects. A mediating effect is considered significant when the 95% confidence interval does not include 0 [42]. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. The CLASS project received ethical approval from the Renmin University of China Ethics Committee (Approval No. RER201902). All respondents provided written informed consent before participation. The current study is based on publicly available secondary data and did not involve any direct contact with participants.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 9,828 participants were included in this study, with males and females accounting for 50.3% and 49.7% respectively, resulting in a balanced gender ratio. The majority of older adults were married, comprising 75.68% of the total. A relatively small proportion of older adults, approximately 25.3%, were still engaged in income-generating work. Older adults generally had an optimistic evaluation of their health status, with 49.2% considering their health to be good, 36.0% deeming it average, and only 14.8% rating their health as poor. The average number of living children among older adults included in the study was 2.34. The proportion of older adults who participated in volunteer activities was low, with only about 26.5% having engaged in volunteer services in the past year. The mean social network score was 14.10 with a standard deviation of 5.10, indicating that the overall strength of social networks among older adults was moderate, yet there was significant individual variation. The sample’s Depressive Symptoms score was 6.77, and the life satisfaction score was 3.78, suggesting that older adults included in the sample had a moderately high level of life satisfaction, although there is still room for improvement.

Stepwise regression analysis

The study then used hierarchical regression analysis to establish three models to explore the extent of the impact of various factors on the life satisfaction of older adults. Model 1 only incorporated demographic factors into the regression equation, with an adjusted R2 of 0.008, indicating that demographic factors could only explain 0.8% of the variance in the life satisfaction of older adults. Among them, marital status had a significant impact on life satisfaction; compared to divorced, widowed, or unmarried older adults, those who were married were more satisfied with life. Model 2 added health factors on top of Model 1, with an adjusted R2 of 19.9%, indicating that the inclusion of health factors increased the explanatory variance of the model by 19.1%. Among these, the self-rated health status of older adults was a positive predictor of life satisfaction; the better older adults’ self-rated health status, the higher their satisfaction with life. The Depressive Symptoms status of older adults had a significant negative impact on life satisfaction; the higher the level of Depressive Symptoms, the lower the life satisfaction. Model 3 included social factors on top of the previous two, with an adjusted R2 of 20.4%, indicating that adding social factors increased the explanatory variance of the model by 0.5%. Among these, social networks of older adults, the number of surviving children, and participation in volunteer services all had a significant positive impact on life satisfaction.

Multicollinearity diagnosis

Before conducting the mediation analyses, we performed multicollinearity diagnostics among the predictor variables. Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) for all variables ranged from 1.03 to 1.55, with a mean VIF of 1.20. Since all VIF values were well below the commonly accepted thresholds of 5 or 10, multicollinearity was not a concern in this study.

Harman’s single factor

The study conducted a Harman’s single-factor exploratory factor analysis on the items included in the CLASS questionnaire, extracting four common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first common factor accounted for 18.93% of the variance, which is less than the critical criterion of 40%, indicating that common method bias is not significant in this study.

The results showed that Hypothesis 1 was supported: older adults’ participation in volunteer activities was significantly associated with life satisfaction after controlling for covariates (β = 0.095, p < .001). The total indirect effect of the mediation model was 0.012 (95% CI [0.003, 0.021]), indicating a statistically significant mediating effect. Regarding specific paths, Hypothesis 2 was supported: the indirect effect through social networks was significant (β = 0.004, 95% CI [0.001, 0.006]), suggesting that volunteering enhances life satisfaction by increasing social connectivity. However, Hypothesis 3 was not supported: the indirect effect through depressive symptoms alone was not statistically significant (β = − 0.003, 95% CI [–0.009, 0.002]). This indicates that depressive symptoms do not serve as an independent mediator in this pathway. In contrast, Hypothesis 4 was supported: the serial mediation pathway—volunteer participation → social networks → depressive symptoms → life satisfaction—was significant (β = 0.005, 95% CI [0.002, 0.009]). This suggests a cascading mechanism in which volunteering improves social relationships, which then helps alleviate depressive symptoms, ultimately enhancing subjective well-being.

Robustness test

Given that the 2020 Chinese Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS) dataset used in this study is cross-sectional, robustness tests were performed to mitigate the risk of endogeneity.

Change the sample size

Selective sampling techniques were employed, whereby the samples of older adults with severe depressive symptoms were removed and the chain mediation effect of the remaining samples was examined. Specifically, individuals with a CES-D Depressive Symptoms score greater than 15 were excluded from the analysis. After removing these individuals, the relationship between participation in volunteer activities and life satisfaction remained significant, and the chain mediation effect through social networks and depressive symptoms continued to hold. Further robustness tests were conducted by eliminating samples with poor mental health (i.e., high Depressive Symptoms scores), while keeping the dependent, independent, mediator, and control variables constant. Subsequent mediation tests yielded the same three equations with positive prediction effects, and the mediation effects remained significant, albeit with slightly reduced influence coefficients. The Bootstrap test confirmed that the research results were not subject to significant endogeneity risks, and the conclusions remained robust.

Variable substitution

To further test the robustness of the model, alternative related variables were used to replace some of the key variables. Specifically, the self-rated health variable was replaced with the number of chronic diseases as a substitute for health status. After testing, the β and effect values showed slight changes, but the three mediation effects (social networks, Depressive Symptoms, and the serial mediation effect of social networks + Depressive Symptoms) remained significant, and the research results were consistent. This further strengthens the robustness of the research conclusion.

Supplementary analysis: reverse serial mediation model

To further explore the potential bidirectional relationships among life satisfaction, depressive symptoms, and social networks, an additional serial mediation analysis was conducted. In this supplementary model, life satisfaction was treated as the independent variable, volunteer participation as the dependent variable, with depressive symptoms and social networks serving as sequential mediators.

The analysis revealed that higher levels of life satisfaction significantly predicted lower levels of depressive symptoms (β = -0.9143, SE = 0.0383, P < .001, 95% CI [-0.9892, -0.8393]). In turn, lower depressive symptoms significantly predicted stronger social networks (β = -0.0900, SE = 0.0169, P < .001, 95% CI [-0.1231, -0.0568]). Moreover, stronger social networks were associated with a greater likelihood of volunteer participation (OR = 1.0186, SE = 0.0047, P < .001, 95% CI [1.0094, 1.0279]).

Life satisfaction also exhibited a direct positive effect on volunteer participation (OR = 1.1705, SE = 0.0311, P < .001, 95% CI [1.1095, 1.2316]).

Discussion

This study, drawing on nationally representative data from the CLASS-2020 survey, provides new empirical support for the psychosocial benefits of volunteering among older adults in China. Specifically, we find that volunteer participation is positively associated with life satisfaction, and this relationship is partially mediated by an integrated structural–emotional pathway: volunteering expands older adults’ social networks, which in turn reduce depressive symptoms, ultimately contributing to greater subjective well-being.

Although our data are cross-sectional, the directional assumptions underlying the serial mediation model are grounded in established theory. Life course theory suggests that behavioral engagement, such as volunteering initiates the development of new social roles and strengthens one’s sense of purpose, thereby influencing psychological well-being over time [14]. Social integration theory explains how formal and semi-formal service roles broaden relational networks among older adults [15], while stress-buffering theory accounts for the protective effect of social connectedness on mental health outcomes [12]. Together, these theories justify the assumed causal pathway from volunteering to social networks, which then influence depressive symptoms and, ultimately, life satisfaction.

To address potential concerns regarding reverse causality, we conducted a supplementary analysis testing the reverse mediation pathway (life satisfaction → depression → social ties → volunteering). While this model yielded some significant associations, the indirect effect was weaker and theoretically less consistent. Therefore, the hypothesized directional model remains conceptually and empirically preferable.

While the total effect of volunteering on life satisfaction is modest (β = 0.095), it remains meaningful from a public health perspective. Volunteering is a modifiable behavior, unlike many other predictors of well-being such as health status or family structure [1]. Moreover, for the nearly half of respondents in our sample who scored below the social isolation threshold (Lubben score ≤ 12) [31], even a small boost in network ties can lift them into a lower-risk category. The significant indirect effect (95% CI: 0.002–0.009) through the two-step mechanism suggests that improvements in network embeddedness and emotional resilience can translate into enhanced life satisfaction, even when the improvement is modest in absolute terms.

Our findings also reveal heterogeneity in who participates in volunteering and the benefits they receive. Older adults who engage in volunteer work tend to be younger, healthier, and more socially embedded [48]. They also report higher levels of psychological resilience [33]. These background characteristics amplify the structural and emotional returns of volunteering. For instance, those living with family or having multiple children report higher life satisfaction (β = 0.024), while those living alone do not experience similar benefits [11]. This pattern suggests a “double filter” phenomenon: older adults with greater resources are both more likely to volunteer and more likely to benefit from it [52]. Future research should explore moderated mediation models to quantify how the strength of the pathway varies across health or household subgroups [39].

While the total effect of volunteer participation on life satisfaction is significant, we observed that the pathway via depressive symptoms alone did not achieve statistical significance. This unexpected result warrants further consideration. One possibility is that social networks, which were significantly influenced by volunteer participation, serve as a precursor to emotional improvement. This suggests that volunteering may first enhance social ties, which then create a foundation for emotional resilience and psychological well-being. This finding aligns with Stress-Buffering Theory, which posits that social support functions as an emotional buffer, helping to mitigate the effects of stress and depressive symptoms [12]. Rather than acting as a direct mediator, depressive symptoms may be indirectly influenced by the strengthening of social networks, underscoring the importance of structural factors in emotional health outcomes.

Moreover, this result may reflect the cultural and social context within China, where emotional support is often derived from familial and community networks, rather than being solely an individual psychological process. Social Integration Theory and Life Course Theory emphasize that social roles and community involvement are vital for mental well-being, particularly in older adulthood [3, 39]. In China, volunteerism is deeply rooted in community practices and collectivist values. Local volunteer practices such as “time bank” programs and neighborhood mutual aid groups offer innovative platforms for older adults to engage in social roles and build relational networks [14, 15]. These community-based initiatives blend both formal and informal support systems, enhancing older adults’ access to emotional support, practical assistance, and social engagement. These initiatives not only provide opportunities for meaningful social participation but also serve as important mechanisms for reducing social isolation and depressive symptoms [30].

The “Silver Volunteer Action” and other national programs designed to promote volunteerism among older adults institutionalize the social function of volunteer work, specifically targeting emotional well-being and social integration [29]. These programs provide institutional support for volunteer engagement and enhance the social connectedness of older adults, which in turn helps reduce depressive symptoms and improves life satisfaction. Given this context, the lack of a direct mediation through depressive symptoms in this study may be attributed to the fact that emotional regulation and well-being among older adults in China are often supported by these robust, culturally embedded social structures, rather than solely through individual psychological processes [53].

Thus, the findings from this study underscore the significance of community-based volunteer practices and policy frameworks in shaping the psychosocial benefits of volunteering for older adults in China. These structures may help older adults maintain strong social ties and a sense of purpose, which are critical to emotional resilience and overall well-being. This cultural specificity adds depth to the understanding of how volunteerism can contribute to well-being in later life, emphasizing the importance of local institutional frameworks and community-based interventions. Future research should further explore the cultural and policy contexts of volunteering in China, particularly examining how time banks and neighborhood mutual aid groups function as essential components of the social support structure for older adults. Additionally, incorporating a more nuanced understanding of these local practices and their integration with national policies could enhance the study’s cultural relevance and provide valuable insights for policymakers seeking to strengthen active aging initiatives.

Limitations and future directions

This study has two main limitations. First, the analysis was based on secondary data from the CLASS 2020 survey, and the measures used may not fully capture the complexity of key constructs. For instance, volunteer participation was recorded as a binary variable, without assessing the frequency, type, or context of volunteering. Similarly, social networks were measured using the Lubben Social Network Scale, which emphasizes structural aspects but lacks detail on the functional quality of ties. Future research would benefit from more comprehensive and standardized measures of volunteer activity and social resources.

Second, the use of cross-sectional data limits the ability to draw causal inferences. While the proposed mediation pathway is grounded in existing theory, it remains possible that life satisfaction influences volunteering behavior, or that depressive symptoms and social networks are shaped by prior psychological states. Reverse causation and unmeasured confounders such as personality, prior health, or community factors may also play a role. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the directionality and stability of these associations over time.

Conclusion

Less attention has been paid to the mechanisms through which volunteer participation influences life satisfaction among older adults. Based on a national sample in China, this study found that volunteering was associated with increased life satisfaction, primarily through expanded social networks and reduced depressive symptoms. The key finding suggests that social connectedness and emotional well-being are important pathways linking volunteering to subjective well-being in later life. Programs aiming to promote active aging should consider strengthening social and emotional resources through inclusive volunteer initiatives.
