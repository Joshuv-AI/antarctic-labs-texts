Integrated deep learning-driven multi-stage steam forecasting and scheduling optimization for converter energy systems
Authors: Yan Hu, Baoshan Huang, Chao Gao, Runying Xu, Xuming Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConverter steam is a valuable secondary energy in steelmaking, and improving its recovery and utilization is essential for reducing energy cost. However, converter steam data are inherently time-segmented: before steelmaking starts, only limited planning information is available, while during production, high-frequency process signals drive rapid steam fluctuations. Existing studies mainly rely on single-stage time series forecasting, and rarely combine planning forecasts with sequential production forecasts to support steam scheduling. To address this gap, this study proposes an integrated two-stage, multi-time-scale forecasting framework for converter steam. First, the planning-to-production nature of converter steam generation is explicitly formulated, and a time-segmented forecasting workflow is established to link long-horizon planning prediction with short-horizon rolling prediction. Then, in the planning stage (approximately 2 hours ahead), a hyperparameter-optimized SVM model (IBKA-SVM) is employed to generate interval steam output forecasts, providing a reliable baseline for upper-level scheduling tasks such as steam network target setting and buffer allocation. Finally, in the production stage, a DSC-Transformer model performs fine-grained rolling steam-flow prediction based on time-series production data, and its outputs are used to dynamically update and correct the planning-stage baseline for operational control. Experiments on industrial converter data demonstrate that the proposed models achieve strong predictive performance (IBKA-SVM: 92.3%, DSC-Transformer: 98.7%) and consistently outperform conventional baselines. The proposed framework provides practical, multi-time-scale predictive support for converter steam scheduling and energy-efficient operation.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

China’s steel industry is vast, with crude steel production accounting for more than 50% of global steel production. Steelmaking is one of the world’s largest industrial energy-consuming sectors, highly dependent on energy and resources. As an important secondary energy in iron and steel enterprises, steam is an important medium for energy conversion, power, and heat. Most domesticiron and steel enterprises’ steam energy consumption accounts for about 10% of the total energy consumption of enterprises. As an important secondary energy source, the recovered steam can be used to drive the power equipment in the converter steelmaking process and heat the plant or drive the turbine to generate electricity to realize the efficient utilization of waste heat. The recovery and reuse of steam waste heat in the converter can significantly reduce production costs and improve energy efficiency.

Converter steelmaking involves high temperature and pressure, multi-influencing factors, and intense physical and chemical reactions1. The main task of the modern converter steelmaking process is to smelt the blast furnace molten iron according to a specific proportion of raw materials (scrap steel, molten iron, slag-making materials, etc.) into molten steel whose temperature and composition meets the requirements of each steel class. The standard converter steelmaking production flow chart is shown in Fig. 1, which mainly goes through several stages: Charging, Oxygen blowing, Tapping , Slag splashing, and Slag removal.

Production flow chart of converter steelmaking.

In the converter steelmaking process, steam is mainly generated by the high-temperature reaction in the converter and the high-temperature flue gas cooling system. The high concentration of oxygen is blown into the converter through the oxygen gun so that the oxidation reaction in the converter releases a lot of heat energy, which is transmitted through the water wall of the furnace to the cooling water, resulting in partial evaporation of the cooling water and the generation of steam. At the same time, the high-temperature flue gas discharged by the converter enters the cooling system through the pipe, and the flue gas exchanges heat with the cooling water in the cooling system. After the cooling water absorbs the heat of the flue gas, part of the cooling water evaporates to form steam. The steam generated by each converter flows through the flue gas pipe to the corresponding drum for temporary storage, and the steam in the drum is adjusted to ensure that the temperature and pressure meet the requirements of the heat accumulator before being sent into the heat storage. The recovered steam can be used to drive the power equipment in the converter steelmaking process and heat the plant or drive the turbine to generate electricity to realize the efficient use of waste heat. As an important secondary energy source, the recovery and utilization of steam are also key links for steel enterprises to optimize the production process, save energy, and reduce costs.

However, because the converter steelmaking process contains multiple links, each has its specific process parameters and requirements; the steelmaking process of different steel types is also different, resulting in a complex steelmaking process. In addition, the rhythm of steelmaking production is affected by many factors, such as equipment status and raw material supply, resulting in an unstable production rhythm, further increasing the difficulty of converter steam prediction.

Related work

Accurate prediction of steam recovery is crucial for improving energy utilization and operational efficiency in converter steelmaking. However, due to differences in production processes, smelting modes, and steam-generation characteristics at different steelmaking stages, it remains difficult to establish a purely mechanism-based prediction model for the converter steam network2. With the rapid development of automation systems and industrial databases, large volumes of historical production data can now be collected and stored in real time, providing a solid foundation for data-driven modeling approaches.

Steam forecasting in sequential production phases

Most existing studies focus on steam prediction during the production stage, where high-frequency process data are available and time-series modeling techniques can be effectively applied. Lu Wenbo et al.3 improved the Informer model by integrating extended causal convolution, relative position encoding, seasonal feature encoding, and gradient centralization, achieving a significant reduction in ERMS and producing forecast curves highly consistent with actual steam trends. Zhao Lei et al.4 employed an LSTM neural network to model historical steam production and consumption data from major equipment, achieving prediction errors below 5% under complex operating conditions. Xin Chen et al.5 proposed a dynamic modeling method based on an extended LSTM–GRU structure combined with dynamic time warping (DTW) and causality analysis to identify time-delay parameters affecting steam flow, with absolute errors controlled within 10 t/h.

Other studies have focused on enhancing prediction accuracy through model structure improvement or feature engineering. Chen Guofeng et al.6 constructed a BP neural network for initial prediction and optimized its weights using an improved whale optimization algorithm, resulting in improved convergence speed and accuracy. Yuan Dandan7 extracted informative features through preprocessing and feature engineering of boiler operating data and constructed multiple machine learning models, achieving an RMSE of 0.106 through model fusion. Feng Zhicong et al.8 applied One-class SVM to remove outliers, used the maximum information coefficient to analyze nonlinear correlations among variables, and combined BLS and Lasso regression models via optimal weighted grouping, yielding lower MSE and MAE than single-model approaches. Wang Mengke et al.9 divided the daily production process into multiple operating periods and adopted different prediction strategies for each period, achieving prediction accuracy exceeding 94%.

Overall, these methods demonstrate strong capability in capturing nonlinear temporal patterns during stable production periods. However, they predominantly rely on historical or real-time measurements and generally assume that sufficient production-stage data are available.

Related studies on converter gas prediction

Since steam recovery is closely correlated with by-product gas generation in the converter steelmaking process, gas prediction studies provide useful methodological references. Xie Linrong et al.10 decomposed gas generation signals using empirical wavelet transform and reconstructed components based on sample entropy before applying LSTM prediction, achieving superior accuracy compared with single-model baselines. Bao Xiangjun et al.11 combined LSTM and SARIMA models to construct a gradient-driven composite prediction framework, improving gas utilization efficiency under varying operating conditions. Zhai Hui et al.12 and Zhang Xin et al.13 employed EMD-based decomposition combined with LSTM and LSSVM models, respectively, to enhance prediction performance. Ma Ying et al.14 used an ARMA–ARCH model after differencing treatment to predict converter gas production, significantly reducing gas discharge rates. Zha et al.15 proposed a CNN–LSTM model that integrates convolutional feature extraction with temporal dependency learning, achieving the lowest MAPE among compared models.

Although these studies demonstrate effective prediction performance for converter gas and provide valuable insights for steam-related modeling, they remain focused on single-stage forecasting based on historical data and do not explicitly incorporate production planning information.

Other industry-related research

In summary, most existing steam prediction studies focus on production-stage modeling and direct time-series learning from historical data. They largely overlook the role of the planning stage and rarely exploit planning information for early decision support, thereby limiting their effectiveness in integrated scheduling and energy management.

In contrast, studies in other industries explicitly incorporate planning-stage modeling. Norvaiša et al.16 mitigated the underestimation of wind power variability caused by time-slice averaging in long-term energy planning by embedding wind power output probability curves into bottom-up planning models, enabling early identification of balancing and reserve requirements under unfavorable scenarios during the planning phase.Similarly, Kovačević et al.17,18 developed multi-stage stochastic optimization models that integrate uncertainties in electricity prices, fuel costs, and carbon emission prices into planning-stage decision-making. Case studies demonstrate that these models support advance evaluation of price volatility impacts on generation strategies, costs, and emissions, thereby enhancing the robustness of medium- to long-term production plans.

Inspired by these ideas, this paper introduces a two-stage steam prediction framework that explicitly connects planning-stage interval forecasting with production-stage rolling prediction, thereby providing continuous and multi-time-scale predictive support for converter steam scheduling.

Data analysis and processing

Data preprocessing

The production data obtained from the steel plant site is subject to many factors, such as acquisition equipment, transmission links, etc., and there are often data quality problems, such as data missing, data inconsistency, data duplication, and inconsistent features. Data quality has an important impact on model training. Therefore, it is necessary to preprocess data with low data quality before model training and clean data of missing data, outliers, and duplicate values to improve data set quality. In addition to standard data problems in industrial field data such as steel mills, there are also data inconsistency problems; that is, the data features required by the model are not in the same table, and the industrial data at different stages are stored in different tables. However, the data of different time dimensions need to be aligned when the model is trained. Therefore, it is necessary to preprocess the data to obtain reliable model learning samples. In the historical data of the converter, the data of raw material composition and process parameters before and after steelmaking in different stages are recorded in detail, which provides abundant data support for model training. All the data used in this study came from 50 days of production data of a steel mill starting from November 2024, and 1100 converter production data were obtained after data preprocessing. The data set is divided into training set, test set, and verification set in a ratio of 7:1.5:1.5.

Smelting mode analysis

In the process of converter steelmaking, converter steam has significant dynamic change characteristics in each stage of smelting, and its production intensity and duration not only reflect the intensity of the smelting reaction but are also closely related to process parameters. There are apparent differences in metallurgical reaction paths and control strategies among different converter smelting modes, which results in heterogeneous steam release behavior. In the process of converter smelting, due to the different production technologies, the converter smelting mode is divided into conventional decarburization, double dephosphorization, and double decarburization, and the steam production amount, production time, and trend are different in different smelting modes. The converter steelmaking process mainly includes five stages: charging, oxygen blowing, steel pumping, slag splashing, and slag discharging. The variation trend of steam flow is closely related to each stage of steelmaking, and the steam generation in each stage has its characteristics.

In conventional mode, the loading stage consists of loading scrap and hot metal, which lasts about 15 min. The reaction in the furnace has not yet begun to produce steam, and the steam production is zero. Then, enter the oxygen-blowing stage, the most important steam production stage. The amount of steam generated accounts for more than 90% of the total steam in the entire steelmaking process; the entire oxygen-blowing stage lasts about 15 min; in this stage, the steam flow does not continue to rise, but a multi-stage rise; after reaching the peak began to decline. The slag splashing stage is between 1 and 5 min, there will be a small amount of steam flow, steam generation is relatively stable, and the fluctuation is slight. However, the duration is short, mostly 1 to 2 min, and very few furnace times will last 3 to 5 min. There is no steam generation in the other three stages. Methods and models

System framework

In order to cope with the complexity and dynamic change of steam demand in the converter production process. This paper proposes a stream forecasting model based on time series segmentation to improve the accuracy and reliability of steam demand forecasting. This method entirely takes into account the role of planning stage prediction on enterprise regulation and control, as well as the influence of different smelting modes and steelmaking stages on steam fluctuations in the sequential production stage; different deep learning methods are adopted at different stages to adapt to different data characteristics and forecasting requirements19, as shown in Fig. 7.

System frame diagram.

In the converter steam prediction model, different models are responsible for the objectives of different periods, providing prediction results and adjustment basis for subsequent stages20. In the planning stage (2 hours before production), the black-wing kite algorithm (BKA) was improved using Latin hypercube sampling, directional differential variation, and Gaussian Cuckoo variation distribution. The SVM model was combined to predict the interval steam of multiple furnace times, optimize the production plan and energy scheduling, and ensure the steam supply and demand match. In the sequential production stage, combined with the dynamic serpentine convolution and Transformer model, the deformable convolution kernel of the dynamic serpentine convolution can accurately and dynamically learn the changes of the sequential data in different smelting modes and different production stages, and the data of the furnace times can be combined to make dynamic prediction of the steam flow rate. Achieve accurate regulation of steam supply, optimize the control of parameters (temperature, pressure, etc.) in the steelmaking process, timely warning when anomalies are found (such as excessive steam recovery, exceeding the storage capacity of the heat accumulator, the need to send steam in advance), reduce energy waste, and improve the overall production efficiency.

Planning stage

Support vector machine regression

Support vector machine (SVM)21is a machine learning method applicable to both classification and regression tasks, whose core idea is to construct an optimal hyperplane that maximizes the margin between samples, thereby improving the generalization ability of the model. When the data are not linearly separable, SVM employs kernel functions22 (such as the radial basis function, RBF) to map the input data into a high-dimensional feature space, where nonlinear problems can be transformed into linearly separable ones.

In the planning stage of converter steelmaking, the input features mainly include steel grade (STEEL_GRADE), molten iron weight (IRON_WEIGHT), oxygen blowing time (OXYGEN_TIME), and converter station identifier (STATION_ID). These features are low-dimensional and vary relatively smoothly over time. Such data characteristics are well aligned with the strengths of SVM in modeling low-dimensional and small-sample problems, enabling the model to effectively capture potential nonlinear relationships while avoiding overfitting. As a result, SVM provides a stable and efficient approach for predicting converter steam output at the planning stage.

IBKA-SVM

The performance of traditional support vector machine (SVM) models largely depends on the choice of hyperparameters27, especially the penalty parameter C, the type of kernel function, and the parameters of the kernel function28. These hyperparameters have a significant impact on SVM training results and generalization ability. In order to improve the modeling ability of SVM and optimize the hyperparameter configuration, an efficient and stable hyperparameter optimization strategy is proposed by integrating the improved IBKA algorithm into SVM. First, Latin hypercube sampling is introduced to enhance the diversity of the population in the initialization phase of the black-winged kites algorithm so that the initial hyperparameters cover the search space evenly and avoid search bias in the local area. Then, in the process of individual updating, the search accuracy is enhanced through the information exchange between individuals combined with reverse difference variation, which ensures that the hyperparameter optimization process of SVM converges towards a better solution. In addition, to prevent the optimization from falling into local optimality, Gaussian Cuckoo variation is introduced in the late convergence of the algorithm. The searchability is enhanced through local perturbation and Levy flight so that individuals can jump out of local extreme points, further improving the global exploration and final optimization effect of the algorithm.

Time series prediction stage

The important task of Bessemer prediction is accurately predicting the steam recovery in advance. In the process of converter smelting, the amount of steam recovery is closely related to the parameters, such as added raw materials and technology, as well as the smelting mode of the converter. Due to the different production technologies, the smelting mode of the converter is divided into conventional decarburization, double dephosphorization, and double decarburization, and the steam production amount, generation time, and trend are different under different smelting modes. The variation trend of steam flow is also closely related to each steelmaking stage under different smelting modes, and the steam generation in each stage has its characteristics.

In the sequential production phase of the converter, the sequential characteristics of the converter include level 1 (LIQUID_LEVEL1), level 2 (LIQUID_LEVEL2), steam temperature (STEAM_TEMPERATURE), steam pressure (STEAM_PRESSURE), and the converter treatment number (STATION_ID). In order to better realize the timing prediction of converter steam, combined with the above analysis, an advanced deep learning model is proposed to be adopted in this study. The model uses dynamic serpentine convolution (DSC) to extract data features, and the dynamic convolution operation is used to capture the dynamic change trend in the steam time sequence so as to identify the dynamic fluctuation of the data effectively. The multi-view feature fusion strategy is used to realize further the multi-angle feature extraction of steam data, which helps the model understand the complexity of data from different levels.

Dynamic snake convolution

Dynamic snake convolution(DSC) is a machine vision method proposed by Yaolei Qi et al.29 in 2023 to solve the problem that tubular structures such as blood vessels in medicine are usually elongated and tortuous, take a small proportion in the image and are easy to be disturbed by complex background, making it difficult to capture images30. The dynamic serpentine convolution has multiple advantages in the timing prediction of converter steam: In the face of complex steam data of different stages or different modes, the dynamic convolution kernel of DSC adapt the size of the convolution kernel to the data fluctuation characteristics of different modes and different periods, to learn dynamic changes in steam data more accurately and effectively, and alleviate the impact of different modes and different smelting stages on model prediction. The model is more robust under the complex conditions of handling multiple smelting modes. Dynamic serpentine convolution can also automatically adjust the weights of each feature view on different time scales through multi-view feature fusion to achieve automatic feature selection and weighted fusion. The model can choose the best feature adaptively according to the changes in different stages, patterns, or data features, thus improving the efficiency and accuracy of the model. In addition, it can also pay attention to the short-term and long-term dependence of steam data. By integrating features of different time scales, the model can more accurately capture short-term fluctuations, understand long-term trends more effectively, and enhance the modeling ability of time series data.

The steam generation process in converter production is complex and has significant nonlinear and time series fluctuation characteristics, and the steam output in different stages is significantly different. There is no steam production in many stages, and the reaction gradually intensifies in the oxygen-blowing stage. Steam production increases rapidly and tends to stabilize or even decline in the later stage. Conventional fixed convolution, which uses convolution cores of fixed size and shape, is suitable for handling specific features but lacks flexibility in capturing complex fluctuations in steam. Variable convolution focuses on the simultaneous extraction of features at different scales and uses multiple preset convolution kernels suitable for scenes with substantial feature diversity. However, only a limited number of convolution kernels can be used, and it is not flexible enough to face complex fluctuations under multiple smelting modes. Unlike the traditional fixed convolution kernel and deformable convolution (see Fig. 11), the dynamic serpentine convolution allows the shape and size of the convolution kernel to be dynamically adjusted according to the characteristics of the input data. The sensing range can be flexibly adjusted according to different stages of the vapor data change to capture information at different scales (see Fig. 12). At different stages of smelting, the dynamic convolution kernel adaptively adjusts according to the data fluctuation characteristics, ensuring that the model uses a more extensive range of convolution kernels to capture trends in the high steam fluctuation stage and uses small-scale convolution kernels in the low fluctuation stage to improve the resolution, pay more attention to minor changes, and meet the prediction needs of different stages so that it can better adapt to the characteristics of the input data–flexibility to capture complex structures and diverse features.

Time series forecasting model

Transformer is a new neural network architecture proposed by Vaswani et al.31 at the NIPS conference 2017. Transformer is a model based entirely on an attention mechanism for processing sequence transcription tasks. The Transformer model does away with the traditional loop (RNNS) and convolutional (CNN) structures and instead relies entirely on attention mechanisms to capture global dependencies between inputs and outputs. The Transformer consists of N encoders and N decoders. Encoders and decoders combine techniques such as self-attention mechanism, multi-head attention mechanism, and residual linkage.

In Transformer, the self-attention mechanism is the core way to capture context within a sequence, associating each element with other elements through Query, Key, and Value vectors to provide global context information for each location. The multi-head attention mechanism enhances self-attention, allowing the model to compute self-attention in parallel in multiple subspaces. The input Query, Key, and Value vectors are split into multiple ”heads,” each of which computes the attention distribution independently in a different subspace to capture multiple semantic relationships in the sequence. Finally, the output of these heads is connected and linearly transformed to produce the final result. This enables the model to more fully represent the rich information in the sequence, enabling the Transformer to more fully capture the multiple potential semantic associations in the sequence.

The DSC-Transformer algorithm proposed in this paper combines the Transformer model and dynamic serpentine convolution, which can better process time series data. The overall model is mainly divided into Encoder (Encoder) and Decoder (Decoder), two symmetric parts; each part contains multiple Layers (Layers). In the encoder, the dynamic serpentine convolution extracts local features to capture the dynamic change trend in the time series. With the deepening of network layers, the model can capture more extensive temporal feature information. Shallow convolution focuses on local features and short-term changes, while deep network layers extract global features and long-term trends. The multi-view feature fusion layer fuses features of different levels to ensure the full use of all information to achieve the best prediction effect.

By dynamically adjusting the convolutional kernel, the DSC layer can be abstracted layer by layer from low frequency to high frequency. The features in the sequence can be extracted from coarse-grained to fine-grained so that the model can effectively identify the dynamic changing features in the time series data and output these features into the fused feature vectors, providing rich timing information for the subsequent encoder layer.

DSC realizes dynamic feature extraction from time series data. The DSC layer can capture local dynamic changes in data on different time scales, adapting to frequent fluctuations and trends in time series. By dynamically adjusting the convolutional kernel, the DSC layer can be abstracted layer by layer from low frequency to high frequency. The features in the sequence can be extracted from coarse-grained to fine-grained so that the model can effectively identify the dynamic changing features in the time series data and output these features into the fused feature vectors, providing rich timing information for the subsequent encoder layer.

The encoder’s main task is to convert dynamic features into hidden representations to capture temporal context information. The encoder consists of multiple feature extraction layers, which are reduced one by one to abstract the input features layer by layer. The whole encoder structure is divided into four layers, composed of several convolutional networks, pooling layers, and residual modules. Data features are abstracted and extracted step by step by setting different parameters such as convolution kernel size and convolution step size. The structure design of the encoder can ensure that the model gradually abstracts and integrates the local and global information of the data from the bottom to the high level and makes full use of the dynamic characteristics provided by DSC.

The main task of the decoder is to recover the original data characteristics and location information to solve the problem of positioning and detail restoration in the time series. The decoder consists of several levels, and the level size increases layer by layer. The overall structure of the decoder is divided into four layers, and the encoder output is reconstructed by layer-by-layer sampling and direction convolution. After up-sampling, the spatial resolution of the data is gradually restored, and the details are retained, but some location information may be lost or offset during the coding process. Therefore, the decoder combines the dynamic features extracted by the encoder and DSC to enhance the location information’s fidelity and accuracy, ensuring that each location’s feature information in the time series data can be restored and accurately positioned.

The encoder and decoder output features are fused with multi-view features to integrate dynamic information at different levels and scales. Through the ReLU activation function, the model performs nonlinear processing on the fused features and calculates the predicted value of each time step. The multi-view feature fusion ensures the model’s balance in capturing short-term fluctuations and long-term trends. It enables the model to accurately predict the dynamic changes of steam flow, providing more comprehensive and stable support for the final steam timing forecast. By leveraging the multi-scale features extracted by DSC at each time step, the Transformer can further capture complex dependencies in time series data, providing more comprehensive and accurate feature support for time series prediction. The experiment used the 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-12400F 2.50 GHz, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU, the Python 3.8 programming environment, and the PyTorch deep learning framework. The development was carried out using PyCharm 2024.1.3 (Professional Edition). This paper adopts several evaluation indexes to comprehensively evaluate the model’s performance. These include the coefficient of determination (R-Square), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The corresponding calculation method is shown in formulas to .

, MAPE, MAE, and RMSE are commonly used to evaluate the performance of regression models. measures the model’s ability to interpret the target value, with values ranging from 0 to 1, with the closer to 1 indicating the better the model fits the data. MAPE is an indicator used to assess the accuracy of predictive models, especially for forecasting time series data. The model’s predictive performance is measured by calculating the percentage of absolute difference between the predicted value and the actual value. MAE quantifies the average absolute error between the predicted and actual values, reflecting the degree of deviation from the model prediction. The smaller the MAE value is, the more accurate the model prediction is. RMSE retains the original unit of error and more intuitively represents the average difference between the predicted and actual values. By combining these indexes, the model’s fitting performance and prediction accuracy can be comprehensively evaluated, and the model’s applicability in different scenarios can be judged.

Through the above training process and evaluation method, the model’s learning and prediction performance in processing different time window data can be understood in detail, providing a scientific basis for predicting and optimizing the converter steelmaking process.

Conclusion

Based on the converter steelmaking process, this study establishes a steam forecasting framework that can not only accurately predict the future fluctuation trend of steam recovery at the furnace level, but also perform steam flow prediction during production. By integrating planning-stage and production-stage forecasting, the proposed model provides data support for the dynamic balance of the converter steam pipeline network, contributing to system safety and improved energy utilization efficiency.

Considering the distinct characteristics of steam data at different production periods, this paper analyzes the limitations of traditional data-driven models in handling heterogeneous sample distributions and their impact on prediction accuracy. By combining process mechanism analysis and data-driven modeling, the key influencing factors of converter steam recovery at different stages are identified, and a period-aware steam prediction method is developed. The effectiveness of the proposed approach is validated using historical production data from an industrial steel plant, and the main contributions and findings can be summarized as follows:

Planning-stage contribution: An IBKA-SVM model is constructed to predict the interval-level steam output approximately two hours in advance, providing reliable guidance for upper-level scheduling and steam network planning. Experimental results show that the proposed planning-stage model achieves a prediction accuracy of 92.3%.

Planning-stage contribution: An IBKA-SVM model is constructed to predict the interval-level steam output approximately two hours in advance, providing reliable guidance for upper-level scheduling and steam network planning. Experimental results show that the proposed planning-stage model achieves a prediction accuracy of 92.3%.

Production-stage contribution: After oxygen blowing starts, a DSC-Transformer model is employed to refine and dynamically update steam flow predictions using furnace-specific information and time-series data. The model achieves a prediction accuracy of 98.7%, and demonstrates stable performance under different sliding-window settings, indicating strong robustness and adaptability in real production environments.
